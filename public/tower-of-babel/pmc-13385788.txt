SNAP-23 mediated vesicular trafficking in oligodendrocytes is necessary to maintain adult myelin integrity in mice
Authors: Chun Hin Chow, Mengjia Huang, Aryan Regmi, Jayant Rai, Hidekiyo Harada, Sarah Eide, Hong-Shuo Sun, Zhong-Ping Feng, Philippe P Monnier, Kenichi Okamoto, Liang Zhang, Olga L Rojas, Shuzo Sugita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdult myelin is dynamic and requires continuous support from oligodendrocytes. Inability to sustain myelin health results in demyelination, evident in multiple sclerosis and neurodegenerative diseases. However, the molecular mechanism by which oligodendrocytes recycle and replenish materials to the myelin sheath remains unclear. Here, we investigate whether the SNARE-dependent vesicular trafficking is important for myelin turnover. We conditionally remove SNAP-23, one of the target SNARE proteins, in mature oligodendrocytes of adult mice. Induction of SNAP-23 deletion causes demyelination within 5-10 weeks. Demyelination is associated with an increase in immune cells, including infiltrated T cells, in the brain. Mechanistically, the removal of SNAP-23 in oligodendrocytes impairs vesicle fusion to the myelin and causes the accumulation of myelin proteins within the cells. Taken together, we show that SNAP-23-dependent transport in oligodendrocytes is necessary for adult myelin maintenance, with failure in vesicular transport leading to demyelination and subsequent neuroinflammation.Subject terms: Oligodendrocyte, Neuroimmunology, Myelin biology and repair

Introduction

Myelin is a lipidic insulation surrounding axons in the central nervous system (CNS) and peripheral nervous system (PNS) to allow saltatory conduction of electrical signals. Loss of myelin in demyelinating diseases, such as multiple sclerosis (MS) and neuromyelitis optica (NMO), leads to profound sensory, motor, and cognitive deficits1. Furthermore, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease, and traumatic brain injuries all demonstrate myelin deficits in the brain2–4. Thus, the ability to maintain myelin health in adults and aging is critical. Unlike neurons, the myelin in adults is highly dynamic in healthy and MS settings5,6. Nevertheless, the basis of how myelin integrity is sustained has not yet been determined.

In the CNS, oligodendrocytes are the glial cells responsible for myelin synthesis and formation. Myelin is composed of 70% lipids and 30% proteins. Proteolipid protein (PLP) and myelin basic protein (MBP) are the most abundant proteins in CNS myelin7. Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) and myelin-associated glycoprotein (MAG) are important glycoproteins for the structure and axon-myelin contact8,9. During development, oligodendrocytes synthesize lipids, cholesterol and proteins to form the myelin sheath10–12. The myelin membrane grows by axonal wrapping via vesicular addition, followed by subsequent compaction13. Wrapping by oligodendrocytes depends on regulated actin dynamics14,15. While the process of myelination is well studied during development, the process of how mature myelin is added and replaced after myelination is established remains elusive.

The maintenance of adult myelin requires oligodendrocytes and the synthesis of new myelin materials. Targeting oligodendrocyte death in adult mice triggers CNS demyelination16–19. Thus, oligodendrocytes are not only necessary during myelin formation but also for sustaining myelin integrity. Conditionally removing myelin gene regulatory factor (MYRF) in mature oligodendrocytes blocks the transcription of important myelin genes, including proteolipid protein and myelin basic protein, which ultimately impacts the generation of critical myelin components leading to damage to adult myelin20. Beyond the disruption of protein synthesis, disruption of lipid synthesis and metabolism by removing Quaking (Qki) in oligodendrocytes similarly impairs adult myelin integrity21. PLP and MBP are also demonstrated to be under continuous turnover in myelin, while removing their replenishment leads to slow myelin damage and axon abnormalities with aging22,23. As such, mature oligodendrocytes synthesizing myelin lipids and proteins is fundamental to adult myelin maintenance. However, the molecular mechanisms of how newly synthesized materials are delivered during the turnover process are unclear.

One hypothesis on myelin materials replenishment during turnover is vesicular trafficking24. During development, myelin is added to the existing sheath via vesicular fusion starting at the inner tongue, the leading edge of oligodendrocytes13. Vesicle fusion relies on SNARE (soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor) proteins that provide the physical force driving the fusion between membranes25. The SNARE complex consists of three components, VAMP that located on the vesicle as the vesicular-SNARE (v-SNARE), as well as syntaxin and SNAP-25 family members situated on the target membrane as the target-SNAREs (t-SNAREs). SNARE proteins are thought to be involved in protein trafficking in oligodendrocytes. Studies using in vitro oligodendrocyte cell culture suggest that syntaxin-3 along with VAMP-3 and VAMP-7 transport PLP to the membrane surface26,27. Meanwhile, syntaxin-4 was reported to facilitate MBP surface expression28. Independent of protein trafficking, in vivo conditional cleavage of VAMP-2/3 in oligodendrocyte lineage cells during development causes severe hypomyelination, with addition impacts to cell maturation and myelination are impacted29–31. Hence, SNAREs seem to be pivotal in oligodendrocytes and their precursor cell in myelin formation. Nevertheless, the role of SNARE-dependent vesicular transport in myelin maintenance and turnover is unknown.

Here, we investigate if SNAP-23, a t-SNARE protein, is necessary for oligodendrocytes to maintain myelin integrity by creating an inducible knockout of SNAP-23 from mature oligodendrocytes in adult mice (SNAP-23 icKO). We observed demyelination in SNAP-23 icKO peaking after 5 weeks of knockout induction with motor, structural, and functional deficits in the CNS. The demyelination induced by SNAP-23 icKO is accompanied by CD4+ and CD8+ T cell infiltration, as well as an increase in F4/80+ microglia/macrophages in the brain. Oligodendrocytes lacking SNAP-23 displayed vesicular impairment in the axon-myelin structure and mislocalization of MOG and MAG proteins. Overall, we demonstrate the role of SNAP-23-dependent vesicular trafficking in adult myelin maintenance.

Results

SNAP-23 removal in mature oligodendrocytes in adults manifests motor and behavioural deficits

To investigate the impact of disrupting vesicular transport in oligodendrocytes, we utilized the Plp1-CreERT line to target inducible knockout in mature myelinating oligodendrocytes32. Tamoxifen application allows for temporal control of the timing of knockout, such that SNAP-23 is removed after myelination is complete. We generated the Plp1-CreERT;SNAP-23flox/flox mouse line to conditionally remove SNAP-23 in mature oligodendrocytes (SNAP-23 icKO) (Fig. 1a). We induced knockout at the age of 3 to 4-week-old. Genotyping by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) of brain cerebellum samples after tamoxifen injection showed the recombinant band in the SNAP-23 icKO but not in the SNAP-23flox/flox (Ctrl) (Fig. 1b). To confirm the knockout strategy was successful, we introduced the Ai9 reporter to the SNAP-23 icKO by crossing SNAP-23 icKO mice with the Ai9 line (Plp1-CreERT; SNAP-23flox/flox; Ai9/+), wherein Cre-mediated recombination induces tdTomato expression. CC1 antibody staining, which labelled mature oligodendrocytes, showed vast majority of oligodendrocytes were Ai9+ in the optic nerves, cerebellum and corpus callosum 2 weeks post tamoxifen injection (pti) (Figs. 1c and S1a). To verify the removal of SNAP-23, we performed immunohistochemistry on SNAP-23 with CC1 in optic nerves 2–4 weeks pti (Fig. 1d). We detected a significant decrease in SNAP-23 protein levels in CC1+ cells (Fig. 1e, f). Therefore, our mouse model had an efficient knockout of SNAP-23 targeting oligodendrocytes.

To evaluate the consequence of removing SNAP-23 in oligodendrocytes, we tracked the mice for motor and behavioural deficits weekly pti. We quantified the deficits scoring the mice on tremor, limp tail, hindlimb weakness and paresis21. The development of motor deficits started 2 to 3 weeks pti with a peak of clinical scores at 5–10 weeks pti in both male and female mice (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Videos 1 and 2). Accompanied by the tremor and hindlimb weakness, weight loss was prominent in SNAP-23 icKO in both sexes (Fig. S1b). Further, at 4 to 5 weeks pti, SNAP-23 icKO performed worse than the control in the accelerating rotarod task, in which they were falling significantly earlier throughout the four-day paradigm (Fig. 1h). While by 3 weeks, most of the myelination ensheathment has been established, myelination happens continuously to adulthood33,34. To avoid myelin development impairment as a confound, we repeated the knockout by injecting tamoxifen into 8-week-old male and female adult mice (Fig. S1c). SNAP-23 icKO in 8-week-old adults also manifested motor deficits and weight loss in both sexes (Fig. S1d, e and Supplementary Videos 3 and 4). The clinical scoring in females was higher than in males, indicating a more severe motor deficit in females (3-way ANOVA, genotype and sex interaction was significant, p < 0.0001). Given the similar phenotype between 3 weeks and 8 weeks old SNAP-23 icKO, experiments were performed in mice with induction at 3 weeks old unless otherwise specified. We speculated that the motor defects and weight loss were attributed to demyelination caused by SNAP-23 removal in myelinating oligodendrocytes.

CNS demyelination in SNAP-23 icKO

Given the tremor and motor weakness of SNAP-23 icKO, we evaluated demyelination by performing structural and ultrastructural analyses on CNS white matter 5–8 weeks pti, matching the peak phase of motor deficits and weight loss. Optic nerves had a dramatic reduction in myelination with a decrease observed by both Luxol fast blue (LFB) and FluoroMyelin staining (Fig. 2a–d). The cerebellum also demonstrated significant demyelination in the white matter by LFB (Fig. 2e, f). Meanwhile, myelination in the corpus callosum was largely intact (Fig. S2a, b). Similarly, we verified demyelination in the optic nerves and cerebellum rather than corpus callosum in 8-week-old adult induced SNAP-23 icKO at 7 weeks pti with LFB staining (Fig. S2c–h). To identify the ultrastructural deficits of myelination, we imaged optic nerve cross-sections by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 2g). A significant reduction in the density of myelinated axons in SNAP-23 icKO was observed compared to the control. Relatively smaller axons with sizes 0.3–0.6 µm were particularly demyelinated in the SNAP-23 icKO (Fig. 2h, i). Meanwhile, some larger myelinated axons remained intact in SNAP-23 icKO. Therefore, the relative proportion of myelinated axons with larger axonal size was higher in the SNAP-23 icKO than in the control. To evaluate the myelin sheath thickness, we quantified the g-ratio, the ratio of total myelinated axon diameter over axon diameter. SNAP-23 icKO optic nerves had a larger g-ratio, indicating a thinner myelin ensheathment (Fig. 2j, k). Together, these structural abnormalities together point towards demyelination in the CNS of SNAP-23 icKO mice.

Demyelination can be due to the loss or death of oligodendrocytes16,17,19. SNAP-23 is known to be critical for certain cell survival35,36, hence removing SNAP-23 in oligodendrocytes might lead to cell loss and subsequently demyelination. To inspect for potential cell loss, we first quantified the number of mature oligodendrocytes. In the optic nerves and cerebellum, there was no significant difference in the number of CC1+ oligodendrocytes (Figs. 2l–n and S2i). TUNEL staining for apoptotic cells revealed no programmed cell death in the optic nerves 5–8 weeks pti in both 3-week-old and 8-week-old induced knockout (Fig. S2j, k). Thus, the demyelination was not a result of oligodendrocyte death due to the absence of SNAP-23. However, aging to 6 months pti, we observed TUNEL-positive cells in SNAP-23 icKO optic nerves (Fig. S3a). In both 3-week- and 8-week-old induced SNAP-23 icKO, there were elevated amounts of TUNEL+ apoptotic cells compared to the respective control (Fig. S3a–c). CC1 staining of oligodendrocytes 6 months pti confirmed a decrease in oligodendrocytes (Fig. S3d–g). Therefore, the late cell death in the white matter might signify eventual damage to oligodendrocytes in SNAP-23 icKO, leading to a loss of oligodendrocytes with aging.

As our knockout approach targeted mature oligodendrocytes in adults, we anticipated that demyelination instead of hypomyelination would happen in SNAP-23 icKO. In other words, myelin development is normal until we induce the SNAP-23 icKO. At 2-week pti, whereas motor deficits had yet to arise, FluoroMyelin staining of optic nerves was comparable between the control and the knockout (Fig. S4a, b). TEM also indicated a similar level of myelination in the optic nerves (Fig. S4c). The number of oligodendrocytes at this point was comparable between control and SNAP-23 icKO (Fig. S4d, e), suggesting no early loss of cells. Therefore, removal of SNAP-23 in oligodendrocytes at the adult stage does not alter myelin development, rather, it induces demyelination.

Plp1-CreERT can drive recombination in Schwann cells, myelinating cells in the peripheral nervous system (PNS)32. Thus, motor deficits could be impacted by PNS demyelination. We observed recombined Ai9+ cells in the sciatic nerves, which are peripheral myelinated fibres (Fig. S5a). Intriguingly, SNAP-23 icKO mice gradually turned from black fur to white fur in coat colouring (Fig. S5b). Changes in fur colour could be due to the re-differentiation of Schwann cells to pigmenting melanocytes upon nerve damage37. Thus, we examined if the PNS had myelin damage. We performed myelin staining in the sciatic nerves 5–8 weeks pti. FluoroMyelin and LFB showed no signs of demyelination in the sciatic nerves (Fig. S5c–f). TEM of sciatic nerve cross-sections in SNAP-23 icKO also demonstrated comparable myelination as control (Fig. S5g). Therefore, SNAP-23 in Schwann cells seems to be dispensable in maintaining healthy myelination in the PNS. We conclude that SNAP-23 in oligodendrocytes regulates CNS myelin without causing cell death.

Demyelination in SNAP-23 icKO impairs functional conduction in the CNS

Myelination is critical for rapid nerve conduction in information relay and synchronization38. To investigate the functional consequences of demyelination, we performed ex vivo recordings of compound action potential (CAP) in the optic nerves 5–10 weeks pti. In the control, we obtained the optic nerves CAP profile with three peaks, P1, P2, and P3 corresponding to the largest to smallest myelinated axons. However, CAP in the SNAP-23 icKO had a delayed P1 and P2 with an altered P3 waveform (Fig. 3a). Latencies to peak were larger and amplitude was smaller in SNAP-23 icKO compared to the control (Fig. 3b, c). The full width at half-maximum (FWHM) of SNAP-23 icKO was significantly larger than that of the control (Fig. 3d), indicating wider waveforms with altered synchrony in nerve conduction. The stimulus artefact remained unchanged, suggesting the weak conduction in SNAP-23 icKO was not due to failure in nerve stimulation (Fig. 3e).

Beyond ex vivo electrophysiology, we measured in vivo visual evoked potential (VEP) to assess signal conduction across the whole visual pathway39. SNAP-23 icKO showed a significant delay in N1 latency and a smaller N1 amplitude compared to the control (Figs. 3f–h and S6a, b). Furthermore, we confirmed that SNAP-23 icKO induced in 8-week-old adult displayed VEP conduction deficits like SNAP-23 icKO induced at 3 to 4 weeks old. A delayed and smaller N1 was observed in 8-week-old induced SNAP-23 icKO 5–8 weeks pti (Fig. S6c–g). Therefore, SNAP-23 icKO caused conduction impairment with demyelination.

Action potential generation and propagation in myelinated axons happen at the nodes of Ranvier. We hypothesized that demyelination induced by SNAP-23 icKO can alter the arrangement of the nodes and lead to conduction deficits. To visualize the nodes of Ranvier in the optic nerves, we performed immunostaining of Ankyrin G (AnkG) and CASPR, which are situated at the node and paranodes, respectively (Fig. 3i). A mature node of Ranvier consists of a node flanked by two paranodes40,41. While the control optic nerves comprised mainly mature nodes, the SNAP-23 icKO has a decrease in the number of mature nodes and increase in CASPR-only paranode clusters. No changes in heminode (AnkG with CASPR on one side) and node only (AnkG only) were observed (Fig. 3j). Hence, demyelination induced by impaired oligodendrocytes disrupted nodes of Ranvier arrangement and subsequently nerve conduction.

Glia activation and immune cell infiltration into the CNS in SNAP-23 icKO

While oligodendrocytes are responsible for the production of myelin sheaths in the CNS, they are also mainly passive targets of the adaptive immune system attack to myelin in the context of neurodegenerative diseases such as MS and AD42. We investigated if central immune response as well as adaptive immune response, occurs with oligodendrocytes-induced demyelination 5–8 weeks pti. Iba-1 labelling of microglia/macrophages indicated an elevated Iba-1+ cell density in the optic nerves and cerebellum of SNAP-23 icKO (Figs. 4a, b and S7a). Despite no overt demyelination in the corpus callosum, Iba-1+ microglia density was elevated (Fig. S7b, c). Examining Iba-1+ microglia morphology from the optic nerves, microglia in the SNAP-23 icKO had larger cell bodies and were less branched than the control, indicating an activated state of microglia (Fig. 4c–e). On the other hand, GFAP staining of astrocyte coverage also increased in the white matter of optic nerves, cerebellum, and corpus callosum (Figs. 4f, g and S7d–f). The increase to microglia was absent in SNAP-23 icKO at 2 weeks pti (Fig. S7j), aligning with the normal myelination at that stage. Thus, a local CNS inflammatory response of microglia and astrocytes was present in SNAP-23 icKO, likely driven by oligodendrocytes and myelin lesions.

In addition to CNS resident glia, peripheral adaptive immune response was also assessed, showing infiltration of CD3+ T cells in the myelin of optic nerves, cerebellum, and corpus callosum (Figs. 5a, b and S7g–i). An intact blood-brain barrier (BBB) should protect the CNS from peripheral immune cell penetration. However, demyelination, especially in the context of MS and autoimmune demyelinating disorders, can recruit and activate T cells and B cells to exacerbate myelin damage43. With the presence of T lymphocytes in the CNS parenchyma, we hypothesized that SNAP-23 icKO and the subsequent myelin damage facilitates lymphocyte infiltration in the brain, which plays a further role in demyelination. To better characterize the immune response, we performed flow cytometry to quantify various immune cell subsets in the brain (Figs. 5c and S8a). Compared to the control, SNAP-23 icKO had significantly elevated amounts of CD45high F4/80+ macrophages and microglia and CD45high F4/80− immune cells in the brain (Fig. 5d, e). CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells were increased in the brain of SNAP-23 icKO (Fig. 5f, g). In contrast, number of B lymphocytes were not elevated in the SNAP-23 icKO (Fig. 5h). Analyzing the relative frequency of immune cells to the total number of live cells revealed a similar trend. Frequency of CD45high F4/80+ macrophages and microglia and CD45high F4/80− immune cells trended an increase in SNAP-23 icKO (Fig. S8b, c). The proportion of CD4+ T cells was significantly higher in the SNAP-23 icKO compared to the control (Fig. S8d). CD8+ T cells and B cells percentage were not significantly altered (Fig. S8e, f). Thus, CNS myelin damage in SNAP-23 icKO can promote immune cell infiltration into the brain, without the need for external immunogen and experimental BBB alteration.

Vesicle and protein trafficking impairment in oligodendrocytes lacking SNAP-23 underlying demyelination

SNARE proteins play a wide range of cellular processes. With SNAP-23 location at the oligodendrocyte cell membrane and myelin sheath44,45, we hypothesize that SNAP-23 is responsible for vesicular fusion at the myelin sheath. In SNAP-23 icKO 5–8 weeks pti, we observed myelinated axons with vesicular structures in the inner tongue area (Fig. 6a, b), which is the site of new myelin vesicle addition13. There was a trend of increased vesicle-containing myelinated axons in the SNAP-23 icKO (Fig. 6c). In addition, SNAP-23 icKO myelinated axons had enlarged inner tongues, especially in instances where we observed myelinated axons having large inner tongues with multiple vesicles inside (Fig. 6d). After normalizing with axonal sizes, the inner tongue size was larger in SNAP-23 icKO than that of the control (Fig. 6e). Although infrequent, SNAP-23 icKO optic nerves contained abnormal myelin and axonal structures. Axonal spheroids were present in 2 out of 3 SNAP-23 icKO, demonstrating mild axonal damage (Fig. S9a–c). Myelin outfoldings had a trend of increase in SNAP-23 icKO compared to the control (Fig. S9d, e). Abnormal myelin structure probed us to investigate changes in myelin structural protein trafficking. In optic nerves and cerebellum of SNAP-23 icKO 5–8 weeks pti, MOG showed an abnormal perinuclear cytoplasmic staining when co-stained with CC1, in contrast to a diffuse myelin-like staining pattern in the control (Figs. 7a, b and S10a). To understand if deficits in protein trafficking are a general problem with SNAP-23 removal, we visualized the location of the most abundant myelin proteins, MBP and PLP. Unlike MOG and MAG, both MBP and PLP did not show colocalization or perinuclear staining in oligodendrocytes (Fig. 7i, j). MBP and PLP intensity was comparable between control and SNAP-23 icKO (Fig. 7k, l). Unchanged PLP and MBP are likely due to the long half-lives of these proteins22,38. Therefore, SNAP-23 is involved in properly localizing MOG and MAG, but not MBP and PLP. Coinciding with the lack of cell death, removing SNAP-23 in mature oligodendrocytes alters the ability to transport MOG and MAG to maintain myelin integrity, but is not necessary to sustain cell viability in short-term.

SNAP-23 is a target-SNARE that is crucial for the final membrane fusion. In cells, vesicles are packaged in the Golgi and transported to the membrane for exocytosis (Fig. S11c)46. Therefore, we hypothesized that removing SNAP-23 causes a failure of membrane-carrying transmembrane proteins to fuse, leading to an accumulation of proteins in close proximity to the Golgi. We co-stained MOG with giantin, a Golgi marker, along with CC1 marking oligodendrocyte cell body 5–10 weeks pti (Fig. 8a). Giantin, as expected, was located in CC1 labelled cytoplasmic area. Comparing the SNAP-23 icKO with the control, we found a higher colocalization between MOG and giantin (Fig. 8b). However, the colocalization was weaker between MOG and EEA1, a marker for the early endosome in endocytosis (Figs. 8c and S11a). We confirmed the finding using SNAP-23 icKO Ai9, with Ai9 labelling recombined oligodendrocytes. We utilized Plp1-CreERT; Ai9 as a control to label oligodendrocytes without removing SNAP-23. Ai9+ cells showed a higher colocalization between MOG and giantin in the knockout compared to the control (Fig. 8d, e). Similarly, MOG colocalization with EEA1 was not significantly altered in the knockout (Figs. 8f and S11b). Mislocalization of MOG with the Golgi signified protein accumulation in the exocytosis pathway due to SNAP-23 removal in oligodendrocytes.

Discussion

Myelin, while thought to be a relatively stable ensheathment around axons, has recently been shown to be under a dynamical turnover5,24,47–49. The molecular process of how oligodendrocytes contribute to this dynamic is an unresolved topic. Here, we demonstrated that SNAP-23-mediated vesicular transport in oligodendrocytes is necessary for maintaining adult myelin integrity. Loss of SNAP-23 in mature oligodendrocytes leads to demyelination with improper MOG and MAG localization and altered axon-myelin structure (Figs. 2 and 6–8). Furthermore, demyelination induced by oligodendrocyte vesicular dysfunction triggers secondary neuroinflammation with T cells, macrophages and microglia involvement (Fig. 5). Altogether, we propose that SNARE-dependent vesicular trafficking in oligodendrocytes is responsible for the continuous addition of myelin materials to the existing myelin sheath. Failure in such an addition process alters myelin stability and causes an inflammatory cascade (Fig. S12). Therefore, understanding oligodendrocyte vesicular mechanism could provide cellular and molecular insights into demyelinating diseases etiology.

Our results suggest that myelin is more dynamic than previously envisioned. Demonstrated in both humans and mice, oligodendrocytes are highly stable cells that turn over slower compared to myelination5,50. Hence, these cells should be critical in supporting myelin dynamics. We showed that blocking vesicular fusion and protein trafficking in mature oligodendrocytes alters myelin structure (Figs. 6–8). This builds on previous studies that knockout of myelin gene regulatory factor (MYRF) and Qki blocks proteins and lipid synthesis, respectively in mature oligodendrocytes impairs CNS myelin integrity20,21. We further the idea that, beyond the synthesis of the myelin components, the transport of these materials to the proper location is equally important. Interestingly, we observed that larger myelinated axons were more affected by vesicular accumulation compared to smaller ones in SNAP-23 icKO. Larger diameter axons require more energy51. Myelin of large-caliber axons also has more cytoplasmic channels than small ones, providing the higher metabolic needs of large axons52. Oligodendrocytes play pivotal roles in metabolic support to axonal firing and support axons by transcellular transport of SIRT2 via exosome at the periaxonal space53–56. Thus, we speculate that a stronger impact of vesicular accumulation could be due to failure in the vesicular transport of materials and formation of cytoplasmic channels in SNAP-23 icKO oligodendrocytes to support axonal activities metabolically. Beyond myelin integrity, myelin addition in adults is fundamental to learning and memory57,58. While myelin plasticity is largely attributed to new oligodendrocyte generation from oligodendrocyte prescursor cells (OPCs)57–59, investigating how the existing pool of mature oligodendrocytes adds new myelin may further the understanding of brain plasticity.

During development, myelin generation relies on lipid and membrane protein addition at the inner tongue29. Without SNAP-23, there is vesicular accumulation and enlargement of the inner tongue (Fig. 6). VAMP-2/3 cleavage in oligodendrocytes similarly reported vesicular accumulation at the inner tongue29. Thus, we suggest that oligodendrocytes use the SNARE-dependent mechanism of vesicular fusion at the inner tongue. We showed that SNAP-23 mediated membrane addition is critical to the transport of MOG and MAG, two major myelin glycoproteins (Fig. 7a–h). MAG is located at the myelin-axon interface serving the role of axonal contact and communication8. Thus, we speculate that altered MAG transport contributes to the enlarged inner tongue and loosened axon-myelin structure. On the other hand, MOG sits at the surface of the myelin sheath as a common target to activate the immune system9. Therefore, MOG mistrafficking may play a crucial role in neuroinflammation observed in the SNAP-23 icKO. Furthermore, we found that MOG was mislocalized with the Golgi in oligodendrocytes (Fig. 8). Thus, important transmembrane proteins to be added to the myelin during the maintenance process likely undergo the vesicular exocytosis pathway, with impairment of fusion leading to abnormal protein accumulation within the cell. On the contrary, PLP and MBP localization is unaffected by SNAP-23 removal (Fig. 7i–l). Continuous synthesis of PLP and MBP contributes to long-term myelin stability, though both proteins have long half-lives22,23. Their stability in the myelin sheath does not match the rapid demyelination observed in SNAP-23 icKO. Alternatively, the relatively normal PLP trafficking could be due to other SNARE isoforms, such as syntaxin-3 and VAMP-7, involved in PLP transport26,27. The unimpaired PLP transport is also in-line with the finding of VAMP-2/3 cleavage29, further strengthening the findings that SNAP-23 and VAMP-2/3 work together in oligodendrocytes. MBP is transported as RNA and locally translated at the myelin sheath, potentially mediated by syntaxin-4-dependent autocrine signalling28. Thus, MBP localization is likely independent of the SNAP-23-related vesicular trafficking mechanism.

SNARE proteins serve multiple functions in cells, including secretion, membrane protein trafficking and insertion. Their functions in oligodendrocyte lineage cells have only been investigated more in-depth recently. VAMP-2/3 in OPC and oligodendrocytes helps in membrane expression, protein trafficking, and cell maturation29–31. VAMP-2 in addition, involves exosome secretion60. Yet it remains unclear the roles of SNAREs in fully myelinating oligodendrocytes and the functions of target SNAREs in vivo. Here, we found an overlapping function of SNAP-23 and VAMP-2/3 in vesicular transport and protein trafficking, especially MAG29. The developmental VAMP-2/3 cleavage model also reported protein trafficking deficits in protein at the node of Ranvier29. Combined with our observation of an improper node of Ranvier arrangement (Fig. 3i, j), we propose that oligodendrocytes utilize SNAP-23 and VAMP-2/3 as cognate SNARE partners to transport essential proteins for myelination and node of Ranvier assembly. To better disentangle the molecular partners, it will be beneficial to study the removal of both SNARE proteins in mature oligodendrocytes. On the other hand, understanding the role of SNAP-23 during myelin development, along with VAMP and syntaxin, will be beneficial.

The corpus callosum myelination of SNAP-23 icKO was not severely impacted, unlike the optic nerves and cerebellum (Fig. S2). The difference could be accounted for by the variation of oligodendrocytes in different brain regions50,61. The stability of oligodendrocytes in the corpus callosum is higher than that of the optic nerves50. The longevity of oligodendrocytes could signify a more stable myelination in the corpus callosum compared to other white matter. Also, myelination in the corpus callosum may be compensated for by other SNARE proteins, such as SNAP-29. Loss of SNAP-29 causes hypomyelination in human leukodystrophy62. However, inflammation signified by astrocytes, microglia and T cells were present in the corpus callosum (Fig. S7). We speculate that the inflammation could be caused by (1) oligodendrocyte dysfunctions that induce local inflammation; (2) immune cells from other demyelinated regions, such as the optic nerves and the cerebellum, travelled to the corpus callosum, leading to subsequent inflammation. Future work can be done to distinguish between mechanisms.

Beyond the CNS, our knockout strategy also impacts Schwann cells in the PNS. Yet, there is no strong evidence of demyelination in the sciatic nerves of SNAP-23 icKO (Fig. S5). This is consistent with a lack of change in PNS myelination with VAMP-2/3 cleavage during development29. Together, it might suggest PNS Schwann cells use a different mechanism in myelin maintenance, or different SNAREs compared to oligodendrocytes. Further work should be done to examine the roles of SNAREs in Schwann cells and PNS myelination. Nevertheless, as time progressed, SNAP-23 icKO mice’s fur colour changed from black to white (Fig. S5b). With nerve injuries, Schwann cells can de-differentiate, becoming Schwann cell precursor cells, and these cells can be reprogrammed into pigmenting melanocytes37. Thus, fur colour changes might be induced from a nerve injury, promoting SNAP-23 removed Schwann cells transitioned into melanocytes, with the lack of SNARE proteins failing to transport melanin causing hypopigmentation. Further investigation into SNARE pathways in Schwann cells might answer unexpected observations related to skin pigmentation.

Demyelination couples tightly with inflammation, on one hand, demyelination can cause a neuroinflammatory response, on the other hand, inflammation can promote demyelination. The first culprit leading to inflammatory demyelination in MS is still obscure. The “outside-in” hypothesis proposes that peripheral immune cells are first activated to drive CNS myelin lesions43. Best described by the classical experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) model, which relies on external immunogen to drive demyelination, the “outside-in” model fails to investigate the etiology of MS due to the artificial nature of the immune system modulation63. Alternatively, the “inside-out” hypothesis suggests that CNS cellular pathologies arise first, followed by peripheral immune cell infiltration and activation43,63. However, the majority of the inside-out models fail to demonstrate a timely adaptive immune response. Cuprizone-induced demyelination is largely limited to local gliosis64, unless coupled with EAE immunization65. Targeted oligodendrocyte death causes rapid demyelination without profound peripheral immune cell infiltration into the CNS parenchyma16,17,19. T lymphocyte involvement is only found with a late onset after oligodendrocyte death is initiated18. With SNAP-23 icKO, we found that disrupting vesicular trafficking without oligodendrocyte death can trigger both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell infiltration in the brain concurrently with demyelination. Therefore, we demonstrated that cellular and myelin pathologies are sufficient to recruit peripheral immune cells into the CNS. However, the BBB integrity was not analyzed in the current study. Immune cells detected in the CNS could originate from the meninges66. Future work analyzing the BBB integrity and immune cell origin will guide how CNS-initiated damage triggers peripheral immune cell attack.

In addition to our findings, other genetic mouse models targeting myelin and oligodendrocytes reported T cell involvement. Conditional deletion of MYRF in adult oligodendrocytes has no T cell involvement in the spinal cord but has recently been reported to be present in the optic nerves20,67. Adult PLP- and MBP-deficient mice also contain T cells at stages of myelin damage22,23. Hence, myelin damage due to disrupting the myelin synthesis and transport processes could be critical to drive T cell infiltration in the CNS without cell loss. Notably, we detected an elevated number of TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells and loss of oligodendrocytes with aging (Fig. S3). The late oligodendrocyte death could be initiated by infiltrated immune cells. Our model will be useful in studying the mechanistic pathway from primary myelin and oligodendrocyte damage to immune cell recruitment. Interestingly, some literature has previously shown how INFg can induce expression of MHC I, involved in antigen presentation, in oligodendrocytes68. Later studies indicated that mature oligodendrocytes from the optic nerve could express MHC-II upon co-stimulation of IFNγ69 and deletion of NF-kB activator 1 (Act1), which is downstream of interleukin (IL-17), specifically in OPCs but not in oligodendrocytes, led to a robust reduction of EAE severity70. Our immune cell flow cytometry data in our model suggest a close communication between oligodendrocytes and local and adaptive immune T cells, which could be either by direct contact or indirectly throughout soluble proteins such as inflammatory cytokines. Elucidating the link between oligodendrocytes and inflammation requires further analysis.

Does SNARE-dependent vesicular impairment in oligodendrocytes contribute to MS pathology? Single-cell transcriptomic analyses of oligodendrocytes in MS patients suggest changes in SANRE expression in diseased settings. SNAP-23 is upregulated in disease-associated oligodendrocytes associated with elevated unfolded protein response (UPR)71. UPR and associated ER stress are pathological features in MS72. Abnormal protein folding and accumulation can cause UPR, thus, the upregulation of SNAP-23 could be a compensation towards reducing protein accumulation, in line with the observed functions of SNAP-23 in protein trafficking. On the other hand, VAMP-3 level decreases in oligodendrocytes of MS patients29,73. Furthermore, in relapsing-remitting MS, genes that are involved in vesicular trafficking and protein transport, such as STXBP3 (Munc18-3), RAB2, RAB5A, and STX7 (syntaxin-7), were shown to be upregulated in the stage of remission, when symptoms partially recover74. Thus, SNARE-related vesicular pathways in oligodendrocytes can be crucial in MS etiology. Furthermore, in 8-week-old adult induced SNAP-23 icKO, we observed more severe motor deficits in females than males (Fig. S1d). This finding might relate to the higher susceptibility of MS in women75. Continue exploration of sex differences in our mouse model might explore intrinsic differences in oligodendrocyte biology across sexes.

To conclude, SNAP-23 is necessary for oligodendrocytes to maintain healthy myelination in adults. Vesicles addition of myelin materials to existing myelin sheath is fundamental in myelin turnover. Impairment of SNAP-23-mediated vesicular trafficking of proteins is sufficient to induce immune cells infiltration to the CNS. Such oligodendrocyte dysfunction promoting inflammatory demyelination could provide insights into the development of early demyelinating diseases, such as MS, and therefore, a potential target to prevent and reverse demyelination at an early stage.

Clinical scoring was performed as previously described with edits21. An 8-point scale was used to investigate 4 categories, with each category having a score of 0–2. Tremor: no tremor (0), tremor activity when the mouse was held with the tail (1), tremor when walking and standing (2). Limp tail: The tail was upright and spinning when holding the base of the tail (0), the tip of the tail was flaccid (1), completely flaccid tail (2). Hind limb weakness: normal (0), the angle between the hind limb and paw was around 90° (1), the angle between the hind limb and paw was around 180° (2). Hind limb paresis: normal movement and gait, can grip wire bars firmly (0), slips through bars when walking on a grid surface, abnormal gait (1), complete hind limb paralysis (2). Clinical score and weight were measured weekly. The grading system allowed better monitoring of phenotype compared to the EAE scoring as SNAP-23 icKO had a slower progression and milder motor deficits compared to classical EAE models. The usual peak score of 5–6 SNAP-23 icKO achieved was comparable to a classical EAE score of 3, which mice showed hindlimb paralysis. Mice with severe paralysis and at a moribund state, weight loss larger than 20%, and euthanized were dropped out of monitoring at the week of euthanasia.

Mouse Genotyping

A mouse tail sample was collected from a 3-week-old mouse for genotyping polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Tail samples were dissolved in 50 mM NaOH at 95 °C for 1 h. PCR was used to detect the presence of CreERT and SNAP-23 flox allele. For Plp1-CreERT, the forward primer is 5′-ATACCGGAGATCATGCAAGC-3′ and the reverse primer is 5′-GGCCAGGCTGTTCTTCTTAG-3′. For the SNAP-23 flox allele, the forward primer is 5′-GGGGGTGAGTTGAAGTCATTGAAG-3′ located at the end of the exon 4, the reverse primer is 5′-AGCTTAAACGGGATGAACTCAGGC-3′ located at the start of the intron 4.

Tamoxifen preparation and injection

Tamoxifen was administered to 3-to-4-week-old and 8-week-old mice at 100 mg/kg body weight. Tamoxifen was first dissolved in ethanol at 20 mg/mL, then layered on an equal amount of corn oil (Sigma-Aldrich, CAS: 8001-30-7). Tamoxifen was dissolved in the corn oil by spinning in a speed vacuum for 1 h. Tamoxifen was injected into mice via intraperitoneal injection for 5 consecutive days.

Rotarod

Mice were subjected to the accelerating rotarod for four consecutive days using the Panlab apparatus. The speed of the rotarod started from 4 rotations per minute (rpm) and reached 40 rpm in 5 min. The time and speed of mice falling off the rotarod were recorded.

Mouse tissue collection for histology and genotyping

Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (75 mg/kg body weight, Bimeda-MTC) for Luxol fast blue, immunohistochemistry, and transmission electron microscopy experiments. Mice were then transcardially perfused with 10 mL PBS followed by 10 mL 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brain, spinal cord, and optic nerves were dissected. The brain was fixed in 4% PFA overnight, while the spinal cord and optic nerves were fixed in 4% PFA for 2 h. Tissues were dehydrated in 30% sucrose dissolved in PBS overnight. Brain and spinal cord samples were mounted with a solution of 50% OCT and 15% sucrose and cryosectioned at 40 μm. Optic nerves were mounted in OCT solution and cryosectioned at 14 μm.

Genotyping for knockout allele

To genotype for the SNAP-23 knockout allele, 1–2 fixed 40 μm cerebellum sections were dissolved in 50 mM NaOH at 95 °C for 1 h. The forward primer, 5′-TCCAACCCAGAGAGACACTTTT-3′, located upstream of exon 3 and the reverse primer, 5′-GGTACTGTTGCCTCTCATCCCAGT-3′, located downstream of exon 4 were used.

Luxol fast blue staining

Cryosectioned tissues were placed in 0.1% LFB solution (Thermo Scientific, CAS: 1328-51-4) at 56 °C overnight. Sections were differentiated with 0.05% Lithium carbonate solution (TCI America, CAS: 554-13-2) and 70% alcohol until grey and white matters were differentiated. After washing with distilled water, sections were mounted with cytoseal 60 (Epredia). Quantification of LFB staining was done in Fiji (ImageJ). LFB positive area was determined by auto thresholding and compared with the original image.

Immunohistochemistry

Sectioned samples were washed and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton ×-100 in PBS and blocked with 10% goat serum. Samples were incubated in the primary antibody at room temperature overnight. Primary antibodies used: mouse anti-APC (CC1) (1:100, Sigma-Aldrich, OP80), rat anti-MBP (1:50, Sigma-Aldrich, MAB386), rabbit anti-PLP (1:500, Abcam, ab254363), rabbit anti-MAG (1:100, Cell Signaling Technology, 9043), rabbit anti-MOG (1:500, Abcam, ab32760), mouse anti-OLIG2 (1:500, Sigma-Aldrich, MABN50), rabbit anti-GFAP (1:100, Cell Signaling Technology, 12389), rabbit anti-IBA1 (1:100, Cell Signaling Technology, 17198), rabbit anti-CASPR (1:500, Abcam, ab34151), mouse anti-Ankyrin G (1:250, Sigma-Aldrich, MABN466), rat anti-CD3 (1:100, Invitrogen, 14-0032-82), rabbit anti-SNAP-23 (1:125, Proteintech, 10825-1-AP), guinea pig anti-giantin (1:200, Synaptic Systems, 263 005), and guinea pig anti-EEA1 (1:500, Synaptic Systems, 237 105). The next day, sections were washed with 0.1% Triton ×-100 in PBS again before staining with DAPI and the secondary antibody that matched the species of the primary antibody for 1 h. Samples were then subjected to another 0.1% Triton ×-100 in PBS wash. If samples were to stain with FluoroMyelin, slides with samples were flooded with 1:300 diluted FluoroMyelin Red (Invitrogen) for 20 min. TUNEL staining was performed in 5–10 weeks pti and 6 months pti using the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit and counter stained by DAPI. Sections were mounted in Mowiol. For confocal imaging, Carl Zeiss confocal microscope was used. Images were taken at oil immersion 40× objective at 1.0 Airy Unit pinhole sizes. Zeiss immersion oil 518F was used. Two-photon imaging was performed with a two-photon microscope (Custom made FV1000 MPE; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan, 60× objective lens, NA 1.0 equipped with Spectra-Physics InSight DeepSee; Spectra-Physics, CA, US). The fluorescence images were prepared from the maximum intensity projection across a z-stack, taken at 1 µm intervals by Fluoview (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Analysis of immunohistochemistry images

Image analyses were performed in Fiji (ImageJ). For SNAP-23 knockout quantification, region of interest (ROI) was drawn around a CC1 labelled cell. SNAP-23 intensity was quantified in each ROI. CC1 and Iba-1 cell counts were done by thresholding the CC1 or Iba-1 channel and the DAPI channel. Overlapped regions of the two channels were obtained followed by analyzing particles to automatically count the number of positive cells. GFAP astrocytic coverage was done by thresholding the image and calculating the percentage of positive GFAP stained area. T cell staining by CD3 was quantified manually by counting the number of CD3 + DAPI in the field of view of optic nerves, cerebellum, and corpus callosum. MBP, PLP, and FluoroMyelin intensity were computed by drawing the region of interest around the myelin area and obtaining the mean intensity. Quantification of nodes of Ranvier quantification was done by manually counting CASPR and Ankyrin G (AnkG) staining pattern as node (two CASPR flanking one AnkG), heminode (CASPR on one side of AnkG), clusters (CASPR only staining), and node (AnkG only staining). MAG and MOG mislocalization was quantified by visualizing how many CC1 cells demonstrated perinuclear MAG/MOG signal accumulation immediately surrounding the nuclei.

Colocalization Index

Colocalization of MOG with intracellular markers was calculated based on the methods of Li et al.77. In short, a region of interest (ROI) was drawn based on the CC1 or Ai9 staining of the oligodendrocyte cell body. In each ROI, the intensity of each channel (i.e., MOG and giantin or EEA1) was obtained at every single pixel and normalized from 0 to 1. For each channel, the normalized mean was subtracted from the pixel intensity. Then, we obtained the products by multiplying the subtracted values from each channel for every pixel. We calculated the ratio of pixels that were positive over the total number of pixels, and subtracted 0.5 from this value to obtain the intensity correlation quotient (ICQ), which is the colocalization index reported in graphs. A value of zero represents completely independent staining, negative values (minimum −0.5) represent dissociated staining, and positive values (maximum 0.5) represent positive correlation of signals. Analyses were performed using MATLAB.

Optic nerves transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed based on with modifications. Mice were anesthetized as above. After transcardial perfusion of 10 mL PBS, mice were perfused with 10 mL 2% glutaraldehyde (in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer, pH 7.3). Optic nerves were dissected and cut into 2 mm sections and fixed with 2% glutaraldehyde overnight. Ex vivo compound action potential recording of optic nerves

Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (75 mg/kg body weight). Mice were transcardially perfused with cold dissection solution (300 mM sucrose, 3.5 mM KCl, 2 mM NaH2PO4, 20 mM glucose, 0.5 mM CaCl2, 2 mM MgCl2 and 5 mM HEPES (pH adjusted to 7.4)). The optic nerves were dissected and placed in artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF, 125 mM NaCl, 25 mM NaHCO3, 3.5 mM KCl, 1 mM NaH2PO4, 1 mM MgSO4, 2 mM CaCl2) with oxygenation (95% O2 and 5% CO2) at room temperature at least 3 h before recording.

Whole optic nerve compound action potential was recorded based on78 with modifications. For recording, optic nerves were placed in 37 °C oxygenated ACSF. Two suction electrodes (A-M System, Sequim, WA, USA) were placed on the two ends of the optic nerves. A stimulating electrode was placed on the retinal end and a recording electrode was at the chiasmal end. Syringes attached to the electrodes were used to create suction at both ends without stretching the nerves. The stimulus was delivered in a 1 ms pulse with an amplitude that produced the maximal intensity of CAP. CAP traces were an average of 20 trials. Tetrodotoxin (TTX, 1 µM) was applied to block CAP conduction to obtain traces of stimulus artefacts. CAP traces minus stimulus artefacts obtained pure CAP waveform for analysis. Signals were recorded with a dual-channel amplifier (700B, Molecular Devices/Axon Instruments, Sunnyvale, California, USA) and digitized at 10 kHz (Digidata 1322A, Molecular Devices). Data was acquired with PClamp software (version 10, Molecular Devices). Stimulus artefact removed CAP data was analyzed in OriginLab. CAP traces were fitted with three Gaussian functions to determine peak amplitude, latency, and full width at half-maximum.

Visual evoked potential recording

The Visual evoked potential (VEP) was recorded as described by ref. 39 with modifications. Mice were anesthetized with Avertin (200 mg/kg) and kept in the dark for 5 min. Mydriacyl (Alcon) eye drops were applied to dilate pupils in both eyes. A steel recording electrode was placed subcutaneously at the visual cortex at 8 mm in depth. A ground electrode was placed at the tail and a reference electrode was placed between the eyes subcutaneously. White light stimulation (6500 K) at pulse intensity 3 cd.s/m2, frequency at 1 Hz, and duration at 4 ms was delivered using the Diagnosys Espion electroretinogram device with a ColorDome Ganzfeld stimulator and Espion software. A total of three runs were performed per mouse with each run containing 100 sweeps of light stimulation. Body temperature was kept at 38 °C throughout. N1 peak detection was done automatically between by Espion.

Brain tissue processing and flow cytometry

Mice were anesthesized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (75 mg/kg body weight). Brains were homogenized using a 70 µm strainer and a 5 mL syringe. The resulting cell suspensions were incubated with DNase I (60 µg/mL, Roche 10104159001) at 37 °C for 40 min. Lymphocytes were subsequently isolated using a 30% Percoll gradient, resuspended in PBS, and prepared for counting and flow cytometry staining.

Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS containing 2% FBS and stained for 30 min at 4 °C using the Live/Dead Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit (Invitrogen L34965). To block non-specific binding, cells were incubated with purified rat anti-mouse CD16/32 (5 µg/mL, clone 93 Biolegend, 101302) for 15 min at 4 °C. Primary antibodies, including CD45 (clone 30-F11, Biolegend, 103154), F4/80 (clone BM8, Biolegend, 123108), CD4 (clone RM4-5, Biolegend, 100528), CD8 (clone 53-6.7, Biolegend, 100750), CD45R/B220 (clone RA3-6B2, Biolegend, 103212), and CD19 (clone 6D5, Biolegend, 115523), were added at pre-determined concentrations in a total volume of 100 µL and incubated for 30 min at 4 °C. Cells were then fixed with 100 µL of 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at 4 °C. After a final wash, the cells were resuspended in PBS containing 2% FBS and analyzed using the CytoFLEX LX Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter). Data acquisition was performed using FlowJo software (10.10.0) (Tree Star, Inc.). All data were pre-gated on live cells, and positive/negative discrimination was determined using fluorescence minus one (FMO) controls.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in RStudio (Posit, PBC), OriginLab 2025 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA), Prism (GraphPad Software) and SPSS (IBM). All data are mean ± SEM. Statistical tests performed were specified in each experiment. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all experiments. All t-tests performed were unpaired, two-tailed. Tukey post-hoc analysis was performed after ANOVA tests. Non-parametric Mann–Whitney tests were done for flow cytometry using PRISM Software. All error bars present were standard error (SEM).
