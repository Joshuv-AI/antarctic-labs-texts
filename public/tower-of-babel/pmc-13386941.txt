The role of school terms in the epidemics of scarlet fever among pre-school-aged and school-aged children: a 15-year population-based surveillance in China
Authors: Weijun Yu, Bingbing Cong, Lian He, Xin Wang, Yingwei Sun, You Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScarlet fever, caused by group A streptococcus (GAS), has resurged globally since the early 2010s. Although schools and pre-schools are well acknowledged as the high-risk settings for scarlet fever outbreaks, little is known about the effect of school terms on scarlet fever epidemics among pre-school-aged and school-aged children. In this study, we aimed to understand the role of school terms in scarlet fever epidemics by leveraging a population-based surveillance across 15 years in China.MethodsWe included weekly scarlet fever cases from 2005 to 2019 in Liaoning Province (one of the provinces with highest incidence of scarlet fever) among children aged ≤ 12 years who were subsequently categorised into three groups: school-attending, pre-school-attending, and non-pre-school-attending. We used wavelet spectrum analysis to identify periodicity patterns of scarlet fever epidemics. We applied negative binomial regression models to estimate the relative risk (RR) of scarlet fever associated with school terms and population categories, adjusting for background risk of infection, meteorological factors, and temporal trends.ResultsA total of 64,950 scarlet fever cases were identified, with non-pre-school-attending children, pre-school-attending children and school-attending children accounting for 16%, 44%, and 40%, respectively. Scarlet fever exhibited a significant semi-annual pattern, usually peaking in May to June (during the spring semester) and November to December (during the autumn semester), with substantial declines during school breaks. Wavelet analysis identified this strong semi-annual signal, plus an additional 3.3-year (171-week) periodicity signal. Compared to non-pre-school-attending children during school breaks, school term was associated with a 2-fold higher risk of scarlet fever in pre-school-attending children (relative risk [RR] 2.14, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.94–2.36) and school-attending children (RR 2.07, 95% CI 1.88–2.27), whereas a reduced risk was observed in non-pre-school-attending children (RR 0.84, 95% CI 0.77–0.91). During school breaks, pre-school-attending children remained at higher risk than non-pre-school-attending children (RR 1.80, 95% CI 1.63–1.99), while school-attending children showed no significantly increased risk (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.88–1.08). Further stratification by school semester indicated that the association between school term and scarlet fever risk for pre-school-attending children and school-attending children was stronger in the autumn semester than in the spring semester.ConclusionsSchool terms have differential effects on scarlet fever risk across population categories. School-attending children have a higher risk for scarlet fever during school terms while pre-school-attending children have a higher risk for scarlet fever all year round than non-pre-school-attending children. By contrast, the risk decreases among non-pre-school-attending children during school terms. These findings support the development of targeted public health interventions to mitigate scarlet fever outbreaks in high-incidence regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27911-2.Keywords: Scarlet fever, Epidemics, School, Children, Pre-school, Students

Introduction

Scarlet fever is a widespread bacterial infection caused by group A streptococcus (GAS), primarily affecting children [1]. Globally, the incidence of scarlet fever has increased sharply in several countries since 2005, including Asia (e.g., Singapore in 2006 [2], China in 2011 [3], and South Korea in 2015 [4]) and Europe (e.g., Germany in 2007 [5], and the UK in 2014 [1]). Potential factors, including natural cyclical patterns [1], antimicrobial resistance [6], shifts in the emm gene types [7], meteorological factors [8], and air pollution [9] have been reported to be associated with the epidemics and resurgence of scarlet fever.

Schools and pre-schools are well acknowledged as high-risk settings for scarlet fever outbreaks. Although studies from mainland China [10], Hong Kong [11], and South Korea [4] showed that the distribution of scarlet fever cases between peak and trough periods aligned with school term schedules, these studies did not assess the specific effects of school terms on scarlet fever epidemics in detail. Moreover, the effects of school terms on the risk of scarlet fever among pre-school-aged children not attending pre-schools remain unknown. Given that no vaccines are currently available against scarlet fever, it is important to gain further insights into the role of school terms in scarlet fever epidemics among school-aged and pre-school-aged children to optimise non-pharmaceutical interventions.

In this study, by leveraging data from long-term population-based regional surveillance in China, we aimed to understand the role of school terms in scarlet fever epidemics among school-aged and pre-school-aged children.

Methods

Data source

The study region was Liaoning Province, one of the provinces with the highest incidence of scarlet fever in China [12]. Located in Northeast China, Liaoning is a coastal province consisting of 14 municipalities, with a total population of 41.9 million and a per-capita GDP of USD 8,290 in 2019. The province has a temperate continental monsoon climate with four distinct seasons, a well-established comprehensive healthcare system covering both urban and rural areas, and key health indicators (including life expectancy, maternal mortality, and infant mortality rates) that consistently outperform the national average.

As described previously [12], data on scarlet fever cases between 2005 and 2019 were obtained from the China Information System for Disease Control and Prevention (CISDCP), an internet-based disease reporting system managed by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The surveillance remained stable during the selected study period; specifically, the year of 2020 onwards was not included considering the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the school on-site teaching schedule and on the transmission of scarlet fever.

For scarlet fever, it is required by the law on Prevention and Treatment of Infectious Diseases to report the disease episodes to the system within 24 h of diagnosis across all types of health facilities [13]. As clinicians might diagnose scarlet fever with or without laboratory testing, scarlet fever cases could be reported as one of the following three types: laboratory-confirmed, clinical and probable cases according to the criteria issued by the former Ministry of Health of the People’s Republic of China [3]. For each disease episode, clinicians are required to complete a standardised form consisting of basic demographic information (e.g., name, sex, date of birth, place of residence, and occupation [population category]) and clinical information (e.g., date of symptom onset, types of diagnosis [laboratory-confirmed, clinical and probable cases], and death [if applicable]). Upon receiving a report of a disease episode, the local county-level CDC is responsible for verifying the reported episode before further reporting to the municipal, provincial, and national CDC.

Study population

The study population consisted of children aged under 12 years old residing in Liaoning Province during the study period. The study population was firstly classified into school-aged children (6 to under 12 years) and pre-school-aged children (under 6 years). Considering that not all pre-school-aged children attend pre-schools (e.g., kindergarten), we further classified pre-school-aged children into pre-school-attending children and non-pre-school-attending children. This yielded three population categories: school-attending children, pre-school-attending children, and non-pre-school-attending children.

Definition of school terms

There are two school semesters in an academic year of China. The autumn semester usually starts in early September and ends in late December, followed by a winter break spanning from January to February; the spring semester starts in early March and ends in late June, followed by a summer break spanning from July to August. As the exact dates of school terms and holiday breaks could vary by up to one week across years, we considered a one-week buffer period at the beginning of the winter and summer breaks, and used the following practical definition of school terms: spring semester as 1 March to 30 June, summer break as 8 July to 31 August, autumn semester as 1 September to 31 December, and winter break as 8 January to 28 February.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis

We visualised the time series of weekly number of scarlet fever cases by population category, alongside the three-week moving average of temperature and relative humidity. We further applied wavelet analysis to identify periodicity patterns of scarlet fever epidemics for the study overall population and each population category.

Main analysis

We conducted a series of negative binomial regression models, including a base model and several alternative candidate models, to assess the role of school term and population category in determining the risk of scarlet fever epidemics. For all models, the dependent variable was the weekly number of scarlet fever cases in a given population category.

The independent variable of exposure in the base model combined the two primary exposures of interest, school opening status (based on the school term schedule defined above), and population category; this yielded a variable of six levels: non-pre-school-attending children during holiday breaks (reference), pre-school-attending children during holiday breaks, school-attending children during holiday breaks, non-pre-school-attending children during school terms, pre-school-attending children during school terms, and school-attending children during school terms. To account for the baseline transmission risk, we included two covariates to adjust for short-term and long-term force of infection: (1) the number of cases in the preceding 2 weeks as an autoregressive term to control for confounding from recent infectious cases; (2) a 52-week exponentially weighted moving average cases (excluding the preceding 2 weeks) as a proxy for persistent background transmission risk and population immunity. We also included the logarithm of the average weekly scarlet fever cases during the corresponding holiday breaks for each population category as an offset term to cancel off the effects of varied population size in the three population categories.

Building upon the base model, we further constructed five additional candidate models that accounted for meteorological factors (from the climate package in R) and trend over time. The first candidate model added weekly average temperature, relative humidity and index of semester (from semester 1 to semester 30 over the 15 years). The second candidate model further allowed non-linear effects of temperature and humidity by including natural spline terms (penalised thin-plate regression splines with a basis dimension of 5, with number of knots optimised by restricted maximum likelihood estimation). Based on the second candidate model, the third, fourth, and fifth candidate models further applied time lags of one, two and three weeks, respectively, to the exposure, temperature and relative humidity variables, considering the time lag between infection and onset of symptoms (i.e., incubation period) and potential reporting lags. Details on the statistical model can be found in the appendix (p 3).

We selected the primary model for assessing the effect of the exposure by comparing Akaike information criterion (AIC) across the base model and candidate models. A smaller AIC indicated better model fit and AIC differences > 10 were considered to be a substantial model improvement [14].

Subgroup analysis

We conducted subgroup analyses stratified by sex, school semester (i.e., spring and autumn semester) and time period (2005–2009, 2010–2014, and 2015–2019 to account for potential changed capacity in diagnosis and reporting over time). Given the reported association of incidence of infectious disease with high urban resident population density and large transient populations [15], we further conducted a subgroup analysis that separated the capital municipality of the province, Shenyang, from the other 13 municipalities.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted a series of one-way sensitivity analyses. The first sensitivity analysis replaced the dependent variable with the three-week moving average of weekly number of scarlet fever cases. The second, third, and fourth sensitivity analyses replaced the covariate of the 52-week exponentially weighted moving average cases with 4-week, 13-week, and 26-week exponentially weighted moving average cases, respectively.

Statistical software

All data analyses were performed in R software (version 4.2.1).

Results

From January 1, 2005, to December 31, 2019, a total of 64,950 scarlet fever cases were reported among children aged under 12 years old in Liaoning Province (Table 1). Most cases (39,392, 60.6%) were male and the vast majority of the cases were clinically diagnosed (63,006, 97.0%). Pre-school-attending and school-attending children jointly accounted for more than 80% of the total cases (Table 1).

Overall, over three quarters of the scarlet fever cases occurred during school terms, with a nearly equal split between the two semesters (Table 1). The peak of scarlet fever epidemics was observed in May to June during the spring semester and in November to December during the autumn semester (Fig. 1). This led to a strong semi-annual signal identified by the wavelet analysis; one additional signal of periodicity was identified around 3.3 years (171 weeks) (Fig. 2). Further stratification by population category showed that the semi-annual periodicity was consistently strong among pre-school-attending and school-attending children but less strong among non-pre-school-attending children (Fig. 2 and appendix p 7). No visible differences were found in the trajectory of scarlet fever cases between male and female children, as well as between the capital municipality and other municipalities (appendix pp 5–6).

Model selection results showed that the fourth candidate model (with non-linear terms on weekly average temperature and relative humidity, and two-week time lag) had the lowest AIC (appendix p 4), and was therefore selected as the primary model. Based on this primary model, we found that compared to non-pre-school-attending children during holiday breaks (reference), pre-school-attending children and school-attending children during school terms had an increased risk of scarlet fever epidemics, with the corresponding RR of 2.14 (95% CI, 1.94–2.36) and 2.07 (1.88–2.27), respectively; by contrast, the risk was found to decrease among non-pre-school-attending children during school terms (RR = 0.84, 0.77–0.91). Additionally, extreme temperatures were significantly associated with risk of scarlet fever epidemics whereas no significant association was found between relative humidity and scarlet fever epidemics (Fig. 3). The effects of baseline covariates including short-term and long-term force of infection were small despite statistical significance (appendix p 8).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that closely examined the effects of school terms on the epidemics of scarlet fever among pre-school and school-aged children, the well acknowledged high-risk population. By leveraging a 15-year surveillance dataset from a high-incidence region in northern China, we identified strong signals of semi-annual periodicity in the epidemics of scarlet fever among pre-school and school-aged children, particularly among those who attend schools or pre-schools, which aligned closely with the two semesters of a year. Compared to non-pre-school-attending children during school breaks, we found that school term was associated with a 2-fold higher risk of scarlet fever for both pre-school-attending children and school-attending children, and a decreased risk for non-pre-school-attending children. During school breaks, the risk among school-attending children was comparable to that among non-pre-school-attending children while the risk among pre-school-attending children was persistently high. These findings highlight the high risk of scarlet fever among school-attending children during school terms and among pre-school-attending children all year round, providing important evidence for targeted public health interventions.

Our study showed that school terms could significantly increase the risk of scarlet fever among pre-school- and school-attending children, consistent with the observations in previous studies that incidence of scarlet fever declined during school breaks and increased sharply during school terms [3, 4, 8]. The increased risk during school terms underscores the important role of schools in scarlet fever epidemics among pre-school- and school-attending children, where more frequent close contacts occurred between these children than during school breaks; this was supported by a prospective social contact survey reporting that the daily number of recorded encounters and close contact encounters fell by approximately half and one third during school breaks, respectively [16]. As the two semesters and corresponding school breaks had near identical durations in our study setting, the increased risk during school terms led to the strong signal of semi-annual periodicity in scarlet fever epidemics. This suggests that schools and school terms played a predominant role in the transmission of scarlet fever in our study setting.

However, we noted that the strong semi-annual periodicity signal in scarlet fever cases could not be observed in other countries with different academic calendars. For example, there were no semi-annual periodicity being detected in England [1], where the number of scarlet fever cases increased in January in some of the years; instead, a significant periodicity of 3–4 years was detected, consistent with the periodicity of 3.3 years identified in this study, possibly suggesting the medium-term variations in herd immunity. However, the role of school terms was not so distinct as our study setting in some of the European countries. For example, a modelling study conducted in the Netherlands showed that school terms could only account for a small proportion of the variability in transmission rates and the role of school terms were not consistent among different cities [17]. Interestingly, in Denmark, an increased transmission was observed even in July when schools were closed [18]. These studies together suggest the differential role of school terms in affecting scarlet fever epidemics across countries and prompt further investigation into possible drivers of regional variation in temporal patterns of scarlet fever.

Unlike school-aged children who all attend schools, pre-school-aged children are naturally split into pre-school-attending and non-pre-school-attending subgroups with contrastingly different contact patterns during school terms and possibly during school breaks. This provides a unique opportunity to further differentiate the role of school terms from the role of being school attenders at educational settings. In this study, we noted that the risk of scarlet fever even decreased during school terms among non-pre-school-attending children; this could be potentially explained by the reduced opportunities for these children to interact with pre-school-attending children during school terms, confirming the role of school terms in the transmission of scarlet fever from a different perspective. Furthermore, compared to non-pre-school-attending children, pre-school-attending children had constantly higher risk for scarlet fever regardless of school terms, confirming the role of being pre-school attendees in the risk of scarlet fever. Nonetheless, the risk of scarlet fever was not significantly higher during school terms than during school breaks (despite a higher point estimate) among pre-school-attending children; we speculate that this may be partly attributable to the continued engagement of these children in extracurricular activities during school breaks, which was relatively common in our study region [19].

Compared to (pre-)schools and school terms, meteorological factors played a less important role based on our estimates. We showed that the risk of scarlet fever was highest when the temperature was close to the median weekly average temperature (approximately 10 °C). This finding was consistent with reports from other two regions from China (Jiangsu Province [20], and Guangzhou municipality [21]) with warmer climates. Different from other reports [9, 21], we did not observe any statistically significant association between relative humidity and risk of scarlet fever.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify the differential effects of school terms among those who attend and do not attend (pre-)schools, and to explicitly separate the role of school terms from that of being school attenders. Our use of 15 years of continuous, population-level surveillance data, combined with extensive subgroup and sensitivity analyses, strengthens the robustness of our findings. Nevertheless, several limitations should be noted. First, our study relied on passive surveillance data and thus might be subject to selection bias due to under-diagnosis or under-reporting, and information bias due to misclassification of scarlet fever cases. Additionally, the surveillance could not include mild cases who did not seek medical care. Nonetheless, we believe that these potential biases were less likely to differ by school opening status and population categories, the main exposure groups of our study; therefore, we did not anticipate our findings to be severely biased. Second, we acknowledged that health-care seeking behaviour, diagnosis criteria and reporting practices could change over time, which affect the number of scarlet fever cases captured by the surveillance; the subgroup analysis that separated the study period into three 5-year intervals confirmed the robustness of the study findings. Third, while we adjusted for proxies of short- and long-term force of infection, time trends and meteorological factors, residual confounding could exist, such as GAS genotypes, co-circulation of other pathogens, and additional environmental factors (e.g., air pollution). Fourth, due to lack of the population denominators of each population category, we used cases during school holiday as a data-driven offset term, assuming proportionality to subgroup size. Although the potential biases may persist, e.g., the difference of healthcare-seeking behaviours during holiday periods across population categories, we speculated this practice was reasonable as it preserved the comparability within the same subgroup. Fifth, as an ecological study, our study could not establish causality of school and school term in risk of scarlet fever at the individual level. Finally, as social contact patterns in and out of school settings, the structure and timing of school calendars, and sensitivity and timeliness of national surveillance systems could vary substantially across countries, our study findings might not be fully generalisable to other countries.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provides robust evidence that pre-schools and schools as well as their associated school term schedules play an important role in the risk of scarlet fever among children. The study findings highlight the high risk of scarlet fever among school-attending children during school terms and among pre-school-attending children all year round; by contrast, the risk decreased among non-pre-school-attending children during school terms. The differential effects support the implementation of targeted pre-school- and school-based public health interventions, such as enhanced surveillance during term time, public health campaigns focusing on hygiene around school openings, and possibly adjusted academic calendars in high-incidence regions, to mitigate scarlet fever epidemics.
