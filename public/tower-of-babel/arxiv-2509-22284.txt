Structured Sparse Transition Matrices to Enable State Tracking in State-Space Models
Aleksandar Terzić aleksandar.terzic1@ibm.com, Nicolas Menet nicolas.menet@ibm.com, Michael Hersche michael.hersche@ibm.com, Thomas Hofmann thomas.hofmann@inf.ethz.ch, Abbas Rahimi abr@zurich.ibm.com

Abstract
Modern state-space models (SSMs) often utilize transition matrices which enable efficient computation but pose restrictions on the model’s expressivity, as measured in terms of the ability to emulate finite-state automata (FSA). While unstructured transition matrices are optimal in terms of expressivity, they come at a prohibitively high compute and memory cost even for moderate state sizes. We propose a structured sparse parametrization of transition matrices in SSMs that enables FSA state tracking with optimal state size and depth, while keeping the computational cost of the recurrence comparable to that of diagonal SSMs. Our method, PD-SSM , parametrizes the transition matrix as the product of a column one-hot matrix ( P \displaystyle P ) and a complex-valued diagonal matrix ( D \displaystyle D ). Consequently, the computational cost of parallel scans scales linearly with the state size. Theoretically, the model is BIBO-stable and can emulate any N \displaystyle N -state FSA with one layer of dimension N \displaystyle N and a linear readout of size N × N \displaystyle N\times N , significantly improving on all current structured SSM guarantees. Experimentally, the model significantly outperforms a wide collection of modern SSM variants on various FSA state tracking tasks. On multiclass time-series classification, the performance is comparable to that of neural controlled differential equations, a paradigm explicitly built for time-series analysis. Finally, we integrate PD-SSM into a hybrid Transformer-SSM architecture and demonstrate that the model can effectively track the states of a complex FSA in which transitions are encoded as a set of variable-length English sentences. The code is available at https://github.com/IBM/expressive-sparse-state-space-model

2.2
Modeling Finite-State Automata with SSMs

In this work, we measure the expressivity of an SSM by considering the type of deterministic finite-state automaton (FSA) it can emulate.
A deterministic FSA is an abstract model of computation, typically defined as a 5-tuple
\displaystyle(Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{\text{init}},F)
where
\displaystyle Q
is a finite set of states,
\displaystyle\Sigma
is a finite input alphabet,
\displaystyle\delta:Q\times\Sigma\rightarrow Q
is the state transition function,
\displaystyle q_{\text{init}}\in Q
is a fixed initial state, and
\displaystyle F\subseteq Q
is the set of accepting states.

Any (deterministic) FSA can be mapped to a time-variant SSM as follows.
Encode each
\displaystyle q\in Q
using
\displaystyle enc:Q\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{|Q|}
such that the encodings of different states are orthogonal. Note that orthogonality is a sufficient but not necessary condition for mapping an FSA to a selective SSM, as certain FSAs such as modular counters allow alternative, more compact, mappings to SSMs.
Given such an orthogonal encoding of states, we can map the state transition function
\displaystyle\delta:Q\times\Sigma\rightarrow Q
to the state transition matrices
\displaystyle A(u_{t})
via
\displaystyle A(\sigma)={\scriptstyle\sum\nolimits}_{q\in Q}\ enc(\delta(q,\sigma))\cdot enc(q)^{T}
,
set
\displaystyle B=0
,
\displaystyle C=I
,
\displaystyle D=0
, and
\displaystyle\psi=id
. Upon identification of
\displaystyle q
with
\displaystyle enc(q)
and
\displaystyle q_{\text{init}}
with
\displaystyle x_{0}
, the SSM corresponds exactly to the FSA.

2.4
Associative Scan for Fully Parallelizable Recurrence in SSMs

We use parallel scans to concurrently compute the states of an SSM
[
Blelloch 1990
,
Martin and Cundy 2018
,
Smith et al. 2023
]
.
Suppose a binary associative operator
\displaystyle\bullet
, i.e.,
\displaystyle(a\bullet b)\bullet c=a\bullet(b\bullet c)
,
and a sequence of
\displaystyle L
elements
\displaystyle[a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{L}]
.
The scan operation (sometimes referred to as
all-prefix-sum
) then returns the sequence

[a_{1},(a_{1}\bullet a_{2}),\ldots,(a_{1}\bullet a_{2}\bullet\cdots\bullet a_{L})].

Note that composition of the linear recurrence of a (time-variant) SSM
\displaystyle x_{t}=A_{t}x_{t-1}+b_{t}
takes precisely the form of a parallel scan
\displaystyle(A_{t+1},b_{t+1})\bullet(A_{t},b_{t})\mapsto(A_{t+1}A_{t},A_{t+1}b_{t}+b_{t+1})
with prefix elements
\displaystyle a_{t}=(A_{t},b_{t})
. According to
Blelloch 1990
(
Blelloch 1990
, Section 1.4), associative scans can be computed in
\displaystyle\Theta(T_{\odot}\,L)
total operations (i.e., in work-efficient manner) with
\displaystyle\Theta(\log L)
sequential steps, where
\displaystyle T_{\odot}
represents the cost of matrix-matrix multiplication. For dense matrices
\displaystyle A_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
,

\displaystyle T_{\odot}=O(N^{3})
and thus the associative scan quickly becomes prohibitively expensive
in deep learning settings. In contrast, if
\displaystyle A_{t}
are diagonal matrices,
\displaystyle T_{\odot}=O(N)
. In terms of memory, parallel scans with dense matrices use
\displaystyle\Theta(N^{2}L)
memory, whereas diagonal matrices lead to a
\displaystyle\Theta(NL)
memory consumption.

3.1
Approximate Gradients

Strictly speaking,
\displaystyle\tfrac{\partial P(u)}{\partial M(u)}
is a generalized function—it vanishes almost everywhere, except at isolated points where it exhibits Dirac delta-like singularities. To smooth these singularities over sets of non-zero measure, we approximate the hardmax with softmax during the backward pass.

\frac{\partial P}{\partial M}=\frac{\partial\text{hardmax}(M)}{\partial M}\approx\frac{\partial\text{softmax}(M)}{\partial M}\\

This is reminiscent of the
slope-annealed straight-through estimator

[
Paulus et al. 2021
]
, yet we use stochasticity neither in the forward nor in the backward pass (ablations with stochastic categorical sampling in
\displaystyle P
are reported in Appendix
E
).
Note that during the forward pass we do not relax the hardmax, since doing so would break the sparsity essential for an efficient parallel scan.
With tempered softmax in the low-temperature limit, the above expression becomes exact.

In this section, we formalize the set of transition matrices
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{N\times N}
that are decomposable into a binary one-hot column matrix
\displaystyle P
and a complex diagonal matrix
\displaystyle D
. Note that our
\displaystyle PD

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}

Definition 1

(One-hot Column Matrices)
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{M\times N}:=\{A\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}:\forall i\ \|A_{:,i}\|_{0}=1\}
where
\displaystyle\|x\|_{0}

\displaystyle\ell^{0}
-"norm" counting the number of non-zero entries in
\displaystyle x

Under matrix multiplication as its binary operation,
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{N\times N}

(Algebraic Structure of One-hot Column Matrices)
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{N\times N}

Closure under multiplication is essential for efficient chained matrix multiplication via parallel scans, because matrix multiplication in
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{N\times N}

3.3
Stability and Expressivity of the PD Parametrization

If the PD parametrization is adopted for state transition matrices and if one ensures matrix entries inside the complex unit circle, then the state space model becomes provably bounded-input, bounded-output (BIBO) stable. Note that our parametrization of
\displaystyle|D(u_{t})|
with a sigmoid-based MLP ensures all conditions for BIBO stability are met.

Proposition 1

(System Stability under PD-Parametrization)
.

Let
\displaystyle\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and consider the state transition
\displaystyle x_{k}=A_{k}x_{k-1}+b_{k}
with
\displaystyle A_{k}\in\mathbb{H}^{N\times N}:\|A_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq 1-\varepsilon
. Let further
\displaystyle\|x_{0}\|_{2}\leq B
and
\displaystyle\|b_{k}\|_{2}\leq B
for
\displaystyle B\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then it holds that

\|x_{k}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{N}B/\varepsilon\quad\forall k.

(2)

As an immediate consequence of the mapping from FSAs to SSMs described in Section
2.2
, Proposition
2
holds:

Proposition 2

(Expressivity of PD Parametrization)
.

Any FSA with
\displaystyle N
states can be exactly represented by a single-layer PD-SSM with a state size
\displaystyle N
and linear readout of size
\displaystyle N\times N
.

Not only can PD-SSMs represent any FSAs, they do so with the (almost) smallest state size possible, i.e., PD-SSMs are maximally expressive for regular languages.

Proposition 3

(Optimality of PD Parametrization)
.

For any
\displaystyle N
there exists a finite-state automaton with
\displaystyle N
states that cannot be emulated by any single-layer SSM with state size less than
\displaystyle N-1
under unique state encodings.

The assumption of unique state encodings, meaning that each state is represented by a single, unique vector, is a practical necessity for readout. Indeed, without unique state encodings (and with arbitrary precision), even a single-layer real SSM with state size
\displaystyle 1
can represent any FSA, albeit in a format that requires exponentially large lookup tables to read out. Proposition
4
makes this statement exact.

Proposition 4

(Arbitrary Precision and Readout)
.

Consider
\displaystyle x_{t+1}=x_{t}+b_{t}
where
\displaystyle b_{t}=u_{t}\cdot k_{t}
with
\displaystyle u_{t}\in\mathbb{Q}
input encodings and
\displaystyle k_{t}=\sqrt{p_{t}}\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}
time encodings, where
\displaystyle p_{t}
is the
\displaystyle t
-th prime. Then, any FSA can be encoded into this scalar-valued SSM under an appropriate lookup table as readout.

5
Conclusion

We have introduced an expressive yet efficient parametrization of transition matrices for selective state-space models manifesting in the PD-SSM architecture. Theoretically, PD-SSMs can emulate arbitrary finite-state automata while reaching the lower bound on the state size required to do so in the worst case. They are significantly more efficient than the similarly performant unstructured SD-SSM, and reach a new state-of-the-art in synthetic state-tracking tasks. We verifed its effectiveness on a novel
state-tracking in natural language
benchmark.
Among the investigated time-variant SSMs, PD-SSM on average performs best on tasks requiring the processing of long sequences (up to 4k), and exhibits high performance on time-series classification with long sequences.

Limitations and Future Work

Although we significantly improve upon the scalability of SD-SSMs while retaining its favorable expressivity, PD-SSMs still incur overhead when generating the
\displaystyle P(u_{t})
matrices.
Our future work will investigate how PD-SSM can benefit from more efficient one-hot column matrix generation strategies, and how it can be utilized in large-scale pretraining.

6
Broader Impact

The paper introduces a novel neural network architecture with increased expressiveness and efficiency. More expressive and efficient models can potentially have societal impacts in the future, but we do not see any immediate and direct negative impact of this work.

A.1
Algebra and Finite-State Automata

Algebra is, broadly speaking, the study of
sets
and
binary operations
on elements of sets. The set elements can be of various nature. An intuitive example of an algebraic structure is the set of integers under addition, often denoted as
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. In more generality, one may consider sets of functions with the binary operation being function composition. This is in fact highly related to the study of finite-state automata (FSAs), where we consider
sets of transition functions corresponding to the input elements
, and we study the effect of
composing transition functions
. This described set is defined for any automaton
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A)}=\{\delta(\cdot,\sigma_{1,\dots,T}):T\in\mathbb{N},\forall i\in[T],\sigma_{i}\in\Sigma\}

\displaystyle\delta(\cdot,\sigma_{1,\dots,T}):Q\rightarrow Q

\displaystyle\delta(\cdot,\sigma_{1,\dots,T})=\delta(\delta(\dots\delta(\cdot,\sigma_{1}),\dots\sigma_{T-1}),\sigma_{T})

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A)}

\displaystyle a\in G

3.

\displaystyle e\in H

\displaystyle a\cdot b

\displaystyle a\in H

\displaystyle a^{-1}

\displaystyle H

Of central interest are

Definition 4

(Normal Subgroup)
.

A

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle N\triangleleft G

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle n\in N

\displaystyle gng^{-1}=n

\displaystyle g\in G

Definition 5

A

\displaystyle G

\{e\}=G_{0}\triangleleft G_{1}\triangleleft\cdots\triangleleft G_{n}=G,

such that each subgroup
\displaystyle G_{i}
is normal in the next one, i.e.,
\displaystyle G_{i}\triangleleft G_{i+1}
for all
\displaystyle i

\displaystyle\{e\}

Apart from subgroups, we need to define
cosets

Definition 6

(Coset)
.

Let
\displaystyle G
be a group and
\displaystyle H
a subgroup of
\displaystyle G

\displaystyle g\in G

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle g

gH:=\{gh:h\in H\}.

Hg:=\{hg:h\in H\}.

Cosets partition the group
\displaystyle G

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{Z^{+}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z^{+}}
, as the sum of two even numbers is again even and the neutral element, 0, is even.
In particular, this is a normal subgroup, an easily verifiable immediate consequence of the commutativity of addition.

The odd integers are a coset of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z^{+}}

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{Z}^{+}

\displaystyle 1+2\mathbb{Z}^{+}

The sets are disjoint, are of equal size by a 1-to-1 mapping of the elements, and together they form
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}

It is precisely the decomposition of a group by use of a normal subgroup that we are interested in. This procedure creates a new group structure called a

Definition 7

(Factor Group)

Let
\displaystyle N
be a normal subgroup of a group
\displaystyle G
. A

(or quotient group)
\displaystyle G/N
is the set of left cosets of
\displaystyle N
in
\displaystyle G
, concretely
\displaystyle(G/N=\{gN:g\in G\}
, equipped with a natural group operation.

Let us again consider the even and odd integers. We have seen that this is the full set of cosets of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z^{+}}
. If we abstract away the individual numbers and only consider the concepts of
even
and
odd

A solvable group is one in which we can create a sequence of such factor groups with each factor group being commutative. The sequence should also end with the trivial group containing only the neutral element.

Definition 8

A finite group

\displaystyle G_{i+1}

A.3
Circuit Complexity Theory

The theory of circuit complexity aims to provide an abstract understanding of highly parallel algorithms. One might imagine that there exist problems for which it is difficult, if not impossible, to design one efficient algorithm which operates on arbitrary-length inputs, yet in which all input lengths admit a bespoke, highly efficient and parallel algorithm
2
2

2

As per
[
Arora and Barak 2009
]
, introductory remark to Chapter 6, Karp and Lipton propose a variant of this idea in 1982.
. Such considerations are especially important in cryptography, as an efficient algorithm for the factorization of n-bit integers would be a significant development for any large
\displaystyle n
. Boolean circuit complexity is the study of such parallel algorithms, or rather
circuit families
. Let us first define a Boolean circuit.

Definition 9

(Boolean Circuit)
.

A
Boolean circuit
is a directed acyclic graph where internal nodes are logic gates (typically AND, OR, and NOT), leaves are input variables or constants (
\displaystyle 0
or
\displaystyle 1
), and there is a single designated output node. The circuit computes a Boolean function by propagating values from the inputs through the gates to the output.

A Boolean circuit family is simply the collection of problem-specific Boolean circuits, with one circuit being defined per input length.

Definition 10

(Boolean Circuit Family)
.

A Boolean circuit family is a sequence of Boolean circuits
\displaystyle\{C_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where each circuit
\displaystyle C_{n}
has
\displaystyle n
input variables and produces a single output bit.

We will now present the two classes of circuits directly relevant to our work.

Definition 11

(
\displaystyle TC^{i}
Circuit Complexity Classes)
.

The class
\displaystyle\mathsf{TC}^{i}
consists of languages decidable by families of Boolean circuits of polynomial size
\displaystyle O(poly(n))
and depth
\displaystyle O(\log^{i}n)
, with unbounded fan-in AND, OR, NOT, and majority (threshold) gates.

\displaystyle TC^{0}
consists of shallow (constant-depth) but arbitrary fan-in Boolean circuits of standard elements (AND, OR, NOT) augmented with a majority gate. Emulating solvable automata is proven to be within
\displaystyle TC^{0}

[
Sarrof et al. 2024
]
.

Definition 12

(
\displaystyle NC^{1}
Circuit Complexity Class)
.

The class
\displaystyle\mathsf{NC}^{1}
consists of languages decidable by families of Boolean circuits of polynomial size
\displaystyle O(poly(n))
and depth
\displaystyle O(\log n)
, with AND, OR, and NOT gates of fan-in 2.

\displaystyle NC^{1}
allows for logarithmic depth, but restricts the fan-in of the gates to 2. Emulating solvable automata is proven to be an
\displaystyle NC^{1}
-complete problem
Barrington 1989
, meaning that all
\displaystyle NC^{1}
problems can be reduced to it.

Many relations between circuit complexity classes remain unknown. It is, for example, not known whether
\displaystyle TC^{0}\neq NC^{1}
.
It is in fact not even known whether
\displaystyle TC^{0}
is equal to
\displaystyle P
, the class of problems decidable in polynomial time
[
Arora and Barak 2009
]
.
As a consequence, certain restrictions have been placed on the generation of Boolean circuits in order to allow for a more realistic and fine-grained study of their properties. The central restriction is that of
uniform generation
, or
uniformity
.

Definition 13

(Uniform Boolean Circuits)
.

A family of Boolean circuits
\displaystyle\{C_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
uniform
if there exists a deterministic Turing machine that, given
\displaystyle 1^{n}
, outputs a description of
\displaystyle C_{n}
in time bounded by some resource (e.g., logarithmic space or logarithmic time). Of particular interest to us is
log-space uniformity
, in which the Turing machine operates using
\displaystyle O(\log n)
space for input length
\displaystyle n
.

The total derivative of a loss
\displaystyle L
with respect to
\displaystyle x_{k}

\displaystyle\frac{dL}{dx_{k}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dL}{dx_{k}}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{k}}+\frac{dL}{\partial x_{k+1}}\frac{\partial x_{k+1}}{\partial x_{k}}

In our notation,
\displaystyle\frac{dL}{dx_{k}}

\displaystyle x_{k}

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{k}}

\displaystyle x_{k}
on
\displaystyle L

\displaystyle x_{k}

The expression above is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dL}{\partial x_{k}}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{k}}+\frac{dL}{dx_{k+1}}A_{k+1}

We can see that computing the derivative of
\displaystyle L
with respect to the states
\displaystyle x_{k}
is a
linear recurrence with sparse transition matrices
\displaystyle A_{k+1}

Computing the gradient thus reduces to a reverse parallel scan with matrix multiplication complexity of
\displaystyle O(N)

Within the framework of the parallel scan introduced in Section 2.4, gradient computation can then be parallelized using the following associative operation:

\displaystyle\displaystyle(A_{i},\frac{dL}{dx_{i-1}})\bullet(A_{i+1},\frac{dL}{dx_{i}})=(A_{i}A_{i+1},\frac{dL}{dx_{i}}A_{i}+\frac{dL}{dx_{i-1}})

We first restate and prove the two properties of the space of column one-hot matrices,
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{N\times N}

Property 3

(Algebraic Structure of One-hot Column Matrices)
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{N\times N}
is a monoid under matrix multiplication, i.e., it is closed under associative matrix multiplication and contains the identity.

Proof.

It is easy to verify that
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{N\times N}
contains the identity, as it is a column one-hot matrix. It is also trivial to verify that the product of two column one-hot matrices remains column one-hot. Given
\displaystyle A,B\in\mathbb{H}^{N\times N}
, the columns of
\displaystyle B

select and scale
the columns of
\displaystyle A
. More precisely, the entry in row
\displaystyle i
, column
\displaystyle j
of the product matrix
\displaystyle C=AB
is given by:

C_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{ik}B_{kj}

Since for each fixed
\displaystyle j
, the matrix
\displaystyle B
has exactly one non-zero entry in column
\displaystyle j
, say at row
\displaystyle k=r
, all other terms in the sum are zero. That is,

B_{kj}=0\quad\text{for all }k\neq r,\quad\text{and }B_{rj}\neq 0

So the sum reduces to a single term:

C_{ij}=A_{ir}\cdot B_{rj}

Now, since matrix
\displaystyle A
also has exactly one non-zero entry in each column, the
\displaystyle r
-th column of
\displaystyle A
has only one non-zero value, say at row
\displaystyle i=s
. Therefore,

A_{ir}=0\quad\text{for all }i\neq s,\quad\text{and }A_{sr}\neq 0

This implies that for each column
\displaystyle j
of the product
\displaystyle C
, there is exactly one row
\displaystyle i
such that
\displaystyle C_{ij}\neq 0
, and all other entries in that column are zero.
∎

\displaystyle PD
parametrization.

Lemma 1
.

Any single-layer complex SSM with state size
\displaystyle d
can be reduced to a single-layer real SSM with state size
\displaystyle 2d
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle x_{t},b_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
\displaystyle A_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
with
\displaystyle x_{t+1}=A_{t}x_{t}+b_{t}
. Write
\displaystyle x^{r}_{t}=\mathcal{R}(x_{t})
,
\displaystyle x^{i}_{t}=\mathcal{I}(x_{t})
,
\displaystyle b^{r}_{t}=\mathcal{R}(b_{t})
,
\displaystyle b^{i}_{t}=\mathcal{I}(b_{t})
,
\displaystyle A^{r}_{t}=\mathcal{R}(A_{t})
, and
\displaystyle A^{i}_{t}=\mathcal{I}(A_{t})
. Then

\displaystyle\displaystyle x^{r}_{t+1}+ix^{i}_{t+1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=x_{t+1}=A_{t}x_{t}+b_{t}=(A^{r}_{t}+iA^{i}_{t})(x^{r}_{t}+ix^{i}_{t})+b^{r}_{t}+ib^{i}_{t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=A_{t}^{r}x_{t}^{r}-A_{t}^{i}x_{t}^{i}+b_{t}^{r}+i(A_{t}^{i}x_{t}^{r}+A_{t}^{r}x_{t}^{i}+b_{t}^{i}).

In block matrix form, the complex SSM can be represented as a real SSM through

\begin{pmatrix}x_{t+1}^{r}\\
x_{t+1}^{i}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{t}^{r}&-A_{t}^{i}\\
A_{t}^{i}&+A_{t}^{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{t}^{r}\\
x_{t}^{i}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}b_{t}^{r}\\
b_{t}^{i}\end{pmatrix}.

∎

The following proposition states that for each
\displaystyle N\in\mathbb{N}
, one can construct an FSA which requires a state dimensionality of at least
\displaystyle N-1
to emulate using an SSM, under the assumption that each automaton state has a unique vector encoding. By combining this with Proposition 2, which states that all
\displaystyle N
-state automata can be emulated using a
\displaystyle PD
-SSM with one layer and state size
\displaystyle N
, the optimality of the
\displaystyle PD
decomposition for emulating FSAs is established.

Proposition 3

(Optimality of PD Parametrization)
.

For any
\displaystyle N
there exists a finite-state automaton with
\displaystyle N
states that cannot be emulated by any single-layer SSM with state size less than
\displaystyle N-1
under unique state encodings.

Proof.

Interpreting complex state size as
\displaystyle 2d
and real state size as
\displaystyle d
for
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, respectively, we can invoke Lemma
1
and stick to real SSMs in the remainder of this proof. Let
\displaystyle N\in\mathbb{N}
. For
\displaystyle N\leq 2
the statement is trivial, so suppose
\displaystyle N\geq 3
. Consider the FSA
\displaystyle\mathbb{A}
with states
\displaystyle s_{1},\ldots,s_{N}
and input vocabulary equal to the set of states mapping to state transitions

s_{x}\mapsto f_{x}\text{ where }f_{x}(s_{i})=\begin{cases}s_{i+1\!\!\!\!\mod N}&i=x\\
s_{i}&i\not=x.\\
\end{cases}

Now, assume by contradiction a unique (one-to-one) encoding
\displaystyle s_{i}\cong v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\displaystyle d\leq N-2
. Then there exist two states
\displaystyle s_{x},s_{y}
, w.l.o.g. set to
\displaystyle s_{N-1},s_{N}
, such that

\mathbb{V}:=\mathrm{span}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}\}=\mathrm{span}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{N-2}\}\text{ and }v_{N-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\alpha_{i}v_{i}\text{ as well as }v_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\beta_{i}v_{i}.

Now, a single-layer SSM that emulates
\displaystyle\mathbb{A}
must satisfy

A_{c}v_{i}+b_{c}=\begin{cases}v_{i}&i\not=c\\
v_{i+1\!\!\!\!\!\mod N}&i=c.\end{cases}

An immediate consequence is that
\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\alpha_{i}\not=1\not=\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\beta_{i}
, since otherwise either

v_{N}=\textstyle A_{N-1}v_{N-1}+b_{N-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\alpha_{i}(A_{N-1}v_{i}+b_{N-1})+b_{N-1}(1-\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\alpha_{i})=v_{N-1}+0

or

v_{1}=\textstyle A_{N}v_{N}+b_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\beta_{i}(A_{N}v_{i}+b_{N})+b_{N}(1-\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\beta_{i})=v_{N}+0,

violating the uniqueness assumption. But then

\mathbb{V}=\mathrm{span}\{v_{1}-v_{N-1},\ldots,v_{N-2}-v_{N-1}\},

since
\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\alpha_{i}(v_{i}-v_{N-1})=v_{N-1}(1-\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\alpha_{i})\not=0
. We can now leverage this spanning property by noting that
\displaystyle A_{N}(v_{i}-v_{N-1})=(v_{i}-v_{N-1})\ \forall i\in\{1,\ldots,N-2\}
and thus
\displaystyle A_{N}|_{\mathbb{V}}=I|_{\mathbb{V}}
. But if
\displaystyle A_{N}
acts as identity on
\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\ni v_{N}
, then
\displaystyle v_{1}=A_{N}v_{1}+b_{N}=v_{1}+b_{N}
and hence
\displaystyle b_{N}=0
. But then we reach the contradiction violating uniqueness of state encodings

v_{1}=A_{N}v_{N}+b_{N}=A_{N}|_{\mathbb{V}}v_{N}+b_{N}=I|_{\mathbb{V}}v_{N}+b_{N}=v_{N}.

∎

The following proposition is meant to highlight the importance of assuming a restricted readout, such as done in Proposition
3
with unique state encodings. Indeed, by using lookup tables that are exponentially large in the maximum sequence length, even a scalar SSM can emulate any finite state automaton. In practice, however, such lookup tables are entirely impractical.

Proposition 4

(Arbitrary Precision and Readout)
.

Consider
\displaystyle x_{t+1}=x_{t}+b_{t}
where
\displaystyle b_{t}=u_{t}\cdot k_{t}
with
\displaystyle u_{t}\in\mathbb{Q}
input encodings and
\displaystyle k_{t}=\sqrt{p_{t}}\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}
time encodings, where
\displaystyle p_{t}
is the
\displaystyle t
-th prime. Then, any FSA can be encoded into this scalar-valued SSM under an appropriate lookup table as readout.

Proof.

According to
Jaffe 2007
,
\displaystyle k_{t}
is linearly independent over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
, i.e., for
\displaystyle u_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
it holds that
\displaystyle u_{1}k_{1}+\cdots+u_{n}k_{n}=0\iff u_{1}=\cdots=u_{n}=0.
Therefore, no two differing input sequences
\displaystyle u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
and
\displaystyle v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
will result in the same state representation
\displaystyle\textstyle x_{t}=x_{0}+\sum_{i}u_{i}k_{i}
. Now, a simple identification of every FSA state with all the state representations
\displaystyle x_{t}
of associated input sequences that lead to said FSA state shows universal expressivity under arbitrary readout.
∎

General Overview of SSMs

LTI SSMs have been proposed as a more scalable alternative to Transformers that is also more performant on long sequences
Gu et al. 2020
,

,

,

A subsequent analysis showed promising language modeling performance with hybrid architectures based on gated SSMs and Attention

,
Fu et al. 2023
.
Time-variant hybrid systems were then shown to perform significantly better on practical tasks with the Mamba SSM which utilizes diagonal transition matrices
Gu and Dao 2023
.
Consequently, a line of work has demonstrated that hybrid models utilizing diagonal time-variant SSMs and attention exhibit better generalization to longer sequences, better general language modeling and reasoning performance
[
Ren et al. 2025
,

,
Lenz et al. 2025
,
Wu et al. 2025
,

.

Overview of Formal Results and SSM Matrix Structures

Concurrently, formal analyses of time-varying SSMs have shown limitations of the diagonal transition matrix structure
Merrill et al. 2024
,
Cirone et al. 2024
, spurring a line of work investigating the utility of block-diagonal and unstructured matrices
Fan et al. 2024
,
Terzić et al. 2025
, as well as more expressive structured transition matrices, most notably diagonal plus low-rank
Yang et al. 2024
,
Grazzi et al. 2024
,
Peng et al. 2025
,
Siems et al. 2025
, with the original inspiration stemming from at least as far back as fast-weight programmers

,

,
Schmidhuber 1992
whose computational structure contains products of generalized Householder matrices

Bischof and Van Loan 1987
.

Details of Formal Results

As a lower bound on diagonal SSMs,
Sarrof et al. 2024
show that a stack of complex diagonal SSM layers can emulate any solvable automaton, with the stack depth proportional to the Krohn-Rhodes (KR) complexity of the transformation semigroup
Krohn and Rhodes 1965
,
Margolis et al. 2024
.
As an upper bound on diagonal SSMs,
Merrill et al. 2024
derives a bound on fixed-depth diagonal selective SSMs with logarithmic precision representation, placing them in the
\displaystyle\text{L-uniform }TC^{0}
circuit complexity class.
This complexity class encompasses solvable automata.
DPLR matrices can emulate any automaton with depth proportional to KR-complexity and width linear in depth and arbitrary readout
Siems et al. 2025
.
