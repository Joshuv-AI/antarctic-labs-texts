Fixed window joint Euler deconvolution for depth estimation of magnetic and gravity data in the Shavaz region
Authors: Seyed Hossein Hosseini, Ahmad Afshar, Maysam Abedi, Saeed Ganbarifar, Behrooz Oskooi, Sobhan Moradi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents an advanced joint Euler deconvolution algorithm for the integrated analysis of magnetic and gravity data, which employs an adaptive fixed window size to enhance subsurface depth estimation in the Shavaz region. By simultaneously solving Euler’s equations for both potential fields, the proposed method effectively mitigates the limitations of traditional independent Euler depth estimation analyses, offering improved accuracy in determining the geometry and depth of mineralized bodies and structural discontinuities. Validation through synthetic modeling confirms the accuracy of the algorithm, while its application to field data from the Shavaz region, supported by known geological structures and drilling results, demonstrates its robustness and practical reliability in revealing the spatial distribution of iron mineralization.The adaptive windowing strategy, in conjunction with comprehensive preprocessing, enhances the delineation of anomaly boundaries and the precision of depth solutions. This integrated geophysical methodology provides a robust and efficient tool for mineral exploration within geologically complex terrains, thereby significantly diminishing both interpretational ambiguity and exploration risk.Keywords: Joint Euler deconvolution, Adaptive fixed window, Depth estimation, Gravity and magnetic data, Anomaly interpretation, Shavaz region, Iron mineralizationSubject terms: Geology, Geomagnetism, Geophysics, Mineralogy

Introduction

Euler deconvolution is established as a fundamental technique for the swift, semi-automated determination of the location and depth of subsurface sources that generate potential field anomalies, particularly within magnetic and gravity geophysical surveys1–3. The theoretical basis for this method is Euler’s homogeneity theorem, which establishes a link between the spatial derivatives of the potential field and the source geometry by means of the structural index (SI). For simple, idealized sources such as sphere, thin sheet, or infinite contact, the SI is an integer that directly corresponds to the scaling exponent of the field’s decay4.

Due to its computational efficiency and cost-effectiveness, the standard Euler deconvolution method is extensively applied in geophysical studies. This technique has been notably utilized for estimating the depth of mineral masses and shallow faults, calculating the depth of geothermal reservoirs, and determining the thickness of surface sediments5–9. Moreover, the Euler equation system has demonstrated its utility for delineating anomaly boundaries and determining the depth of structural discontinuities3,10.

Notwithstanding its merits, the conventional Euler method exhibits significant limitations when applied to more geologically representative scenarios, wherein subsurface sources may be thick, composite, or irregular. In such contexts, the SI is often utilized as a tunable or even fractional parameter, a practice that can yield depth estimates that might be either unreliable or misleading4 This study is one of the first to address the limitations of the traditional Euler deconvolution method by combining adaptive windowing and joint analysis of magnetic and gravity data. This approach ensures more accurate depth estimates and improves interpretation in complex geological environments like the Shavaz region. Recent theoretical analyses have demonstrated that non-integer or negative SI values lack a legitimate physical basis in potential field homogeneity and instead indicate a violation of the core assumptions upon which the standard Euler equation is based4,10–14. For instance, gravity anomalies arising from thick contact faults or finite steps cannot be accurately modeled using a single integer SI; rather, these cases necessitate more generalized formulations that consider all relevant spatial parameters, sometimes yielding negative or spatially variable SI values11.

Although these approaches maintain mathematical rigor and physical interpretability, they do so at the expense of greater computational complexity. A further persistent challenge is the significant dependence of the Euler solution on the size and positioning of the analysis window. Whereas the standard Euler method assumes a single local source within the window, real-world data frequently include multiple, overlapping anomalies, which can lead to solution scattering, depth smearing, and general instability. To counteract these effects and improve the robustness of depth estimation, advanced processing strategies such as adaptive windowing, solution clustering, and the joint inversion of gravity and magnetic data have been introduced15.

Recent work by the authors introduced a dynamic window-based algorithm, which provides a comprehensive approach to optimizing window sizes for Euler deconvolution16. Building upon this line of research, the present study focuses on the implementation and validation of a joint Euler deconvolution framework that utilizes an adaptive fixed-window strategy. This method is investigated for its robustness and practical utility, offering a streamlined workflow particularly advantageous for rapid initial assessment in mineral exploration contexts.

Recent advancements have also encompassed the application of higher-order derivatives and Hilbert transform-based formulations, which exhibit reduced sensitivity to regional trends and permit geometric validation of the derived solutions2. Techniques such as the second-order Euler equation produce “depth parabolas”, which serve as an internal consistency check and facilitate the identification of physically plausible solution clusters.

The process begins with the input of preprocessed gravity and magnetic data grids, along with user-defined parameters: the SI, the analysis window size, and a tolerance (ε) for solution acceptance. For computational efficiency, the spatial derivatives of the potential fields (∂P/∂x, ∂P/∂y, ∂P/∂z) are pre-calculated across the entire grid. The core of the algorithm is an iterative loop that moves a fixed-size window across the data grid.

Computational workflow of the adaptive fixed-window joint Euler deconvolution algorithm.

At each window position (cx, cy), the algorithm assembles a joint matrix system, in a generic form of Ax = b, as formulated in Eq. (6), incorporating data points from both potential fields within the window. This system is solved to yield a preliminary source location (x₀, y₀, z₀). Subsequently, each solution undergoes a rigorous validation check to filter out physically implausible or mathematically unstable results. A solution is considered valid only if it meets several criteria: the estimated depth (z₀) must be positive (i.e., below the observation surface), the residual error of the linear system solution must be less than the specified tolerance (ε), and the location must be stable inside the window. Solutions that pass this validation are stored, while all others are discarded. After the entire grid has been processed, the collected set of valid solutions is clustered to produce the final output: a map that delineates the locations and depths of the underlying geological sources.

Prior to the application of the Euler deconvolution algorithm to field data, a comprehensive preprocessing workflow is essential to enhance data quality and isolate anomalies corresponding to the targeted geological sources. This workflow begins with the removal of large-scale background fields to ensure that the depth estimation is focused on local features. For the magnetic data, this involves the subtraction of the International Geomagnetic Reference Field (IGRF). Subsequently, for both gravity and magnetic datasets, a first-order regional trend is removed using polynomial fitting, which effectively eliminates long-wavelength effects that could otherwise bias the depth solutions. To suppress high-frequency noise from surficial sources and emphasize signals from deeper structures, the residual potential field data are then upward continued. As a final step before detailed interpretation, an edge-detection technique such as the analytical signal filter is applied. This transform is highly effective at delineating the boundaries of causative bodies, thereby guiding the selection of regions for detailed analysis and aiding in the effective application of the moving window for Euler deconvolution.

Depth estimation of synthetic models

To evaluate the effectiveness and accuracy of the proposed joint Euler deconvolution method, synthetic potential field data were generated and analyzed. The synthetic modeling framework consisted of two scenarios: a horizontal prism (Model 1) and a dipping prism (Model 2). Synthetic gravity and magnetic data were generated on a 3D regular grid using analytical potential field solutions, with all calculations performed using custom MATLAB algorithms that leverage fast-modeling techniques19,20 and were validated against MAG3D and GRAV3D results. To simulate realistic field conditions, Gaussian noise was added to the final datasets. For subsequent data processing, visualization, and forward modeling, Geosoft Oasis montaj, Version 8.3 (Seequent, a Bentley Systems company; https://www.seequent.com/products-solutions/oasis-montaj/), was used, incorporating the MAG3D and GRAV3D modules for magnetic and gravity modeling, respectively. The results of our forward models were validated by comparison with outputs from these modules.

Synthetic model 1: horizontal prism

Following the methodology outlined by Guo et al.20, the subsurface was discretized into a 3D regular grid, and the theoretical responses of magnetic and gravity anomalies were computed for a single horizontal rectangular prism[21]. The forward modeling of both magnetic total field anomaly and gravity anomaly was performed by calculating the analytical solutions for a uniformly magnetized and dense rectangular prism, as described by Bhattacharyya22 and Guo et al.20. Gaussian noise with a standard deviation equal to 2% of the maximum data amplitude was added to simulate realistic measurement conditions.

Synthetic model 2: dipping prism

To further evaluate the robustness of the joint Euler deconvolution method, we modelled a more geologically realistic case involving a two-block configuration, with the northern block dipping 30° eastward. The geometric and physical parameters, as listed in Table 4, include density contrasts of 1.5 g/cm3 and 1.0 g/cm3, and magnetic susceptibilities of 0.10 and 0.085 SI units for the northern and southern blocks, respectively. These susceptibility values are at or below the practical 0.1 SI threshold for significant self-demagnetization in elongated bodies. Under the assumption of linear magnetization with no remanent component—which is applicable when the magnetic susceptibility is less than 1 in SI units—the induced magnetization remains proportional to the applied geomagnetic field. A check using demagnetization tensor corrections showed amplitude variations of less than 3% and depth shifts of less than 5 m, both within the solution uncertainty, confirming negligible self-demagnetization effects.The geomagnetic field parameters matched those of Model 1, and the data grid spanned 1000 × 1000 m at 10 m intervals.

The method remains robust for realistic noise amplitudes up to approximately 5% of the maximum anomaly level; however, performance improves markedly with higher signal-to-noise ratios, as is the case for standard Euler deconvolution and other inversion approaches. For field data, we recommend noise suppression prior to applying the method.

Case study: Euler depth estimation in the Shavaz region

This study employed the Euler depth estimation method on gravity and magnetic potential field data to delineate subsurface geological structures and estimate the source depths of mineralized bodies in the Shavaz region, Yazd Province, Iran. This integrated geophysical analysis provides critical insights into the spatial geometry and structural controls governing the area’s iron-bearing deposits.

Geological setting of the study area

The Shavaz region lies in the western sector of the Dehshir fault system, a major tectonic feature in central Iran. The geological map of the area, derived from the 1:100,000-scale Taghestan quadrangle (Fig. 13), highlights the lithological diversity and structural complexity of the region.

The predominant lithologies within the study area are Cretaceous limestone formations, which constitute the highest topographic elevations, surpassing 2000 m above sea level. These carbonate units display two principal structural orientations: a north–south trend in the eastern sector and an east–west alignment in the central region. Despite their close spatial association with Oligocene–Pliocene magmatic intrusions, the limestones largely preserve their primary textures with limited evidence of metamorphic overprinting. This preservation implies either a relatively shallow emplacement of the intrusions or subsequent fault displacement postdating emplacement, thereby shielding the carbonate units from significant thermal alteration..

Mafic–ultramafic intrusive rocks, dated to the Oligocene-Pliocene epoch, occur adjacent to the limestone formations and serve as hosts for the region’s principal iron mineralization.These intrusive bodies exhibit significant hydrothermal alteration, notably sericitization and diopside formation, indicative of rigorous fluid–rock interactions. The iron deposits are categorized as skarn-type mineralization, distinguished by their association with calcic lithologies and the notable absence of dolomite, reflecting a calcium-skarn affinity. Mineralization predominantly occurs along the contact zones between carbonate and intrusive units, where metasomatic alteration was most pronounced.

The geological setting is further complicated by the occurrence of metamorphic lithologies, predominantly slates and phyllites, which are distributed along the boundaries of both limestone units and intrusive bodies. These metamorphic formations exhibit variable deformation intensities, indicative of the region’s intricate tectonic evolution..

The spatial relationships between these lithological units, coupled with hydrothermal alteration evidence, indicate that iron mineralization in the Shavaz region formed through metamorphic-hydrothermal processes associated with Oligocene-Pliocene magmatism. Structural discontinuities, such as faults and fractures, likely acted as fluid conduits, concentrating mineralization along carbonate-intrusive contacts.

Potential field geophysical survey for iron-bearing exploration

A geophysical survey of gravity and magnetic potential fields was conducted across the Shavaz region to identify subsurface anomalies indicative of iron mineralization. The survey consisted of 18 north–south profiles, spaced at intervals of 50 to 100 m. Measurement stations along each profile were positioned at intervals of 25 to 50 m, covering a total study area of 500 × 600 m. The Earth’s magnetic field in the study area was characterized by an intensity of 46,500 nanoteslas (nT), an inclination angle of 49°, and a declination angle of 3°. The main iron source exhibited a NW–SE orientation, influenced by the Dehshir-Baft fault system. This survey provided critical input data for subsequent Euler depth estimation.

Magnetic survey

The magnetic survey grid was designed to align with the geological structures and the geometry of the target deposit. To achieve optimal coverage and resolution, two grid designs were implemented: a 10 × 10-m grid for areas with high magnetic intensity and variability, and a 20 × 10-m grid for regions with moderate intensity and variability. Magnetic data were collected along 25 profiles, with profile lengths varying between 300 and 700 m. The total study area spanned 2000 square meters, and 1137 ground magnetic data points were recorded using a Gem-19 T magnetometer. This survey design ensured thorough and detailed coverage, enabling the precise mapping of magnetic anomalies associated with geological structures and potential mineralization zones.

Gravity survey

Gravity measurements were carried out using a high-precision Sintrex CG-5 gravimeter, which offers an accuracy of 0.01 milligals, ensuring exceptional precision in data acquisition. Survey stations were marked with centimeter-level accuracy utilizing advanced mapping techniques, guaranteeing high spatial precision and reliability of the collected data. The principal aims of the gravity survey encompassed the characterization of gravity anomalies associated with subsurface geological structures, delineation of their lateral and vertical boundaries, investigation of the relationships between gravity anomalies and magnetite vein distributions, and the validation and enhancement of findings derived from the magnetometric survey.

The survey comprised 18 north–south profiles, spaced 50 to 100 m apart, with station intervals along each profile ranging from 25 to 50 m. This systematic data acquisition strategy was designed to achieve high resolution and comprehensive areal coverage, thereby facilitating the precise identification and characterization of subsurface features.

To assess the reliability of the interpreted subsurface structures and ensure the credibility of the proposed framework, a multi-faceted validation approach was employed. The initial validation was performed on the synthetic models, which confirmed the algorithm’s accuracy under controlled conditions where the true source depth was known. For the Shavaz field data, the validation was achieved by rigorously comparing the depth estimation results with ground-truth data. The interpreted depths and locations of mineralized bodies show a strong correlation with known geological features, regional structural trends, and, most importantly, direct evidence from drilling results and previous geophysical studies conducted in the area23–27. This congruence between our non-invasive geophysical interpretation and direct geological evidence provides robust validation for the accuracy of the joint Euler deconvolution method in this complex geological setting. To further substantiate these findings, we directly compared our results with lithological data from two exploratory wells drilled in the area (Fig. 21).

Lithological logs from Well 1 and Well 2 in the Shavaz study area, providing ground-truth validation for the depth estimation results. The logs show alluvial cover (ALL), low-grade (LG) and high-grade (HG) iron mineralization, and barren units (BS)23,24.

Well 1, located in the northwestern zone of high magnetic intensity, intersected a substantial, 28-m-thick body of high-grade iron mineralization at a relatively shallow depth. This discovery aligns perfectly with the results shown in Fig. 11c, where our joint Euler analysis produced a dense cluster of shallow solutions (depths < 50 m) in this exact area. In contrast, Well 2, drilled in the southeastern portion of the anomaly, revealed a thinner, 18-m mineralized zone at a greater depth, beneath a thicker layer of alluvial cover. This again corroborates our model, which shows the Euler solutions becoming deeper and more scattered towards the southeast, accurately tracing the plunging geometry of the ore body.

This direct, one-to-one correlation between our non-invasive geophysical depth estimates and the physical evidence from drilling provides an appropriate validation for our framework’s reliability and applicability in mineral exploration. The integration of geological and geophysical investigations in the Shavaz region has yielded a comprehensive understanding of the processes governing iron mineralization and the associated structural controls. The findings underscore the exploration potential along carbonate-intrusive contacts and structural corridors that have likely acted as conduits for fluid flow and metasomatic alteration. This study underscores the utility of combining Euler-based depth estimation with geophysical survey data as a robust approach for targeting mineralization in geologically complex terrains.

Discussion and Conclusion

This study introduces a novel and robust methodology for estimating the depth of subsurface sources through the integrated analysis of gravity and magnetic data using a joint Euler deconvolution algorithm within an adaptive fixed-size window framework. The approach effectively overcomes a key limitation of conventional Euler deconvolution—namely, the reliance on static and suboptimal window selection—by incorporating intelligent window management and internal optimization mechanisms. Validation through systematic synthetic modeling and application to field data from the Shavaz region demonstrates notable improvements in the accuracy and reliability of depth estimations compared to traditional techniques..

Synthetic modeling confirmed the algorithm’s capability to accurately reconstruct source geometries and demonstrated its robustness in the presence of noise, affirming its applicability to geologically complex settings. The field implementation in the Shavaz region, underpinned by integrated geological and geophysical analyses, elucidated the spatial distribution and structural controls governing iron mineralization. These findings emphasize the exploration potential along carbonate-intrusive boundaries and fluid migration pathways. Moreover, preprocessing procedures—including IGRF subtraction, regional trend removal, and upward continuation—contributed to the generation of high-quality input data, thereby enhancing the reliability of the depth estimation workflow.The findings highlight the efficacy of joint inversion in disentangling overlapping geophysical anomalies and minimizing interpretative uncertainty, thereby offering a more robust foundation for mineral exploration in structurally intricate regions. The implementation of an adaptive windowing strategy, coupled with the integration of multiple potential field datasets, constitutes a significant methodological advancement—enhancing interpretative accuracy, computational efficiency, and overall applicability within mineral exploration frameworks.

Despite its demonstrated robustness and practical utility, it is important to acknowledge the potential limitations of the fixed-window joint Euler deconvolution framework. The method’s performance may be challenged in scenarios with multiple, closely-spaced sources, particularly when these sources exhibit different geometries and require different SI values, as a single SI may not be optimal for all causative bodies. Furthermore, the underlying assumption of homogeneity means the framework could produce less reliable depth estimates for sources with a highly inhomogeneous distribution of physical properties, such as disseminated mineralization with variable susceptibility and density. The approach also faces challenges with very sparse datasets where both gravity and magnetic data coverage is poor. Finally, for deposits that generate very wide surface anomalies, the fixed-window method may struggle to precisely delineate source boundaries, potentially averaging the depth solution over too large an area and losing resolution.

It is important to acknowledge the sensitivity of the proposed method to its primary input parameters. The selection of the SI is highly dependent on prior geological knowledge; an incorrect SI, chosen due to ambiguous or complex source geometry, can lead to biased depth estimates. Similarly, while the fixed-window approach is computationally efficient, its accuracy is contingent on the selection of an appropriate window size. A window that is too small may fail to capture the full wavelength of an anomaly, leading to scattered solutions, whereas a window that is too large can average signals from multiple sources, degrading the resolution. The results of this study are therefore contingent on the careful selection of these parameters, and a detailed sensitivity analysis for various geological scenarios remains a valuable direction for future research to better quantify these effects for potential users.

Future research should focus on extending this framework to include additional geophysical datasets, such as electromagnetic or seismic data, and validating the approach in other geologically challenging regions. Overall, the proposed joint Euler deconvolution method represents a significant step toward more reliable subsurface characterization, offering a practical framework to de-risk and accelerate targeted mineral exploration in complex geological terrains.
