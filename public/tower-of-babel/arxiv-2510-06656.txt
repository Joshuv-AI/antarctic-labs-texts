Global weak solutions to nonlinear kinetic Fokker–Planck equations in bounded domains under physical initial data
Young-Pil Choi, Sihyun Song

Abstract
. We establish the global existence of weak solutions to a nonlinear kinetic Fokker–Planck equation with degenerate diffusion, under either inflow or partial absorption-reflection boundary conditions. The novelty of our approach lies in constructing solutions under solely the physical assumptions on the initial and boundary data, namely finite mass, kinetic energy, and entropy, with no additional regularity imposed. To overcome the lack of uniform ellipticity, we develop a new compactness principle based on weighted Fisher information, which yields strong L 1 L^{1} convergence of approximate solutions. This framework provides a robust existence theory under only the physically relevant conditions, and applies uniformly to both inflow and reflection boundary settings.



1.
Introduction

The main purpose of this work is to investigate nonlinear kinetic Fokker–Planck equations in a bounded spatial domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with general boundary conditions. Our focus is on the initial-boundary value problem and the compactness properties of solutions, established under only the physically relevant assumptions of finite mass, energy, and entropy.

We consider a particle distribution function
f=f(t,x,v)\geq 0
, depending on time
t\geq 0
, position
x\in\Omega
, and velocity
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. From
f
, one naturally associates the macroscopic quantities: the local density
\rho_{f}
, mean velocity
u_{f}
, and temperature
\mathcal{T}_{f}
, defined by

\displaystyle\rho_{f}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\textnormal{d}v,\quad u_{f}:=\begin{cases}0&\rho_{f}=0,\\
\frac{1}{\rho_{f}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}vf\,\textnormal{d}v&\rho_{f}\neq 0,\end{cases}\quad\mathcal{T}_{f}:=\begin{cases}0&\rho_{f}=0,\\
\frac{1}{d\rho_{f}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v-u_{f}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}v&\rho_{f}\neq 0.\end{cases}

With these notations, the nonlinear kinetic Fokker–Planck equation under study takes the form

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\nu_{f}\nabla_{v}\cdot\Big(\mathcal{T}_{f}\nabla_{v}f+(v-u_{f})f\Big)\quad\text{in}\quad(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
f|_{t=0}=f^{0},\end{cases}

where
\nu_{f}
denotes the collision frequency, which we assume throughout this work satisfies

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\textnormal{$\nu_{f}=\nu(\rho_{f},j_{f},V_{f})$ where $j_{f}:=\rho_{f}u_{f}$ and $V_{f}:=\rho_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}$},\\
\nu\in C^{0}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathbb{R}_{+}),\\
\textnormal{either $\nu>0$, or $\nu(\rho,j,V)\geq 0$ with equality if and only if $\rho=0$}.\end{cases}

This covers the cases of, for instance

\displaystyle\nu(\rho,j,V)=1,\;\frac{\rho}{1+\rho},\;\frac{\rho^{\alpha}\mathcal{T}^{\beta}}{1+\rho^{\alpha}\mathcal{T}^{\beta}},\ \ldots.

The study of generalized Fokker–Planck type equations can be traced back to the classical works of
[
Kir46
,
Gre52
]
, where they were introduced to describe the evolution of dilute gases. Variants of these models also arise in the context of inelastic granular flows
[
BDS99
]
. Despite this long-standing interest, the mathematical analysis of the Cauchy problem (
1.1
) has remained incomplete for decades, with major obstacles stemming from the nonlinear structure of the collision operator.

In the linear Fokker–Planck case, which corresponds to (
1.1
) with
\nu\equiv\mathcal{T}_{f}\equiv 1
and
u_{f}\equiv 0
, a comprehensive theory has been developed even in the presence of Poisson interactions. Classical and weak solutions in the whole space have been extensively investigated; see, for instance
[
Bou93
,
Deg86
,
HJ13
,
RW92
,
VO90
]
and
[
CS95
,
CS97
,
Cas98
,
Vic91
]
, respectively, as well as the smoothing effects described in
[
Bou95
]
. For initial-boundary value problems, significant progress has also been achieved in
[
BCS97
,
Car98
,
HK19
,
MH22
,
Mis10
]
, covering various types of boundary mechanisms including specular and diffusive reflections.

Nonlinear Fokker–Planck equations, where the diffusion and drift coefficients depend on the solution itself, remain much less understood. Existing results are mostly confined to perturbative regimes near global Maxwellians, where the nonlinearity can be treated as a small perturbation. In this framework, global strong solutions have been obtained, for instance, in
[
Bed17
,
LY21
]
. Further developments for the ellipsoidal Vlasov–Fokker–Planck model, designed to capture correct Prandtl numbers, were carried out in
[
MM17
,
SJ19
]
and subsequent works. Numerical studies have also addressed the nonlinear Fokker–Planck dynamics, particularly in the design of asymptotic-preserving schemes and the investigation of long-time behavior
[
AC23
,
FN22
]
. These contributions provide a rich perturbative theory for strong solutions near equilibrium, but they rely essentially on the smallness of perturbations and do not extend to large-data weak solutions.

Indeed, the theory of weak solutions for nonlinear Fokker–Planck operators remains largely unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, almost all previous works have been restricted to the case of constant collision frequency
\nu\equiv 1
, and to the spatially periodic setting
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The first rigorous treatment of the Cauchy problem for (
1.1
) in this framework was given in
[
CG04
]
(see also
[
CG03
]
). Their approach relied on two fundamental structural features of (
1.1
). The first is that the nonlinear Fokker–Planck equation can be interpreted as a gradient flow of the relative entropy with respect to the local Maxwellian. This insight becomes clear once one observes that

\displaystyle\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\mathcal{T}_{f}\nabla_{v}f+(v-u_{f})f\right)=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(f\mathcal{T}_{f}\nabla_{v}\log\frac{f}{M[f]}\right),\quad M[f]:=\frac{\rho_{f}}{(2\pi\mathcal{T}_{f})^{d/2}}e^{-\frac{|v-u_{f}|^{2}}{2\mathcal{T}_{f}}}.

The second feature is the transport structure in the
(t,x)
variables. By combining both perspectives into a discrete splitting scheme, Carlen and Gangbo constructed approximate solutions and proved their (weak) convergence to a weak solution of (
1.1
). A key requirement in their argument was the set of strong a priori assumptions

(1.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|v|^{6}+|\log f^{0}|)f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v<+\infty,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f^{0}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v<+\infty,

which played a crucial role in displacement convexity estimates controlling entropy dissipation.

More recently, in the same periodic setting with
\nu\equiv 1
, the Cauchy problem for (
1.1
) was resolved in
[
CHY25
]
. Their analysis was carried out under a different set of assumptions, namely

(1.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|v|^{3}+|\log f^{0}|)f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v<+\infty,\qquad\|f^{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}<+\infty.

Compared with the earlier work
[
CG04
]
, the requirement on the initial velocity moments is significantly relaxed (from sixth-order moments down to third-order). On the other hand, the condition that
f^{0}
be a member of
L^{\infty}
is substantially stronger. To be more precise, following
[
CHY25
]
, one can observe that the third-moment condition can be replaced by the weaker assumption

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2+\varepsilon}f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v<+\infty\quad\textnormal{for some $\varepsilon>0$},

but both the boundedness condition on
f^{0}
and the requirement of moments beyond the energy level appear essential in their approach, and cannot be entirely removed.

In this work, we extend the framework of nonlinear Fokker–Planck theory to general bounded domains and physically admissible initial data. Namely, by developing a new compactness method, based on a weighted Fisher information, we tackle the existence theory for (
1.1
) under
solely the physical assumptions
on the initial data:

(1.5)

\displaystyle f^{0}\geq 0,\quad\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|v|^{2}+|\log f^{0}|)f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v<+\infty.

The condition (
1.5
) ensures that the total mass, kinetic energy, and entropy are finite at
t=0
. Our main result is twofold: we construct solutions to (
1.1
) under no additional hypotheses beyond (
1.5
), and we establish a new
strong compactness property
for approximating sequences, a phenomenon not previously observed for nonlinear Fokker–Planck operators of the form (
1.1
).

To clarify the novelty, it is instructive to first recall the linear Fokker–Planck equation,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\Delta_{v}f+\nabla_{v}\cdot(vf).

The diffusion in (
1.6
) endows the system with significant regularization; indeed, since the seminal work of Hörmander
[
H6̈7
]
, the hypoelliptic operator
\mathcal{L}f:=\Delta_{v}f+\nabla_{v}\cdot(vf)
is known to provide smoothing in all variables
(t,x,v)
(see, for instance,
[
HN04
,
LBL08
]
). Consequently, if
\{f_{m}\}
is a sequence of smooth solutions to (
1.6
) with uniform bounds of the type (
1.5
), then
{f_{m}}
converges
strongly
in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
to a limit
f
, which is again a solution to (
1.6
). This strong compactness mechanism was crucially exploited in
[
DL88
]
to construct solutions to the Fokker–Planck–Boltzmann equation, where the nonlinearity stems from the Boltzmann collision operator but the diffusion remains linear.

However, the approach in
[
DL88
]
relies in an essential way on the Green’s function of the linear operator
-\Delta_{v}
, and thus cannot be directly applied when the diffusion coefficient depends nonlinearly on the solution. It is therefore natural to expect difficulties when treating nonlinear equations of the type (
1.1
), or even simpler variants such as

(1.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\mathcal{T}_{f}\Delta_{v}f.

Suppose we attempt to construct solutions to (
1.7
) by approximating with a sequence
\{f_{m}\}
of smooth solutions to a regularized system. Under the basic assumption (
1.5
), one of the few a priori bounds available (disregarding boundary contributions) is the entropy dissipation estimate

\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\log f\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+4\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{T}_{f}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{0}\log f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

This yields the uniform control

\sup_{m}\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{T}_{f_{m}}\left|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{m}}\right|^{2}\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v<C<+\infty,

which may be viewed as a “nonlinearly weighted Fisher information.”

The central question we address in this work is whether such a bound is sufficient to recover, in the nonlinear setting, a phenomenon analogous to the linear case:

Can one deduce that the sequence
\{f_{m}\}
is
strongly compact
in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
?

We show that under mild structural assumptions on the diffusion coefficient (in the model case
\mathcal{T}_{f_{m}}
) and on the velocity averages of
f_{m}
, one can indeed recover strong convergence:
f_{m}\to f
almost everywhere and in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. These assumptions are not artificial; they arise naturally in the construction of approximate solutions to the Cauchy problem for (
1.1
) via regularization schemes. Formulated precisely in Proposition
2.1
, this new compactness principle establishes strong
L^{1}
compactness for nonlinear kinetic Fokker–Planck operators under weighted Fisher information bounds. It is flexible enough to handle merely measurable diffusion profiles and provides the essential compactness tool for treating bounded domains and rough initial data.

Informal compactness principle.

Under the assumption of bounded mass, energy, and entropy, we demonstrate that approximate solutions enjoy

strong
L^{1}
compactness
, driven through the weighted Fisher information bound
together with mild hypotheses on the velocity-averages. Roughly speaking: if
\{f_{m}\}
is weakly compact in
L^{1}
, velocity averages of the form
\left\{\int f_{m}\psi(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}
are compact in
L^{1}_{t,x}
, and the weighted Fisher information

\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}a_{m}(t,x)\left|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{m}}\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

remains uniformly bounded, then provided that
a_{m}\to a\geq 0
pointwise, we can obtain
f_{m}\to f
almost everywhere and in
L^{1}
, under a certain condition on the support of
a
. The precise formulation and proof of this result are given in Proposition
2.1
of Section
2
.

As a consequence, we are able to solve the initial-boundary value problem associated with (
1.1
) under
only
the assumption (
1.5
), up to a mild hypothesis on the regularity of the spatial domain
\Omega
and appropriately prescribed boundary conditions. Our new compactness principle allows us to extract limits of approximate solutions in the strong topology of
L^{1}
, thereby overcoming the lack of classical regularity and considerably streamlining the overall argument. A detailed outline of the proof strategy will be presented in Section
1.2
.

1.1.
Main results

We are now in a position to state our main existence theorems. Two natural boundary-value problems are treated: the inflow problem and the partial absorption-reflection problem. Both are established under only the physically relevant assumptions on the initial and boundary data.

Our first result addresses the inflow problem associated with (
1.1
), where boundary values are prescribed on the incoming set of characteristics. We adopt the following nomenclature for
t\in[0,T]
:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\pm}^{t}:=\{(s,x,v)\in[0,t]\times\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}:\pm(n(x)\cdot v)>0\},

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}^{t}:=\{(s,x,v)\in[0,t]\times\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}:(n(x)\cdot v)=0\},

\displaystyle\Sigma^{t}:=\Sigma_{+}^{t}\cup\Sigma_{-}^{t}\cup\Sigma_{0}^{t},

and denote by
\textnormal{d}\sigma=\textnormal{d}\sigma(x)
the Hausdorff measure on
\partial\Omega
.

Theorem 1.1
.

We assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded open set with
C^{1}
boundary, and outwards normal
n(x)
. Suppose the collision frequency
\nu
satisfies the assumptions in (
1.2
). Let
f^{0}
given with only the physically relevant assumptions (
1.5
). Suppose
g
is such that

\displaystyle g\geq 0,\quad\int_{(0,T)\times\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|v|^{2}+|\log g|)g\,|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v<+\infty.

Then for any finite time horizon
T>0
, there exists a weak solution
(f,\gamma f)
to the following inflow problem associated to (
1.1
),

(1.8)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\nu_{f}\nabla_{v}\cdot\Big(\mathcal{T}_{f}\nabla_{v}f+(v-u_{f})f\Big)\quad\text{in}\quad(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
f|_{t=0}=f^{0},\\
\gamma f|_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}=g,\end{cases}

in the sense that the following properties are satisfied:

(I)

The solution satisfies

\begin{split}&(1+|v|^{2})f\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})),\quad f\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L\log L(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})),\\
&(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f\in L^{1}(\Sigma^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v),\quad\gamma f\in L\log L(\Sigma^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v).\end{split}

(II)

The following very weak Green’s formula holds for all
t\in[0,T]
and
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,t]\times\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})

(1.9)

\begin{split}&\int_{\{t\}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\varphi\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{0}\varphi(0,\cdot,\cdot)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\gamma f\,\varphi\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad=-\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}g\varphi(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+\int_{(0,t)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}\Delta_{v}\varphi-\nu_{f}\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot(v-u_{f})f\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.\end{split}

(III)

The weak gradient
\nabla_{v}f
is well-defined as a measurable function on
(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v<+\infty.

Correspondingly, the weak Green’s formula also holds, in other words we can replace the term
\int f\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}\Delta_{v}\varphi
of (
1.9
) with
\int-\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}\nabla_{v}f\cdot\nabla_{v}\varphi
.

(IV)

The energy inequality below holds for all
t\in(0,T]

\begin{split}&\int_{\{t\}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|v|^{2})f\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|v|^{2})f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad\leq\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})g\,|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v.\end{split}

(V)

The entropy inequality below holds for all
t\in(0,T]

\begin{split}&\int_{\{t\}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\log f\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\gamma f\log\gamma f\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad+\int_{(0,t)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\nu_{f}}{f\mathcal{T}_{f}}|\mathcal{T}_{f}\nabla_{v}f+(v-u_{f})f|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&\leq\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}g\log g|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{0}\log f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.\end{split}

Remark 1.2
.

Several remarks are in order.

(1)

By using the same strategies, one can obtain an analogous result for the periodic spatial domain
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{d}
(neglecting boundary conditions). This substantially improves the Cauchy theory for (
1.1
), showing that (
1.5
) suffices for weak solutions (compare with (
1.3
) and (
1.4
)).

(2)

Assuming additional spatial moment bounds on the initial and boundary datum, the result extends to unbounded domains, including
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The adaptations are straightforward.

(3)

By
L\log L
we refer to the (equivalence class of) measurable functions for which
\int|f|(1+|\log f|)<+\infty.

(4)

For a given
f
, the function
\gamma f
satisfying (
1.9
) is unique, and we refer to it as the
trace
of
f
. However, due to the lack of regularity, it is unclear whether a bounded trace operator exists in the corresponding function spaces (see
[
Zhu24
, Section 1.2.2]
). For this reason, we will always define a solution as a pair
(f,\gamma f)
.

It remains an open question whether the weak solutions obtained in Theorem
1.1
are renormalized in the sense of DiPerna–Lions
[
DL89c
]
. In related contexts such as alignment models
[
Shv25
]
, every weak solution was shown to be renormalized, but the argument there relies on a ”thickness condition” for the diffusion coefficient, a structural property not present in our model. Establishing renormalization in the present setting is therefore an interesting and nontrivial direction for future research.

Using the same techniques, we can also address the case where both the collision frequency and the temperature are constant, in which case the model reduces to

(1.10)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g+v\cdot\nabla_{x}g=\Delta_{v}g+\nabla_{v}\cdot((v-u_{g})g).

For this equation, global weak solutions were obtained in
[
KMT13
]
assuming initial data of class
L^{\infty}
. Classical solutions to (
1.10
) within a perturbative framework were constructed in
[
Cho16
]
, while the incompressible Euler and Navier–Stokes limits were subsequently established in
[
CJ24
,
CJ26
]
. In contrast, our compactness method (see in particular Lemma
2.3
) provides weak solutions under only the physically relevant assumptions (
1.5
).

In addition, if the collision frequency
\nu
fails to satisfy assumption (
1.2
), for example when
\nu(\rho,j,V)=\rho
, the weak formulation of (
1.1
) may not even be well defined. Nevertheless, related models have been investigated in
[
IM21
,
AZ24
]
, where global solutions were obtained for

\partial_{t}h+v\cdot\nabla_{x}h=\rho_{h}(\Delta_{v}h+\nabla_{v}\cdot(vh)),

under Gaussian-type bounds on the initial data. The crucial observation there is that such Gaussian control propagates in time thanks to a
maximum principle
, which ensures
\rho_{h}
remains uniformly bounded. This effectively reduces the situation to one with bounded collision frequency, bringing it back within the scope of assumptions analogous to (
1.2
).

Our second main theorem concerns boundary conditions of absorption-reflection type. In kinetic theory, such laws arise naturally when part of the incoming flux is reflected specularly while the rest is absorbed. This setting generalizes the inflow problem treated in Theorem
1.1
. Under the same physically relevant assumptions on the data, we again construct global weak solutions.

Theorem 1.3
.

We assume that
\Omega
,
\nu
,
f^{0}
, and
T
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
. Let
\theta\in[0,1)
. Then the partial absorption-reflection problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\nu_{f}\nabla_{v}\cdot\Big(\mathcal{T}_{f}\nabla_{v}f+(v-u_{f})f\Big)\quad\text{in}\quad(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
f|_{t=0}=f^{0},\\
\gamma f(t,x,v)=\theta\gamma f(t,x,L_{x}v),\quad\forall(t,x,v)\in\Sigma_{-}^{T},\\
L_{x}(v):=v-2(n(x)\cdot v)n(x)\end{cases}

admits a weak solution
(f,\gamma f)
in the sense that

(I)

The solution satisfies

\begin{split}&(1+|v|^{2})f\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})),\quad f\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L\log L(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})),\\
&(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f\in L^{1}(\Sigma^{T}_{+},|n(x)\cdot v|\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v),\quad\gamma f\in L\log L(\Sigma_{+}^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v).\end{split}

(II)

The following very weak Green’s formula holds for all
t\in[0,T]
and
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,t]\times\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

\displaystyle\int_{\{t\}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\varphi\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{0}\,\varphi(0,\cdot,\cdot,)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\gamma f(s,x,v)\,[\varphi(s,x,v)-\theta\varphi(s,x,L_{x}^{-1}v)]\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,t)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}\Delta_{v}\varphi-\nu_{f}\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot(v-u_{f})f\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

(III)

The weak gradient
\nabla_{v}f
is well-defined as a measurable function on
(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v<+\infty.

In particular, in similar manner as Theorem
1.1
, the very weak formulation can be replaced with the weak formulation.

(IV)

The energy inequality below holds

\displaystyle\int_{\{t\}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|v|^{2})f\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+(1-\theta)\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\leq\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|v|^{2})f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

(V)

The entropy inequality below holds

\displaystyle\int_{\{t\}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\log f\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+(1-\theta)\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\gamma f\log\gamma f\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{(0,t)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\nu_{f}}{f\mathcal{T}_{f}}|\mathcal{T}_{f}\nabla_{v}f+(v-u_{f})f|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{0}\log f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

Remark 1.4
.

Theorem
1.3
does not cover the case
\theta=1
, corresponding to purely specular reflection without absorption. This limitation reflects a well-known obstruction: see, for instance,
[
GHJO20
]
for related discussions. In fact, when
\theta=1
the dominance of grazing collisions is known to cause a loss of regularity in the trace
\gamma f
, and one cannot in general expect
\gamma f\in L^{1}(\Sigma_{+}^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v)
.
A formal way to observe this obstruction is through parts (IV) and (V) of Theorem
1.3
: when
\theta=1
, the boundary contributions vanish, leaving no available a priori control on the outgoing trace
\gamma f|_{\Sigma_{+}^{T}}
.

Remark 1.5
.

A classical approach to reflection-type problems is to approximate them by a sequence of inflow problems, see for example
[
Ham92
,
Car98
]
. In this work, we adopt a different strategy. Since the theory of reflection problems has already been developed to a satisfactory level in the
linear
Fokker–Planck setting
[
Zhu24
, Section 2]
, we may rely on those results and approximate directly by reflection problems themselves, rather than reducing to the inflow case.

1.2.
Strategy of proof

The proof of our main existence results proceeds through a classical approximation procedure, carefully adapted to the nonlinear diffusion structure of (
1.1
) and to the lack of uniform ellipticity. The idea is to construct a family of approximate solutions, establish estimates uniform in the approximation parameter, and then pass to the limit by combining compactness arguments with stability properties of the nonlinear operator. Each step in the argument is designed to overcome a specific difficulty: solvability of the regularized problems, preservation of renormalization properties, derivation of uniform entropy bounds, compactness of velocity averages, and finally the identification of quadratic quantities such as the temperature.

Step 1: Regularization and linearization.

We begin by considering a regularized version of the inflow problem (
1.8
), where both the collision operator and the boundary data are smoothed. The regularization is designed so that the resulting system admits well-defined solutions for each fixed parameter
\varepsilon>0
. At this stage we further linearize the problem, producing a linear kinetic Fokker–Planck equation with given coefficients. Existence of solutions to such linear problems is well established, and uniqueness was proved in
[
Zhu24
, Theorem 1.1]
. This uniqueness plays a central role in our approach, since it allows us later to view the nonlinear problem as a fixed point of a solution map.

Step 2: Fixed-point construction.

To recover the nonlinear structure, we rely on a fixed point argument, defining a suitable solution operator on the coefficient space. The uniqueness of the linear problem is instrumental in verifying the continuity of this operator (see
[
Abd94
]
). Moreover, one can check directly that the operator is compact. Then through Schaefer’s fixed-point theorem, we obtain that this map admits a fixed point
(f_{\varepsilon},\gamma f_{\varepsilon})
which is a solution to the nonlinear regularized problem. A key advantage of this construction is that
(f_{\varepsilon},\gamma f_{\varepsilon})
inherits properties of the linear solutions, most importantly the renormalization property in the sense of DiPerna–Lions
[
DL89c
]
. This structural inheritance is crucial in the next step, where we prove uniform a priori estimates and apply an averaging lemma.

Step 3: Uniform estimates.

The renormalization property enables us to rigorously derive energy and entropy inequalities for
(f_{\varepsilon},\gamma f_{\varepsilon})
. These provide uniform control of the mass, the kinetic energy, and the entropy production, all independent of
\varepsilon
. Such bounds already imply weak compactness of the sequence
\{f_{\varepsilon}\}
by the Dunford–Pettis criterion, so that we can pass to a weakly convergent subsequence. However, weak convergence of
(f_{\varepsilon},\gamma f_{\varepsilon})
is not sufficient: to handle the nonlinear structure of (
1.1
), and to ensure that the energy and entropy inequalities persist in the limit (see
Step 6
), we aim to establish strong convergence of
f_{\varepsilon}
.

Step 4: Velocity averages.

We strive to demonstrate strong convergence of
f_{\varepsilon}
, by verifying that these approximate solutions satisfy the hypotheses of the compactness principle stated in Proposition
2.1
. A delicate part is showing convergence of the velocity averages
\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{\varepsilon}\varphi(v)\textnormal{d}v\right\}
for each
\varphi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Standard velocity averaging lemmas are not directly applicable here: they require
L^{p}
control with
p>1
, while in our setting we only have
L^{1}
bounds. It is well known that averaging can fail in
L^{1}
due to concentration phenomena along characteristics
[
GLPS88
, p. 123]
,
[
GSR02
, p. 558]
. To overcome this, we exploit the renormalization property of
(f_{\varepsilon},\gamma f_{\varepsilon})
. By applying averaging lemmas to nonlinear transforms
\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})
with suitable decay, we obtain convergence of the averages
\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})\varphi\textnormal{d}v\}
for each
\delta>0
. Then, using equiintegrability provided by the entropy bounds, we can pass from convergence of the averages of
\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})
back to convergence of the averages of
f_{\varepsilon}
. Our procedure is greatly motivated by the works
[
DL89a
,
GSR05
]
.

Step 5: Higher moments and temperature convergence.

Another obstacle is the identification of the temperatures
\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}
in the limit. Strong convergence of the velocity averages is not enough to control these quadratic quantities. The crucial new ingredient is a
moment-gain lemma
, which shows that solutions to (
1.1
) acquire additional velocity moments once integrated over time. This phenomenon was first observed for the pure transport equation in
[
LP92
]
and has since become a standard tool in kinetic theory, but to our knowledge it has not been applied to nonlinear Fokker–Planck operators. By adapting this technique we show that
\{|v|^{3}f_{\varepsilon}\}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{1}
, which in turn ensures almost everywhere convergence of
\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}
on the set where
\rho_{f}>0
. This suffices to identify the limiting temperature, and in particular, we obtain that all assumptions of Proposition
2.1
hold.

Step 6: Passage to the limit.

Having verified that the approximate solutions satisfy the necessary compactness criteria, Proposition
2.1
yields strong convergence
f_{\varepsilon}\to f
in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. With this convergence in hand, we can pass to the limit
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
in the nonlinear terms of the equation and recover a global weak solution to (
1.1
). It remains to verify that the energy and entropy inequalities also hold for
f
, by passing to the limit in those satisfied by
f_{\varepsilon}
. Since these inequalities are formulated at fixed times
t\in[0,T]
, one typically needs to identify the weak limit of
f_{\varepsilon}(t)
. This step is often delicate, as it usually requires establishing some temporal equicontinuity of
{f_{\varepsilon}}
(cf.
[
GSR05
]
). Here, however, the strong convergence guaranteed by Proposition
2.1
allows us to bypass this difficulty entirely. This concludes the proof strategy. The same reasoning applies to both types of boundary conditions considered in this work. For clarity we present the inflow case in full detail in Section
3
, while in Section
4
we indicate the minor adjustments required for partial absorption–reflection.

1.3.
Organization of the paper

Section
2
is devoted to the proof of our main compactness result, Proposition
2.1
.
In Section
3
we address the initial-boundary value problem with inflow data and establish Theorem
1.1
.
Section
4
provides a sketch of the proof of Theorem
1.3
, whose detailed arguments are omitted since they closely parallel those of Section
3
.
Finally, Appendix
A
contains the construction of the regularized initial and boundary data used throughout the paper.

2.
Compactness from the weighted Fisher information

The compactness theory developed in this section provides the essential input for
our analysis of the initial–boundary value problems in Sections
3

and
4
. The key observation is that dissipation in the velocity variable, even when modulated by a possibly degenerate coefficient, can be combined with velocity averaging in the space-time variables to produce strong compactness in all variables. More precisely, provided that we have control of a weighted Fisher information, convergence of velocity averages, and compatibility of the weights with the non-vacuum region of the limiting density, we can guarantee strong convergence of the sequence in
L^{1}_{t,x,v}
.

This principle is made precise in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open subset (possibly unbounded). Assume that
\{f_{m}(t,x,v)\}
is a nonnegative sequence such that

(P1)

f_{m}\rightharpoonup f
weakly in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

(P2)

For each
\psi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the sequence of averages
\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}\psi(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}
converges in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.

(P3)

The following weighted Fisher information is bounded:

\displaystyle\sup_{m}\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}a_{m}(t,x)\left|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{m}}\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C.

Here we require that each
a_{m}:(0,T)\times\Omega\to[0,\infty]
is Lebesgue measurable.

(P4)

a_{m}\to a
a.e. in
D_{+}:=\{(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega:a(t,x)>0\}
, and
a
has the property that
D_{+}=\{(t,x):\rho_{f}(t,x)>0\}
up to a Lebesgue-negligible set.

Then modulo a subsequence it holds that
f_{m}\to f
a.e. and strongly in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The proof of Proposition
2.1
proceeds in two main steps.
We first establish the constant-coefficient case
a_{m}\equiv 1
(Lemma
2.3
),
which is already nontrivial yet closely related to the recent work
[
Sam24
]
.
Our argument relaxes several of the structural assumptions used there, showing
that they can be replaced by the more natural entropy and moment bounds available
for kinetic Fokker–Planck models.
We then treat general measurable coefficients
a_{m}
, which may vanish on subsets of

(0,T)\times\Omega
.
The main difficulty is that pointwise convergence
a_{m}\to a
does not imply convergence of
level sets
\{a_{m}\geq c\}
, and naive truncation arguments may fail.
To overcome this, we implement a careful level-set decomposition which localizes
the Fisher information and reduces the problem to the constant-coefficient setting on each level. This strategy allows us to avoid any uniform ellipticity assumptions, while still
retaining the strong compactness conclusion.

Remark 2.2
.

The assumptions in Proposition
2.1
are natural and arise in diverse applications.

(1)

(P1) is expected (along some subsequence) once one can verify that the physically relevant bounds in (
1.5
) propagate with time. If
\Omega
is unbounded, additional spatial moment conditions may be imposed on the initial data.

(2)

(P2), the convergence of
\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}\psi(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}
for each
\psi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, is typically a consequence of velocity averaging lemmas
[
GLPS88
,
GPS85
,
Gla96
]
. While these lemmas usually yield convergence only along subsequences, assumption (P1) guarantees uniqueness of the limit and thus removes the need for subsequence extraction.

(3)

(P3) naturally arises from entropy dissipation estimates for kinetic Fokker–Planck equations such as (
1.1
), (
1.6
), and (
1.7
), which provide precisely the required Fisher-information bounds.

(4)

(P4) is satisfied, for instance, when
a_{m}=\mathcal{T}_{f_{m}}
and
a=\mathcal{T}_{f}
, provided that velocity averaging lemmas ensure convergence of the second moments of
f_{m}
in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.

2.1.
Constant diffusion coefficient case

We first establish the compactness in the baseline case
a_{m}\equiv 1
, which is the
key building block for the general measurable weights.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(not necessarily bounded), and
\{f_{m}(t,x,v)\}
a sequence of nonnegative functions such that

(L1)

The sequence
\{f_{m}\}
is weakly convergent in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
f
.

(L2)

For each
\psi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the sequence of averages
\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}\psi(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}_{m}
is compact in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.

(L3)

For each
\alpha,\varepsilon>0
, there exists a measurable set
E\subset(0,T)\times\Omega
with
\mathscr{L}^{d+1}(E)\leq\varepsilon
, such that the Fisher information enjoys the following bound

\displaystyle\int_{(K_{\alpha}\cap E^{c})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{m}}\right|^{2}\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C(\varepsilon,\alpha),

where
\mathscr{L}^{d+1}
is the
(d+1)
-dimensional Lebesgue measure and

\displaystyle K_{\alpha}:=\{(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega:\rho_{f}(t,x)>\alpha\}.

Then modulo a subsequence
f_{m}
is convergent a.e. and strongly in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Remark 2.4
.

The proof of Lemma
2.3
is an adaptation and generalization of that of
[
Sam24
, Proposition 2]
; for completeness, we provide all details. Indeed, in
[
Sam24
]
, the sequence
f_{m}
is assumed to converge weakly
∗
in
L^{\infty}
(due to the quantum structure of the Landau–Fermi–Dirac equation), and assumption (L3) is replaced by an
L^{2}
-bound on the velocity gradients,

\displaystyle\int_{(K_{\alpha}\cap E^{c})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla_{v}f_{m}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C(\varepsilon,\alpha).

Our contribution is to relax both requirements:
weak
∗
convergence in
L^{\infty}
is replaced by equiintegrability and tightness in
L^{1}
,
and the
L^{2}
-gradient bound is replaced by boundedness of the Fisher information.

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

We first treat the case of bounded
\Omega
. Indeed, in the general case of unbounded
\Omega
, one can set
B_{R}
as the standard open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of radius
R>0
, apply the below proof to
\Omega\cap B_{R}
, and then extract diagonally by taking
R\uparrow\infty
.

First we remark that the weak convergence assumption (L1) and the Dunford–Pettis theorem imply that

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{m}\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times\{|v|>R\}}f_{m}(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=0.

In particular, for each
\psi(v)\in C^{0}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, it follows that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{m}\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times\{|v|>R\}}f_{m}(t,x,v)\psi(v)\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=0.

By utilizing the above tightness, and standard density arguments, we may therefore assume that assumption (L2) holds for each
\psi\in C^{0}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Then, we observe that we can extract a subsequence such that (L2) holds for every
\psi\in C^{0}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
along this subsequence. Indeed, let us fix any
R>0
and consider those
\psi\in C_{c}(B_{R})
. Now
C_{c}(B_{R})\subset L^{1}(B_{R})
, and by standard approximation theory there is a Schauder basis of
L^{1}(B_{R})
which consists of smooth, compactly supported functions: thus, by a diagonal extraction we obtain a (
R
-dependent) subsequence for which
\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}(t,x,v)\psi(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}_{m}
is convergent in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
for every
\psi\in C_{c}(B_{R})
. Then taking
R\uparrow\infty
, we can utilize (
2.1
) to extract another diagonal subsequence, for which

(2.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}\psi(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\psi(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\text{ a.e. and in $L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)$}\quad\forall\psi\in C^{0}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

We remark that in particular, along this subsequence:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\rho_{f_{m}}\to\rho_{f}\quad\text{a.e. and in }L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega).

Now fix a mollifier
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta(v)\,\textnormal{d}v=1
and
\textnormal{supp}(\eta)\subset B_{1}
. Denote

\displaystyle\eta_{\delta}(v)=\frac{1}{\delta^{d}}\eta\left(\frac{v}{\delta}\right),\quad f_{m}^{\delta}:=f_{m}*\eta_{\delta},\quad f^{\delta}:=f*\eta_{\delta}.

Then, assumption (L1) shows that for any
\varphi\in L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}^{\delta}(t,x,v)\varphi(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\textnormal{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}(t,x,w)\eta_{\delta}(v-w)\varphi(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}w\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\textnormal{d}t

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t,x,w)\eta_{\delta}(v-w)\varphi(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}w\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\textnormal{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{\delta}(t,x,v)\varphi(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\textnormal{d}t,

in other words

(2.4)

\displaystyle f_{m}^{\delta}\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}f^{\delta}\quad\text{weakly in}\quad L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).

On the other hand, the velocity averaging (
2.2
) implies that

\displaystyle f_{m}^{\delta}\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}f^{\delta}\quad\text{a.e. }(t,x,v)\in(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Combining this with (
2.4
), we deduce via the Vitali convergence theorem (see for instance
[
DS88
, IV.8]
) that

(2.5)

\displaystyle f_{m}^{\delta}\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}f^{\delta}\quad\text{strongly in}\quad L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Next, we write out using the definition of
f_{m}^{\delta}
:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f_{m}-f_{m}^{\delta}|\,\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f_{m}(v)-\int_{B_{\delta}}\eta_{\delta}(v_{*})f_{m}(v-v_{*})\,\textnormal{d}v_{*}\right|\,\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{B_{\delta}}\eta_{\delta}(v_{*})|f_{m}(v-v_{*})-f_{m}(v)|\,\textnormal{d}v_{*}\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{B_{\delta}}\int_{0}^{1}\eta_{\delta}(v_{*})|v_{*}||\nabla_{v}f_{m}(v-hv_{*})|\,\textnormal{d}h\textnormal{d}v_{*}\textnormal{d}v,

the last line following by the mean-value theorem. Utilizing
|v_{*}|\leq\delta
and the Fubini–Tonelli theorem, we can then estimate further as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f_{m}-f_{m}^{\delta}|\,\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\leq\delta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{B_{\delta}}\int_{0}^{1}\eta_{\delta}(v_{*})|\nabla_{v}f_{m}(v-hv_{*})|\,\textnormal{d}h\textnormal{d}v_{*}\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\leq\delta\int_{0}^{1}\int_{B_{\delta}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla_{v}f_{m}(v)|\,\textnormal{d}v\right)\eta_{\delta}(v_{*})\,\textnormal{d}v_{*}\textnormal{d}h

\displaystyle\leq\delta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla_{v}f_{m}(v)|\,\textnormal{d}v.

Integrating this over
(t,x)\in K_{\alpha}\cap E^{c}
we therefore obtain a Poincarè–Wirtinger type inequality

\displaystyle\int_{K_{\alpha}\cap E^{c}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f_{m}-f_{m}^{\delta}|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\leq\delta\int_{(K_{\alpha}\cap E^{c})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla_{v}f_{m}(v)|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

To control this with the Fisher information, we observe first that since
\{f_{m}\}
is weakly convergent,
\{f_{m}\}
is a bounded subset of
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Thus we can compute

\displaystyle\int_{(K_{\alpha}\cap E^{c})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla_{v}f_{m}(v)|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{(K_{\alpha}\cap E^{c})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}2\sqrt{f_{m}}\frac{|\nabla_{v}f_{m}|}{2\sqrt{f_{m}}}\mathbf{1}_{f_{m}>0}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\left(\int_{(K_{\alpha}\cap E^{c})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{(K_{\alpha}\cap E^{c})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla_{v}f_{m}|^{2}}{4f_{m}}\mathbf{1}_{f_{m}>0}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\left(\int_{(K_{\alpha}\cap E^{c})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{m}}\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\quad=C\sqrt{C(\varepsilon,\alpha)}.

Hence, we have

(2.6)

\int_{(K_{\alpha}\cap E^{c})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f_{m}-f_{m}^{\delta}|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C\delta\sqrt{C(\varepsilon,\alpha)}.

Now, we can verify that
f_{m}
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Indeed, let us write out

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f_{m}-f_{n}|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle=\int_{K_{\alpha}^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f_{m}-f_{n}|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{E\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f_{m}-f_{n}|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{(K_{\alpha}\cap E^{c})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f_{m}-f_{n}|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.

For
I_{1}
, the triangle inequality and (
2.3
) give

\displaystyle\limsup_{m,n\to\infty}I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{m,n\to\infty}\int_{K_{\alpha}^{c}}(\rho_{f_{m}}+\rho_{f_{n}})\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\xrightarrow[m,n\to\infty]{}\int_{K_{\alpha}^{c}}2\rho_{f}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\leq 2\alpha\mathscr{L}^{d+1}((0,T)\times\Omega),

the last line following by definition of
K_{\alpha}
.

For
I_{2}
, we find

\displaystyle I_{2}\leq\int_{E}(\rho_{f_{m}}+\rho_{f_{n}})\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x.

Since
\{\rho_{f_{m}}\}
is strongly convergent in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
(see (
2.3
)), it is also weakly convergent; in particular
\{\rho_{f_{m}}\}
is equiintegrable in
(0,T)\times\Omega
. This implies the existence of a modulus of continuity
\omega:[0,\infty]\to[0,\infty]
(independent of
m
) such that

\displaystyle\sup_{m}\int_{E}\rho_{f_{m}}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\leq\omega(\varepsilon),\quad\text{where}\quad\omega(\varepsilon)\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}0.

We deduce that

\displaystyle I_{2}\leq 2\omega(\varepsilon).

For
I_{3}
, we split it as

\displaystyle\limsup_{m,n\to\infty}I_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{m,n\to\infty}\Bigg(\int_{(K_{\alpha}\cap E^{c})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f_{m}-f_{m}^{\delta}|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{(K_{\alpha}\cap E^{c})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f_{n}-f_{n}^{\delta}|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\int_{(K_{\alpha}\cap E^{c})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f_{m}^{\delta}-f_{n}^{\delta}|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq C\delta\sqrt{C(\varepsilon,\alpha)},

the last line following by (
2.6
) and the fact that
\{f_{m}^{\delta}\}_{m}
is Cauchy in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
(see (
2.5
)). Collecting the estimates, we conclude that for any
\alpha,\delta,\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f_{m}-f_{n}|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq 2\alpha\mathscr{L}^{d+1}((0,T)\times\Omega)+2\omega(\varepsilon)+C\delta\sqrt{C(\varepsilon,\alpha)},

where
C>0
is independent of
\varepsilon,\delta,\alpha
. We fix
\varepsilon,\alpha>0
and take
\delta\downarrow 0
first to deduce that the left-hand side is bounded by
2\alpha\mathscr{L}^{d+1}((0,T)\times\Omega)+2\omega(\varepsilon)
. Then we take
\alpha,\varepsilon\downarrow 0
to complete the proof.
∎

2.2.
General diffusion coefficient case

We now prove Proposition
2.1
for general measurable weights,
following the level-set localization strategy outlined above.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

We assume that
\Omega
is bounded, since the case of unbounded domains follows
by applying the argument to
\Omega\cap B_{R}
and then resorting to a diagonal process.

Our starting observation is that we can extract a strictly decreasing sequence
c_{k}\downarrow 0

with

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{d+1}(\{(t,x):a(t,x)=c_{k}\})=0\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

This is based upon the simple fact that a measurable function on a bounded domain cannot have “too many level sets.” Indeed, since

\displaystyle(0,T)\times\Omega\supset\bigcup_{c\in(0,1]}\{(t,x):a(t,x)=c\},

we deduce that only at most countably many of the level sets can have non-zero Lebesgue measure. Without loss of generality, we set

\displaystyle c_{k}=\frac{1}{k},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

The advantage of distinguishing level sets in this way is that, now, the pointwise convergence assumption (P4) implies convergence of the following indicator functions:

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}(t,x)\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}\mathbf{1}_{\{a\geq 1/k\}}(t,x)\quad\text{for almost every }(t,x)\in D_{+}.

Let us view
\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}
as a sequence bounded in
L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We will show that the sequence
\{\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}f_{m}\}
satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
2.3
, for each fixed
k
.

Toward this end, we first verify that

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}f_{m}\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a\geq 1/k\}}f\quad\text{weakly in }L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).

The starting step here is localization in the velocity variable. Owing to assumption (P1) and the Dunford–Pettis criterion, given
\varepsilon>0
, we fix
R_{\varepsilon}>0
for which

\displaystyle\sup_{m}\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R_{\varepsilon}})}f_{m}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq\varepsilon.

Similarly, since
f\in L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we may assume without loss of generality that the same
R_{\varepsilon}>0
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R_{\varepsilon}})}f\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq\varepsilon.

Now the pointwise convergence in (
2.7
) and Egorov’s theorem show we can find a measurable subset
E_{\varepsilon}\subset(0,T)\times\Omega\times B_{R_{\varepsilon}}
with
\mathscr{L}^{2d+1}(E_{\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon
such that

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a\geq 1/k\}}\quad\text{uniformly in }[(0,T)\times\Omega\times B_{R_{\varepsilon}}]\setminus E_{\varepsilon}.

Since the family
\{f_{m}\}
is equiintegrable and
f\in L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we remark that there is a modulus of continuity
\omega:[0,\infty]\to[0,\infty]
, independent of
m
, for which

\displaystyle\sup_{m}\int_{E_{\varepsilon}}f_{m}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v,\;\int_{E_{\varepsilon}}f\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq\omega(\varepsilon)\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}0.

Hence, for an arbitrary function
\varphi\in L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}(\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}f_{m}-\mathbf{1}_{\{a\geq 1/k\}}f)\varphi(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{E_{\varepsilon}}(f_{m}+f)\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{[(0,T)\times\Omega\times B_{R_{\varepsilon}}]\setminus E_{\varepsilon}}\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\,(\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}-\mathbf{1}_{\{a\geq 1/k\}})f\varphi\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{[(0,T)\times\Omega\times B_{R_{\varepsilon}}]\setminus E_{\varepsilon}}\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}(f_{m}-f)\varphi\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R_{\varepsilon}})}\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}(f_{m}+f)\varphi\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\omega(\varepsilon)+\|\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}-\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a\geq 1/k\}}\|_{L^{\infty}([(0,T)\times\Omega\times B_{R_{\varepsilon}}]\setminus E_{\varepsilon})}\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\|f\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{[(0,T)\times\Omega\times B_{R_{\varepsilon}}]\setminus E_{\varepsilon}}\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}(f_{m}-f)\varphi\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+2\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\varepsilon.

Taking
m\to\infty
, the uniform convergence (
2.9
) and the weak convergence
f_{m}\rightharpoonup f
show

\displaystyle\limsup_{m\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}f_{m}-\mathbf{1}_{\{a\geq 1/k\}}f)\varphi(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq 2\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}(\omega(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon).

As
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary we then take it to zero in order to conclude that (
2.8
) holds true.

Next, we find that (L2) of Lemma
2.3
holds for
\{\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}f_{m}\}
. Indeed, for each
\psi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, writing
\psi
into its positive and negative parts
\psi=\psi^{+}-\psi^{-}
, we see that the following dominations hold:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}f_{m}\psi^{+}(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right|

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}\psi^{+}(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}\psi^{+}(v)\,\textnormal{d}v,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}f_{m}\,\psi^{-}(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}\psi^{-}(v)\,\textnormal{d}v.

Since (P2) gives that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}\psi^{\pm}(v)\,\textnormal{d}v
is strongly convergent in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
, it follows by the generalized Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem (or one can also use the Vitali convergence theorem) to deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}\psi(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a\geq 1/k\}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\psi(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega).

Finally, (L3) holds for
\{\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}f_{m}\}_{m}
, since only
f_{m}
is
v
-dependent:

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{\mathbf{1}_{D_{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}f_{m}}\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{m}}\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\leq k\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}a_{m}(t,x)\left|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{m}}\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\leq Ck\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

From Lemma
2.3
we deduce that

(2.10)

\displaystyle f_{m}\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k\}}\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}f\mathbf{1}_{\{a\geq 1/k\}}\quad\text{a.e. in }D_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

We can now assume, after a diagonal extraction, that we are along a subsequence for which (
2.10
) holds for every
k
. It is then straightforward to check that
f_{m}\to f
almost everywhere in
D_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Indeed, fix
(t,x,v)\in D_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which (
2.10
) holds. Then by definition,
a(t,x)>0
. Due to the Archimedean property, we can locate
k_{*}=k_{*}(t,x)\in\mathbb{N}
for which

\displaystyle a(t,x)>1/k_{*},

and by assumption (P4) we can suppose
a_{m}(t,x)>1/k_{*}
for all
m
large enough. Since this implies
\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k_{*}\}}(t,x)\equiv 1
for all large enough
m
, we use (
2.10
) to deduce

\displaystyle f_{m}(t,x,v)=f_{m}(t,x,v)\mathbf{1}_{\{a_{m}\geq 1/k_{*}\}}(t,x)\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}f(t,x,v)\mathbf{1}_{\{a\geq 1/k_{*}\}}(t,x)=f(t,x,v).

Hence
f_{m}\to f
a.e. in
D_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Finally, we need to discuss the convergence
f_{m}\to f
on
\{a=0\}
. Notice that thanks to the weak compactness of
\{f_{m}\}
, the arguments preceding (
2.2
)–(
2.3
) show
\varphi\equiv 1
is admissible in assumption (P2). Hence

\displaystyle\rho_{f_{m}}\to\rho_{f}\quad\text{a.e. and in }L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega).

We remark
\rho_{f}(t,x)=0
is equivalent to
f(t,x,v)=0
a.e.
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and that (P4) implies
D_{0}:=\{(t,x):a(t,x)=0\}=\{(t,x):\rho_{f}(t,x)=0\}
. In this way,

\displaystyle\int_{D_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f_{m}-f|\,\textnormal{d}v\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{D_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}\,\textnormal{d}v\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x=\int_{D_{0}}\rho_{f_{m}}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}\int_{D_{0}}\rho_{f}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x=0,

and therefore
f_{m}\to f
in
L^{1}(D_{0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Altogether, modulo a subsequence,
f_{m}\to f
a.e. on
(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the strong convergence in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
follows from
the Vitali convergence theorem.
∎

The following corollary is a direct consequence, and discusses the case where
a(t,x)
does not vanish anywhere.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open subset. Assume that
\{f_{m}(t,x,v)\}
satisfies

(P1)

f_{m}\rightharpoonup f
weakly in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

(P2)

For each
\psi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the sequence of averages
\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{m}\psi(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}
is compact in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.

(P3)

The following weighted Fisher information is bounded:

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}a_{m}(t,x)|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{m}}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C,

where
a_{m}:(0,T)\times\Omega\to[0,\infty]
is Lebesgue measurable.

(P4’)

a_{m}(t,x)\to a(t,x)>0
almost every
(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega
.

Then modulo a subsequence it holds that
f_{k}\to f
a.e. and strongly in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.
Following the proof of Proposition
2.1
, we have that
f_{m}\to f
a.e. in
D_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
D_{+}:=\{(t,x):a(t,x)>0\}
. Since (P4’) implies
D_{+}=(0,T)\times\Omega
(up to a zero measure set), the result follows.
∎

3.
The inflow problem

In this section we construct solutions to (
1.1
) regarding the inflow problem, and namely prove Theorem
1.1
. Recall that
f^{0}
is assumed only to satisfy (
1.5
), whereas the inflow data
g
satisfies

(3.1)

\displaystyle g\geq 0,\qquad\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}(1+|v|^{2}+|\log g|)g\,|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v<+\infty.

Setup of the problem.
We let
\Omega
denote a bounded, open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
C^{1}
-boundary. Specifically, let us assume there exists
\mathfrak{b}\in C^{1}\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
with nonvanishing gradient such that

\displaystyle\Omega=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mathfrak{b}(x)>0\},\qquad n(x)=-\frac{\nabla\mathfrak{b}(x)}{|\nabla\mathfrak{b}(x)|}.

The effective boundary portion,
i.e.
the set on which we impose the initial and boundary conditions, is

\displaystyle\reflectbox{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\mathbb{L}$}}:=\left(\{0\}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)\cup\left((0,T]\times\Gamma_{-}\right),

where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{-}:=\{(x,v)\in\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}:n(x)\cdot v<0\}.

Naturally, we also denote

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}:=\{(x,v)\in\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}:n(x)\cdot v=0\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{+}:=\{(x,v)\in\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}:n(x)\cdot v>0\},

\displaystyle\Gamma=\Gamma_{-}\cup\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{+},

and also, for
t\in[0,T]
, recall the notations

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\pm}^{t}:=[0,t]\times\Gamma_{\pm}.

Regarding the phase domain, we use the notation

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}:=\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

A priori estimates.
Recall that the nonlinear operator can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\nu_{f}\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\mathcal{T}_{f}\nabla_{v}f+(v-u_{f})f\right)=\nu_{f}\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(f\mathcal{T}_{f}\nabla_{v}\log\frac{f}{M[f]}\right),

where
M[f]
is the local Maxwellian

\displaystyle M[f]=\frac{\rho_{f}}{(2\pi\mathcal{T}_{f})^{d/2}}e^{-\frac{|v-u_{f}|^{2}}{2\mathcal{T}_{f}}}.

Taking
1,v_{i},|v|^{2}
as test functions, we find the formal conservation of mass, momentum, and energy:

\displaystyle\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1,v,|v|^{2})f\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Gamma}(1,v,|v|^{2})\gamma f\,v\cdot n(x)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=0.

Next, regarding the
\mathcal{H}
-functional, we take
(1+\log f)
as a test function in (
1.1
) to find that

\displaystyle\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\int_{\mathcal{O}}f\log f\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Gamma}(\gamma f\log\gamma f)(v\cdot n(x))\,\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\mathcal{O}}4\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=d\int_{\mathcal{O}}\nu_{f}f\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

Clearly, the mass-conserving nature of
f
shows that we can then obtain natural bounds for the entropy and also of the weighted Fisher information. Formal manipulations also show that

\displaystyle\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\int_{\mathcal{O}}f\log f\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Gamma}(\gamma f\log\gamma f)(v\cdot n(x))\,\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\mathcal{O}}\frac{1}{f\mathcal{T}_{f}}|\mathcal{T}_{f}\nabla_{v}f+(v-u_{f})f|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=0,

and thus the (negative of the) entropy is decreasing provided there is no net flux through the boundary.

3.1.
The regularized system

Given
r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}>0
, we introduce the
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}
-renormalization of
r
as

\displaystyle\left[r\right]_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}}:=\frac{r}{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}(1+r)}.

Similarly, for a vector
{\bf r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define

\displaystyle\left[{\bf r}\right]_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}}:=\frac{{\bf r}}{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}(1+|{\bf r}|)}.

Using the above notations, for fixed
\varepsilon>0
, we define the regularized temperature as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{(\varepsilon)}_{f}:=\left[V_{f}\right]_{\rho_{f},\varepsilon}+\varepsilon=\frac{V_{f}}{\rho_{f}+\varepsilon(1+V_{f})}+\varepsilon,

and the regularized bulk velocity as

\displaystyle u_{f}^{(\varepsilon)}:=\left[j_{f}\right]_{\rho_{f},\varepsilon}=\frac{j_{f}}{\rho_{f}+\varepsilon(1+|j_{f}|)},\quad j_{f}:=\rho_{f}u_{f}.

It can be easily seen that in this way

(3.2)

\begin{split}&|\left[{\bf r}\right]_{1,\varepsilon}|\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon},|{\bf r}|\right\}\quad\forall{\bf r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\mathcal{T}_{f}^{(\varepsilon)}\leq\min\left\{\mathcal{T}_{f}+\varepsilon\;,\;\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\right\},\quad|u_{f}^{(\varepsilon)}|\leq\min\left\{|u_{f}|,\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right\}.\end{split}

We then consider the following system of equations

(3.3)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f_{\varepsilon}=\left(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon\right)\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}+(\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})f_{\varepsilon}\right),\\
f_{\varepsilon}|_{t=0}=f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\in C_{c}(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}_{+}),\\
\gamma f_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}=g_{\varepsilon}\in C_{c}([0,T]\times\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}_{+}),\end{cases}

where the regularized initial and boundary datum are assumed to satisfy the conditions (
1.5
) and (
3.1
) uniformly in
\varepsilon
, and also

(3.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\to f^{0}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}(\mathcal{O}),\\
\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathcal{O}}|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=\int_{\mathcal{O}}|v|^{2}f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v,\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=\int_{\mathcal{O}}f^{0}\log f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v,\\
g_{\varepsilon}\to g\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}(\Sigma_{-}^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v),\\
\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}|v|^{2}g_{\varepsilon}|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v=\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}|v|^{2}g|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v,\\
\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}g_{\varepsilon}\log g_{\varepsilon}\,|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v=\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}g\log g\,|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v.\end{cases}

Since the construction of such regularizations is rather delicate, we provide a proof in Appendix
A
.

The solution
(f_{\varepsilon},\gamma f_{\varepsilon})
to (
3.3
) will be found by means of a fixed point argument. Toward that end it is necessary for us to also discuss the linear counterpart of (
3.3
). Consider the convex space

\displaystyle\mathscr{X}:=L_{+}^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)\times[L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)]^{d}\times L_{+}^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega),

and let
(\varrho,{\bf j},V)\in\mathscr{X}
be given. The linear system corresponding to (
3.3
) then reads (for fixed
\varepsilon>0
)

(3.5)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}F+v\cdot\nabla_{x}F=(\nu(\varrho,{\bf j},V)+\varepsilon)\left(\left[V\right]_{\varrho,\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\right)\Delta_{v}F+(\nu(\varrho,{\bf j},V)+\varepsilon)\nabla_{v}\cdot\Big((\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-\left[{\bf j}\right]_{\varrho,\varepsilon})F\Big),\\
F|_{t=0}=f_{\varepsilon}^{0},\\
\gamma F|_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}=g_{\varepsilon}.\end{cases}

We refer to the following preliminary result for the well-posedness of (
3.5
).

Lemma 3.1
.

[
Zhu24
, Theorem 1.1, Corollary 2.9]
Let the domain
\Omega\in C^{1,1}
be bounded, and consider the inflow problem

(3.6)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}h+v\cdot\nabla_{x}h=\nabla_{v}\cdot(A\nabla_{v}h)+{\bf B}\cdot\nabla_{v}h+ch,\\
\gamma h|_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}=g.\end{cases}

Assume that for some
\Lambda>0
we have

(3.7)

\begin{split}&\textnormal{$A$ is a symmetric $d\times d$ matrix with bounded measurable coefficients,}\\
&\Lambda^{-1}|\xi|^{2}\leq A\xi\cdot\xi\leq\Lambda|\xi|^{2}\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad|{\bf B}|+|c|\leq\Lambda.\end{split}

Then for any
k\geq 0
, there exists
l>0
dependent only on
d,k
such that the following conditions are fulfilled:

(I)

Whenever
h^{0}:=h|_{t=0}
and
g
satisfy
\langle v\rangle^{l}h^{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\langle v\rangle^{l}g\in L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then there exists a unique bounded weak solution
(h,\gamma h)
to (
3.6
) such that
h|_{t=0}=h^{0}
and
\gamma h|_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}=g
.

(II)

In particular, we have that

\displaystyle h\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\mathcal{O}))\cap L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega;H^{1}_{v}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),

\displaystyle\gamma h\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\Sigma^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v),

and the following Green’s formula holds for all
\varphi\in C_{c}^{1}([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
t\in[0,T]
:

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{O}}h(t)\varphi(t)-\int_{\mathcal{O}}h^{0}\varphi(0)+\int_{\Sigma^{t}}\gamma h\,\varphi\,(n(x)\cdot v)\\
&\quad=\int_{[0,t]\times\mathcal{O}}h\Big(\partial_{s}\varphi+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi\Big)-A\nabla_{v}f\cdot\nabla_{v}\varphi+\varphi{\bf B}\cdot\nabla_{v}h+ch\varphi.\end{split}

(III)

There is a constant
C>0
dependent only on
d,T,\Lambda,k,\Omega
such that

\displaystyle\|\langle v\rangle^{k}h\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\Big(\|\langle v\rangle^{l}h^{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|\langle v\rangle^{l}g\|_{L^{\infty}(\Sigma_{-}^{T})}\Big).

To the case of (
3.5
), we remark that in the notations of (
3.6
), we have

(3.8)

\begin{split}&A=(\nu(\varrho,{\bf j},V)+\varepsilon)\left(\left[V\right]_{\varrho,\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\right)\mathbb{I}_{d\times d},\\
&{\bf B}=(\nu(\varrho,{\bf j},V)+\varepsilon)\Big(\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-\left[{\bf j}\right]_{\varrho,\varepsilon}\Big),\\
&c=\left(\nu(\varrho,{\bf j},V)+\varepsilon\right)\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}=\left(\nu(\varrho,{\bf j},V)+\varepsilon\right)\frac{d+\varepsilon d+\varepsilon(d-1)|v|}{(1+\varepsilon(1+|v|))^{2}},\end{split}

and that (
3.7
) are satisfied as

(3.9)

\begin{split}&\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{I}_{d\times d}\leq A\leq\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\right)\mathbb{I}_{d\times d},\quad|{\bf B}|+|c|\leq\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}\left(d+\frac{10d}{\varepsilon}\right).\end{split}

We also remark that the bounds in (
3.9
) are dependent on
\varepsilon
but independent of choice of
(\varrho,{\bf j},V)\in\mathscr{X}
.

Prior to commencing the fixed point argument, let us recall and investigate some basic properties of the general equation (
3.6
). First of all, it is clear that thanks to Lemma
3.1
(II), (
3.9
), and the compact supports of
f^{0}
,
g
, the solution
h
to (
3.5
) has arbitrary algebraic decay. Written explicitly, we have that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
there is an appropriate constant
C_{T,d,k,\varepsilon}>0
(again independent of
(\varrho,{\bf j},V)
) for which

(3.10)

F(t,x,v)\leq\frac{C_{T,d,k,\varepsilon}}{1+|v|^{k}}.

The decay (
3.10
), although
\varepsilon
-dependent, helps justify many of the integration by parts that are to follow. Clearly, the above also implies that

(3.11)

\textnormal{$F\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}))$, \; independently of choice of $(\varrho,{\bf j},V)\in\mathscr{X}$}.

Further, we recall from
[
Zhu24
, Lemma 2.5]
that the solution
(h,\gamma h)
to (
3.6
) is renormalized in the sense of DiPerna–Lions
[
DL89c
]
. For convenience of the reader we recall the general definition here.

Definition 3.2
.

A pair
(h,\gamma h)
is said to be a renormalized solution to (
3.6
) if, for any pair
(\chi,\varphi)
such that
\chi\in C^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})
,
\chi(h)=O(h^{2})
,
\chi^{\prime\prime}(h)=O(1)
as
|h|\to\infty
, and
\varphi\in C_{c}^{1}([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
, it holds for every
t\in(0,T]
that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}\chi(h(t))\varphi(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\mathcal{O}}\chi(h^{0})\varphi(0)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma^{t}}(n(x)\cdot v)\chi(\gamma h)\varphi\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\chi(h)(\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x})\varphi\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\Big(-A\chi^{\prime}(h)\nabla_{v}h\cdot\nabla_{v}\varphi-\varphi\chi^{\prime\prime}(h)A\nabla_{v}h\cdot\nabla_{v}h+\varphi{\bf B}\cdot\nabla_{v}(\chi(h))+ch\chi^{\prime}(h)\varphi\Big)\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

In our equation (
3.5
), this formula provides the nonnegativity of
F
and
\gamma F
. Let us provide a proof in a slightly more general case.

Lemma 3.3

(Maximum principle)
.

Suppose there is a constant
\Lambda>0
for which
A,{\bf B}
satisfy (
3.7
). Let
(h,\gamma h)
be a renormalized solution to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}h+v\cdot\nabla_{x}h=\nabla_{v}\cdot(A\nabla_{v}h)+{\bf B}\cdot\nabla_{v}h+ch\quad\text{in}\quad[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
h|_{t=0}\geq 0,\\
\gamma h|_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}\geq 0.\end{cases}

We assume that
c
is possibly unbounded, but
{\bf B}
admits a
v
-divergence
\nabla_{v}\cdot{\bf B}
in the sense of distributions for which

\displaystyle|-\nabla_{v}\cdot{\bf B}+2c|\leq C<\infty.

If
h\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
and has sufficient decay at infinity, e.g.
h=o(|v|^{-(d-1)/2})
, then

\displaystyle h\geq 0,\qquad\gamma h\geq 0.

Proof.
We take
\chi(h):=\mathbf{1}_{h<0}|h|^{2}
in the renormalization formula. Since
h\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
, it is straightforward to check (through approximation) that
\varphi\equiv 1
is admissible in the weak formulation of the renormalized equation as a test function. Let us notice that
\chi^{\prime\prime}=2\cdot\mathbf{1}_{h<0}\geq 0
, and
\chi\left(\gamma h|_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}\right)=\chi(h^{0})\equiv 0
. Moreover, recalling the ellipticity condition (
3.7
) on
A
, we find

(3.12)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{O}}\chi(h(t))\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\chi(\gamma h)\,\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad=\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}-2\cdot\mathbf{1}_{h<0}A\nabla_{v}h\cdot\nabla_{v}h+{\bf B}\cdot\nabla_{v}(\chi(h))+ch\chi^{\prime}(h)\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad\leq\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\chi(h)(-\nabla_{v}\cdot{\bf B})+ch\chi^{\prime}(h)\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad=\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\chi(h)(-\nabla_{v}\cdot{\bf B}+2c)\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad\leq C\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\chi(h)\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.\end{split}

In the above, we integrated by parts with respect to
v
, which is justified due to the fact that
{\bf B}
is bounded and
h
decays sufficiently. Since
\chi\geq 0
, we can discard the boundary term on the left-hand side in order to apply Grönwall’s lemma, deducing that for each
t\in[0,T]
it holds
\chi(h(t))=0
a.e.
(x,v)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Fubini’s theorem then shows
\chi(h)=0
a.e. in
(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Eq. (
3.12
) then simply reduces to

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{T}}(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\chi(\gamma h)\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq 0,

and this implies
\gamma h\geq 0
in
\Sigma_{+}^{T}
, as desired.
∎

The application of Lemma
3.3
to (
3.5
) is clear. Indeed, in the notations of (
3.8
), we simply have

\displaystyle|-\nabla_{v}\cdot{\bf B}+2c|=|c|,

the latter of which is bounded (see (
3.9
)). Hence
F\geq 0
and
\gamma F\geq 0
.

Furthermore, in regard of the solution
(F,\gamma F)
to (
3.5
), by utilizing the fact that
F
is bounded, we can use the renormalization formula to deduce
L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}
-bounds for
\gamma F
as well. Namely, we can obtain that

(3.13)

\begin{split}&\textnormal{$\gamma F\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v)$,}\textnormal{ independently of choice of $(\varrho,{\bf j},V)\in\mathscr{X}$}.\end{split}

As the arguments which lead to this are well-organized in
[
Mis00
,
Mis10
]
, here we provide only the ideas. One considers the renormalization formula for (
3.5
) with nonlinearity
\chi(F)=F^{p}
and
\varphi\equiv 1
(done by approximation, recalling (
3.11
)). Splitting the boundary as
\Sigma^{T}=\Sigma_{+}^{T}\cup\Sigma_{-}^{T}
, and using that
g\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Sigma_{-}^{T},(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v)
, we can deduce an appropriate bound for
\chi(\gamma F)=|\gamma F|^{p}
. This can also be done in the following way. By methods such as convolution-translation regularizations
[
BLD01
]
, or by further regularizing the initial and boundary datum, we can assume
F
is sufficiently regular, say Hölder continuous; then the trace of
F(t)
on
\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
is naturally well-defined, and it clearly coincides with
\gamma F(t)
. The usual trace theorem for Sobolev regular functions then shows (
3.13
). To make this logic rigorous, one then exploits the lower semicontinuity of
L^{p}
-norms.

Let us now enter the details of the fixed point theorem.

3.2.
Fixed point operator

Given
(\varrho,{\bf j},V)\in\mathscr{X}
let
(F,\gamma F)
denote the solution to (
3.5
). We define the operator
\mathscr{R}:\mathscr{X}\to\mathscr{X}
with

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}(\varrho,{\bf j},V)=(\rho_{F},j_{F},V_{F})=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F\,\textnormal{d}v,\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}vF\,\textnormal{d}v,\;\frac{1}{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v-u_{F}|^{2}F\,\textnormal{d}v\right).

It is straightforward to observe that a solution to (
3.3
) is realized by a fixed point of
\mathscr{R}
.

Expanding the variance, a direct computation shows that

(3.14)

\displaystyle V_{F}=\frac{1}{d}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}F\,\textnormal{d}v-\frac{|j_{F}|^{2}}{\rho_{F}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}F\,\textnormal{d}v,

and therefore in view of (
3.10
) it is clear that
\rho_{F},j_{F},
and
V_{F}
are all integrable over
(0,T)\times\Omega
. Furthermore, since the solution
(F,\gamma F)
to (
3.5
) is unique, we readily check that the operator
\mathscr{R}
is well-defined.

The next lemmas demonstrate that all conditions of Schaefer’s fixed-point theorem are verified.

Lemma 3.4
.

The map
\mathscr{R}:\mathscr{X}\to\mathscr{X}
is continuous.

Proof.
It is enough to show sequential continuity. We consider a sequence for which

(3.15)

(\varrho_{n},{\bf j}_{n},V_{n})\to(\varrho,{\bf j},V)\quad\text{in }\mathscr{X}.

Denote by
(F_{n},\gamma F_{n})
the unique solution to (
3.5
) which corresponds to
(\varrho_{n},{\bf j}_{n},V_{n})
. Analogously, denote by
(F,\gamma F)
the solution corresponding to
(\varrho,{\bf j},V)
.

Let us pass to a subsequence for which the convergences in (
3.15
) hold almost everywhere; later we will show that this passage to the subsequence is unnecessary. By the dominated convergence theorem, we can then first remark that

(3.16)

\begin{split}&\left[V\right]_{\varrho_{n},\varepsilon}\to\left[V\right]_{\varrho,\varepsilon}\quad\text{in }L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega),\\
&\left[{\bf j}_{n}\right]_{\varrho_{n},\varepsilon}\to\left[{\bf j}\right]_{\varrho,\varepsilon}\quad\text{in }L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega)\quad\forall p\in[1,\infty).\end{split}

Similarly, by the continuity and boundedness assumption of
\nu
, we second note that

(3.17)

\nu(\varrho_{n},{\bf j}_{n},V_{n})\to\nu(\varrho,{\bf j},V)\quad\textnormal{a.e. and in }L^{p}((0,T)\times\Omega),\quad\forall p\in[1,\infty).

Next, in virtue of (
3.10
) and (
3.13
) it is clear that
F_{n}
(respectively
\gamma F_{n})
) is weakly
∗
compact in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}))
(respectively
L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v)
). Passing to the weakly
∗
convergent subsequence, in view of the strong convergences in (
3.16
) and (
3.17
), we can straightforwardly pass to the limit in (
3.5
) (that is satisfied by the
(F_{n},\gamma F_{n})
) to deduce that the weak
∗
limit is a solution to (
3.5
) with
(\varrho,{\bf j},V)
. Owing to the uniqueness property in Lemma
3.1
, we deduce that the weak
∗
limit is necessarily
(F,\gamma F)
. Moreover, since the limit is uniquely identified along any weakly
∗
convergent subsequence, we deduce that
(F_{n},\gamma F_{n})\to(F,\gamma F)
weakly
∗
without having to pass to subsequences.

Then, we notice from (
3.10
) that the application of classical averaging lemmas (see for example
[
DL89a
,
DLM91
,
PS98
]
) show that for any
\varphi\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with at most polynomial growth, the sequence of averages

(3.18)

\displaystyle\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi(v)F_{n}(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}

is strongly compact in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
. Choosing
\varphi(v)=1,v_{i}
in (
3.18
) we deduce that
\rho_{F_{n}}
and
j_{F_{n}}
are compact in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
. To obtain the compactness of
V_{F_{n}}
we argue as follows. Taking
\varphi(v)=|v|^{2}
we have that
\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{2}F_{n}\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}_{n}
is strongly compact in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
. Then, from (
3.14
) we recall

\displaystyle V_{F_{n}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v-u_{F_{n}}|^{2}F_{n}\,\textnormal{d}v=\frac{1}{d}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{2}F_{n}\,\textnormal{d}v-\frac{|j_{F_{n}}|^{2}}{\rho_{F_{n}}}\right),

and that all terms on the right-hand side are pointwise convergent (up to some further subsequence). It follows that
V_{F_{n}}
itself is a.e. convergent. Also, since

\displaystyle 0\leq V_{F_{n}}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v|^{2}F_{n}\,\textnormal{d}v,

the right-hand side of which is strongly convergent in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
, we deduce via the Vitali convergence theorem that
V_{F_{n}}
is strongly convergent in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.

To conclude the proof, we recall that
F_{n}\overset{\star}{\rightharpoonup}F

without
passing to subsequences. Thus any convergent subsequences of
\{\rho_{F_{n}}\},\{j_{F_{n}}\},
and
\{V_{F_{n}}\}
necessarily converge to
\rho_{F},j_{F}
, and
V_{F}
. Again we deduce that, from the unique identification of the limit, it must be that the full sequences
\rho_{F_{n}},j_{F_{n}}
, and
V_{F_{n}}
are strongly convergent in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
to
\rho_{F},j_{F}
, and
V_{F}
. The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Strictly speaking, velocity averaging lemmas such as those stated in
[
DLM91
]
are stated for kinetic equations which are imposed to hold in the whole domain
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. These lemmas have been subsequently employed to general open bounded domains, but the authors could not locate a suitable reference with a satisfactory explanation on why this is possible. The argument can proceed as follows. One represents
\Omega
appropriately as the union of countably many open balls
\{\Omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
. Then we take a partition of unity
\{\eta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
subordinate to the
\Omega_{i}
, satisfying

(3.19)

\displaystyle\eta_{i}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega_{i}),\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\eta_{i}(x)=1\quad\forall x\in\Omega.

Let us suppose we have a sequence
F_{m}=F_{m}(t,x,v)
satisfying an appropriate system of kinetic equations in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then we note that since
\textnormal{supp}(\eta_{i})\Subset\Omega
, the function
\eta_{i}(x)F_{m}(t,x,v)
now satisfies an appropriate kinetic equation in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Hence, for each fixed
i
we apply the velocity averaging lemma (for
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
) to deduce, for each
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{v})
, the compactness in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
of the averages
\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(v)\eta_{i}(x)F_{m}(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}_{m}
. By diagonally extracting and passing to further subsequences as necessary, we can assume that we are now along some subsequence for which
\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(v)\eta_{i}(x)F_{m}(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}_{m}
is convergent in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
for every
i
. The convergence of the averages
\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(v)F_{m}(t,x,v)\textnormal{d}v\right\}
then follows by using (
3.19
) and the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem to sum over
i
.

Lemma 3.6
.

The map
\mathscr{R}:\mathscr{X}\to\mathscr{X}
is compact.

Proof.
Since the proof is much easier than showing continuity of
\mathscr{R}
, we provide only a rough sketch of the ideas. Let
(\varrho_{n},{\bf j}_{n},V_{n})
be bounded in
\mathscr{X}
. Denote by
(F_{n},\gamma F_{n})
the corresponding solution to (
3.5
). We want to show that

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}(\varrho_{n},{\bf j}_{n},V_{n}):=(\rho_{F_{n}},j_{F_{n}},V_{F_{n}})

is convergent in
\mathscr{X}
modulo some subsequence. As before, it is enough to show that
\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varphi(v)F_{n}\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}_{n}
is strongly compact in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
for
\varphi(v)=1,v,|v|^{2}
, which follows easily owing to (
3.10
) and averaging lemmas.
∎

Finally, it is straightforward to check that

Lemma 3.7
.

The set of eigenvectors

\displaystyle\mathscr{X}_{\mathscr{R}}:=\left\{(\varrho,{\bf j},V)\in\mathscr{X}\;\middle|\;(\varrho,{\bf j},V)=\lambda\mathscr{R}(\varrho,{\bf j},V)\text{ for some }\lambda\in[0,1]\right\}

\mathscr{X}
.

We can now state the existence of solutions to (

).

.

There exists a weak solution

Proof of Lemma

.

Collecting Lemmas

,

,

), (

), (

∎

3.3.
Uniform estimates

We begin this subsection by providing energy estimates which are uniform in the regularization parameter
\varepsilon
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
(f_{\varepsilon},\gamma f_{\varepsilon})
denote the solution to the regularized problem (
3.3
) as constructed in Lemma
3.8
. Then

\displaystyle\textnormal{$\{(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}$ is uniformly bounded in $L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\mathcal{O}))$},

\displaystyle\textnormal{$\{(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}$ is uniformly bounded in $L^{1}(\Sigma^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v)$}.

Proof.
Owing to (
3.10
), we readily notice that
\varphi(v)=1+|v|^{2}
is admissible as a test function into the (very) weak formulation of (
3.3
). The rigorous derivation can be done by taking the standard method of using smooth cutoffs, which we omit here and refer to for instance
[
Abd94
]
. The identity that is obtained is

(3.22)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad=-\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})g_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\\
&\qquad+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\left(2d\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}-2v\cdot(\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})f_{\varepsilon}\right)\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.\end{split}

It is enough to estimate the last integral of the right-hand side. We note that
\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\leq\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}+1
holds for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, which gives:

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\leq C(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}})\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\quad(\because\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}=O(1))

\displaystyle\leq C(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}})\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(1+\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}})f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\quad(\because\eqref{eq: reg major})

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

Next, we estimate

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)(-2v\cdot(\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})f_{\varepsilon})\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq C(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}})\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\left(|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}+|v||u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}|f_{\varepsilon}\right)\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq C(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}})\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{3}{2}|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}+|u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}\right)\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq C(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}})\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\left(|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}+|u_{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}\right)\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\quad(\because\eqref{eq: reg major})

\displaystyle\quad\leq C(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}})\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v,

the last line following by Jensen’s inequality
\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}|u_{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}\textnormal{d}v
. Collecting these estimates, we deduce via (
3.22
) and Grönwall’s lemma that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{T},\quad\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{T}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{T},

with the constant
C_{T}>0
dependent on
g
and
f^{0}
but independent of
\varepsilon
(recall the uniform bounds that are due to (
3.4
)).
∎

However, the lemma above is not enough for the passage to
\varepsilon\to 0
, as it does not provide a method for us to obtain compactness for the temperature in any way. For that reason we make the observation that multiplier methods
[
LP92
,
Per96
,
Per04
]
, originally stated for the transport equation and utilized thorougly in the study of the BGK equation
[
CZ16
,
CY20
]
, can be adapted to kinetic Fokker–Planck equations under fairly mild assumptions. We provide the statement in rather general form for the sake of future applications.

Lemma 3.10

(Higher moments)
.

Let
(h_{m},\gamma h_{m})
denote a sequence of nonnegative very weak solutions to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}h_{m}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}h_{m}=a_{m}(t,x)\Delta_{v}h_{m}+b_{m}(t,x)\nabla_{v}\cdot({\bf c}_{m}(t,x,v)h_{m})+d_{m}(t,x,v)h_{m},\\
h_{m}|_{t=0}=h_{m}^{0}\end{cases}

in
(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}
. Suppose that for some
k\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\textnormal{$\{h_{m}^{0}\}$ is bounded in $L^{1}\left(\mathcal{O},\,\left<v\right>^{k}\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\right)$},

\displaystyle\textnormal{$\{h_{m}\}$ is bounded in $L^{\infty}\left(0,T;L^{1}\left(\mathcal{O},\left<v\right>^{k}\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\right)\right)$},

\displaystyle\textnormal{$\{a_{m}h_{m}\}$ is bounded in $L^{1}\left((0,T)\times\mathcal{O},\left<v\right>^{k-2}\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\right)$},

\displaystyle\textnormal{$\{b_{m}|{\bf c}_{m}|h_{m}\}$ is bounded in $L^{1}\left((0,T)\times\mathcal{O},\,\left<v\right>^{k-1}\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\right)$},

\displaystyle\textnormal{$\{d_{m}h_{m}\}$ is bounded in $L^{1}\left((0,T)\times\mathcal{O},\,\left<v\right>^{k}\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\right)$},

\displaystyle\textnormal{$\{\gamma h_{m}\}$ is bounded in $L^{1}\left(\Sigma^{T},\left<v\right>^{k}|n(x)\cdot v|\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\right)$}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\left\{\left<v\right>^{k+1}h_{m}\right\}_{m}\textnormal{ is bounded in $L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})$}.

Proof.
The proof is an adaptation of the results in
[
LP92
]
. For a fixed
x_{0}\in\Omega
, we admit

\displaystyle\varphi(x,v)=\left<v\right>^{k-1}\frac{v\cdot(x-x_{0})}{\left<x-x_{0}\right>}

as a test function in the weak formulation for
h_{m}
. The overcoming of the non-compact support of
\varphi
can be done through the usual cutoff procedure, which is well-illustrated in say
[
CZ16
, Lemma 2.2]
and thus omitted here. By using the computation

\displaystyle v\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi=\frac{\left<v\right>^{k+1}}{\left<x-x_{0}\right>}\left(1-\frac{(v\cdot(x-x_{0}))^{2}}{|v|^{2}\left<x-x_{0}\right>^{2}}\right),

we obtain that

(3.23)

\begin{split}&\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}h_{m}\frac{\left<v\right>^{k+1}}{\left<x-x_{0}\right>}\left(1-\frac{(v\cdot(x-x_{0}))^{2}}{|v|^{2}\left<x-x_{0}\right>^{2}}\right)\;\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad=\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}{\bf c}_{m}(t,x,v)b_{m}(t,x)h_{m}\cdot\nabla_{v}\varphi\;\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}a_{m}(t,x)h_{m}\Delta_{v}\varphi\;\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&\qquad+\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}d_{m}h_{m}\varphi\;\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\mathcal{O}}(h_{m}(T)-h_{m}(0))\,\varphi\;\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma^{T}}\gamma h_{m}\,\varphi\,v\cdot n(x)\;\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\cr&\quad=:\sum_{i=1}^{5}R_{i}.\end{split}

We find a lower-bound for the left-hand side first. Since

\displaystyle 1-\frac{(v\cdot(x-x_{0}))^{2}}{|v|^{2}\left<x-x_{0}\right>^{2}}\geq 1-\frac{|x-x_{0}|^{2}}{\left<x-x_{0}\right>^{2}}=\frac{1}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{1+[\textnormal{diam}(\Omega)]^{2}},

we discover that the left-hand side of (
3.23
) satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}h_{m}\frac{\left<v\right>^{k+1}}{\left<x-x_{0}\right>}\left(1-\frac{(v\cdot(x-x_{0}))^{2}}{|v|^{2}\left<x-x_{0}\right>^{2}}\right)\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\geq\frac{1}{1+[\textnormal{diam}(\Omega)]^{3}}\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}\left<v\right>^{k+1}h_{m}\;\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

The remaining terms are estimated by using:

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle=O(|v|^{k}),\quad\nabla_{v}\varphi=\left((k-1)\left<v\right>^{k-3}\frac{v\cdot(x-x_{0})}{\left<x-x_{0}\right>}v+\left<v\right>^{k-1}\frac{x-x_{0}}{\left<x-x_{0}\right>}\right),

\displaystyle\Delta_{v}\varphi

\displaystyle=\left((k-1)(k-3)\left<v\right>^{k-5}|v|^{2}\frac{v\cdot(x-x_{0})}{\left<x-x_{0}\right>}+4(k-1)\left<v\right>^{k-3}\frac{v\cdot(x-x_{0})}{\left<x-x_{0}\right>}\right),

which lead immediately to

\displaystyle R_{1}\lesssim_{k}\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}|b_{m}{\bf c}_{m}|\left<v\right>^{k-1}h_{m}\;\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C,\quad R_{2}\lesssim_{k}\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}|a_{m}h_{m}|\left<v\right>^{k-2}\;\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C,

\displaystyle R_{3}\leq\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}\left<v\right>^{k}|d_{m}h_{m}|\;\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C,\quad R_{4}\leq\int_{\mathcal{O}}\left<v\right>^{k}(h_{m}(T)-h_{m}(0))\;\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C,

\displaystyle R_{5}\leq\int_{\Sigma^{T}}|v|^{k}\gamma h_{m}|v\cdot n(x)|\;\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\leq C,

by assumption on the coefficients. Collecting the estimates we deduce the result.
∎

Particularly, it is thus deduced that

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
(f_{\varepsilon},\gamma f_{\varepsilon})
denote the solution to (
3.3
) as constructed in Lemma
3.8
. Then

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}|v|^{3}f_{\varepsilon}(t)\;\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{T}

for some constant
C_{T}>0
.

Proof.
We set (in the notations of Lemma
3.10
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}a_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)},\\
b_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon),\\
{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)},\\
d_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)\equiv 0.\end{cases}

Owing to Lemma
3.9
, to show that all conditions of Lemma
3.10
are satisfied (with
k=2
), it is enough to obtain bounds for
a_{\varepsilon}f_{\varepsilon}
and
b_{\varepsilon}|{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}|f_{\varepsilon}
. Now (
3.2
) shows

\displaystyle|a_{\varepsilon}f_{\varepsilon}|\leq(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)(1+\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}})f_{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle|b_{\varepsilon}|{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}|f_{\varepsilon}|\leq(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)(|\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}|+|u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}|)f_{\varepsilon}\leq(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)(|v|+|u_{f_{\varepsilon}}|)f_{\varepsilon}

Then clearly for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1]

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}|a_{\varepsilon}f_{\varepsilon}|\;\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\leq C(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}})\left(\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}+\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega}V_{f_{\varepsilon}}\right)\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\leq C(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}})\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}\;\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\leq C(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}},f^{0}).

Next, we estimate

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}b_{\varepsilon}|{\bf c}_{\varepsilon}|f_{\varepsilon}\left<v\right>\;\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)\left(\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}+\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}|v||u_{f_{\varepsilon}}|f_{\varepsilon}\right)\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq C(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}},f^{0})\left(1+\left(\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega}\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}|u_{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\right)^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq C(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}},f^{0})\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq C(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}},f^{0}).

From the second line, we used Lemma
3.9
and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to get the third line, then
\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}|u_{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}
(Jensen’s inequality) to obtain the fourth, and finally Lemma
3.9
again to deduce the last bound.
∎

As one final step before passage to the limit, we now prove the entropy identity.

Lemma 3.12
.

The solution
(f_{\varepsilon},\gamma f_{\varepsilon})
to the regularized problem (
3.3
) satisfies the entropy evolution identity

\displaystyle\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}(t)\log f_{\varepsilon}(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Gamma_{+}}\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(t)\log\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(t)(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+4\int_{\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\left|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad=-\int_{\Gamma_{-}}g_{\varepsilon}(t)\log g_{\varepsilon}(t)(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)f_{\varepsilon}(t)\left(\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}\right)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}(t)\log f_{\varepsilon}(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{T},

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{T}}\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\log\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{T},

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{T}

for some
C_{T}>0
independent of
\varepsilon
.

Proof.
Let us first remark that the decay (
3.20
), and the assumption that
\Omega
is bounded, imply that
f_{\varepsilon}\log f_{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and
\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\log\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}(\Sigma^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v)
(although not necessarily uniformly in
\varepsilon
). This can be seen, for instance, by following the classical estimate performed in
[
JKO98
, pp.7–8]
.

Now, although the solution
f_{\varepsilon}
is renormalized, we notice that we cannot take
z\mapsto z\log z
as a renormalizing function since the Hessian of
z\log z
blows up near
z=0
. From this perspective we take the “regularized” renormalizing function

\displaystyle\chi_{\delta}(z):=z\log(\delta+z)+\delta\log\left(1+\frac{z}{\delta}\right),\quad z\geq 0,\quad\delta\in(0,1].

In this way, the classical inequality
\log(1+z)\leq z
shows

(3.24)

\displaystyle\chi_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})\leq\frac{(f_{\varepsilon})^{2}}{\delta}+f_{\varepsilon}(\log\delta+1),

and therefore
\chi_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})
enjoys the same tail decay as in (
3.20
). Next, by direct computations:

\displaystyle\chi_{\delta}^{\prime}(z)=\log(\delta+z)+1,\quad\chi_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{\delta+z}.

Hence, we readily find that
(\chi_{\delta}(z),1)
is an admissible pair in the renormalization formula for
f_{\varepsilon}
. The identity that is obtained is

(3.25)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{O}}\chi_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\mathcal{O}}\chi_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon}^{0})\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\chi_{\delta}(\gamma f_{\varepsilon})(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\frac{4f_{\varepsilon}}{\delta+f_{\varepsilon}}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&=-\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}\chi_{\delta}(g_{\varepsilon})(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v-\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\frac{1}{\delta+f_{\varepsilon}}\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}\cdot(\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})f_{\varepsilon}\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.\end{split}

We now investigate the limit of each term in (
3.25
) as
\delta\downarrow 0
. In regard of
\chi_{\delta}
, we first observe that pointwise:

\begin{split}|\chi_{\delta}(z)-z\log z|&=\left|z\log\left(1+\frac{\delta}{z}\right)+\delta\log\left(1+\frac{z}{\delta}\right)\right|=\left|z\log\left(1+\frac{\delta}{z}\right)+\delta\log(\delta+z)-\delta\log\delta\right|\end{split}

tends to zero as
\delta\downarrow 0
. In fact, from

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\delta}\chi_{\delta}(z)=\log\left(1+\frac{z}{\delta}\right)\geq 0,

we find that the function
\chi_{\delta}(z)
decreases to
z\log z
as
\delta\downarrow 0
. Therefore, to apply the dominated convergence theorem to the integrals
\int\chi_{\delta}(\cdot)
, it is sufficient to prove that the integral is finite when
\delta=1
(the maximal value). It is clearly from (
3.24
) and (
3.20
) that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}\chi_{1}(f_{\varepsilon})(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq\int_{\mathcal{O}}\Big(|f_{\varepsilon}(t)|^{2}+f_{\varepsilon}(t)\Big)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{\varepsilon},

and thus we conclude

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}\chi_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\xrightarrow[\delta\downarrow 0]{}\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}(t)\log f_{\varepsilon}(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

In precisely the same manner, using (
3.21
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\chi_{\delta}(\gamma f_{\varepsilon})(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\xrightarrow[\delta\downarrow 0]{}\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\log\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v.

The convergences of
\int_{\mathcal{O}}\chi_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon}^{0})
and
\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}\chi_{\delta}(g_{\varepsilon})(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}
follow analogously.

Next we discuss the weighted Fisher information, in other words the second line of (
3.25
). Since
\frac{4z}{\delta+z}\uparrow 4
as
\delta\downarrow 0
, it is immediately deduced via the monotone convergence theorem that

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\frac{4f_{\varepsilon}}{\delta+f_{\varepsilon}}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=4\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

Finally, let us focus on the last integral of the right-hand side of (
3.25
). We use the simple algebraic manipulation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta+f_{\varepsilon}}f_{\varepsilon}\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{v}\Big(f_{\varepsilon}-\delta\log(\delta+f_{\varepsilon})\Big)=\nabla_{v}\Big(f_{\varepsilon}-\delta\log(\delta+f_{\varepsilon})+\delta\log\delta\Big)

in order to rewrite the integral as

\displaystyle-\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\frac{1}{\delta+f_{\varepsilon}}\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}\cdot(\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})f_{\varepsilon}\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad=-\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\nabla_{v}\Big(f_{\varepsilon}-\delta\log(\delta+f_{\varepsilon})+\delta\log\delta\Big)\cdot(\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

(3.26)

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\Big(f_{\varepsilon}-\delta\log(\delta+f_{\varepsilon})+\delta\log\delta\Big)\,\left(\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}\right)\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

Using the relations

\displaystyle 0\leq z-\delta\log(\delta+z)+\delta\log\delta=z+\delta\log\left(1-\frac{z}{\delta+z}\right)\leq z-\frac{\delta z}{\delta+z}\leq z,

\displaystyle\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}=O(1),

we deduce from the dominated convergence theorem that (
3.26
) converges as
\delta\downarrow 0
to

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)f_{\varepsilon}\,\left(\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}\right)\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

Collecting all, we find that (
3.25
) reads in the limit

(3.27)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}(t)\log f_{\varepsilon}(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\log\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad+4\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&=-\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}g_{\varepsilon}\log g_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)f_{\varepsilon}\,\left(\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}\right)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v,\end{split}

which is precisely the desired entropy identity.

To obtain the final assertion, we simply note that (
3.27
) and the assumptions on the initial and boundary datum give us

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}(t)\log f_{\varepsilon}(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{T}}\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\log\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}4(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq C_{T}+C(d,\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}})\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega}f_{\varepsilon}\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C(d,T,\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}},f^{0}),

where the last inequality is due to Lemma
3.9
.
∎

Lemmas
3.9
and
3.12
imply by virtue of the Dunford–Pettis criteria that modulo a subsequence there exists a weak limit
(f,\gamma)
such that

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup f\quad\text{weakly in }L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O}),\quad\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\gamma f\quad\text{weakly in }L^{1}(\Sigma_{+}^{T},(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v).

From hereon we will always assume that we are along this weakly convergent subsequence.

3.4.
Renormalization and compactness

We begin this section by showing that we can pass to the limit in the macroscopic variables. Let us first record the specific averaging lemma we will use, see
[
DLM91
, Theorem 6]
.

Lemma 3.13
.

Assume that
h_{m}\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
is a sequence of solutions to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h_{m}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}h_{m}=H_{m}^{1}+\nabla_{v}\cdot H_{m}^{2}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O}),

where the
H_{m}^{i}
are bounded in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
for
i=1,2
. Then for each
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the sequence of averages
\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h_{m}\varphi(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}_{m}
is compact in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.

However, this lemma is not applicable in our situation since the
L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
-norm of
f_{\varepsilon}
is not uniformly bounded (it depends on the regularized initial datum
f_{\varepsilon}^{0}
). Toward this end, inspired by the works
[
DL89a
,
DL89b
,
GSR05
]
(see also
[
EGM10
]
), we resort to the advantage that
f_{\varepsilon}
is renormalized.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
(f_{\varepsilon},\gamma f_{\varepsilon})
denote the solution to (
3.3
) as constructed through Lemma
3.8
. Then for any
\varphi\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with subcubic growth, the averages
\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{\varepsilon}\varphi(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}_{\varepsilon}
are compact in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.

Proof.
Consider the renormalizing function

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})=\frac{f_{\varepsilon}}{1+\delta f_{\varepsilon}},

which is a bounded weak solution to

(3.28)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})&=\Gamma_{\delta}^{\prime}(f_{\varepsilon})(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}\right)+\Gamma_{\delta}^{\prime}(f_{\varepsilon})(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\left(\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}\right)f_{\varepsilon}\\
&\quad+(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)(\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})\cdot\nabla_{v}\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon}).\end{split}

We observe that the following identities

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\delta}^{\prime}(f_{\varepsilon})\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}\right)=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\Gamma_{\delta}^{\prime}(f_{\varepsilon})2\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\right)-\Gamma_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}(f_{\varepsilon})\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}4f_{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle(\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})\cdot\nabla_{v}\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left((\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})\right)-\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon},

allow us to rewrite the right-hand side of (
3.28
) as

(
3.28
)

\displaystyle=(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\Gamma_{\delta}^{\prime}(f_{\varepsilon})2\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}+\left(\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\left(-\Gamma_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}(f_{\varepsilon})\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}4f_{\varepsilon}+\left(\Gamma_{\delta}^{\prime}(f_{\varepsilon})f_{\varepsilon}-\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})\right)\left(\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}\right)\right).

That is, we can write

(3.29)

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})=H_{\varepsilon}^{1}+\nabla_{v}\cdot H_{\varepsilon}^{2},\\
&H_{\varepsilon}^{1}:=(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\left(-\Gamma_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}(f_{\varepsilon})\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}4f_{\varepsilon}+\left(\Gamma_{\delta}^{\prime}(f_{\varepsilon})f_{\varepsilon}-\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})\right)\left(\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}\right)\right),\\
&H_{\varepsilon}^{2}:=(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\left(2\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\Gamma_{\delta}^{\prime}(f_{\varepsilon})\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}+\left(\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})\right).\end{split}

Then, the relations

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{\delta},f_{\varepsilon}\right\},\quad\Gamma_{\delta}^{\prime}(f_{\varepsilon})=\frac{1}{(1+\delta f_{\varepsilon})^{2}},\quad\Gamma_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}(f_{\varepsilon})=-\frac{2\delta}{(1+\delta f_{\varepsilon})^{3}},

along with the bounds

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\;\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{T}\ (\because\textnormal{Lemma \ref{lem: entropy}}),\quad\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}=O(1),\quad|u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}|\leq|u_{f_{\varepsilon}}|,

allow us to observe that

\displaystyle\textnormal{$\{H_{\varepsilon}^{1}\},\{H_{\varepsilon}^{2}\}$ are bounded in $L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})$}.

Since
\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
, we deduce from Lemma
3.13
that for each
\delta>0
and
\varphi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the sequence of averages
\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})\varphi(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}_{\varepsilon}
is compact in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.

To see that the compactness propagates to
f_{\varepsilon}
, we proceed as in
[
GSR05
]
. Recalling
\{f_{\varepsilon}\}
is weakly convergent in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
, we write, for any
M>0

\displaystyle|\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})-f_{\varepsilon}|=\frac{\delta(f_{\varepsilon})^{2}}{1+\delta f_{\varepsilon}}=\frac{\delta(f_{\varepsilon})^{2}}{1+\delta f_{\varepsilon}}\mathbf{1}_{f_{\varepsilon}\leq M}+\frac{\delta(f_{\varepsilon})^{2}}{1+\delta f_{\varepsilon}}\mathbf{1}_{f_{\varepsilon}>M}\leq\delta Mf_{\varepsilon}+f_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{1}_{f_{\varepsilon}>M}.

The weak convergence of
f_{\varepsilon}
implies that
f_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{1}_{f_{\varepsilon}>M}=o(1)_{L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})}
, uniformly in
\varepsilon
, as
M\uparrow\infty
. We fix
M
large enough, next take
\delta\downarrow 0
to deduce that

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{\delta}(f_{\varepsilon})-f_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})}\xrightarrow[\delta\downarrow 0]{}0.

This then implies that for each
\varphi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the averages
\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{\varepsilon}\varphi(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}_{\varepsilon}
are also compact in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
. Finally, the compactness can be extended to those
\varphi\in C(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with subcubic growth by using the tightness that is provided by the third moment bounds in Lemma
3.11
.
∎

We can now show the strong convergence of the distributions
\{f_{\varepsilon}\}
themselves.

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
f
denote the weak
L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
limit of
\{f_{\varepsilon}\}
. Then up to some subsequence,

f_{\varepsilon}\to f\quad\text{a.e. and strongly in }L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O}).

Proof.
We check that the conditions of Proposition
2.1
are valid for
f_{\varepsilon}
and
a_{\varepsilon}:=(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}
. We already know that
f_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup f
weakly, and that the averages
\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{\varepsilon}\varphi(v)\textnormal{d}v\right\}
are compact in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
. This gives (P1) and (P2). On the other hand, we recall from Lemma
3.12
that

\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\;\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{T},

and thus (P3) is true. Note that Lemma
3.14
provides, up to some subsequence, that the following convergences hold almost everywhere and in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
:

(3.30)

\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\to\rho_{f},\quad j_{f_{\varepsilon}}\to j_{f},\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}v\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}f\,\textnormal{d}v.

The last convergence of the second moments, and the relation in (
3.14
), allows us to deduce again that (recall the arguments after (
3.18
))

(3.31)

\displaystyle V_{f_{\varepsilon}}\to V_{f}\quad\text{a.e. and in}\quad L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega).

The convergences (
3.30
), (
3.31
) then imply also that

(3.32)

\begin{split}u_{f_{\varepsilon}}\to u_{f}\quad\text{a.e. in }D_{+}:=\{(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega:\rho_{f}(t,x)>0\},\\
\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}\to\mathcal{T}_{f}\quad\text{a.e. in }D_{+}:=\{(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega:\rho_{f}(t,x)>0\}.\end{split}

Moreover,

(3.33)

\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}:=\left[V_{f_{\varepsilon}}\right]_{\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}},\varepsilon}+\varepsilon=\frac{V_{f_{\varepsilon}}}{\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\to\frac{V_{f}}{\rho_{f}}=\mathcal{T}_{f}\quad\text{a.e. in }D_{+}.

By the assumption that
\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}
is continuous, the above convergences also imply

(3.34)

\displaystyle\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon=\nu(\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}},j_{f_{\varepsilon}},V_{f_{\varepsilon}})\to\nu(\rho_{f},j_{f},V_{f})=\nu_{f}\quad\text{a.e. in }(0,T)\times\Omega.

Finally, by definition of the temperature
\mathcal{T}_{f}
(and the assumption (
1.2
) on
\nu
), we have that

\displaystyle\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}=0\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\rho_{f}=0,

therefore this and (
3.33
), (
3.34
) show (P4) holds with
a=\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}
. Applying Proposition
2.1
we deduce that up to some subsequence
f_{\varepsilon}
converges almost everywhere and strongly in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
.
∎

3.5.
Passing to the limit: the very weak formulation

Using the convergences implied by Lemma
3.15
, (
3.30
), (
3.32
), (
3.33
), (
3.34
), and the weak convergence
\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\gamma f
in
L^{1}(\Sigma_{+}^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v)
, we aim to pass to the limit
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
in the very weak formulation of (
3.3
). We recall that
f_{\varepsilon}
satisfies, for any
t\in(0,T]
and
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,t]\times\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})

(3.35)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}(t)\varphi(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\varphi(0,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\,\varphi\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad=-\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}g_{\varepsilon}\,\varphi\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}(\partial_{s}\varphi+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi)\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&\qquad+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\left(\Delta_{v}\varphi\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}-\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot\left(\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)f_{\varepsilon}\right)\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.\end{split}

Step 1: Convergence of the left-hand side.

Since Lemma
3.15
implies

\left\|\|f_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot,\cdot)-f(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathcal{O})}\right\|_{L^{1}(0,T)}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}0,

there exists a subsequence such that for almost every
t\in(0,T]
:

\|f_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot,\cdot)-f(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathcal{O})}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}0.

Hence, we have

\int_{\mathcal{O}}|f_{\varepsilon}(t)-f(t)||\varphi(t,x,v)|\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\mathcal{O}}|f_{\varepsilon}(t)-f(t)|\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\to 0\quad\text{a.e. }t\in(0,T],

from which we deduce that the first term of the left-hand side converges as

\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}(t)\varphi(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\to\int_{\mathcal{O}}f(t)\varphi(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v,

at least a.e.
t
.

The term containing the initial data converges as
\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\varphi(0,x,v)\to\int_{\mathcal{O}}f^{0}\varphi(0,x,v)
since we assume
f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\to f^{0}
at least weakly in
L^{1}(\mathcal{O})
.

For the term containing the trace, the weak convergence
\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\gamma f
in
L^{1}(\Sigma_{+}^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v)
implies

\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\,\varphi\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\to\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\gamma f\,\varphi\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\quad\forall t\in(0,T].

We conclude that the left-hand side of (
3.35
) is convergent for a.e.
t
in the limit
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.

Step 2: Convergence of the right-hand side.
Since
g_{\varepsilon}\to g
in
L^{1}(\Sigma_{-}^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v)
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}g_{\varepsilon}\,\varphi\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\to\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}g\varphi\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v.

The convergence of the terms that are linear in
f_{\varepsilon}
is clear. The only nontrivial remaining convergence is that of the last line of (
3.35
). Let us begin by demonstrating

Lemma 3.16
.

For each
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,t]\times\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

(3.36)

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\Delta_{v}\varphi\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\to\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\nu_{f}\Delta_{v}\varphi\mathcal{T}_{f}f\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

Proof.
Resorting to a density argument, we may assume without loss of generality that the test function
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,t]\times\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
takes the form

\varphi(s,x,v)=\psi(s,x)\eta(v),\quad\psi\in C^{\infty}([0,t]\times\overline{\Omega}),\quad\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Then, with the notations

\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}(s,x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{\varepsilon}(s,x,v)\Delta\eta(v)\,\textnormal{d}v,\quad\rho_{\Delta\eta}(s,x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(s,x,v)\Delta\eta(v)\,\textnormal{d}v.

Note that (
3.36
) is equivalent to showing that

(3.37)

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\Omega}\psi(s,x)\left[(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}-\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}\rho_{\Delta\eta}\right]\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\to 0.

Hence we only need show that
(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}\rho_{\Delta\eta}
weakly in
L^{1}([0,T]\times\Omega)
. We will prove a stronger statement and show that

(3.38)

\displaystyle\left[(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}-\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}\rho_{\Delta\eta}\right]\to 0\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega).

By the averaging effect of Lemma
3.14
, up to some subsequence it holds that

(3.39)

\displaystyle\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}\rho_{\Delta\eta}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,\Delta\eta(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\quad\text{a.e. and in}\quad L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega).

Taking the product with the (regularized) temperature, we can then observe the following bound, due to Young’s inequality

(3.40)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}|\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}|\leq C\|\Delta\eta\|_{L^{\infty}}\left(1+\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}=C(\|\Delta\eta\|_{L^{\infty}})(\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}+V_{f_{\varepsilon}}),

the right-hand side of which is strongly convergent in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
by (
3.30
), (
3.31
). As a result, the family
\left\{\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}\right\}_{\varepsilon}
is equiintegrable in
(0,T)\times\Omega
. Since (
3.39
) implies

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}\mathcal{T}_{f}\rho_{\Delta\eta}\quad\text{a.e. in}\quad D_{+}:=\{(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega:\rho_{f}(t,x)>0\},

we deduce from the Vitali convergence theorem that

(3.41)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{T}_{f}\rho_{\Delta\eta}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}(D_{+}).

On the other hand, on the set
D_{0}:=[(0,T)\times\Omega]\setminus D_{+}
, we have
f(t,x,v)=0
a.e.
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which gives that
\rho_{\Delta\eta}=\mathcal{T}_{f}=0
in
D_{0}
. Therefore,

(3.42)

\begin{split}\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{D_{0}}\left|\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}-\mathcal{T}_{f}\rho_{\Delta\eta}\right|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x&=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{D_{0}}\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\left|\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}\right|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\\
&\leq C(\|\Delta\eta\|_{L^{\infty}})\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{D_{0}}(\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}+V_{f_{\varepsilon}})\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\\
&=C\int_{D_{0}}(\rho_{f}+V_{f})\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\\
&=0,\end{split}

the inequality due to (
3.40
). In other words this proves
\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{T}_{f}\rho_{\Delta\eta}
in
L^{1}(D_{0})
. Combining this with (
3.41
), we find

(3.43)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}\to\mathcal{T}_{f}\rho_{\Delta\eta}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega).

To complete the proof, we recall that
(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\to\nu_{f}
pointwise a.e. in
(0,T)\times\Omega
(see (
3.34
)), and
\|\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1
. By Egorov’s theorem we can extract a set
E\subset(0,T)\times\Omega
with arbitrarily small Lebesgue measure for which
\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}\to\nu_{f}
uniformly outside
E
. Then, writing the difference in (
3.37
) as

(3.44)

\begin{split}\left|(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}-\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}\rho_{\Delta\eta}\right|&\leq\left|(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon-\nu_{f})\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}\right|+\left|\nu_{f}\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}-\mathcal{T}_{f}\rho_{\Delta\eta}\right)\right|\\
&=:J_{1}+J_{2},\end{split}

we see that
J_{1}\to 0
in
L^{1}([(0,T)\times\Omega]\setminus E)
and
J_{2}\to 0
in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
, thanks to (
3.43
). Taking the Lebesgue measure of
E
to zero, we then see that
\|J_{1}\|_{L^{1}(E)}\to 0
since
\nu
is bounded and the family
\left\{\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}\right\}
is equiintegrable. It follows that

\displaystyle(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}\to\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}\rho_{\Delta\eta}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega),

and this proves (
3.38
). Clearly, the latter then implies (
3.37
).
∎

Finally, we check that:

Lemma 3.17
.

In the limit as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, we have the following convergences regarding the right-hand side of (
3.35
):

(3.45)

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}f_{\varepsilon}\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\to\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\nu_{f}\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot vf\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v,

(3.46)

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\to\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\nu_{f}\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot u_{f}f\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

Proof.
Since
\|\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1
, we have that

\displaystyle(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)f_{\varepsilon}\leq C(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}})f_{\varepsilon},

which implies that the sequence
\{(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)f_{\varepsilon}\}
is weakly compact in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
; by (
3.34
) and Lemma
3.15
, the sequence is almost everywhere convergent. By the Vitali convergence theorem

\displaystyle(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)f_{\varepsilon}\to\nu_{f}f\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O}).

It follows that the difference of (
3.45
) can be estimated as

\displaystyle\left|\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}f_{\varepsilon}\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\nu_{f}\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot vf\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\left|(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)f_{\varepsilon}-\nu_{f}f\right||\nabla_{v}\varphi|\left|\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}\right|\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}|\nabla_{v}\varphi|\left|\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-v\right|\nu_{f}f\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq\textnormal{diam}(\textnormal{supp}\varphi)\|\nabla_{v}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}|(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)f_{\varepsilon}-\nu_{f}f|\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\qquad+\varepsilon(1+\textnormal{diam}(\textnormal{supp}(\varphi)))\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}f\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\quad\left(\because\left|\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-v\right|=\frac{\varepsilon(1+|v|)}{1+\varepsilon(1+|v|)}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}0.

Toward the end of (
3.46
) now, we proceed similarly as in the proof of (
3.37
). Taking
\varphi
to be of the form
\varphi(s,x,v)=\psi(s,x)\eta(v)
, it is enough to show that

\displaystyle(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}\to\nu_{f}u_{f}\rho_{\Delta\eta}\quad\text{weakly in}\quad L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega).

Again we will prove the stronger statement that the convergence holds strongly. As aforementioned the ideas are almost the same and thus we provide only a sketch of proof. Using the relation

\displaystyle|u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}|\leq C(\|\Delta_{\eta}\|_{L^{\infty}})\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}|u_{f_{\varepsilon}}|,

we deduce similarly as in the arguments in (
3.40
) and (
3.42
) that
u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}
is strongly convergent in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
. In particular,
\left\{u_{f^{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}\right\}
is equiintegrable over
(0,T)\times\Omega
. Then borrowing Egorov’s theorem again, we have that
(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\to\nu_{f}
uniformly outside of a small Lebesgue-measure set. Combining the two given information we deduce through telescoping as in (
3.44
) that

\displaystyle(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{\Delta\eta}^{\varepsilon}\to\nu_{f}u_{f}\rho_{\Delta\eta}\quad\text{strongly in}\quad L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega).

This completes the proof of (
3.46
).
∎

Collecting all, we find that for a.e.
t\in(0,T]
it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}f(t)\varphi(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\mathcal{O}}f^{0}\varphi(0,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\gamma f\,\varphi\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad=-\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}g\,\varphi\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\nu_{f}\left(\Delta_{v}\varphi\,\mathcal{T}_{f}f-\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot(v-u_{f})f\right)\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

That is, up to a modification of
f
in a
\mathscr{L}^{1}([0,T])
-zero set, the map

\displaystyle(0,T]\ni t\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{O}}f(t)\varphi(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

is absolutely continuous, and up to this version, the very weak formulation holds for all
t\in(0,T]
.

3.6.
Passing to the limit: the weak formulation

If we are to use the weak formulation (instead of the very weak formulation), the only difference is that we must show

(3.47)

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\to\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\nu_{f}\nabla_{v}\varphi\cdot\mathcal{T}_{f}\nabla_{v}f\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

To give a sense to this identity, in virtue of the entropy dissipation in Lemma
3.12
, we rewrite

(3.48)

\displaystyle(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}=2\left[\sqrt{\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\right]\left[\sqrt{\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\right].

Let us investigate the convergence of each term in the brackets.

Lemma 3.18
.

The sequence
\left\{\sqrt{\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\right\}
converges strongly in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
to
\sqrt{\nu_{f}}\mathcal{T}_{f}^{1/2}\sqrt{f}
.

Proof.
First, we prove weak convergence. Using the inequality:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}\leq C(1+\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}})f_{\varepsilon},

and also that
\nu
is universally bounded, we can straightforwardly observe that
\left\{\sqrt{\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\right\}
is bounded in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
. To see that the weak
L^{2}
limit is
\sqrt{\nu_{f}}\mathcal{T}_{f}^{1/2}\sqrt{f}
, we first fix a compactly supported test function
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
. Then, using the convergences provided by Lemma
3.15
, (
3.32
), (
3.34
), we resort to Egorov’s theorem, extracting a set
E\subset\textnormal{supp}(\psi)\cap(D_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
with small Lebesgue measure, for which

\sqrt{\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\to\sqrt{\nu_{f}}\mathcal{T}_{f}^{1/2}\sqrt{f}
uniformly in
(\textnormal{supp}(\psi)\cap(D_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}))\setminus E
.

Then, using that
\sqrt{\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}
is equiintegrable over
(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}
(owing to it being bounded in
L^{2}
), we let the measure of
E
tend to zero to deduce that

(3.49)

\displaystyle\int_{D_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sqrt{\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\,\psi(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\to\int_{D_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sqrt{\nu_{f}}\mathcal{T}_{f}^{1/2}\sqrt{f}\,\psi(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

In regard of the complement
D_{0}=((0,T)\times\Omega)\setminus D_{+}
, we can easily check that

(3.50)

\begin{split}&\int_{D_{0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\sqrt{\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}-\sqrt{\nu_{f}}\mathcal{T}_{f}^{1/2}\sqrt{f}\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad=\int_{D_{0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad=\int_{D_{0}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\\
&\quad\leq(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)\int_{D_{0}}(1+\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}})\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\\
&\quad=C\int_{D_{0}}(\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}+V_{f_{\varepsilon}})\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}0.\end{split}

Combining this with (
3.49
), it is therefore clear that for all
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}\sqrt{\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\,\psi(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\to\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}\sqrt{\nu_{f}}\mathcal{T}_{f}^{1/2}\sqrt{f}\,\psi(t,x,v)\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

To relax the condition on the test function to
\psi\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
, we simply use that
C_{c}^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
is dense in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
to conclude that (
3.49
) holds for all
\psi\in L^{2}
. Hence the weak
L^{2}
limit of
\sqrt{\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}
is necessarily
\sqrt{\nu_{f}}\mathcal{T}_{f}^{1/2}\sqrt{f}
.

In virtue of the arguments so far, to show that the
L^{2}
convergence is in fact strong, it is enough to prove the norm convergence

(3.51)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}f\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

Notice that in (
3.50
), we already showed

\displaystyle\sqrt{\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\to\sqrt{\nu_{f}}\mathcal{T}_{f}^{1/2}\sqrt{f}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{2}(D_{0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}),

and therefore, it is enough to prove that the integral of the left-hand side of (
3.51
) converges in the domain
D_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Towards this goal, we first observe that (
3.51
) is equivalent to showing

(3.52)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{D_{+}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x=\int_{D_{+}}\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}\rho_{f}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x.

Using (
3.30
), (
3.32
), (
3.34
), and Egorov’s theorem, we can again extract a set
E\subset D_{+}
with small Lebesgue measure for which

(3.53)

\displaystyle(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\to\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}\rho_{f}\quad\text{uniformly in $D_{+}\setminus E$}.

Hence, it is enough to prove that
\left\{(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\right\}
cannot concentrate on the small set
E
. By using the definition of the (regularized) temperature, we note the estimate

(3.54)

\begin{split}\int_{E}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+1)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x&\leq(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)\int_{E}\left(1+\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}\right)\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\\
&=(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)\left(\int_{E}\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x+\int_{E}V_{f_{\varepsilon}}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\right).\end{split}

Since
\{\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\}
and
\{V_{f_{\varepsilon}}\}
are both equiintegrable over
(0,T)\times\Omega
(they are strongly convergent in
L^{1}
by (
3.30
) and (
3.31
)), we observe that the right-hand side of (
3.54
) vanishes as
\mathscr{L}^{d+1}(E)\downarrow 0
. This combined with (
3.53
) proves (
3.52
) and hence the assertion of the lemma.
∎

Finally, we discuss the Fisher information arising in (
3.48
).

Lemma 3.19
.

The sequence
\left\{\sqrt{\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\right\}
converges weakly in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
to
\sqrt{\nu_{f}}\mathcal{T}_{f}^{1/2}\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f}
.

Proof.
From Lemma
3.12
, we observe that
\left\{\sqrt{\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\right\}
is bounded in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
. It is thus enough to identify the weak limit. Clearly, the arguments surrounding (
3.49
) and (
3.50
) imply that for all
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
:

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}\sqrt{\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\,\partial_{v_{i}}\psi\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\to\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}\sqrt{\nu_{f}}\mathcal{T}_{f}^{1/2}\sqrt{f}\,\partial_{v_{i}}\psi\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v,\quad i=1,2,\ldots,d.

By definition of the weak derivative, we find

\displaystyle\textnormal{$\sqrt{\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\to\sqrt{\nu_{f}}\mathcal{T}_{f}^{1/2}\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f}$ \quad in \quad$\mathcal{D}^{\prime}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})$}.

Then, through a density argument, we obtain the assertion of the lemma.
∎

Combining Lemma
3.18
and Lemma
3.19
, we conclude that the term in (
3.48
) converges weakly in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
as

\displaystyle 2(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\left[\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\right]\left[\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)^{1/2}\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}\right]\rightharpoonup 2\nu_{f}\left[\mathcal{T}_{f}^{1/2}\sqrt{f}\right]\left[\mathcal{T}_{f}^{1/2}\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f}\right].

This proves (
3.47
).

3.7.
The energy inequality

In this section we revisit Lemma
3.9
and prove an energy inequality which holds for the solution
f
.

Proposition 3.20
.

The limit
f
satisfies the following energy inequality:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq-\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})g(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}.

The proof of the proposition consists in showing that, in the energy inequality satisfied by the regularized solution
f_{\varepsilon}
, the left-hand side is lower semicontinuous whereas the right-hand side converges in the limit
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. Indeed, for the convenience of the reader, let us recall (
3.22
) here:

(3.55)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)2v\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&=-\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})g_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\left(2d\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}+2v\cdot u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}\right)\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.\end{split}

3.7.1.
Lower semicontinuity of the left-hand side

By the lower semicontinuity of the moments under weak convergence:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v.

On the other hand, the assumption (
3.4
) on the regularized initial data gives

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

Lastly, by Lemma
3.15
, (
3.34
),
\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}\to v
, and Fatou’s lemma, the final integral of the left-hand side is lower semicontinuous as

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}2\nu_{f}|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)2v\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}f_{\varepsilon}\;\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

3.7.2.
Convergence of the right-hand side

We now investigate the right-hand side of (
3.55
). First, again by the conditions (
3.4
) for the regularized boundary data:

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})g_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v=\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})g(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v.

Only the convergence of the last integral in (
3.55
) remains in question. Let us consider the convergence of the following integral first:

Lemma 3.21
.

In the limit
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, we have that

(3.56)

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)d\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\to\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\nu_{f}d\mathcal{T}_{f}f\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

Proof.
Notice that proving (
3.56
) is equivalent to showing

(3.57)

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\Omega}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\to\int_{(0,t)\times\Omega}\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}\rho_{f}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x.

By (
3.30
), (
3.33
), and (
3.34
), the integrand of the left-hand side of (
3.57
) converges pointwise to
\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}\rho_{f}
in
D_{+}:=\{(t,x):\rho_{f}(t,x)>0\}
. We claim now that the convergence holds strongly in
L^{1}(D_{+})
. Similarly as before, we can resort to Egorov’s theorem thus it is enough to prove that
\{(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\}
does not concentrate on Lebesgue-null sets of
(0,T)\times\Omega
. Since

\displaystyle(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\leq(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)(1+\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}})\rho_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq C(\rho_{\varepsilon}+V_{f_{\varepsilon}}),

the right-hand side of which is convergent in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
, we deduce that
\{(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\}
is indeed equiintegrable over
(0,T)\times\Omega
.

On the other hand, on
D_{0}:=((0,T)\times\Omega)\setminus D_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{D_{0}}|(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}-\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}d\rho_{f}|\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{D_{0}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x

\displaystyle\leq(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{D_{0}}(1+\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}})\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{D_{0}}(\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}+V_{f_{\varepsilon}})\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x

\displaystyle=C\int_{D_{0}}(\rho_{f}+V_{f})\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\quad(\because\eqref{eq: MAC convergence},\eqref{eq: V convergence})

\displaystyle=0.

Altogether, we deduce

\displaystyle(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\to\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}\rho_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega),

and therefore the integral in (
3.57
) converges as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. This proves the convergence of (
3.56
).
∎

Finally, we check the convergence of the last term remaining in (
3.55
):

Lemma 3.22
.

In the limit
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)v\cdot u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\to\int_{(0,t)\times\Omega}\nu_{f}\rho_{f}|u_{f}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x.

Proof.
Notice first that the integral in concern can be rewritten as

(3.58)

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)v\cdot u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=\int_{(0,t)\times\Omega}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}u_{f_{\varepsilon}}\cdot u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x.

On one hand, it is clear from (
3.30
), (
3.32
) that the integrand of the right-hand side converges pointwise to

(3.59)

\displaystyle(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)(\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}u_{f_{\varepsilon}}\cdot u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})\to\nu_{f}\rho_{f}|u_{f}|^{2}\quad\text{a.e. in}\quad D_{+}.

On the other hand, due to (
3.2
) and Jensen’s inequality, the following domination also holds:

\displaystyle(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\left|\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}u_{f_{\varepsilon}}\cdot u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right|\leq(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}|u_{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\leq C(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}v.

By the averaging Lemma
3.14
,

(3.60)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}v\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}f\,\textnormal{d}v\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega).

This and the pointwise convergence (
3.59
) imply, through the Vitali convergence theorem, that

\displaystyle(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)(\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}u_{f_{\varepsilon}}\cdot u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})\to\nu_{f}\rho_{f}|u_{f}|^{2}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}(D_{+}).

On
D_{0}:=[(0,T)\times\Omega]\setminus D_{+}
, we have
f(t,x,v)=0
a.e.
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Therefore, it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{D_{0}}\left|(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)(\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}u_{f_{\varepsilon}}\cdot u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})-\nu_{f}\rho_{f}|u_{f}|^{2}\right|

\displaystyle=\int_{D_{0}}\left|(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)(\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}u_{f_{\varepsilon}}\cdot u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})\right|\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x

\displaystyle\leq(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)\int_{D_{0}}\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}|u_{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x

\displaystyle\leq(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)\int_{D_{0}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}\textnormal{d}v\right)\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x

\displaystyle\to(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)\int_{D_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}f\,\textnormal{d}v\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\quad(\because\eqref{eq: v^2})

\displaystyle=0.

We conclude together that

\displaystyle(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)(\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}u_{f_{\varepsilon}}\cdot u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})\to\nu_{f}\rho_{f}|u_{f}|^{2}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega),

and thus the integral in (
3.58
) converges.
∎

3.7.3.
Proof of Proposition
3.20

It is enough to collect the results so far. Altogether, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}2\nu_{f}|v|^{2}f\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\leq-\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})g(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+2\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\nu_{f}d\mathcal{T}_{f}f\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+2\int_{(0,t)\times\Omega}\nu_{f}\rho_{f}|u_{f}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})g(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+2\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}2\nu_{f}|v|^{2}f\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

in other words

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq-\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}(1+|v|^{2})g(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v.

3.8.
The entropy inequality

In this section we prove the entropy inequality is satisfied by
f
.

Proposition 3.23
.

The limit
f
satisfies the following entropy inequality

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}f(t)\log f(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\mathcal{O}}f^{0}\log f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\gamma f\log\gamma f(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\nu_{f}\frac{1}{f\mathcal{T}_{f}}\left|\mathcal{T}_{f}\nabla_{v}f+(v-u_{f})f\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\leq-\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}g\log g(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v.

The proof method is similar as in the previous section. We recall from Lemma
3.12
(namely (
3.27
)) that

(3.61)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}(t)\log f_{\varepsilon}(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\log\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad+4\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&=-\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}g_{\varepsilon}\log g_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)f_{\varepsilon}\left(\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}\right)\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.\end{split}

Through subsequent integration by parts (validated through the pointwise decay (
3.20
)), we can easily check that the above is equivalent to

(3.62)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}(t)\log f_{\varepsilon}(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\log\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\frac{1}{f_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}}\left|\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}+(v-u_{f_{\varepsilon}})f\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&=-\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}g_{\varepsilon}\log g_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\left(\frac{|v-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}}{\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}}-d\right)f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad+\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\left(\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-d\right)f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&=:-R_{1}+R_{2}+R_{3}.\end{split}

The goal is to show that the left-hand side is lower semicontinuous as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, whereas the right-hand side converges.

Let us check each term of the left-hand side first. Using the uniform energy estimates provided by Lemma
3.9
, we can find by classical lower semicontinuity arguments
[
JKO98
, p.9]
that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}f(t)\log f(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}(t)\log f_{\varepsilon}(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

The assumption (
3.4
) on the regularized initial data provides also that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=\int_{\mathcal{O}}f^{0}\log f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

For the trace term
\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\log\gamma f_{\varepsilon}
, we handle it in the same way as in
f_{\varepsilon}\log f_{\varepsilon}
. Namely, we use the energy estimates of Lemma
3.9
, the weak convergence

\displaystyle\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\gamma f\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}(\Sigma_{+}^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v),

and the arguments of
[
JKO98
]
to find

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\gamma f\log\gamma f\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\log\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v.

Regarding the entropy dissipation, we save it for last. Let us move on to the right-hand side. The assumption (
3.4
) on
\{g_{\varepsilon}\}
provides that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}R_{1}:=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}g_{\varepsilon}\log g_{\varepsilon}\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v=\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}g\log g\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v.

We now prove that
R_{2}\to 0
in the limit. Let us begin by discussing the convergence of the following integral:

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\frac{|v-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}}{\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

Integrating with respect to
v
first, we find

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\frac{|v-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}}{\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=\int_{(0,t)\times\Omega}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\frac{dV_{f_{\varepsilon}}}{\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x.

On
D_{+}:=\{\rho_{f}>0\}
, due to (
3.30
), (
3.32
), and (
3.34
), it is evident that the integrand converges pointwise to

(3.63)

\displaystyle(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\frac{dV_{f_{\varepsilon}}}{\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}}\to\frac{\nu_{f}}{\mathcal{T}_{f}}V_{f}=\nu_{f}\rho_{f}.

Then, we observe that the relation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}}\leq\frac{1}{\left[V_{f_{\varepsilon}}\right]_{\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}},\varepsilon}}:=\frac{\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon(1+V_{f_{\varepsilon}})}{V_{f_{\varepsilon}}}\leq\frac{\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}}{V_{f_{\varepsilon}}}+\frac{1}{V_{f_{\varepsilon}}}+1

and the
L^{1}
convergences in (
3.30
), (
3.31
) show that for any measurable set
E\subset(0,T)\times\Omega
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\int_{E}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\frac{dV_{f_{\varepsilon}}}{\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\leq d(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\int_{E}\left(\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}+1+V_{f_{\varepsilon}}\right)\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad\mathscr{L}^{d+1}(E)\downarrow 0.

The above, along with (
3.63
) and Egorov’s theorem (applied in
(0,T)\times\Omega
), prove that

(3.64)

\begin{split}\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\frac{|v-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}}{\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v&=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{(0,t)\times\Omega}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\frac{dV_{f_{\varepsilon}}}{\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\\
&=\int_{(0,t)\times\Omega}\nu_{f}d\rho_{f}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x.\end{split}

To fully obtain the convergence of
R_{2}
, we must show that the following integral converges:

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)df_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=\int_{(0,t)\times\Omega}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)d\rho_{f_{\varepsilon}}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\to\int_{(0,t)\times\Omega}\nu_{f}d\rho_{f}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x,

but this is easily checked using similar methods as before. Combining this with (
3.64
), it follows that
R_{2}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, as desired.

Next we similarly claim that
R_{3}\to 0
. A direct computation shows that

\displaystyle\left|\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-d\right|

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\left|\frac{d+(d+1)|v|+\varepsilon d(1+|v|)^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon(1+|v|))^{2}}\right|\leq\varepsilon\left|d+(d+1)|v|+\varepsilon d(1+|v|)^{2}\right|\leq\varepsilon C_{d}(1+|v|^{2}),

which, combined with the uniform bounds for the energy, gives

\displaystyle|R_{3}|\leq\varepsilon\,C(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}},d)\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}0.

It only remains for us to discuss the entropy dissipation, the final term in the left-hand side of (
3.62
). We need to show that it is lower semicontinuous in the limit. By our arguments so far, the right-hand side of (
3.62
) is convergent in the limit
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, and thus bounded independently of
\varepsilon
. This provides first that the dissipation is bounded:

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\frac{1}{f_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}}\left|\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}+(v-u_{f_{\varepsilon}})f_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{T},

from which we deduce that the sequence

\displaystyle\left\{\sqrt{\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}+(v-u_{f_{\varepsilon}})f_{\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}}}\right\}

is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O})
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. Owing to the pointwise convergence of all terms in the sequence, the weak
L^{2}
-limit can be identified in the same way as in the beginning of the proof of Lemma
3.18
. Since the
L^{2}
-norm is lower semicontinuous with respect to convergence in the weak topology,

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\nu_{f}\frac{1}{f\mathcal{T}_{f}}\left|\mathcal{T}_{f}\nabla_{v}f+(v-u_{f})f\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\frac{1}{f_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}}\left|\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}+(v-u_{f_{\varepsilon}})f_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v,

as desired.

Collecting everything established so far, we have shown that the left-hand side of (
3.62
) is lower semicontinuous whereas the right-hand side is convergent in the limit
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. The assertion of Proposition
3.23
follows.

Remark 3.24
.

One can choose to pass to the limit in (
3.61
) instead. We then obtain using similar methods that

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{O}}f(t)\log f(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v-\int_{\mathcal{O}}f^{0}\log f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{t}}\gamma f\log\gamma f(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad+4\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\nu_{f}\mathcal{T}_{f}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&\leq-\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{t}}g\log g(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+d\int_{(0,t)\times\mathcal{O}}\nu_{f}f\,\textnormal{d}s\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.\end{split}

The passage to the limit of (
3.61
) is simpler than passing to the limit in (
3.62
) and thus we omit the proof of the above identity.

Conclusion.
The results of Section
3.5
,
3.6
,
3.7
, and
3.8
demonstrate that all assertions of Theorem
1.1
hold, thereby completing its proof.

4.
The partial absorption-reflection problem

Compared with the inflow case studied in Section
3
, the partially absorbing-reflecting boundary condition introduces a new mechanism at the boundary: when particles reach
\Sigma_{-}
, a fraction
\theta
of the incoming flux is reflected specularly, while the remaining part is absorbed. This mixture of absorption and reflection modifies the energy and entropy balances by introducing boundary terms weighted by
(1-\theta)
. In particular, one expects the uniform bounds for moments and entropy to weaken as
\theta\uparrow 1
, reflecting the fact that pure reflection preserves the boundary flux. Nevertheless, as we shall see, the fundamental structure of the a priori estimates is still preserved, and the compactness arguments developed in Section
2
continue to apply without essential changes. Our task in this section is therefore to adapt the approximation, renormalization, and compactness framework to this mixed boundary setting and establish the existence of weak solutions under the same physical assumptions on the initial data.

Let us recall:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\nu_{f}\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\mathcal{T}_{f}\nabla_{v}f+(v-u_{f})f\right),\\
f|_{t=0}=f^{0},\\
\gamma f(t,x,v)=\theta\gamma f(t,x,L_{x}v),\quad\forall(t,x,v)\in\Sigma_{-}^{T},\quad\theta\in[0,1),\\
L_{x}v:=v-2(n(x)\cdot v)n(x).\end{cases}

We emphasize once more that the only assumptions on the initial data are in (
1.5
).

In analogy with the previous section, we fix
\varepsilon>0
and consider the following regularized problem

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f_{\varepsilon}=(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}\nabla_{v}f_{\varepsilon}+(\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})f_{\varepsilon}\right),\\
f|_{t=0}=f_{\varepsilon}^{0},\\
\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=\theta\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,L_{x}v)\quad\forall(t,x,v)\in\Sigma_{-}^{T},\end{cases}

in
(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The regularized initial data is assumed to be of class

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\in C_{c}(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}),

and to satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2}+|\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0}|)f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v<+\infty,\\
\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\to f^{0}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}(\mathcal{O}),\\
\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathcal{O}}|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=\int_{\mathcal{O}}|v|^{2}f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v,\\
\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=\int_{\mathcal{O}}f^{0}\log f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.\end{cases}

Similarly, we also consider the corresponding linear counterpart of (
4.1
) as

(4.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}F+v\cdot\nabla_{x}F=(\nu(\varrho,{\bf j},V)+\varepsilon)\left(\left[V\right]_{\varrho,\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\right)\Delta_{v}F+(\nu(\varrho,{\bf j},V)+\varepsilon)\nabla_{v}\cdot\left((\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-\left[{\bf j}\right]_{\varrho,\varepsilon})F\right),\\
F|_{t=0}=f^{0}_{\varepsilon},\\
\gamma F(t,x,v)=\theta\gamma F(t,x,L_{x}v)\quad\forall(t,x,v)\in\Sigma_{-}^{T},\end{cases}

where the coefficients are extracted from (see Section
3.2
)

\displaystyle(\varrho,{\bf j},V)\in\mathscr{X}.

We refer again to
[
Zhu24
, Theorem 1.1]
for the existence, uniqueness, renormalization properties, and weighted estimates for the (bounded) solution
(F,\gamma F)
to (
4.2
). Properly speaking, Zhu’s main result is written only for the purely reflecting case of
\theta=1
, but following that work we note that the cases of
\theta\in[0,1)
can be treated within their framework (see
[
Zhu24
, Sections 2, 5]
).

Remark 4.1
.

It is also worth mentioning that the solution
(F,\gamma F)
to (
4.2
) is nonnegative (in analogue with Lemma
3.3
. Indeed, the solution
(F,\gamma F)
is constructed in
[
Zhu24
, Section 2.2.2]
by approximating (
4.2
) with a sequence of inflow problems (see also the more classical work
[
Car98
, Section 5]
). Since the maximum principle Lemma
3.3
demonstrates that we can guarantee nonnegativity of the solutions to the inflow problem, this carries over to the solution to (
4.2
) in passage to the limit.

Then through the same fixed point argument, we obtain the existence of a weak solution to (
4.1
).

Lemma 4.2
.

There exists a weak and renormalized solution
(f_{\varepsilon},\gamma f_{\varepsilon})
to the regularized system (
4.1
) which satisfies (not necessarily uniformly in
\varepsilon
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}f_{\varepsilon},\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\geq 0;\\
\left<v\right>^{q}f_{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}))\quad\forall q\geq 0,\\
\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v).\end{cases}

Proof.
Since the scheme is the same as that of Section
3.2
we only provide a sketch of proof. We note that the solution to (
4.1
) is realized through a fixed point of the operator

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}:(\varrho,{\bf j},V)\mapsto(\rho_{F},j_{F},V_{F}).

We can then check, in the same way as before, that
\mathscr{R}
is continuous and compact as a map from
\mathscr{X}
into itself. Schaefer’s theorem then yields that
\mathscr{R}
has a fixed point in
\mathscr{X}
, from which we deduce the existence of a solution
(f_{\varepsilon},\gamma f_{\varepsilon})
to (
4.1
).
∎

Then, using the renormalization properties of (
4.1
), we can derive the following uniform estimates in similar manner as that of Lemmas
3.9
,
3.12
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\{f_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
denote the family of solutions to (
4.1
). Then the following estimates hold uniformly in
\varepsilon
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{T},\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{T}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\leq\frac{C_{T}}{1-\theta},\\
\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}(t)\log f_{\varepsilon}(t)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{T},\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{T}}\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\log\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\leq\frac{C_{T}}{1-\theta},\\
\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{T}.\end{cases}

Proof.
We only provide a formal proof, and refer to Lemmas
3.9
and
3.12
for the rigorous derivation of these identities. A direct computation and integrating by parts allow us to find

\displaystyle\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Gamma_{+}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad=-\int_{\Gamma_{-}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\left(2d\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}-2v\cdot(\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})f_{\varepsilon}\right)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

Using the prescribed boundary condition, and also that the reflection map
L_{x}
has unit Jacobian, we observe

\displaystyle-\int_{\Gamma_{-}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{-}\,\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v=\theta\int_{\Gamma_{+}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v.

Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+(1-\theta)\int_{\Gamma_{+}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\left(2d\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}f_{\varepsilon}-2v\cdot(\left[v\right]_{1,\varepsilon}-u_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)})f_{\varepsilon}\right)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

The right-hand side of the above was already estimated in Lemma
3.9
, and from there, applying Grönwall’s lemma yields

(4.3)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{T}.

Using this bound, we can then in turn deduce

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{T}}(1+|v|^{2})\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\leq\frac{C_{T}}{1-\theta}.

In similar nature, we can find the following formal identity for the entropy:

(4.4)

\begin{split}&\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}\log f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+(1-\theta)\int_{\Gamma_{+}}\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\log\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\,(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+4\int_{\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad=\int_{\Gamma_{+}}\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(\theta\log\theta)(n(x)\cdot v)_{+}\,\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v+d\int_{\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad\leq d\int_{\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\quad(\because\theta\log\theta\leq 0).\end{split}

We note that since
\nu\in L^{\infty}
, (
4.3
) allows us to estimate the right-hand side of (
4.4
) as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq(\|\nu\|_{L^{\infty}}+1)C_{T}\quad\forall\varepsilon\in(0,1].

Therefore, we deduce from (
4.4
) and Grönwall’s lemma

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}\log f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{T},

which in turn yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{T}}\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\log\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\leq\frac{C_{T}}{1-\theta},\qquad\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}(\nu_{f_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon)\mathcal{T}_{f_{\varepsilon}}^{(\varepsilon)}|\nabla_{v}\sqrt{f_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{T}.

∎

The estimates of Lemma
4.3
imply through the Dunford–Pettis criteria that we can pass to a subsequence such that there exists a limit
f
satisfying

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}\to f\quad\text{weakly in}\quad L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O}),

\displaystyle\gamma f_{\varepsilon}\to\gamma f\quad\text{weakly in}\quad L^{1}(\Sigma_{+}^{T},(v\cdot n(x))\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v).

We now assume always that we are along this subsequence.

Next, analogously as with Lemma
3.11
, we provide the gain of moments, which is key to obtaining compactness for the temperature.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\{f_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
denote the family of solutions to (
4.1
). Then

\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\int_{(0,T)\times\mathcal{O}}|v|^{3}f_{\varepsilon}(t)\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{T}.

Proof.
We just remark that it is clear from the boundary condition and Lemma
4.3
that
\left\{\gamma f_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}\right\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
also has uniformly bounded second moments. Indeed, changing variables shows

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}|v|^{2}\gamma f_{\varepsilon}|v\cdot n(x)|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle=\theta\int_{\Sigma_{+}^{T}}|v|^{2}\gamma f_{\varepsilon}(v\cdot n(x))_{+}\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\leq\theta C_{\theta,T}.

Therefore, it is enough to apply Lemma
3.10
with
k=2
and in the same setting as with Lemma
3.11
.
∎

We may then provide the analogue of Lemma
3.14
.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any
\varphi\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with subcubic growth, it holds that the averages

\displaystyle\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{\varepsilon}\varphi(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}

are compact in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.

Proof.
Using the renormalization formula, namely the same identity as in (
3.29
), we may apply the velocity averaging Lemma
3.13
to deduce the compactness of the averages

\displaystyle\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{f_{\varepsilon}}{1+\delta f_{\varepsilon}}\varphi(v)\,\textnormal{d}v\right\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}

for each
\delta>0
. Then, in the same way as in the proof of Lemma
3.14
, we deduce using the equiintegrability of
\{f_{\varepsilon}\}
and taking

that the averages

are also compact in

.
∎

Finally, we may apply Proposition
2.1
to deduce that

Lemma 4.6
.

Modulo a subsequence,

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}\to f\quad\text{a.e. and in}\quad L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathcal{O}).

Proof.
The proof is the same as that with Lemma

, and it is a simple application of Proposition
2.1
applied with

Appendix A
Construction of regularized initial and boundary data

In this appendix, we present a construction of regularizations satisfying the assumption (
3.4
).

Lemma A.1
.

Let
(1+|v|^{2}+|\log f^{0}|)f^{0}\in L^{1}_{+}(\mathcal{O})
. Then there exists a family
\{f^{0}_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\subset C_{c}(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

(A.1)

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\to f^{0}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}(\mathcal{O}),

(A.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=\int_{\mathcal{O}}f^{0}\log f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v,

(A.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathcal{O}}|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v=\int_{\mathcal{O}}|v|^{2}f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

Proof.
Step 1:
f^{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.
We assume first that
f^{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, where

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\Omega;L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})):=\{h\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})\,:\,\textnormal{supp}_{v}(h)\text{ is a compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^{d}$}\}.

Fix
r>0
such that
f^{0}(x,v)=0
outside
\Omega\times B_{r}
. Denote
M=\|f^{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})}
. Define
\{f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
to be a sequence of continuous functions such that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\textnormal{supp}(f_{\varepsilon}^{0})\subset\Omega\times B_{r}\quad\forall\varepsilon\in(0,1],\\
f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\to f^{0}\quad\text{a.e. in}\quad\Omega\times B_{r},\\
0\leq f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\leq M.\end{cases}

Then, it follows immediately from the compact
v
-support and the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem that (
A.1
), (
A.2
), (
A.3
) all hold.

Step 2: General case.
Next, we assume only that
f^{0}
satisfies the conditions given in the lemma. Thanks to Step 1, it is enough for us to find a sequence
\{f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\}
such that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),\\
\textnormal{$f_{\varepsilon}^{0}$ satisfies \eqref{eq: INIT L1 conv}, \eqref{eq: INIT ENT conv}, \eqref{eq: INIT EGY conv}}.\end{cases}

Let us adapt some of the arguments in
[
JKO98
]
. For any
\zeta\in(0,1)
, we know there exists a constant
C>0
for which

\displaystyle|\min\{z\log z,0\}|\leq Cz^{\zeta}.

This gives us the following estimate:

(A.4)

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R})}|\min\{f^{0}\log f^{0},0\}|\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad\leq C\int_{\Omega\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R})}(f^{0})^{\zeta}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\\
&\quad\leq C\left(\int_{\Omega\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R})}(1+|v|^{2})f^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\right)^{\zeta}\left(\int_{\Omega\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R})}\frac{\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v}{(1+|v|^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{(1-\zeta)}}}\right)^{1-\zeta}\\
&\quad\leq C_{d}\left(\frac{1}{1+R^{2}}\right)^{\zeta-\frac{d}{2}(1-\zeta)}.\end{split}

Now take

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}^{0}(x,v):=\min\left\{\mathbf{1}_{|v|\leq 1/\varepsilon}f^{0}(x,v),\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right\}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})).

Then
f_{\varepsilon}^{0}
increases to
f^{0}
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. Hence, the monotone convergence theorem immediately implies (
A.1
) and (
A.3
). Moreover,

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathcal{O}}(1+|v|^{2})f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C,

and therefore the same estimate of (
A.4
) gives

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R})}|\min\{f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0},0\}|\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq C_{d}\left(\frac{1}{1+R^{2}}\right)^{\zeta-\frac{d}{2}(1-\zeta)},\quad\forall\zeta\in(0,1).

Consequently, for any
\tau>0
, we may fix
\zeta>\frac{d}{d+2}
and
R
so large that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R})}|\min\{f^{0}\log f^{0},0\}|\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq\tau,\quad\sup_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R})}|\min\{f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0},0\}|\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v\leq\tau.

Now write

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\times B_{R}}\min\{f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0},0\}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v+\int_{\Omega\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R})}\min\{f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0},0\}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathcal{O}}\max\{f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0},0\}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle=:A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}.

For
A_{1}
, we observe that
|\min\{f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0},0\}|\leq 1/e
, the right-hand side of which is integrable over
\Omega\times B_{R}
. By the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, we deduce that

\displaystyle A_{1}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}\int_{\Omega\times B_{R}}\min\{f^{0}\log f^{0},0\}\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v.

For
A_{2}
, our previous estimate gives

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon}A_{2}\leq\tau.

Finally, since
f_{\varepsilon}^{0}
increases to
f^{0}
, we have that
\max\{f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0},0\}
increases to
\max\{f^{0}\log f^{0},0\}
. The monotone convergence theorem yields

\displaystyle A_{3}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\downarrow 0]{}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\max\{f^{0}\log f^{0},0\}.

In conclusion,

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathcal{O}}(f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0}-f^{0}\log f^{0})\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\Omega\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R})}\left(|\min\{f_{\varepsilon}^{0}\log f_{\varepsilon}^{0},0\}|+|\min\{f^{0}\log f^{0},0\}|\right)\,\textnormal{d}x\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\tau.

Since our choice of
\tau>0
was arbitrary, we deduce (
A.2
).
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Assume that
g
is a measurable function defined on
\Sigma^{T}
, and that
(1+|v|^{2}+|\log g|)g\in L^{1}_{+}(\Sigma_{-}^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v)
. Then there exists a sequence
\{g_{\varepsilon}\}\in C_{c}([0,T]\times\partial\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

(A.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}g_{\varepsilon}\to g\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}(\Sigma_{-}^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v),\\
\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}(1+|v|^{2})g_{\varepsilon}|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v=\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}(1+|v|^{2})g|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v,\\
\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}g_{\varepsilon}\log g_{\varepsilon}|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v=\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}g\log g|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v.\end{cases}

Proof.
The proof follows the same lines as Lemma
A.1
, so we only indicate the main modifications. By proceeding as in Step 1 of the previous lemma, it is enough to find a sequence
\{g_{\varepsilon}\}
in
L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\partial\Omega;L^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
which satisfies the conditions in (
A.5
). We set

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v):=\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\;,\;\mathbf{1}_{|v|\leq 1/\varepsilon}g(t,x,v)\right\}.

In this way,
g_{\varepsilon}
increases pointwise to
g
, and thus the monotone convergence theorem shows

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}\to g\quad\text{in}\quad L^{1}(\Sigma_{-}^{T},|n(x)\cdot v|\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v),

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}(1+|v|^{2})g_{\varepsilon}|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\leq\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}(1+|v|^{2})g|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\leq C,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}(1+|v|^{2})g_{\varepsilon}|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v=\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T}}(1+|v|^{2})g|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v.

To complete the proof of (
A.5
) it only remains to obtain the convergence of the entropy. We can obtain as in (
A.4
), that for any
R>0
and
\zeta\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T},|v|\geq R}|\min\{g_{\varepsilon}\log g_{\varepsilon},0\}||n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\left(\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T},|v|\geq R}(1+|v|^{2})g_{\varepsilon}|n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\right)^{\zeta}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T},|v|\geq R}\frac{|n(x)\cdot v|}{(1+|v|^{2})^{\frac{\zeta}{1-\zeta}}}\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\right)^{1-\zeta}

\displaystyle\quad\leq CT\sigma(\partial\Omega)\left(\frac{1}{1+R^{2}}\right)^{\zeta-\frac{(d+1)(1-\zeta)}{2}}.

Thus, for any
\tau>0
, we can then choose
\zeta>\frac{d+1}{d+3}
and set
R
arbitrarily large so that

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T},|v|\geq R}|\min\{g_{\varepsilon}\log g_{\varepsilon},0\}||n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\leq\tau,

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{-}^{T},|v|\geq R}|\min\{g\log g,0\}||n(x)\cdot v|\,\textnormal{d}t\textnormal{d}\sigma\textnormal{d}v\leq\tau.

The remaining parts of the proof are then the same as with Lemma
A.1
.
∎
