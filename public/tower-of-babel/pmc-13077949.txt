Introduction

Missed cardiac syncope is one of the most dangerous diagnostic failures in pediatric emergency medicine. Children presenting with transient loss of consciousness may appear well, yet a small subset harbor lethal arrhythmias or structural heart disease that can lead to sudden death if not recognized. The central challenge for the emergency physician is to rapidly identify these high-risk patients while safely discharging the majority with benign etiologies [1].

Pediatric syncope represents 1–3% of ED visits and up to 6% of adolescent hospitalizations [2, 3]. Although most cases are reflex-mediated or orthostatic, approximately 4% are cardiac and 3% neurologic [4]. Because clinical appearance alone is unreliable, emergency department evaluation must be structured, protocol-driven, and anchored by early ECG screening and risk stratification [5, 6].

Mechanisms and evidence-based framework for pediatric syncope in the ED

Pediatric syncope is a common clinical presentation in ED, with incidence peaking during adolescence and recurrence occurring in up to one-third of patients. Despite this high prevalence, fewer than 5% of cases are caused by life-threatening disease [2, 3]. The lifetime cumulative incidence in children and adolescents is estimated to be between 15% and 25% [7]. It has a high recurrence rate and can significantly disrupt daily activities [8]. The peak incidence occurs during adolescence (12–18 years), coinciding with rapid growth and autonomic instability [2, 9]. The purpose of ED evaluation is not to identify the exact mechanism in every child, but to reliably separate dangerous causes from benign ones using a structured, evidence-based framework.

Pathophysiological mechanisms

Reflex (Vasovagal) Syncope (VVS) is the most common mechanism, resulting from an inappropriate autonomic reflex leading to vasodilation and/or bradycardia, which causes transient cerebral hypoperfusion [8, 10]. It is often triggered by orthostatic stress, pain, or emotion and involves the Bezold–Jarisch reflex, characterized by paradoxical vagal activation [11].

Orthostatic Intolerance and Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome (POTS) are also common. Orthostatic syncope results from an inadequate autonomic response to standing, leading to venous pooling and reduced venous return [12]. POTS, increasingly recognized in adolescents, is defined by at least 3 months of symptoms of chronic orthostatic intolerance AND a sustained excessive heart rate increase (≥ 40 beats/min in adolescents) within 10 min of standing, in the absence of orthostatic hypotension [13, 14].

Cardiogenic Syncope results from a sudden reduction in cardiac output due to structural or electrical heart disease. Although less common in children, it is the most life-threatening cause.

Arrhythmic causes include long QT syndrome, short QT syndrome, Brugada syndrome, Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome (WPW), and catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia. While structural causes include hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and coronary artery anomalies, myocarditis is also a cause of syncope/sudden death [15–17]. Syncope during exertion or without a prodrome strongly suggests a cardiac etiology.

Other causes include neurologic conditions that mimic syncope, such as seizures [17], and metabolic disturbances like hypoglycemia [18]. Psychogenic pseudosyncope is an important consideration in adolescents, presenting as an apparent loss of consciousness without true cerebral hypoperfusion [19]. Gaps in current practice

Despite these guidelines, significant gaps in care persist. There are no universal standards for the timing and communication of ECG interpretation, and the accuracy of interpretation by non-cardiologists is variable [24]. In the ED, patients with syncope frequently undergo extensive, low-yield testing (e.g., head CT, broad lab panels) that is inconsistent with guideline recommendations, leading to increased healthcare costs. Furthermore, outpatient follow-up rates are often low, limiting continuity of care [25].

Diagnostic approach

The diagnostic approach begins with a comprehensive history and physical examination—including orthostatic vital signs, medication review, family history of sudden cardiac death, and detailed characterization of the syncopal event (triggers, prodrome, posture, and recovery)—performed prior to or in parallel with the initial ECG. This step represents the cornerstone of syncope evaluation and enables early identification of high-risk features and appropriate triage pathways, as emphasized in contemporary guideline-based approaches [20–22, 24].

The ECG is the first diagnostic test in all pediatric syncope. No child should be dispositioned without a documented ECG review. The ED physician must interpret and document the 12-lead ECG before disposition, with a pediatric cardiologist over-read within 24–48 h. A standardized recall system should ensure families are promptly contacted if concerning findings arise. High parasternal leads (V1–V2) may be used selectively when Brugada syndrome is suspected, also the long QTc interval must manually verified, as automated algorithms often overestimate QT, especially with U waves [4, 22, 28].

If POTS is suspected (syncope with chronic orthostatic symptoms), perform a standardized 10-minute standing test: after 5 min supine rest with baseline HR/BP, the patient stands while HR and BP are recorded every minute for 10 min, in a quiet setting with minimal movement and no leaning [33–35].

Following this initial assessment, patients are categorized into “presumptive diagnosis,” “suggestive diagnosis,” and “unclassified,” which should be clarified as transitional diagnostic states reflecting the degree of diagnostic certainty rather than formal risk categories, consistent with contemporary risk-stratification frameworks [6, 22, 34].

The “presumptive diagnosis” group includes patients with clinically evident benign etiologies - most commonly reflex syncope, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, or orthostatic hypotension - which can often be diagnosed based on history and examination alone [9, 10, 13]. The “suggestive diagnosis” category includes cases with features concerning for cardiac pathology, prompting further evaluation with echocardiography and ambulatory rhythm monitoring to establish a definitive diagnosis [2, 21, 36]. The “unclassified” group comprises patients with inconclusive initial evaluation, requiring additional targeted testing for less common metabolic, neurological, or psychogenic causes [18, 19, 23].

Laboratory testing should be limited to glucose and pregnancy testing, with additional studies ordered only when clinically indicated. Routine imaging, including chest X-ray and head CT, is not part of standard syncope evaluation [35, 37].

Targeted laboratory testing is recommended over broad panels. A point-of-care blood glucose measurement is essential to rule out hypoglycemia, which can mimic syncope [18]. A pregnancy test is required for all adolescent females due to the physiological changes of pregnancy that can precipitate syncope [37]. Other labs, such as a complete blood count (CBC) to check for anemia or a metabolic panel for electrolyte disturbances, or urine drug/toxins screen should be ordered selectively based on clinical suspicion [38, 39].

More selective testing is reserved for intermediate- or high-risk patients. Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) may be considered conditionally at the bedside to assess for gross structural abnormalities like significant pericardial effusion or severe left ventricular dysfunction, but it is not a routine part of the initial workup [5, 40]. For patients with high-risk features or abnormal ECGs, continuous telemetry monitoring is required [18]. Ambulatory monitors like a Holter monitor (24–48 h) or a Zio patch (up to 14 days), or event monitors/recorders worn for up to 30 days are useful for diagnosing intermittent arrhythmias in patients with recurrent, unexplained episodes [35, 36].

The diagnostic algorithm (Fig. 2) should follow a risk-based sequence. For low-risk patients with a normal ECG and clear vasovagal history, the disposition is typically discharge with education. For intermediate-risk patients (e.g., recurrent episodes, atypical features), further testing like ambulatory monitoring and specialist referral is appropriate. For high-risk patients (e.g., abnormal ECG, exertional syncope), the disposition is admission for inpatient monitoring and urgent specialist consultation. Neurological red flags warrant an EEG and neurology referral to differentiate syncope from seizure [18]. Finally, the algorithm emphasizes iterative reassessment and selective referral, ensuring a dynamic, resource-conscious approach that optimizes diagnostic yield while avoiding unnecessary investigations [24, 26–28].

Common final diagnoses

Analysis of pediatric syncope presentations to the ED consistently demonstrates that reflex and orthostatic etiologies predominate. Vasovagal syncope is the most frequent diagnosis, accounting for 52.5% to 74% of cases, followed by postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) at approximately 13.1% [27, 41, 42]. In contrast, serious etiologies are uncommon, with cardiac causes identified in ~ 4% and neurological causes (primarily seizures) in ~ 3.1% of cases [27].

Benign etiologies (Reflex and orthostatic causes)

Vasovagal syncope and POTS are the most common final diagnoses and are considered benign. In multiple retrospective studies, VVS was the leading cause, identified in over 70% of patients in some cohorts [41, 42]. Although these conditions are not life-threatening, they can cause significant disruption to a child’s life. A quality improvement initiative suggested that for these low-risk patients, adequate history gathering, physical exam, and a baseline ECG are sufficient for diagnosis, and extensive, costly testing does not improve patient outcomes [43].

Serious etiologies (Cardiac and neurological causes)

Although rare, serious causes corresponding to the high-risk categories are rare but clinically critical to identify. Cardiac causes account for approximately 4% of cases, with arrhythmias representing the most common subtype (16.9% of cardiac cases), while inherited channelopathies (e.g., long QT syndrome) and cardiomyopathies are considerably less frequent (3.4% and < 2%, respectively) [27]. Patients with clinical features suggestive of cardiac syncope (as defined by red flags in Sect. Gaps in current practice) require urgent cardiology evaluation, often including hospital admission for continuous monitoring and consideration of advanced therapies (e.g., ablation or device implantation). Conversely, patients with isolated concerning family history but otherwise normal ED evaluation may be safely managed with expedited outpatient cardiology follow-up within 48–72 h, following direct specialist consultation [27]. Neurological etiologies, primarily seizures misclassified as syncope, account for approximately 3.1% of cases. Other rare but important diagnoses—including anemia, trauma, and intracranial hemorrhage—each contribute to less than 1% of presentations [27].

Disposition framework

Current recommendations for addressing pediatric syncope are consensus-based, meaning timelines are derived from clinical convention rather than evidence-based trials [33]. Therefore, careful risk stratification is needed for assessing syncope severity and determining disposition, such as identifying which cases may be managed as an outpatient versus those that require inpatient observation. The disposition decision should be directly linked to the risk tier identified during the initial evaluation.

Low-risk tier disposition

Patients with diagnoses such as vasovagal syncope and POTS are generally classified as low-risk typically safe for ED discharge, they frequently require acute symptom management with parenteral saline in the ED. Furthermore, discharge planning must include comprehensive education on managing their wide array of systemic symptoms (beyond just presyncope) focusing on trigger avoidance, recognition of presyncope symptoms, and counter-pressure maneuvers. Maintaining adequate oral hydration and increasing salt intake has been shown to be an imperative and effective step in treatment, significantly reducing recurrence rates and a direct referral to a specialist or clinic familiar with the multidisciplinary care of POTS [33–35].

Intermediate-risk tier disposition

Disposition for intermediate-risk cases requires systematic evaluation. Observation or admission may be considered if the clinical presentation suggests a strong likelihood of early complications. Otherwise, patients may be discharged with structured follow-up visits, generally within 1–2 weeks, to a specialized syncope clinic or an appropriate specialist (e.g., neurology for suspected seizures, cardiology for recurrent episodes without clear red flags) [33, 34].

High-risk tier disposition

Children who fall into the high-risk tier, such as those with exertional syncope, abnormal ECG findings, or a concerning family history, warrant hospital admission for further targeted testing, continuous monitoring, and urgent cardiology or neurology evaluation [33, 44]. This approach ensures that patients with potentially life-threatening conditions receive prompt and comprehensive inpatient management.

Research agenda

Future research should prioritize the large-scale validation of pediatric syncope clinical decision rules. Current guidelines are largely consensus-based, and there is limited evidence that existing rules can be reliably generalized to safely identify low-risk children for discharge while accurately detecting dangerous etiologies [34]. Prospective studies are needed to substantiate the role of emerging technologies like POCUS in refining risk stratification at the bedside [45]. Given the diverse causes of pediatric syncope, routine testing in low-risk patients is costly and low-yield. Therefore, future studies should focus on the cost-effectiveness of targeted, minimalist diagnostic algorithms that prioritize safety and resource stewardship by forgoing unnecessary laboratory investigations, imaging, and admissions in low-risk patients.

Conclusion

Pediatric syncope must be managed as an emergency-department safety problem. A risk-stratified, ECG-first pathway ensures that children with cardiac or neurologic disease are rapidly identified and admitted while benign cases are safely discharged. Emergency departments should implement standardized syncope pathways based on red flags, ECG findings, and disposition tiers to reduce missed diagnoses and unnecessary hospitalizations.
