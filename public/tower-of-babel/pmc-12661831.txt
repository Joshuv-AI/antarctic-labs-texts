AI as a partner in assessment: generating situational judgment tests with large language models
Authors: Liming Jiang, Fang Luo, Xuetao Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSituational Judgment Tests (SJTs) are important tools for evaluating individual abilities by presenting specific scenarios and assessing the responses. This study explores the potential of Large Language Models (LLMs) in developing SJTs, with emotional regulation as an example. We proposed and evaluated two approaches using ERNIE 4.0, an advanced Chinese LLM: generating new items from scratch (Approach 1) and adapting items from existing examples (Approach 2). The quality of generated items was assessed through expert evaluations and empirical testing with 93 psychology undergraduates and 184 participants from a general population pool. Results indicate that items generated by ERNIE 4.0, particularly new items from Approach 1, met or exceeded the quality of existing tests in both expert evaluations and empirical validations, while adapted items showed some limitations. This research demonstrates LLMs’ potential for developing valid assessments, offering insights for future applications.Keywords: Situational judgment tests, Large language models, Emotional regulation, Assessment, Artificial intelligence

Introduction

Emotional regulation, also known as emotional management, is a critical component of emotional intelligence [1]. It involves processes by which individuals regulate their emotions, including their type, intensity, and duration [2, 3]. This is essential not only for personal well-being and mental health, but also for effective social interactions [2]. In organizational contexts, effective emotional regulation fosters healthier relationships, better communication, and enhanced conflict resolution [3], leading to improved performance and higher job satisfaction [4, 5]. Recognizing its importance across various professions [6–8], emotional regulation has become a critical competency emphasized in selection processes.

Common tools for measuring emotional regulation include self-report scales and situational judgment tests (SJTs) [9]. Self-report scales are susceptible to self-deception and impression management [10]. Individuals with lower ability may also lack awareness of their limitations in this domain [11], leading to inflated self-assessments. For instance, when faced with a statement such as “I am very good at controlling my emotions,” candidates may be inclined to select “strongly agree”. In contrast, SJTs present participants with realistic scenarios and ask them to judge the effectiveness of different responses [12, 13]. This approach minimizes reliance on self-perception and better reflects participants’ true ability [14, 15].

However, the development of SJTs has traditionally been a time-consuming process. Test developers need to gather diverse scenarios and responses from a group of Subject Matter Experts (SMEs) through interviews. Additionally, the risk of item exposure necessitates the continuous creation of new items. Therefore, exploring more efficient methods for developing SJT tests is crucial. In the field of automated item generation (AIG), traditional AIG approaches based on predefined rules or template manipulations [16, 17] are not suitable for the flexible, complex nature of SJTs. More recent deep learning techniques, such as those using recurrent neural networks for item generation [18], require end-to-end training on large datasets. However, these large datasets are often unavailable for psychological assessments aimed at specific constructs.

The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs), a significant advancement in AI, offers a new solution to the development of SJTs. LLMs are deep neural networks that are pretrained on extensive datasets comprising billions of texts from diverse sources including books, articles, websites, and social media conversations [19]. Through this pretraining, LLMs learn language patterns and acquire broad knowledge across various domains, making them highly versatile. As a result, they can perform tasks by generating responses to brief natural language prompts [20, 21]—such as generating scenarios that reflect emotional regulation. Similar to human experts who apply psychometric knowledge to develop assessments for different constructs, LLMs, with their extensive knowledge of psychology and human behavior, are capable to support the development of tests even for novel and low-resource constructs. Furthermore, their exposure to countless real-world situations allows them to produce authentic, typical, and diverse scenarios that fulfill the core requirements of SJTs. Consequently, with a well-defined construct and clear task instructions, LLMs hold potential to generate effective SJTs without depending on predefined templates or existing item banks. Recognizing these advantages, recent studies investigated LLMs’ capability in generating SJTs assessing personality [22, 23], yielding encouraging results. However, important distinctions exist between personality and ability assessments, requiring systematic examination of LLMs’ effectiveness in developing emotional regulation SJTs. Furthermore, while previous studies have conducted detailed analyses on specific personality traits, there is a lack of a general framework to guide LLM-based SJT development.

Thus, this study explores and evaluates the feasibility of LLM-based SJT development for emotional regulation. We propose two approaches: Approach 1 follows the traditional test development process by constructing SJTs from scratch, while Approach 2 adapts existing SJT items to generate new items, akin to item cloning [24, 25]. For each approach, we outline the implementation steps and the prompts provided to the model. In the second phase of this study, we employed ERNIE 4.0, a state-of-the-art Chinese LLM [26], to create items using both approaches. The quality of LLM- generated items was assessed through expert evaluation and empirical testing. In the third phase of this study, psychological counselors were invited to assess the quality of the scenarios and options generated by ERNIE 4.0, addressing the following research questions:

RQ1: How does the quality of scenarios generated by ERNIE 4.0 using the two approaches compare to that of the original SJT scenarios based on expert evaluation?

Two approaches to LLM-based SJT development

Approach 1: developing from scratch

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the traditional development of an SJT involves three key stages, each requiring significant involvement from SMEs. In the new approach, LLMs take over the generation of scenarios and options, shifting the SMEs’ role from content generators to content reviewers, thereby reducing the process to two key stages. Below, we describe each stage.

Two Approaches to LLM-based SJT Development

Stage 1: developing scenarios

To develop scenarios, test developers traditionally use the Critical Incident Method [27], which involves interviewing SMEs to identify key incidents relevant to the target construct [12]. For example, when assessing emotional regulation, test developers may rely on psychological counselors to provide scenarios demonstrating effective or ineffective emotional management from their professional experiences.

In the LLM-based approach, test developers first use specific prompts (Prompt1 in Fig. 1) to guide the LLM in generating scenarios. Prompts provide clear guidelines and specify the desired output form and content [28]. Prompt1 includes Basic Settings and Task-specific Descriptions, as detailed in Fig. 2 (the original text is in Chinese and has been translated into English for this paper). Basic Settings are defined with a system prompt, which establishes consistent guidelines for the SJT development task, outlining the LLM’s role, goal, and constraints. Task-specific Descriptions, provided through a user prompt, detail the requirements for the current output [19]. The configuration of system and user prompts are supported by many LLMs and utilized in many studies [29, 30].

Content of Prompt1

Task-specific Descriptions include several rounds of interaction within the same chat window. The first round’s prompt includes the definition of emotional regulation, the criteria for developing SJT scenarios, and instructions for the LLM to generate scenarios focusing on three emotions: sadness, anger, and fear, which are commonly addressed in existing emotional regulation SJTs [31, 32]. The second and third rounds prompt the LLM to generate new scenarios that differ from previous ones. This setting considers the LLM’s limited text output length and leverages its ability to remember prior interactions, thus improving scenario diversity. In this study, we collected three rounds, generating six scenarios in each round.

Additionally, three tips are given in the first-round prompt, such as avoiding repeated character names in different scenarios. These tips help refine the LLM’s output, based on extensive trials by test developers to find the optimal combination.

After generating scenarios, test developers invite SMEs to evaluate them, alongside existing SJT scenarios based on the following three criteria:

Typicality: how well the scenarios represent the target construct

Typicality: how well the scenarios represent the target construct

Authenticity: how well the scenarios reflect real-world situations

Authenticity: how well the scenarios reflect real-world situations

Diversity: how well the test covers diverse scenarios

Diversity: how well the test covers diverse scenarios

Authenticity and typicality are assessed for individual scenarios, allowing test developers to select the best ones as the final items. Diversity is evaluated at the test level to compare different combinations of scenarios.

Stage 2: developing options and scoring key

Options represent different response behaviors in each scenario, reflecting varying levels of the target construct [33]. Traditionally, SMEs create a range of responses, from most to least effective and select one highly effective, two moderately effective, and one ineffective option [33]. In forced choice SJTs, these options are usually scored as 2, 1, and 0 point, respectively. In Likert-style SJTs, participants rate each option’s effectiveness, with the final score based on the difference between participant and expert ratings.

In the LLM-based approach, test developers use specific prompts (Prompt2 in Fig. 1) to guide the LLM in generating options with varying effectiveness for each scenario. Prompt2 also includes Basic Settings and Task-Specific Descriptions, as detailed in Fig. 3. The Basic Settings remain consistent with the first stage. Task-Specific Descriptions provide the definition of emotional regulation, criteria for developing SJT options, a specific scenario, and instructions for generating options. In this study, the LLM was tasked with creating 5 highly effective, 5 moderately effective, and 5 ineffective options, with two additional tips specified.

Content of Prompt2

SMEs then rank the LLM-generated options from highest to lowest in terms of effectiveness. This approach helps capture subtle distinctions in the options’ effectiveness, thus avoiding the issue of too many options receiving the same score. The final options are selected based on these expert rankings.

Approach 2: developing based on existing tests

When existing tests are available, test developers can efficiently create new items by adapting existing ones, a process often regarded as item cloning [24, 25]. For the LLM-based approach, test developers first utilize Prompt3 (detailed in Fig. 4) which includes Basic Settings and Task-Specific Descriptions. The Basic Settings maintain consistency with Approach 1. The Task-Specific Descriptions involve several rounds of interaction within the same chat window. In the first round, the prompt integrates descriptions from Stages 1 and 2 in Approach 1, including the definition of emotional regulation, the criteria for developing SJT scenarios and options, three example items (one for each emotion), and instructions for the LLM to generate adapted items. The following rounds prompt the LLM to generate more items that differ from previous ones. In this study, we conducted three rounds, each generating six items.

Content of Prompt3

Subsequently, SMEs rate the adapted scenarios and options. The rating of the scenarios follows the same criteria in Approach 1: authenticity, typicality, and diversity. For the rating of options, a Likert scale is used, ranging from “very ineffective” to “very effective.” The experts’ ratings of option effectiveness are then used to determine the scoring key.

Expert evaluation of LLM-generated items

In this section, we engaged psychological counselors as SMEs to evaluate scenarios and options generated by LLM using two approaches and compared them to an existing emotional regulation SJT test, the Situational Test of Emotion Management (STEM). STEM was initially developed by MacCann [31] and later refined by Allen et al. [34] into an 18-item short version covering emotions of sadness, anger, and fear. From this, we selected the two most discriminative items for each emotion to form the original test.

Recognizing the impact of cultural background on emotional regulation [35], we selected ERNIE, an advanced Chinese LLM developed by Baidu [26]. Specifically, we utilized ERNIE 4.0, which was released on March 29, 2024. We set the temperature and top_p hyperparameters to 0.8, which are relatively high within their range of 0 to 1. Temperature controls the randomness, and top_p controls the diversity of the output [35]. Setting these parameters to relatively high values helps increase the creativity and diversity of the generated text.

We collected 18 new scenarios and 15 options for each scenario. Additionally, we adapted 18 scenarios, with each accompanied by four options. In the following sections, we first evaluate the new and adapted scenarios (Sect. 3.1). Subsequently, we evaluate the options, considering the differing numbers of options produced by the two approaches and the varying scoring methods used by experts, thus we conducted separate analyses (Sect. 3.2 and 3.3).

Evaluating new and adapted scenarios (for RQ1 and RQ2)

Scenarios generated by ERNIE 4.0 were mixed with scenarios from the original test and rated by nine psychological counselors. Counselors’ evaluations were based on authenticity and typicality, using a 1 to 5 scale. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for authenticity and typicality on all scenarios is 0.70 and 0.85, indicating high consistency [36]. The average ratings of all the counselors served as the final authenticity and typicality scores for each scenario.

As shown in Table 1, the new scenarios generated by ERNIE 4.0 had the highest average scores for both authenticity and typicality. The adapted scenarios followed, and the original scenarios scored the lowest. Among the 18 new scenarios, five had authenticity scores exceeding the highest of the original scenario, and only one scored below the lowest. For typicality, ten new scenarios surpassed the highest original scores, with none falling below the lowest. In contrast, none of the adapted scenarios exceeded the highest original scores in authenticity or typicality. Three adapted scenarios scored below the lowest original score for authenticity, and one did so for typicality.

Evaluating options in new items (for RQ3)

For each new scenario, ERNIE 4.0 generates 5 highly effective, 5 moderately effective, and 5 ineffective options. These options are randomly shuffled once, and the same shuffled list is presented to all nine psychological counselors, who rank them from most to least effective. Furthermore, the presentation order of the 18 new scenarios in the scale is consistent across all counselors, starting with six sadness scenarios (two generated in each of the three rounds), followed by six anger scenarios, and six fear scenarios.

The ICCs for the rankings of each scenario range from 0.72 to 0.99, with only three scenarios recording ICCs below 0.90, indicating high consistency among the counselors. The mean rankings from counselors are used for effectiveness categorization: the top five are categorized as “highly effective,” the next five as “moderately effective,” and the last five as “ineffective.” To assess the alignment between LLM-generated option effectiveness and expert evaluations, four probabilistic metrics are proposed:

Ph: The probability that a randomly selected option from the five deemed highly effective by ERNIE 4.0 is also considered highly effective by counselors.

Pm: The probability that two randomly selected options from the five deemed moderately effective by ERNIE 4.0 are both considered moderately effective by counselors.

Pm: The probability that two randomly selected options from the five deemed moderately effective by ERNIE 4.0 are both considered moderately effective by counselors.

Pi: The probability that a randomly selected option from the five deemed ineffective by ERNIE 4.0 is also considered as ineffective by counselors.

Pi: The probability that a randomly selected option from the five deemed ineffective by ERNIE 4.0 is also considered as ineffective by counselors.

Pall: The probability that a randomly selected set of four options from the 15 ERNIE-generated options, consisting of one highly effective, two moderately effective, and one ineffective option, is considered as such by experts. Thus, Pall = Ph × Pm × Pi.

Evaluating options in adapted items (for RQ3)

When adapting items, ERNIE 4.0 generates four options for each scenario, consistent with the example items. These options vary in their effectiveness, and ERNIE 4.0 annotates the relative effectiveness of each option. For each item, we identified the most effective option (2 points), two moderately effective options (1 point each), and the least effective option (0 points). Nine psychological counselors then rated these options on a scale from 1 to 5, ranging from highly ineffective to highly effective. The ICC for all ratings was 0.96, indicating high consistency. Based on the mean of the experts’ ratings, we categorized the options as the most effective (2 points), moderately effective (1 point), and the least effective (0 points) deemed by the counselors. We then evaluated the alignment between ERNIE 4.0 and the counselor at each score level.

The results indicated that, of the 18 options ERNIE 4.0 assigned 2 points, 16 were also awarded 2 points by the counselors, while the remaining two were rated 1 point by the counselors. For the options that ERNIE 4.0 rated as 1 point, in 11 out of 18 cases, both options were also judged as 1 point by the counselors. In the remaining seven cases, four included one option rated as 0 points, and three included one option rated as 2 points by the counselors. Of the 18 options ERNIE 4.0 deemed 0 points, 15 were also viewed as 0 points by the counselors, while three were rated as 1 point. Overall, these results indicate ERNIE 4.0’s capability to judge the effectiveness of options in adapting items, though there are few discrepancies.

However, we observed that some adapted items might have overly obvious correct or incorrect options, making the SJT more susceptible to faking [39]. As shown in Fig. 11, contrasting with the original items where moderately effective options were predominantly clustered just below the highly effective options, the effectiveness distribution of moderately effective options in the adapted items is remarkably flat. This lack of differentiation may reduce the discrimination of the test. Moreover, the ineffective options in adapted items received lower scores while the highly effective options in adapted items received higher scores compared to original items. These characteristics suggest that the adapted items may be more susceptible to social desirability bias which could potentially reduce the difficulty of the test.

Distribution of the Effectiveness of Options for Adapted and Original Items

To further investigate this issue, we recruited an additional 48 participants via the Credamo platform to rate the social desirability of both original and adapted item options using a 7-point Likert scale (ICC = 0.99). We then calculated the correlation between average social desirability ratings and experts’ effectiveness ratings. Results revealed a correlation of 0.78 for original items and 0.93 for adapted items. These high correlations indicate that emotional regulation SJT items—even those developed by psychometric experts—are inherently influenced by social desirability. This reflects the nature of the construct itself, as low-level emotional regulation behaviors (e.g., losing one’s temper in public) are typically socially undesirable while high-level emotional regulation behaviors (e.g., calmly negotiating) are typically socially desirable. More importantly, the higher correlation for adapted items indicates that ERNIE 4.0 did not adequately control for social desirability influences, resulting in a situation where participants might be able to identify the “correct” responses based on social desirability cues rather than their emotional regulation abilities. Future studies may guide LLMs to avoid extreme effectiveness by prompting responses within a moderate range (e.g., 2.0–4.0 on a 5-point scale).

Test administration and validation (for RQ4)

Participants and procedures

To compose the final test for our empirical study, we selected items based on experts’ evaluations. For the new test, we identified two items with the highest typicality for each emotion (sadness, anger, and fear). ERNIE 4.0 generated 15 options per scenario, which were then ranked by experts. We chose options ranked 3rd, 7th, 9th, and 13th, corresponding to scores of 2, 1, 1, and 0 points, respectively. Rankings that were too high (e.g., above 3rd) or too low (e.g., below 13th) were excluded to avoid social desirability effects. For the middle scores (1 point), we chose 7th and 9th rankings to ensure clear differentiation from both 3rd (2 points) and 13th (0 points), maintaining the clarity of the scoring system. For the adapted test, we followed a similar method, selecting two high-typicality items for each emotion from the 18 adapted items. The options for the adapted items were scored 2, 1, 1, and 0 based on experts’ rating. Thus, the new items and adapted items, like the original items, were all designed as single-choice questions with four options. In the test, the 18 items were presented in the following order: six sadness scenarios (2 original, 2 new, and 2 adapted), followed by six anger scenarios, and six fear scenarios.

We recruited 93 master’s students majoring in psychology from a university in China (17 males, 76 females, mean age = 26.90, SD = 4.79). Among them, 33 majored in Psychological Counseling and 60 in other areas such as User Experience. Since addressing emotions is an integral part of counseling, emotional regulation is considered a key competency in counselor training [40, 41]. Accordingly, we expected students majoring in Psychological Counseling to perform better on emotional regulation assessments. Additionally, to obtain a more diverse sample and allow for comparisons with the psychology students, we recruited 200 subjects on the Credamo platform. Among them, 14 participants who took less than 300 s to complete the assessment and two participants who failed the attention-check items (e.g., “This question checks if you are answering seriously. Please select 2.“) were rejected. Ultimately, 184 valid subjects remained. The subjects comprised 59 males and 125 females; ages were distributed as follows: under 20 years (7), 21–30 years (69), 31–40 years (83), 41–50 years (14), and 51–60 years (11). Educational backgrounds were below bachelor’s degree (12), bachelor’s degree (123), master’s degree (43), and doctoral degree (6). Subjects were from 28 provinces in China. Sample sizes were determined by assuming a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), a two-tailed significance level of 0.05, and statistical power of 0.80, resulting in an estimated requirement of 64 participants per group. Our actual sample sizes of 93 and 184 exceeded this requirement, ensuring robust statistical analysis for the planned comparisons. Participants gave informed consent before participating in the study and declared that they participated voluntarily.

Both samples completed the original, new, and adapted tests, along with the Wong and Law Emotional Intelligence Scale (WLEIS) [42]. They also completed a brief version of the Big Five personality inventory [43], following MacCann’s examination of the correlation between the STEM test and Big Five personality traits to assess discriminant validity [31]. Additionally, they completed the Depression Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS) [44], due to the association between effective emotional regulation and reduced symptoms of mental health issues [45–47].

To evaluate reliability, we calculated the internal consistency coefficients for the original, new, and adapted tests using McDonald’s ω [48]. For validity, we compared the performance of different samples, with the expectation that students majoring in Psychological Counseling would demonstrate higher emotional regulation. Additionally, we examined the correlations among the total scores of the original, new, and adapted tests, and their relationships with other scales.

Results

Internal consistency

The McDonald’s ω values were 0.41 for Credamo subjects (N = 184) and 0.46 for psychology students (N = 93) on the original test, 0.48 and 0.55 on the new test, and 0.40 and 0.60 on the adapted test. These results indicate that the internal consistency of the new and adapted tests was comparable to, or exceeded, that of the original test. The relatively low internal consistency suggests multiple underlying factors, which is typical for SJTs [49]. Specifically, the scenarios in this study covered a wide range of topics, potentially allowing respondents’ personal experiences to influence their answers.

Additionally, selecting only six items for each test likely contributed to the reduced internal consistency. With a small number of items, the scoring format—selecting the most effective option—may have reduced total score variance, thereby underestimating internal consistency [50]. To further examine this issue, we conducted a supplementary analysis using data from an additional 79 participants recruited via the Credamo platform. Results showed that the McDonald’s ω was 0.69 for 18 new items, and 0.76 for 18 adapted items. These results highlight the importance of item pool size in maintaining adequate reliability in SJT design.

Total Score of Two Samples on the Original, New, and Adapted Tests

To understand the differences in total scores between the two groups on the original, new, and adapted tests, we analyzed the performance of Credamo subjects and psychology students on each item, as shown in Fig. 13. In the new test, psychology students consistently outperformed Credamo subjects on all items. However, in the original and adapted tests, they underperformed on half of the items. Notably, score differences were pronounced in the fourth original item (anger2) and the third adapted item (anger1). The fourth original item depicts a scenario where an individual’s work shifts are changed without prior consultation. Here, 82% of Credamo subjects chose to seek a reasonable explanation, compared to 49% of psychology students. Instead, 43% of psychology students opted to express dissatisfaction directly to the manager. Similarly, in the third adapted item, where an individual’s efforts in a team project are overshadowed by a colleague, 77% of Credamo subjects chose to calmly analyze the situation and seek improvement opportunities, while only 47% of psychology students chose this option, with 49% preferring to address the issue with a leader to seek fairness. These scenarios, centered around workplace injustices and resulting in feelings of anger, highlight the differences between the two groups caused by working experience. Credamo subjects, who mostly possess professional experience, tend to make choices that involve suppressing emotions. In contrast, psychology students, who have not yet entered the workforce, lack such experience and are therefore more likely to choose relatively impulsive solutions. This pattern likely reflects cultural influences on emotional regulation strategies, where emphasis on maintaining social order correlates with higher levels of emotional suppression [51].

Performance of Credamo Subjects and Psychology Students on Each Item

Additionally, these findings suggest that the original test, developed within a Western cultural context, may exhibit cultural specificity, which could persist in the adapted test. This cultural bias might limit the applicability of the test across diverse populations. Adopting Approach 1, where LLMs generate test items without reference to the original items, may yield better results. This is likely because interaction with LLMs in certain language naturally incorporates cultural constraints [52, 53], as LLMs internalize cultural stereotypes shaped by societal value orientations [54].

Convergent and divergent validity

Correlation analyses revealed significant associations between the total scores of the new, original, and adapted tests for both psychology students (Table 5) and Credamo subjects (Table 6). The correlations were of similar magnitude for psychology students. However, for Credamo subjects, the correlation coefficients for the associations between the adapted test and the original test were generally smaller. These results indicate that the convergent validity of the new test is consistent across the two samples, while the adapted test’s convergent validity reaches the level of the new test only among psychology students.

The relationship between SJTs and the self-reported emotional management (WLEIS) demonstrated weak correlations in this study, aligning with previous research [55, 56]. This pattern is consistent with the understanding that SJTs assess knowledge of appropriate responses rather than self-perceived abilities, as self-assessments often lack accuracy [57]. For psychology students, the original, new, and adapted tests all showed positive correlations with WLEIS, but only the new test reached statistical significance. For Credamo subjects, all versions of SJTs demonstrated significant positive correlations with WLEIS scores. Additionally, the original, new, and adapted tests showed weak correlations with DASS for psychology students, while the adapted test demonstrated significant negative correlations with DASS for Credamo subjects. Overall, the correlations between the original, new, and adapted tests with WLEIS and DASS were at similar levels, except that the correlations for Credamo subjects were higher than those for psychology students on the adapted test.

Regarding the relationship between performance on emotional regulation SJTs and personality traits, psychology students showed no significant correlations between any SJT tests and the Big Five factors. MacCann argued that emotional intelligence tests should not strongly correlate with personality traits to be classified as intelligence measures [31]. The results from psychology students in this study align with MacCann’s hypothesis and findings. Among Credamo subjects, similar patterns were observed, with correlations between the original and new tests and personality traits being generally weak. Although the adapted test showed slightly higher correlations, they did not reach a strong level.

The relatively higher correlations between emotional regulation SJTs and personality traits demonstrated by Credamo subjects could be explained by the relationship between emotional regulation strategies and personality traits. Meta-analytic evidence suggests that lower neuroticism and higher levels of extraversion, openness, agreeableness, and conscientiousness are associated with adaptive emotional regulation strategies (e.g., reappraisal and mindfulness) and a reduced use of maladaptive strategies (e.g., avoidance and suppression) [58]. Additionally, Connor-Smith and Flachsbart found that the strength of the relationship between personality traits and emotional regulation strategies may vary across different samples [59]. Thus, the results of this study may reflect that emotional regulation may be more closely related to personality traits in the general population than it is among psychology students.

Discussions

AI is increasingly impacting educational and psychological measurement [60]. This study explores the potential of using LLMs to develop SJT for emotional regulation. In the absence of a structured framework and practical implementation processes for utilizing LLMs in SJT development, we adapted traditional SJT methodologies to accommodate the characteristics of LLMs, presenting two LLM-based approaches. We assessed the quality of items generated by ERNIE 4.0 using these approaches through expert evaluation and empirical testing. The results indicate that the scenarios generated by ERNIE 4.0 surpassed the originals in authenticity, typicality, and diversity. Additionally, the options generated by ERNIE 4.0 closely matched expert ratings in terms of effectiveness. Empirical testing confirmed that the tests created by ERNIE 4.0 aligned well with the psychometric properties of original tests.

Existing research on using LLMs to develop SJTs remains limited and has primarily focused on personality assessment [22, 23]. This study contributes to the emerging literature on LLM-based SJT development mainly in two ways. First, it addresses the development of SJTs targeting an ability construct, which involves distinct considerations compared to personality assessment. For example, our findings highlight significant social desirability bias in LLM-generated items—a concern that is particularly critical in the context of ability assessments. Second, prior work conducted more fine-grained experiments—such as examining the effects of prompt design and temperature settings on content validity [23]—they did not provide a comprehensive framework to guide the item generation process. Therefore, this study complements existing research by extending the application of LLMs to the ability domain and introducing a systematic framework to support future implementations.

It should be noted that the original baseline comprised only six highly discriminative items selected from the full 18-item STEM scale, which may limit the scope of comparison. Moreover, this study focused solely on a relatively advanced Chinese LLM and examined only one psychological construct. Nonetheless, it provides empirical support for the feasibility of using LLMs to develop SJTs. The proposed methodologies, processes, prompt templates, and evaluation metrics could potentially be applied to other constructs and different LLMs. The framework shows potential for adaptation across diverse cultural contexts. Recognizing that constructs such as the judgement of emotional regulation can be culture-dependent, this study was deliberately conducted within a Chinese context. We recommend applying the proposed methodological framework in other cultural settings to facilitate the development of culturally relevant SJT tools.

Comparing the performance of ERNIE 4.0 using two approaches we proposed can provide insights into more efficient LLM application for SJT generation. The new scenarios received higher ratings from counselors for authenticity, typicality, and diversity, outperforming the adapted scenarios. Additionally, the new scenarios demonstrated low similarity to the original scenarios, whereas the adapted scenarios showed higher overall similarity to the original items. This tendency may reflect a generalizable feature of LLMs—their ability to create more diverse and appreciate scenarios when not anchored to specific exemplars.

We observed a declining trend in both authenticity and typicality for the new scenarios as the rounds progressed, whereas the adapted scenarios initially declined before increasing. Furthermore, the similarity between new scenarios within each round was significantly higher than the similarity between scenarios from different rounds. This trend was less pronounced in the adapted scenarios but became more apparent over time. These findings suggest that the adaptation process increasingly resembles the creation of new scenarios as constraints diminish. This further supports the idea that providing examples constrains the quality of scenarios generated by ERNIE 4.0—and potentially by other LLMs as well.

The empirical testing results showed that the new and adapted tests had the same level of internal consistency, indicating that the presence of example items had minimal impact on the consistency of ERNIE 4.0’s item generation. The convergent validity of the new test is consistent across both samples, whereas the adapted test’s convergent validity reaches the same level as the new test only among psychology students. This suggests a lower cross-group consistency of the adapted test compared to the new test. Additionally, psychology students consistently outperformed the general population represented by Credamo subjects on all items in the new test. However, psychology students scored lower than Credamo subjects on half of the items in the adapted test, with the most notable underperformance on items addressing anger caused by unfair treatment in the workplace, similar to items observed in the original test. Although the original items from the STEM were designed for a general population, our findings suggest that certain scenarios may not be suitable for university students, particularly Chinese university students. Therefore, the items generated by ERNIE 4.0 without examples may be more general and thus more appropriate for Chinese university students, while the adapted items may retain the characteristics of example items selected from original test.

Among psychology students, neither the new nor the adapted scenarios showed significant correlations with personality and mental health scales, indicating similar discriminant validity. However, for Credamo subjects, the adapted test generally had higher correlations with these self-report scales, potentially due to greater susceptibility to social desirability effects. Expert evaluation revealed that the adapted items might be more influenced by social desirability bias. While this bias may not significantly affect psychology students, it could impact the general population, as represented by Credamo subjects. These results suggest that a strategy worth considering is to generate a larger number of options and exclude those with the highest and lowest effectiveness.

Overall, this study demonstrates the feasibility of using ERNIE 4.0, a relatively advanced Chinese LLM, to develop SJTs for emotional regulation but also highlights two main limitations. First, when producing five options per effectiveness category for new scenarios, ERNIE 4.0 sometimes confuses highly effective and moderately effective options. Second, when generating four options for each adapted item, ERNIE 4.0 often introduced social desirability bias, and the distinctiveness of option effectiveness in some adapted items was less than ideal. While it remains to be examined whether these tendencies are also present in other LLMs, these findings indicate the importance of expert evaluation and modifications in utilizing LLMs for SJT development.

Despite these limitations, the efficiency gains are substantial. For example, traditional SJT development requires lengthy interviews (often last about 1 h) with multiple experts to identify relevant scenarios, with time requirements increasing exponentially as suitable scenarios become harder to generate. In contrast, LLMs make scenario generation nearly instantaneous, requiring only expert evaluation—approximately one hour in our study. This represents significant savings in time and resources for test developers.

Our work is also connected to ongoing debates about whether LLMs can simulate human cognition. Some researchers argue that LLMs can serve as cognitive models [61], while others contend that the lack of embodiment fundamentally distinguishes LLMs from human cognition [62]. In this study, we employed LLMs to generate emotional regulation SJTs, a process that can be interpreted as simulating the cognitive process of human test developers. The scenarios constructed by LLMs reflect, to some extent, their understanding of emotional regulation. However, human emotional regulation involves not only conceptual knowledge but also experiential feeling, whereas LLMs lack genuine emotion. Whether LLMs can truly “understand” emotional regulation without emotional experience remains an open question, and its implications for test development warrant further exploration.

Limitations and future direction

This study aims to reveal the basic performance of LLM-generated SJTs for emotional regulation, using ERNIE 4.0 as a representative example. Therefore, it does not represent the best possible performance of LLMs in generating SJTs. Nonetheless, this study can serve as a foundation and provide possible directions for future optimization efforts:

Psychometric implications of original test

The original test consisted of only six items, which may be insufficient for robust psychometric evaluation. A notable consequence was low internal consistency. Additionally, the scoring format—selecting the most effective option—may have reduced total score variance, thereby underestimating internal consistency. Future work could expand the original items and explore alternative scoring formats (e.g., Likert-type scales), thereby enhancing reliability.

Generalizability to other LLMs

This study exclusively employed a relatively advanced Chinese LLM. The findings may not be fully generalizable to other LLMs due to differences in model architecture, training data, and language context. Therefore, the conclusions should be interpreted as a proof-of-concept. Future research is needed to examine whether similar patterns and limitations apply across LLMs with varying architectures and scales.

Comparing different setups

This study did not compare different parameters or conditions. For example, we asked ERNIE 4.0 to produce five choices for each effectiveness category, and found it struggled to distinguish between very effective and moderately effective options. This issue may arise from generating too many options, as more options may reduce the clarity of effectiveness differences. Future research could consider reducing the number of options to improve clarity. Additionally, adjusting parameters such as temperature may either improve or reduce the LLMs’ performance in creating SJTs. It is especially insightful to identify which setups have universally optimal values and which vary across different constructs.

Specifying the difficulty levels

This study did not specify difficulty levels for the items, and the empirical testing showed that the items generated by ERNIE 4.0 were too easy, especially the adapted items. Whether this tendency is also present in other LLMs remains to be examined. But future prompts might benefit from explicitly specifying item difficulty during the generation process. Therefore, a key prior question is whether LLMs can accurately evaluate item difficulty levels.

Cultural impact on generalizability

This study was conducted entirely within a Chinese context, including the use of a Chinese LLM, Chinese participants, and Chinese experts. The findings suggest some culturally specific understandings of emotional regulation—for instance, the perceived effectiveness of response options may differ in Western cultural settings. Future research is needed to examine the applicability of the proposed framework across diverse cultural contexts and to assess its validity in capturing culturally relevant behavioral norms.

Conclusion

This study investigates the potential of using LLM for developing SJTs in emotional regulation assessment, using ERNIE 4.0, an advanced Chinese LLM. We found that allowing ERNIE 4.0 to generate items without example items led to higher authenticity, typicality, and diversity. Regardless of whether example items were provided, the generated items achieved satisfactory psychometric properties, with items generated without references showing better cross-group consistency. While these findings may not be generalized to all LLMs, they demonstrate the potential of using LLMs for SJT development. However, ERNIE 4.0 occasionally struggled to distinguish between highly and moderately effective options, and adapted items were susceptible to social desirability bias. These findings highlight the importance of expert evaluation in LLM-assisted test development.
