Orofacial pain and dysfunction: From a TMD-centered discipline to a network of interconnected conditions
Authors: Frank Lobbezoo, Ginger Bousché, Merel C. Verhoeff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The field of Orofacial Pain and Dysfunction (OPD) has evolved from a discipline centered on temporomandibular disorders (TMD) into a broad network of interconnected conditions. This letter highlights the biopsychosocial nature of TMD, its links with headache, bruxism, sleep disorders, and systemic health, and emphasizes the need for interdisciplinary collaboration. As OPD increasingly intersects with medicine, integrating dental and medical expertise becomes essential in education, research, and patient care.

Dear Editor-in-Chief,

The discipline that is now called “Orofacial Pain and Dysfunction” (OPD) has undergone a metamorphosis. In the old days, OPD was known by various names. In the Netherlands, Gnathology and Craniomandibular Dysfunction were commonly used. Regardless of the terminology, individuals and teams active in the field of temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) generally worked within the same classical paradigm: deviations from “normal” occlusal and orofacial anatomical relationships were considered major etiological factors [1]. These factors were thought to cause the classical TMD triad, i.e., pain in the masticatory system, temporomandibular joint sounds, and mandibular movement limitations. Many dental professionals were trained within this paradigm, and some are still active proponents of this now-outdated view.

Over the past decades, evidence has been accumulating that TMD has a multifactorial pathophysiology [2], recognizing its biomedical and psychosocial nature, including lifestyle aspects like smoking, alcohol and caffeine intake, sports performance, and playing a musical instrument. These insights underline the need for interdisciplinary collaboration when assessing and managing individuals with a (possible) TMD. As such, other healthcare professionals alongside dentists are instrumental for good-quality care of TMD patients.

Apart from the current view of TMD as a complex, multifactorial condition, another aspect has even more profoundly changed the OPD discipline, namely the emerging notion that TMD is part of a complex network of interconnected conditions. Without claiming even the slightest completeness, we provide three examples of associations that are noteworthy in the context of the metamorphosing OPD discipline:•Painful TMD is part of the larger umbrella of orofacial pain, which in turn links to headache disorders and even comorbid widespread pain, i.e., pain outside of the head area. Several associations have been described within this cluster of conditions, including the increasingly recognized secondary headache attributed to TMD [3].•Bruxism is a jaw-muscle activity that has traditionally been associated with TMD, as well as mechanical tooth wear. Interestingly, it has been found difficult to provide an evidence base for these clinically plausible associations [4]. Possibly, this is due to the difficulties related to accurately assessing the time-related presence, duration, and magnitude of bruxism, to which end a broad spectrum of techniques is currently employed, some of them with roots in a medical discipline like clinical neurophysiology (e.g., electromyography).•Multiple associations have been described between painful TMD, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), sleep bruxism, and sleep-related gastroesophageal reflux. Interestingly, sleep bruxism has been identified as a possible protective factor for OSA (by reinforcing the patency of the upper airway) and for mitigating the possible negative effects of reflux on the intra-oral hard and soft tissues by increasing salivation through mechanically stimulating the salivary flow [5].

These, and many other network-like associations, have traditionally been studied in otherwise healthy individuals [6]. However, in our ageing society, OPD-related conditions need to be studied in older adults and individuals with health problems like neurological and systemic diseases, cognitive impairments, and psychiatric conditions [7]. Such research will provide healthcare professionals with accurate and effective strategies for the assessment and management of OPD-related conditions in non-healthy individuals, and may yield an even broader view on the OPD discipline.

Clearly, based on the above disquisition, patients with OPD-related conditions require assessment and management by interdisciplinary teams, with close working relationships not only within dentistry, including oral and maxillofacial surgery, but also outside the traditional dental silo, expanding into medicine, where disciplines like neurology, ENT, pulmonology, anesthesiology, rehabilitation medicine, psychiatry, pharmacy, and internal medicine could be involved depending on the specific needs of the individual patient. Ultimately, this underlines our recent plea for a better integration of dentistry and medicine in education, research, and patient care [8].
