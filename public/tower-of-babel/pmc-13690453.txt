Surgical treatment of pulsatile tinnitus arising from sigmoid sinus wall anomalies
Authors: Dong Woo Nam, Jung Kyu Lee, Jae-Jin Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPulsatile tinnitus (PT) caused by sigmoid sinus (SS) wall anomalies, most commonly diverticulum and dehiscence, represents a prevalent and potentially reversible venous etiology. This narrative review summarizes contemporary concepts in diagnosis and treatment and proposes a practical framework for clinical decision-making. The characteristic features of venous-type PT are first outlined, with emphasis on strict clinicoradiologic correlation using high-resolution temporal bone computed tomography and magnetic resonance venography to distinguish SS wall abnormalities from other vascular causes, such as dural arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs), high-riding jugular bulbs, and idiopathic intracranial hypertension. Patient selection, contraindications, and clinical “red flags” are then discussed, with particular attention to scenarios in which transmastoid surgery may be ineffective or pose substantial risk and in which systemic therapy or venous sinus stenting should be prioritized. The main transmastoid strategies include sinus wall resurfacing, tailored re-roofing with autologous bone, and diverticulectomy with multilayer reconstruction. These strategies are compared with endovascular approaches in terms of their technical principles, indications, and reported outcomes. Particular attention is given to reconstructive materials, such as polymethylmethacrylate cement, hydroxyapatite, and bone grafts, with consideration of their short- to midterm performance and the current limitations of evidence regarding long-term durability. Integrating these considerations, a stepwise algorithm is proposed to guide the selection of conservative, surgical, or endovascular treatment based on symptom profiles and imaging findings. In carefully selected patients, these strategies can achieve high rates of PT improvement and meaningful gains in quality of life, while highlighting the need for prospective outcome studies with long-term follow-up.Keywords: Diverticulum, Pulsatile tinnitus, Vascular surgical procedures

1. INTRODUCTION

Tinnitus is an auditory symptom characterized by the conscious perception of sound in the absence of an external stimulus. It can be broadly classified into two categories: (1) non-pulsatile tinnitus, which is associated with ascending auditory pathways, including auditory and non-auditory cortices, and (2) PT, which is the perception of pulse-synchronous sound. Non-pulsatile tinnitus, often referred to as “subjective tinnitus,” is typically linked to functional or pathological changes in the auditory system.1 By contrast, PT is sometimes referred to as “objective tinnitus” or “vascular tinnitus”; however, these terms are not used interchangeably in this review. The term “objective tinnitus” should be reserved for the minority of cases in which an examiner can detect a sound using auscultation or transcanal recording, whereas most cases of venous PT remain subjectively perceived despite having an identifiable structural substrate.2 Three potential mechanisms have been proposed for PT generation: (1) altered vascular hemodynamics resulting in turbulent blood flow and sound transmission; (2) vibration of a dehiscent vascular wall even in the absence of turbulent flow; and (3) abnormal sound perception associated with third-window lesions, such as superior semicircular canal dehiscence.3

Although less frequent than its non-pulsatile counterpart, PT affects approximately 10% of patients with tinnitus, translating to approximately 4% of the general population.4 Its clinical significance lies in the fact that 50% to 85% of cases can be traced to identifiable and potentially treatable structural abnormalities.5,6 These include arterial and venous abnormalities, as well as bony defects that can create third-window lesions. Among these, sigmoid sinus (SS) wall anomalies have emerged as a leading cause of venous-type PT,7,8 including SS diverticulum and dehiscence.9,10 Studies of surgical interventions targeting SS wall anomalies have reported promising outcomes, with complete or near-complete resolution of symptoms in most cases.3,11,12

The surgical approach for PT caused by SS wall anomalies depends on the lesion’s specific anatomy and the surgeon’s expertise. Relatively simple cases of SS dehiscence may require resurfacing alone, whereas diverticula of varying sizes and shapes often require more extensive reconstruction, with particular attention to minimizing the risk of major intraoperative bleeding.13

SS wall anomalies may represent a progressive process rather than separate, static entities. Progression may begin with high venous flow leading to diverticulum formation and increased stress on the surrounding bone. In one longitudinal study, approximately 28.6% of patients showed progression over time.14 This pattern has been reported most commonly in younger women (20-40 years old), often in association with increased intracranial pressure (IICP) or unilateral venous dominance.15 Some cases demonstrate substantial morphological changes, such as diverticular expansion and progressive bone erosion. Transition from dehiscence to diverticulum has also been observed, with marked expansion of the affected wall, further supporting the dynamic nature of these anomalies and their contribution to symptomatic PT.14

Given the importance of SS wall anomalies as a major cause of venous PT and the continued evolution of surgical options, this review reexamines the current understanding of PT caused by SS wall anomalies and summarizes established and emerging surgical approaches. Treatment selection according to the presence of diverticulum, dehiscence, or both is discussed, as well as future research directions concerning the relationships among intracranial pressure, bone erosion, and venous flow abnormalities.

Herein, the term “sinus wall resurfacing” denotes the reconstruction of the bony covering over the SS without intentional narrowing of the venous lumen. By contrast, “re-roofing” refers to reconstruction of the deficient bony covering using an autologous bone chip to restore the physiologic contour of the sinus while preserving luminal patency. The following sections examine the criteria for surgical intervention, preoperative evaluation, and surgical techniques for managing SS wall anomalies, with an emphasis on appropriate procedural selection and optimization of patient outcomes.

2. SURGICAL INDICATIONS

2.1. General indications

Surgical intervention for venous-origin PT should be strongly considered when unilateral symptoms persist and significantly interfere with daily activities or quality of life. In such cases, two critical elements should be confirmed. First, high-resolution temporal bone CT (TBCT) should demonstrate a distinct SS diverticulum or sizable bony dehiscence, typically at the transverse–sigmoid junction or along the course of a dominant sinus. The imaging findings should correlate with the patient’s symptoms and localize to the side of the perceived PT. Second, physical examination findings should support a venous origin, strengthening the inference that the SS anomaly is responsible for the sound.

Although TBCT, audiological evaluation, and physical examination are crucial for confirming the diagnosis, additional imaging with brain magnetic resonance imaging with angiography (MRI/A) or CT angiography is essential to exclude coexisting vascular pathologies, such as dural arteriovenous fistula (AVF), paraganglioma, aberrant internal carotid artery, or other arteriovenous anomalies.16,17 This comprehensive imaging also provides an overview of venous drainage patterns and other intracranial or vascular abnormalities that may influence surgical planning. If no coexisting vascular lesion is identified and the clinical and imaging findings conclusively implicate an SS abnormality as the PT source, surgical treatment can provide symptomatic relief in most cases.

2.2. Patient selection, contraindications, and clinical red flags

Although SS wall reconstruction is highly effective in carefully selected patients, it is not universally indicated. Surgery is generally recommended for patients with (1) unilateral, venous-type PT that significantly impairs quality of life; (2) clear radiologic evidence of a focal SS diverticulum or dehiscence that closely correlates with the side and character of the symptom; and (3) a positive bedside maneuver or test, such as ipsilateral jugular compression, head rotation, or the water occlusion test, supporting a venous origin.

Several scenarios warrant caution or constitute relative contraindications. First, patients with uncontrolled idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH), often women with obesity and papilledema or visual disturbances, may experience PT as a secondary manifestation of globally elevated intracranial pressure. In such cases, transmastoid decompression alone does not address the primary disease and may worsen venous outflow obstruction. These patients should undergo ophthalmologic assessment and formal intracranial pressure evaluation; weight reduction, acetazolamide, cerebrospinal fluid diversion, or venous sinus stenting may be prioritized before or instead of transmastoid surgery. Second, when the affected sinus is markedly dominant and contralateral venous drainage is hypoplastic or aplastic, excessive sinus compression could precipitate intracranial pressure elevation. In these patients, techniques that preserve luminal caliber, such as tailored re-roofing (TRR) with autologous bone or endovascular stenting, are generally preferred over aggressive compression or bulky resurfacing that narrows the sinus. Third, high-risk alternative diagnoses, such as dural AVF, dehiscent high jugular bulb (HJB), or glomus tumor, must be rigorously excluded using MRI/A or CT angiography (CTA). When such lesions are identified, treatment should follow disease-specific algorithms rather than routine SS wall reconstruction. Finally, surgery is discouraged in patients with poor radiologic–clinical correlation, minimal functional impairment, or unrealistic expectations despite thorough preoperative counseling.

3. PREOPERATIVE EVALUATION

Preoperative evaluation for suspected venous-origin PT combines audiometric testing, physical examination, and advanced imaging to establish the role of an SS lesion and its correlation with the patient’s symptoms.

Audiometry often reveals pseudo-low-frequency hearing loss on pure-tone audiometry, most pronounced at 250 and 500 Hz.18,19 This apparent hearing deficit does not result from cochlear dysfunction but instead reflects masking by the low-frequency venous hum. Restoration of normal thresholds during audiometry with ipsilateral jugular vein compression implicates the venous anomaly as the masking source and supports consideration of surgical intervention.20

Physical examination is also key in preoperative evaluation. Tinnitus relief with ipsilateral internal jugular vein compression, which transiently reduces venous flow and often decreases or eliminates the pulsatile sound, strongly suggests a venous origin. Head rotation can also aid diagnosis: rotation to the contralesional side typically increases venous return and intensifies PT, whereas ipsilesional rotation transiently reduces venous return and attenuates PT.21 Additionally, the water occlusion test, in which the external auditory canal is filled with water to restrict tympanic membrane motion, can provide further evidence of a venous sinus origin if PT is reduced or eliminated during the test.22,23

Imaging is essential for identifying the anatomical and physiological factors contributing to PT. TBCT is the primary modality, as it provides detailed visualization of the sinus walls and mastoid air cells. Bony erosion, focal diverticulum formation, or extensive thinning can indicate potential sites of abnormal venous sound generation.24 Contrast-enhanced TBCT may provide additional information about bilateral venous drainage, helping the surgeon anticipate postoperative IICP, particularly in patients with SS wall anomalies involving a markedly dominant sinus and limited contralateral venous return. MRI/A or MR venography complements TBCT by identifying dural AVFs, sinus thrombosis, and imaging features associated with intracranial hypertension that require careful preoperative evaluation. MRI-based flow studies can quantify velocity changes in the transverse sinus (TS) and SS and demonstrate high-velocity flow disturbances.25–27

Careful preoperative assessment of vascular fragility and the mastoid cortex is also important for minimizing surgical risk. Identification of mastoid cortex defects or direct contact between subcutaneous tissues and the SS can guide technical modifications, facilitate safe dissection, and reduce the risk of major bleeding that might preclude complete reconstruction of the bony defect and restoration of normal venous anatomy. Integration of these findings allows a tailored surgical plan to be developed for each anatomical defect, including an assessment of the expected benefits and the likelihood of resolving or substantially reducing PT.

4. SURGICAL MANAGEMENT

4.1. Surgical management of isolated SS diverticulum

In patients whose PT originates from an isolated SS diverticulum, the lesion is generally located at or near the transverse–sigmoid junction, where an acute curvature in the venous pathway may direct outward pressure from blood flow toward the overlying cortical mastoid bone. When an outpouching diverticulum extends into the surrounding mastoid air cells or erodes the mastoid cortex, turbulent venous flow may develop, and the resulting sound can be transmitted toward the middle ear and perceived as PT. Middle-aged women with well-pneumatized mastoids and marked unilateral venous dominance may be more susceptible to these abnormalities, possibly because well-aerated and relatively thin mastoid air cells are more vulnerable to pressure generated by dominant venous flow.

Surgical treatment of an SS diverticulum has two main objectives. First, reduction or removal of the diverticulum eliminates a potential source of turbulence. Second, reinforcement of the bony sinus wall reduces the risk of recurrence and decreases transmission of low-frequency sound toward the inner ear (Fig. 1). Bone chips or bone dust were traditionally used to reinforce the sinus wall; however, concerns about material durability and symptom recurrence in some cases have led to the greater use of complete surgical exposure of the diverticulum followed by resurfacing with bone cement (Fig. 2).3,28 The procedure typically uses a transmastoid approach, with careful exposure of the mastoid cortex until the entire diverticulum is visible. The surgical field should be sufficiently exposed to permit precise placement of hemostatic materials and manipulation of the sinus wall while minimizing the risk of unintended bleeding. Diamond burrs are recommended to reduce the risk of injury to the diverticulum or adjacent sinus wall during drilling, particularly when the overlying bone is markedly thinned. When the affected area shows wall thinning or partial erosion through the external mastoid cortex, a meticulous technique must be used to preserve sinus stability during tissue elevation and bone removal. The authors prefer to leave a small soft-tissue island over the exposed diverticular wall until the end of the procedure to reduce the risk of substantial bleeding when the diverticular wall is exposed through the mastoid cortex and is in contact with the periosteal layer.

After adequate exposure, the venous diverticulum can be reshaped, often with the application of a fibrinogen/thrombin-based collagen fleece, such as TachoComb® or TachoSil®, to control bleeding and reduce the bulging diverticulum to the normal contour of the adjacent sinus wall. A second layer of structural reinforcement is then commonly added. Despite the reported effectiveness of autologous bone dust or fascia, polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) or hydroxyapatite cement (HAC) is preferred in the authors’ practice because of its durability and ability to reconstruct the sinus to the intended contour. This additional support strengthens the bony acoustic barrier and helps prevent recurrence of the diverticulum. The procedure concludes with standard mastoid closure, followed by an early postoperative hearing assessment. Complete resolution or marked improvement in PT may be apparent immediately after surgery; however, temporary middle-ear fluid can affect the intensity of residual tinnitus for several weeks postoperatively.

4.2. Surgical management of SS dehiscence

SS dehiscence poses different technical challenges from an isolated SS diverticulum because several anatomical factors must be considered when determining the extent of reconstruction. This defect is particularly important when the sinus is markedly dominant and carries a large proportion of intracranial venous return. SS dehiscence and diverticula more commonly occur on the right side, consistent with the greater prevalence of right-sided dominance in the venous drainage system.18,29,30 Even a relatively small bony defect in a dominant sinus can generate substantial turbulence, producing clearly audible PT that is synchronous with the heartbeat. Patients commonly report a direct relationship between head position and tinnitus intensity, reflecting the effect of subtle postural changes on venous flow through the high-flow sinus.

Earlier approaches to SS dehiscence in a dominant sinus included placement of bone chips over the defect.31,32 This technique was intended to reduce venous flow by compressing the dominant SS but had inherent limitations, including residual dehiscence and postoperative IICP. Other investigators who used hydroxyapatite bone substitute or autologous bone pâté reported successful postoperative control of PT.24 Because incomplete symptom control or recurrence remains a concern, bone cement has increasingly been used to reconstruct the dehiscent area.3,9,28 Although these approaches have generally achieved favorable postoperative outcomes, incomplete resolution can occur when an adjacent broad-based diverticulum is not addressed. Accordingly, the authors’ current practice has evolved toward meticulous cortical mastoidectomy with complete exposure of the affected area, reduction of any adjacent broad-based diverticulum to eliminate a potential coexisting source of turbulent flow, and reconstruction with bone cement (eg, PMMA) to create a robust “acoustic barrier” over the repaired sinus wall (Fig. 3). Compared with conventional resurfacing of the bony dehiscence with or without bone–chip onlay, this more comprehensive strategy of diverticulum reduction plus bone-cement reconstruction may provide greater stability, more reliable symptom improvement, and a lower risk of recurrence.

The procedure typically begins with a retroauricular incision, periosteal flap elevation, and mastoidectomy to expose the entire margin of the bony dehiscence. After the complete circumference of the dehiscent zone has been visualized, any adjacent broad-based diverticulum identified on preoperative CT is then fully skeletonized for manual reduction. Diverticulum reduction may involve careful folding or patching, and bleeding risk can be reduced by precise placement of fibrinogen/thrombin-based collagen fleece before manual compression. Bone cement is subsequently applied over both the bony dehiscence and the adjacent diverticulum to restore a firm bony barrier, reduce sound transmission to the middle and inner ears by restoring the sinus contour, and limit transmission or resonance through the mastoid cavity (Fig. 4).

A Chinese group has introduced alternative approaches to SS dehiscence repair, including a method using local anesthesia, limited surgical exposure, and mechanical compression of the SS.33 This technique facilitates intraoperative adjustment of the degree of compression based on real-time patient feedback. The investigators reported that reducing the cross-sectional area of the sinus by approximately 54% was associated with symptom relief, particularly in patients with SS enlargement. This minimally invasive approach may be advantageous in selected patients, particularly those with limited tolerance for general anesthesia or extensive mastoid surgery. The same group also described dehiscence resurfacing without diverticulum reduction using intraoperative microphone monitoring under general anesthesia.11 Although the optimal surgical approach for SS dehiscence remains debated, particularly when an adjacent broad-based diverticulum is present, most recent studies have reported favorable outcomes after SS resurfacing in appropriately selected patients. Collaboration among otologists, neurosurgeons, and interventional neuroradiologists remains important as advances in imaging, reconstructive materials, and operative techniques continue to refine treatment strategies.

4.3. Technical considerations

Management of technical challenges and prevention of complications are critical during diverticulum reduction and dehiscence repair. Bleeding control is particularly important because a thin, exposed sinus wall can tear during aggressive drilling or even during periosteal flap elevation in patients with mastoid cortical erosion. Fibrinogen/thrombin-based collagen fleece, such as TachoComb® or TachoSil®, is commonly used to control small tears and allow repair completion. In cases of more substantial venous injury, direct pressure combined with layered hemostatic materials can usually control bleeding without the need for emergency sinus ligation.

Long-term surgical success depends not only on complete defect coverage but also on selection of a durable reconstructive material that can withstand continuous venous blood flow in a dominant SS. PMMA and HAC are often used because they provide structural stability, resist deterioration or displacement under pressure, and can be molded to match the normal contour of the SS.28,34 Short- to mid-term follow-up series (typically 12-36 months) have demonstrated sustained symptom control and stable radiologic appearance without evidence of cement fragmentation or migration.13,32 However, systematic long-term (>5-10 years) imaging data for temporal-bone applications remain limited, and much of the confidence in PMMA durability is extrapolated from its long-standing use in orthopedic and neurosurgical procedures.3 Continued surveillance and prospective registries are important for clarifying very long-term outcomes and identifying rare complications, such as delayed infection or material degradation, if they occur.

Complete repair of the entire affected circumference is also essential; untreated margins or an unrecognized adjacent diverticulum may allow turbulent flow or sound transmission to persist or recur. Surgeons should also assess contralateral venous drainage, particularly when the ipsilateral sinus is markedly dominant and critical to overall intracranial venous outflow. Excessive narrowing of this pathway can result in IICP or compensatory elevation of venous pressure on the contralateral side.

After surgery, a measurable decrease or resolution of previously identified pseudo-low-frequency hearing loss on audiometry may indicate successful reduction of the pulsatile masking effect.20 Endovascular procedures, such as coiling of SS diverticula or stenting of stenotic segments, may be considered when transmastoid surgery is not recommended or the lesion is not readily accessible.35 As noted above, some surgeons use local anesthesia or limited-incision approaches to assess symptom reduction intraoperatively, whereas others prefer complete mastoid exposure under general anesthesia to thoroughly identify and safely address all potential anatomical sources of PT.

4.4. Comparative overview of surgical and endovascular strategies

Transmastoid sinus wall resurfacing, with or without diverticulum reduction, has become the most widely adopted surgical strategy for SS wall anomalies.32 Its main advantages include direct visualization of the lesion, the ability to address both dehiscence and a coexisting diverticulum, and the creation of a robust bony barrier that reduces sound transmission.3 When performed with meticulous cortical mastoidectomy and complete circumferential exposure, resurfacing with PMMA or HAC offers high rates of symptom resolution and low recurrence in the short- to mid-term follow-up.13

TRR has recently been introduced as an alternative transmastoid technique to restore a physiologic bony “roof” over the sinus using an autologous bone chip rather than synthetic cement.32 In TRR, the thinned cortical bone is carefully elevated, reshaped, and replaced over the exposed sinus after diverticulum reduction.32 This approach has several theoretical advantages: it avoids intraluminal compression, eliminates concerns about exothermic reactions during PMMA polymerization, reduces the amount of foreign material, and may shorten the operative time.32 Early series suggest that TRR provides at least comparable, and in some cases, superior, symptom relief relative to cement-based reconstruction, although larger cohorts and long-term data are still needed.32

Pure diverticulectomy with sinus preservation, performed without formal resurfacing, has been described but is less commonly used as a stand-alone procedure. The absence of a reinforced bony barrier raises concern about recurrence, particularly in patients with underlying TS–SS stenosis or IIH in whom the driving hemodynamic forces remain.3 Thus, most contemporary protocols incorporate multilayer reconstruction after diverticulum reduction, using either fascia and cement or autologous bone.

Endovascular approaches play a complementary role. Venous sinus stenting is primarily indicated for patients with TS–SS stenosis and a significant trans-stenotic pressure gradient, particularly when accompanied by IIH symptoms such as headache or visual obscurations.36 In such patients, stenting can improve venous outflow, reduce intracranial pressure, and often abolish PT without the need for transmastoid surgery. Stenting may also be considered in patients with unfavorable mastoid anatomy, medical contraindications to general anesthesia, or lesions that are not readily accessible through a transmastoid route. However, the need for long-term antiplatelet therapy and the low but clinically relevant risk of in-stent thrombosis must be weighed against the potential benefits.37

Taken together, these options can be incorporated into a treatment algorithm in which patients with isolated, anatomically confined SS wall anomalies, normal intracranial pressure, and no significant venous stenosis are primarily managed with transmastoid reconstruction (resurfacing or TRR), whereas those with predominant hemodynamic abnormalities, such as significant venous stenosis or uncontrolled IIH, are better suited to an endovascular-first strategy. Individualized decision-making should incorporate symptom severity, imaging findings, venous dominance, and patient comorbidities.

5. FURTHER CONSIDERATIONS

Although SS wall anomalies are the primary focus of this review, clinicians should remain vigilant for other vascular conditions that may mimic or coexist with SS anomalies and cause similar PT symptoms. In particular, HJB abnormalities and dural AVFs require distinct diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

An HJB with dehiscence or diverticulum, as well as a glomus tumor, can underlie PT and therefore warrants thorough otoendoscopic examination and radiologic evaluation.34,38 The mere presence of an HJB does not establish it as the cause of PT because this anatomical variant is often found in individuals without PT; therefore, surgical intervention should be considered only after other pathologies have been excluded. A reddish or bluish vascular mass in the middle ear or visible pulsation of the tympanic membrane or tumor should raise suspicion for a glomus tympanicum or jugulotympanicum tumor.38 Treatment options include surgery, radiation therapy, and observation. The choice depends on the lesion location and size. Some glomus tumors are highly vascular and may require preoperative embolization, underscoring the importance of precise imaging diagnosis.

Dural AVF is another important cause of PT, and brain MRI/A (or CTA) should be considered as part of the routine diagnostic evaluation, even when no specific high-risk finding is apparent. Dural AVFs can generate pulse-synchronous sounds because of high-velocity blood flow at the fistulous site,30,39 and prompt diagnosis is important even when PT is the sole presenting symptom.16 Once a dural AVF has been confirmed, the decision to treat depends on the patient’s symptoms, risk of intracranial hemorrhage, and lesion characteristics. Treatment options include endovascular interventions such as embolization.40 When necessary, a multimodal approach incorporating surgical ligation or stereotactic radiosurgery can be considered. Surgery is generally indicated when endovascular treatment has failed or is not feasible, particularly for lesions involving the floor of the anterior cranial fossa.41 Stereotactic radiosurgery is often used for low-grade (Borden type I) dural AVFs with persistent symptoms, such as PT, in patients with medical comorbidities or anatomical conditions unfavorable for other interventions, or as salvage therapy for incompletely treated lesions.42 Therefore, comprehensive imaging of the arterial and venous systems is fundamental to accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment planning for PT.

6. CONCLUSION

From a practical standpoint, this review supports an algorithmic approach to SS-related PT. Patients with anatomically confined SS diverticulum or dehiscence, normal intracranial pressure, and no significant TS–SS stenosis are ideal candidates for transmastoid reconstruction, either conventional sinus wall resurfacing with cement or TRR with autologous bone. Conversely, patients with prominent hemodynamic abnormalities, such as a marked trans-stenotic pressure gradient or uncontrolled IIH, are more appropriately managed with venous sinus stenting as first-line therapy, with transmastoid procedures reserved for residual symptoms or coexisting focal wall defects. This stratification helps match the mechanism and invasiveness of each intervention to the underlying pathophysiology.

For patients in whom SS anomalies are considered the primary cause of PT, surgical intervention, including diverticulum reduction, dehiscence resurfacing, or a combination of these approaches, can eliminate or substantially improve symptoms. This review highlights that diverticulum reduction with fibrinogen/thrombin-based materials followed by bone cement reconstruction has yielded more durable outcomes than earlier techniques using bone chips alone. For dehiscence, complete exposure of the affected area and comprehensive reconstruction of any adjacent diverticulum appear to be most effective in preventing symptom recurrence. Operative techniques continue to evolve alongside advances in the understanding of venous hemodynamics, imaging modalities, and biomaterials. Further collaborative studies are needed to establish consensus on diagnostic algorithms, including comprehensive radiologic workup protocols, and on optimal surgical approaches. Standardized treatment-selection criteria and refined operative techniques may help ensure that consistent, evidence-based care is provided for patients with SS-related PT.
