Understanding Patient Anxiety and Satisfaction in Stage I and Stage II Dental Implant Surgery: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Pooja Mohan Pharne, Laxman Singh Kaira, Virag Srivastava, Neelam Vijaywargiya, Ajay Kumar Pillai, Sahana Shivakumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Dental implant procedures are typically performed in two stages—Stage I (implant placement) and Stage II (abutment connection). Understanding patient experiences during these stages is essential for improving clinical care and reducing perioperative anxiety.Aim:To evaluate patient anxiety, perceived pain, and satisfaction during Stage I and Stage II dental implant surgery using a Likert scale-based scoring system.Materials and Methods:A prospective observational study was conducted on 40 adult patients undergoing both stages of implant surgery. A 5-point Likert scale questionnaire assessed anxiety, pain, communication, and overall satisfaction postoperatively for each stage. Data were analyzed using paired t-tests.Results:Patients reported significantly higher anxiety and pain during Stage I (mean anxiety = 4.3; pain = 4.0) than Stage II (mean anxiety = 2.5; pain = 2.1). Satisfaction with clinician communication remained consistently high across both stages.Conclusion:Stage I implant surgery induces more anxiety and discomfort. Preoperative counseling and supportive communication are vital to enhancing patient experience.

INTRODUCTION

Dental implant surgery has revolutionized the rehabilitation of edentulous patients, offering superior functional and aesthetic outcomes compared to conventional prostheses. Implant therapy typically involves a staged surgical approach, with Stage I encompassing implant placement and Stage II involving abutment connection and prosthetic rehabilitation. While clinical outcomes of implant therapy are well documented, the psychosocial experiences of patients—particularly anxiety and satisfaction across different surgical stages—remain underexplored.

Anxiety in dental implant patients can be influenced by multiple factors, including fear of pain, surgical complexity, cost implications, and the perceived permanence of the procedure. Previous studies have shown that preoperative anxiety can affect physiological responses and perceived pain, potentially influencing surgical outcomes and patient cooperation during both Stage I and Stage II interventions.[1] Furthermore, satisfaction with implant treatment is multifactorial, influenced not only by prosthetic function and aesthetics but also by the intraoperative experience and the quality of communication with the dental team.[2]

Stage I surgery, being invasive and associated with osseointegration uncertainty, may evoke higher anxiety than Stage II, which is perceived as less invasive. However, satisfaction post Stage II, when the functional benefits are realized, often shapes the overall patient perception of treatment success.[3] This qualitative study aims to explore the lived experiences of patients undergoing Stage I and Stage II dental implant surgeries, with a focus on identifying factors contributing to anxiety and satisfaction at each stage.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective observational study aimed to quantitatively assess patient-reported anxiety, pain, and satisfaction during Stage I (implant placement) and Stage II (abutment connection) of dental implant surgery.

A purposive sampling strategy was adopted to enroll 40 systemically healthy adult patients aged 25 to 70 years, who had undergone both stages of implant surgery within the previous 3-12 months. Patients with diagnosed psychiatric disorders, those on anxiolytic medications, or those who experienced intraoperative complications or implant failures were excluded to minimize bias in psychological response reporting.

Data were collected using a prevalidated, self-administered questionnaire designed on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). The questionnaire consisted of three domains, (i) anxiety (4 items), (ii) perceived pain (2 items), and (iii) satisfaction (4 items), separately for Stage I and Stage II procedures. Participants rated their experiences retrospectively for both stages during a single follow-up visit.

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize mean scores and standard deviations. Paired t-tests were applied to compare the mean domain scores between Stage I and Stage II surgeries. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

The present study assessed patient anxiety, perceived pain, and satisfaction during Stage I and Stage II of dental implant surgery using a Likert scale-based quantitative approach. The findings indicate a statistically significant difference in patient-reported experiences between the two stages, with Stage I being associated with higher levels of anxiety (mean = 4.3) and pain perception (mean = 4.0) compared to Stage II (anxiety mean = 2.5, pain mean = 2.1). This aligns with previous literature suggesting that surgical invasiveness and duration strongly influence patient apprehension and perceived discomfort during implant placement procedures.[4]

Stage II, often involving minimal soft tissue manipulation, elicited lower anxiety and discomfort scores, which could be attributed to patients’ prior exposure to the surgical environment and familiarity with the procedure. Similar trends have been reported in longitudinal implant therapy studies, where patients expressed greater confidence during subsequent stages of treatment.[5] The increased overall satisfaction and willingness to recommend implant surgery after Stage II (mean scores: 4.5 and 4.6, respectively) reflect a positive retrospective assessment of their treatment journey.

Interestingly, communication and clarity of instructions received consistently high scores across both stages. This reinforces the role of dentist-patient communication in managing expectations and reducing anxiety, as emphasized by Pjetursson et al.[6] Despite the significant differences in pain and anxiety, satisfaction with the clinician’s explanation and overall interaction remained steady, suggesting that interpersonal factors may buffer procedural stress.

The findings underscore the importance of targeted preoperative counseling and individualized anxiety management, especially before Stage I. Using structured patient feedback through Likert scales enables dental practitioners to better understand patient psychology during implant therapy and improve perioperative care strategies. Further multicenter studies with larger sample sizes and qualitative triangulation are warranted to generalize these findings and refine patient experience metrics.

CONCLUSION

This study concludes that Stage I dental implant surgery induces significantly more anxiety, pain, and postoperative discomfort than Stage II. Despite these differences, satisfaction with clinician communication remained consistently high, emphasizing the importance of provider interaction. Overall satisfaction was higher after Stage II, reflecting patients’ appreciation of functional outcomes. However, the study’s limitations include a small sample size and retrospective self-reporting, which may introduce recall bias. Future studies with larger cohorts and qualitative interviews are recommended to validate and enrich these findings.
