Exposure to clinical decision support and training increases primary care clinician confidence in managing cognitive impairment care but not confidence to diagnose
Authors: Meghan M JaKa, Rebecca C Rossom, Bethany Crouse, A Lauren Crain, Sally K Gustafson, Heidi L Ekstrom, Laura J Zibley, Soo Borson, Patrick J O’Connor, Ann M Werner, Deepa Appana, Thomas L von Sternberg, Leah R Hanson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive impairment (CI) is projected to significantly increase as the population ages. While primary care clinicians are often the first point of contact for patients with cognitive decline, many clinicians report significant barriers to addressing CI in primary care, including lack of time, lack of established workflow, and low confidence in diagnosing and managing CI.MethodsTo address these barriers, a CI clinician decision support system (CI-CDSS) was developed and is being tested in a cluster randomized, controlled clinical trial in 38 primary care clinics. A secondary aim of the study was to assess whether the CI-CDSS increased clinician confidence in CI diagnosis and management, as measured by surveys administered before and 8-months after CI-CDSS implementation. Primary outcome results of CI diagnosis are awaiting the end of the follow-up period; however, clinician surveys are complete and reported here. The odds ratio (OR) for the interaction between survey time and study arm, estimated in a generalized linear mixed model, tested the significance of change in clinician confidence across study arms. The average marginal effect (AME), estimated in a generalized estimating equation, provided a treatment effect estimate.ResultsWhile self-reported use of the CI-CDSS was modest (32% remembered the tool and 65% of those used it), those who used it reported high satisfaction (97% said the tool helped provide better CI care). Clinicians in CI-CDSS clinics reported increased confidence in managing CI care relative to usual care (UC) clinicians (AME = 16.8, OR = 2.24 [1.05, 4.80]). Trending, but not significant, increases in clinician confidence in diagnosing CI were observed in CI-CDSS clinics when compared to usual care (e.g., distinguishing types of dementia AME = 9.8, OR = 2.08[0.84, 5.14]).ConclusionsThe results of this study demonstrate the potential value of a CI-CDSS in promoting primary care clinician confidence in caring for and diagnosing patients with CI.Trial registrationThis trial is registered on clinicaltrials.gov, NCT05723523 on 02/02/2023.Keywords: Dementia; Cognitive impairment; Screening, detection, and diagnosis; Primary care; Healthy aging; Clinical decision support

Background

As the U.S. population ages, the prevalence of cognitive impairment (CI) is projected to continue increasing, posing significant challenges for primary care clinicians [1, 2]. Among individuals 70 years old and older, 14% have dementia and another 14% have mild cognitive impairment [3]. Primary care clinicians are routinely tasked with diagnosing and managing care for CI due to their frequent role as the first and trusted point of contact for patients exhibiting symptoms of cognitive decline [4]. Similarly, detection of CI is a required part of the Medicare Annual Wellness Visit in the United States, a prevention-oriented visit that primarily occurs in primary care [5]. However, there are still many barriers preventing primary care clinicians from implementing systematic processes for detection, diagnosis, and post-diagnosis management of individuals with CI. These include persistent reports of low confidence in CI diagnosis and management among primary care clinicians [6–9]. Clinician confidence is an important and necessary mechanism of increasing clinician diagnosis and treatment of clinical conditions, although notably not the only factor impacting these behaviors [10, 11]. Confidence is also highly interconnected with other mechanisms of clinical action like skills and ability, fear of negative consequences, and patient characteristics [12, 13]. One result of lack of clinician confidence is that both patients and families feel less support and more uncertainty about their situations. CI carries with it emotional stress about its implication and future prognosis [14].

Primary care clinicians also frequently cite system barriers as major obstacles to providing effective CI care, including insufficient time and lack of available resources [7], compounded by existing electronic health records systems (EHRs) that lack appropriate support for diagnosing and managing care for CI [15]. The complexity of this care, including the need for a nuanced approach to diagnosis and the involvement of families in care planning, adds to the challenge. Clinical decision support systems (CDSS) paired with detection algorithms and targeted training have been proposed to address these issues [16, 17]. CDSS can enhance primary care clinicians’ confidence – one possible predictor of clinician actions – in some contexts, but not in others [18, 19]. For example, in a recent systematic review describing clinician-reported barriers and facilitators to CDSS implementation, improvement of clinician confidence was noted as a facilitator in 7 of 45 systems across various areas of primary care. In one study, a CDSS designed for pulmonary embolism diagnosis did not improve clinician confidence, but confidence was relatively high at baseline [20]. In cancer care, primary care clinician confidence in assessing cancer risk and managing care improved following CDSS access and training [21]. Our team has developed a CI-focused CDSS with an accompanying clinician training and is testing its effectiveness in a pragmatic clinic-randomized controlled trial. The CDSS and training are designed to support clinicians in identifying individuals at risk for undetected CI and confidently diagnose and manage their care. While the primary outcome analysis of CI diagnosis is pending completion of the follow-up period, here we report the impact of this intervention on clinician confidence.

Methods

Study design

The results reported in this manuscript come from a clinician survey that was administered in the context of a larger pragmatic clinic-randomized control trial (clinicialtrials.gov NCT 05723523, 02/02/2023). The main trial is designed to evaluate the impact of a CDSS plus a clinician training on the diagnosis and post-diagnosis management of CI care among patients at elevated risk for CI. Thirty-eight primary care clinics were randomly assigned to receive training and access to a CDSS that provides clinician support for detecting CI at the point of care (CI-CDSS, n = 19 clinics) or to continue with usual care (UC, n = 19 clinics). Clinic randomization was selected to minimize potential for intervention delivery contamination across clinicians and patients from the same clinic. Clinicians were attributed to the study arm of the clinic where they practiced. The primary aim of the main trial is to assess the effect of the intervention on CI diagnosis among study-eligible patients. For the main trial, eligible patients (e.g., those age 65 or older without a CI diagnosis) attending CI-CDSS or UC clinics are automatically accrued into the study at eligible primary care visits and followed for up to 18 months to assess subsequent diagnosis and care management. The results of these patient-level EHR-derived endpoints will be forthcoming.

Here we report on changes in clinician confidence to diagnose and manage care for CI among the clinicians working in randomized clinics prior to and then following implementation. Clinicians practicing in all randomized clinics (CI-CDSS and UC) were invited to complete surveys developed for this study that were administered prior to CI-CDSS implementation and again at 8 months post-implementation. The current analysis presents pre-post changes in clinician self-reports of confidence in diagnosing and managing care for CI among CI-CDSS clinicians relative to UC clinicians. Protocols were reviewed and approved by the HealthPartners Institutional Review Board.

Setting

The trial is being carried out at a large integrated health system in the upper Midwest that has more than 50 primary and specialty care clinics and 8 hospitals and offers health insurance plans. Primary care clinicians practicing in this health system use an existing CDSS that assesses risk and provides prioritized best-practice treatment options for cardiometabolic and other chronic diseases and is designed to engage patients in shared decision-making [22, 23]. As a part of the existing system-wide CDSS, a best practice advisory (BPA) alerts rooming team members to print clinician-facing and plain-language patient-facing CDSS handouts. The handouts are given to clinicians to review before entering the exam room.

Intervention description

CI-CDSS

The CI-CDSS was implemented at the clinic level in CI-CDSS randomized clinics and was integrated into the clinical workflow through the pre-existing system-wide CDSS. The CI-CDSS has three primary components: (1) an electronic risk-identification tool with diagnosis and care recommendations focused on brain health, (2) a paper version of the same tool, and (3) additional interactive tools and resources clinicians can click into via the EHR to guide diagnosis and care decisions. These components were developed to provide information on detection, diagnosis, and post-diagnosis management of CI. At every primary care visit, a passive web service call gathers and analyzes CI risk-related patient and visit data (i.e., vitals, diagnosis, current medications, medical history, procedures, laboratory and assessment results) to determine patient eligibility. In CI-CDSS arm clinics, the web service call initiates the CI-CDSS at eligible visits. This includes the paper and electronic CI risk-related content presented in a prioritized list of care opportunities. Through the CI-CDSS, clinicians can also access additional CI-related resources electronically that provides the clinician with additional relevant information on the patient’s condition (previous lab values and test scores), enables the clinician to administer cognitive assessments (e.g. MoCA, SLUMS, etc.), provides individualized guidance on additional labs or imaging needed for CI assessment, and provides quick access to resources and referrals for management of CI. The CI-CDSS was implemented in CI-CDSS study arm clinics in August 2023 and remained active through the study period.

CI-CDSS clinician training

A one-hour clinician training focused on CI diagnosis and care as well as use of the new CI-CDSS that was developed for this study and held for clinicians practicing in clinics randomized to the CI-CDSS arm. Trainings were held virtually, and clinic managers, clinicians, nurses, and rooming team members were invited to attend. The training emphasized CI education as well as appropriate workflow for using the CI-CDSS with demonstration of CI-CDSS features and content through case-based learning. Trainings were given jointly by the study principal investigators with expertise in neuroscience and geriatric psychiatry and a senior program manager with extensive experience with the CDSS system. Lunch was provided to clinics to encourage attendance, and one CME credit was offered to those who attended. Clinic trainings were held in June 2023 prior to CI-CDSS implementation in August 2023.

Data collection

Intervention data

The CI-CDSS consists of a web service that automatically documents every web service call made from the EHR and whether the CI-CDSS is printed or clicked on during a visit. This information was extracted by the study team and summarized for visits with study-eligible patients in CI-CDSS study arm clinics from August 2023 through April 2024. Below we report the proportion of visits with CI-CDSS eligible patients at which treatment recommendations were printed by rooming team members or clicked on electronically by clinicians were calculated. Data on clinician attendance at the CI-CDSS training was recorded electronically by clinic managers and is reported to describe intervention fidelity below.

Clinician survey design and implementation

Baseline surveys were administered to primary care clinicians practicing in all study clinics (CI-CDSS and UC) in Spring 2023, prior to clinician training or CI-CDSS implementation. Follow-up surveys were administered in all study clinics approximately 8 months after the CI-CDSS implementation. The survey, which was designed for this study, and a supplemental file of the full survey have been previously published [9]. Briefly, surveys were designed to be completed in less than 10 min and used tailored existing measures where available and newly drafted items where necessary. Surveys were pilot-tested with clinicians at clinics not included in the study. Measured constructs included clinician confidence in diagnosing and managing care for CI as well as other barriers related to CI care and diagnosis. Follow-up surveys also asked clinicians who practiced at a CI-CDSS study arm clinic about their use and satisfaction with the CI-CDSS. At each time point, surveys were emailed to clinicians with a personalized link to complete the survey online. Up to three reminder emails were sent over the course of two weeks to clinicians who did not respond. A $100 electronic gift card was offered at each time point as a thank you for completing the survey.

Measures

Clinician characteristics

At baseline, clinicians self-reported the number of days they practice on average per week and the number of years they have been practicing as well as their gender and racial or ethnic identities. Clinicians who did not complete the baseline survey were asked to report these characteristics in the follow-up survey.

Intervention engagement and satisfaction

Intervention participation was characterized by clinician attendance at the CI-CDSS training, and as the percentages of eligible study visits at which the CI-CDSS content was printed for or clicked on by clinicians during the visit. Clinician-reported participation or use of the printed or electronic version of the CI-CDSS was measured via the follow-up survey for clinicians practicing at CI-CDSS clinics. For those reporting some use of the CI-CDSS, additional items asked about satisfaction and perceived usefulness of the CI-CDSS with response options on a 4-point Likert scale (e.g., strongly agree, somewhat agree, somewhat disagree, strongly disagree).

Clinician confidence

Clinician confidence in diagnosis was measured first by a single item asking about overall confidence in diagnosing CI, followed by 5 items asking about confidence with specific components or steps of diagnosis (e.g., confidence conducting a cognitive assessment using tools like the MoCA, or distinguishing between types of dementia). Confidence in managing CI care was queried similarly, starting with an overall confidence item followed by 9 items addressing confidence in conducting specific care management activities (e.g., providing pharmacological treatment, accessing patient education materials). All clinician confidence response options were rated on a 4-point scale ranging from ‘very confident’ to ‘not at all confident’.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics including frequencies, percents, means, and standard deviations were used to characterize the population and describe results of intervention participation and satisfaction. Clinician confidence response options were dichotomized as confident (moderately or very confident) or not confident (a little or not at all confident). Generalized linear mixed models (GLMM; binomial distribution, logit link, random clinician intercept) predicted the likelihood of a “confident” response to each survey item from study arm, survey time, and their interaction. The 95% confidence limits around the odds ratio (OR [95% CL]) for the study arm by survey time interaction tested the hypothesis that confidence increased more from baseline to follow-up among CI-CDSS relative to UC clinicians. The average marginal effect (AME), calculated from a comparably structured generalized estimating equation (GEE), is presented for each survey item for ease of interpretation. OR > 1 and AME > 0 denote more increase from baseline to follow-up in the likelihood of a confident rating among CI-CDSS than among UC clinicians. All ratings from all clinicians who responded to items in at least one survey were included in the analyses, regardless of whether the clinician participated in the intervention activities to which their randomized clinic was assigned. All statistical analyses were conducted in SAS v9.4.

Results

Descriptive characteristics

At baseline, 408 clinicians practicing at 38 study clinics were invited and eligible to participate in the survey (M = 11 clinicians/clinic, SD = 7). Of these, 249 submitted survey responses (61% response rate, M = 7 clinicians/clinic, SD = 4). At follow-up, 295 of 470 eligible clinicians practicing at 38 study clinics provided survey responses (63% response rate, see Fig. 1). A total of 335 clinicians completed a baseline or follow-up survey. See Table 1 for descriptive characteristics of these clinicians by study arm. Most clinicians practiced 4 days a week or more, had been practicing for 10 years or less, and identified as female, white, and not Hispanic or Latino.

Intervention engagement and satisfaction

60% (n = 121) of primary care clinicians practicing at CI-CDSS clinics at baseline attended the one-hour clinician training, with at least 2 clinicians attending from with all 19 CI-CDSS clinics (M = 6 clinicians/clinic attended, SD = 3). In the 8 months after implementation of the CI-CDSS, the electronic tool was visible in the EHR at all eligible visits in CI-CDSS clinics and the paper tool was printed for the clinician at 78% of eligible visits (M = 79% print rate/clinic, SD = 13%). Among the 159 CI-CDSS arm clinicians who completed the follow-up survey, 32% (n = 51 clinicians) remembered seeing either the electronic tool or the paper handout; 25% remembered seeing the electronic tool (n = 40 of 159 clinicians); 17% remembered seeing the paper handout (n = 27 of 159 clinicians).

While the number of clinicians reporting seeing the electronic tool was modest, almost all of these clinicians reported reviewing the content at least some of the time (n = 34 of 40 clinicians, 85%). Similarly, almost all clinicians who remembered seeing the paper handout reported actively reviewing it at least some of the time during visits (n = 24 of 27 clinicians, 89%) and about half reported reviewing it often or always (n = 13 of 27 clinicians, 48%). Further, of those who saw either the paper or electronic CI-CDSS (n = 51; 32%), 90% somewhat or strongly agreed that it correctly identified patients at risk for CI (n = 46 of 51 clinicians, 90%) and that they would somewhat or definitely recommend the CI-CDSS to other clinicians (n = 47 of 51 clinicians, 92%).

Among those who remembered seeing or reviewing the CI-CDSS (n = 51; 32%), 33 clinicians reported actively using the electronic or paper tool for detection, diagnosis, or care management. Of those reporting use of the tool (n = 33), most thought it helped them better diagnose and care for patients with CI, took the same amount of time or less to diagnose with the tool, and intended to continue using the tool. (See Table 2 for additional details.) Clinicians only clicked into the additional EHR-embedded resources and tools that supplemented the CI-CDSS in 1% of eligible visits (M = 1% click rate/clinic, SD = 3%).

Discussion

These analyses provide some backing for decision support systems and brief training as a means of improving primary care clinician confidence in CI care. These results appear to be primarily driven by increased confidence in care management after diagnosis, specifically in addressing patient safety issues like driving and home safety. Although attendance at the CI training, display of the electronic tool, and print rates of the paper tool were relatively high, clinician self-reported receipt and use of the tool itself were modest, and click rates indicating use of the additional supplemental CI-CDSS components were low. Although some variability was seen in these measures across clinics, future research should explore how individual clinic or clinician participation influences confidence directly. Nevertheless, confidence in managing care improved, suggesting an important component of clinician confidence is bringing awareness of a diagnosis and having access to tools and resources when needed. In fact, qualitative results in other disease contexts have suggested that decision support tools are perceived by clinicians as most useful in simply identifying and screening patients who need specific types of care [24]. This is an important learning as creating these supplemental tools is time and resource intensive for CDS developers. Further research must better uncover and address the barriers to CDS receipt and use to increase impact, as well as studying whether there is a need for supplemental CDS tools and if so, better understand and improve their accessibility or utility.

Interestingly, providing access to this CI-CDSS with training in primary care clinics did not appear to be sufficient to significantly improve clinician confidence in diagnosing CI overall or in most of the associated diagnostic steps. This lack of effect could be driven by modest use of core CI-CDSS components, limited use of the supplemental tools within the CI-CDSS, or by other outside barriers or factors in the clinic, such as parallel initiatives in this health system that were being implemented concurrently across both arms during the study period, including a quality improvement initiative to standardize workflows following a positive CI screen at the annual wellness visit which could have confounded the effects of this intervention. Clinician confidence in diagnosing CI was indeed higher in both arms at follow-up compared to baseline that may have reduced the ability to see a treatment arm by time interaction, which would have indicated added benefit of the CI-CDSS for diagnosis in the Intervention arm. Confidence in diagnosis in the intervention arm was also higher at follow-up (59%) than at baseline (45%) but the difference in differences between arms and timepoints was not statistically significant.

Another important finding is the high satisfaction with the tool among clinicians who did report using the paper or electronic versions for CI diagnosis or management. Over 90% of the 30 clinicians who used the CI-CDSS said it helped them both better diagnose CI and provide better CI care. The perceived usefulness and usability of the tool were high, and it was recognized as either saving time or being worth the time it took to use. Yet even with this very high satisfaction, electronic use of the additional CDSS tools in the EHR remained low, suggesting other barriers were present. This was also evidenced by clinician feedback in the open-ended comments pointing to systems level factors making CI diagnosis and care challenging. These findings echo similar results about the lack of uptake of decision support tools across clinical care domains as well as the body of literature on barriers and facilitators to implementation [19]. Research on strategies to improve the use of CDSS driven by implementation science frameworks provides an opportunity to solve these challenges in partnership with clinicians, care teams, clinic leaders, patients and care partners [25]. Future efforts should focus on systematically understanding the barriers to CDSS use and designing implementation strategies that directly address these barriers.

This work has several important strengths including a randomized controlled trial design, high response rates among primary care clinicians at both baseline and follow up, and a statistical approach that adjusts for the fact that clinicians are clustered within clinics and could respond at multiple timepoints. However, there are some important limitations. The landscape of dementia care has advanced rapidly from the onset of this work including the FDA approval of new medications and emergence of blood-based biomarkers to aid in identification of Alzheimer’s disease as a cause of CI. Additionally, the intervention was implemented within a single, integrated health system in the upper Midwest in which a CDSS was already in place for some non-CI conditions. Results may vary in other settings with different clinic cultures, patient populations, and leadership. Future research should be done to understand whether the CDSS yields similar results in other settings. Furthermore, although print rates of the CI-CDSS were high, click rates and clinician self-report use of the additional EHR embedded tools were low, suggesting the need for improved implementation strategies. Lastly, this analysis only examined the effect of CI-CDSS on clinician confidence in diagnosing and managing CI care, but there are other important care team roles and decision-making factors that influence CI diagnosis and care. Focusing on clinician confidence assumes that confidence is the primary driver of clinician action. Research in other contexts has shown that capability (e.g., knowledge), opportunity (e.g., social and environmental factors) and other motivation-related factors (e.g., fear of negative consequences, accountability for quality measures) also influence clinician action. It is even possible that increasing confidence could be inversely associated with clinical knowledge, known as the Dunnigan-Kruger effect, which might be more or less prevalent among certain professionals [26]. It is even possible that increasing confidence could be inversely associated with actions like diagnosing [27]. Future work can build on learnings from this study to better capture other factors influencing clinician behaviors using a systems lens and implementation science frameworks [28]. Ultimately, possible drivers of clinician decisions should be evaluated in relation to subsequent actions such as making a new CI diagnosis and engaging in specific care management activities. Despite its limitations, the present work takes a first step toward establishing the potential value of CDSS in promoting primary care clinician confidence in diagnosing and caring for patients with CI.
