Biochemical metabolic bone disease in preterm infants: clinical determinants, markers and risk of fracture in a cohort study
Authors: Gaffari Tunç, İpek Güney Varal, Ayşe Ören, Dilara Ülker, Yasemin Denkboy Öngen, Onur Bağcı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeBiochemical metabolic bone disease (MBD) is a frequent complication in very preterm infants, yet its independent risk factors and relationship with fracture have not been fully elucidated. This study aimed to investigate the clinical characteristics, laboratory parameters, independent predictors, and fracture risk associated with biochemical MBD in very low birth weight (VLBW) infants.Materials and methodsBetween January 2020 and December 2024, 4,555 neonates admitted to the Neonatology Department of Bursa Yüksek İhtisas Training and Research Hospital were screened. Of these, 915 were preterm, and 413 infants with a gestational age ≤ 32 weeks and/or birth weight ≤ 1500 g met the inclusion criteria and were retrospectively analyzed. Biochemical MBD was defined as serum ALP ≥ 500 IU/L, serum phosphorus < 4.5 mg/dL, and tubular phosphate reabsorption > 95% at 4–6 weeks of life. Fractures were confirmed by X-ray. Multivariable logistic regression and ROC curve analyses were used to identify independent predictors and assess discriminative performance.ResultsAmong the 413 very low birth weight infants included in the study, biochemical MBD was identified in 82 (19.8%); the overall fracture rate was 5.3% (22/413), whereas it was 26.8% (22/82) among infants with biochemical MBD. Male sex, lower 5-minute Apgar scores, need for resuscitation, caffeine administration, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, patent ductus arteriosus, postnatal steroid exposure, cholestasis, delayed attainment of full enteral feeding, prolonged total parenteral nutrition, and ≥ 3 red blood cell transfusions were significantly associated with biochemical MBD. Multivariate analysis identified male sex (OR 2.29; 95% CI: 1.317–3.970; p = 0.003), parenteral nutrition (PN) duration (OR 1.019 per day; 95% CI: 1.007–1.032; p = 0.003), and ≥ 3 red blood cell transfusions (OR 3.96; 95% CI: 2.193–7.149; p < 0.001) as independent predictors of biochemical MBD. The regression model demonstrated good calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow p = 0.790) and acceptable discriminative ability (AUC 0.788; 95% CI: 0.733–0.844). Infants with biochemical MBD had significantly elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels and lower serum phosphate concentrations (698 vs. 392 U/L and 3.63 vs. 5.4 mg/dL, respectively; p < 0.001), while serum calcium levels were similar. ROC analysis indicated that a PN duration exceeding 19.5 days (AUC 0.730; 95% CI: 0.671–0.789; p < 0.001) was a strong predictor of biochemical MBD. Comparisons between infants with and without fractures revealed no significant differences in ALP, phosphate, or tubular reabsorption of phosphate levels; however, serum calcium levels were significantly lower in infants who developed fractures (9.1 vs. 9.5 mg/dL; p = 0.022).ConclusionA PN duration ≥ 19.5 days is an early, clinically useful predictor of biochemical MBD in preterm infants and is associated with fracture risk. Male sex, prolonged PN, and multiple erythrocyte transfusions are independent predictors of biochemical MBD in VLBW infants. Lower serum calcium in infants with fractures suggests a more severe disease course. Introduction

Metabolic bone disease (MBD), which is seen especially in very low birth weight (VLBW<1500 g) premature infants, is a multifactorial clinical condition characterised by bone mineral insufficiency. The disease is identifed from radiological findings and biochemical changes due to reduced bone mineralisation [1–3]. The incidence has been reported to be 55%-60% in extremely low weight infants with a birth weight of < 1000 g, and 23% in those with birth weight of 1000–1500 g [4, 5]. Postnatal immobility and loss of oestrogen procurement from the placenta contribute to skeletal demineralisation. As most calcium and phosphorus reserves form in the last 3 months of pregnancy, premature infants are at risk of biochemical MBD.

Metabolic bone disease emerges most often between 4 and 16 weeks postnatally. Bone metabolism is deteriorated with the high rate of skeletal growth in the first few weeks of life and insufficient mineral intake [6, 7].

Calcium, phosphorus, and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels can be of guidance for early diagnosis. When abnormal laboratory results are observed, advanced examinations should be performed [7]. Insufficient intake of parenteral and enteral phosphorus increases the risk of early and late hypophosphatemia. In the perinatal period, insufficient calcium and phosphorus intake can lead to MBD characterized by a decrease in bone mass, a change in bone mineralisation, and increased risk of fracture [1, 4, 7–9]. MBD can cause fractures, insufficient weight gain, short stature, and growth disorders [10]. Although the incidence of MBD in premature infants seems to have decreased with developments in neonatal care and nutrition, the recent practice of only providing phosphate supplements is known to exacerbate MBD by leading to secondary hyperparathyroidism and associated bone loss. There is no single diagnostic or screening test for MBD [9]. It is recommended that preterm infants receive nutritional support containing calcium and phosphate to meet the bone mineralisation requirements. However, there are no clear data about the frequency of MBD in preterm infants who do not receive phosphate supplementation.

The definition, screening, and treatment of MBD vary according to different publications. Although the recently published American Pediatrics Academy Consensus Declaration may be useful in closing the gaps in institutional differences, evidence-based practice guidelines remain unclear because of the lack of normative data and clinical studies [1].

Metabolic Bone Disease continues to be a significant source of morbidity, especially in premature and low birth weight infants. Moreover, recommendations for disease definition, screening methods, and treatment show significant heterogeneity in the literature. This makes it difficult to develop a standard approach in clinical practice and leads to varying practices in different centres.

Early detection of biochemical MBD in premature infants can reduce the risk of developing serious long-term complications by allowing the implementation of timely and targeted management strategies.

Biochemical MBD in very low birth weight infants is an important but insufficiently identified problem and the clinical determinants and factors related to fracture risk are not clear. The aim of this study was to determine the clinical characteristics, laboratory findings, and fracture risk associated with the development of biochemical MBD in very low birth weight infants.

Materials and methods

Between January 2020 and December 2024, a total of 4,555 patients were admitted to the Neonatology Unit of Bursa Yüksek İhtisas Training and Research Hospital, among whom 915 very low birth weight (VLBW) infants were screened, and 413 preterm infants with a gestational age of ≤ 32 weeks and/or birth weight of ≤ 1,500 g met the study inclusion criteria and were retrospectively evaluated. The cases were separated into two groups as Group 1 (n = 82) determined with biochemical MBD, and Group 2 (n = 331) without biochemical MBD. The Group 1 cases diagnosed with biochemical MBD of prematurity were separated into two subgroups as those who developed fracture (n = 22) and those without fracture (n = 60).

In all the cases, serum ALP, calcium, and phosphorus levels were assessed between the 4th and 6th postnatal weeks as part of the initial screening. Subsequently, serial measurements of these parameters were obtained during follow-up, with the frequency determined according to the infant’s clinical status and risk profile. When enteral feeding reached the level of 100 mL/kg/day, oral Vitamin D (800 IU/day) was added. Tubular reabsorption of phosphate (TRP) was calculated with the standard fractional reabsorption formula using spot urine and simultaneous serum samples. The TRP values were stated as a percentage (%). The diagnosis of biochemical MBD was made for cases with levels of ALP ≥ 500IU/L, phosphorus < 4.5 mg/dL, and TRP > 95% measured between 4 and 6 weeks of life during the follow up [11, 12]. The diagnosis of biochemical MBD was established in neonates who met al.l three criteria: ALP ≥ 500 IU/L, serum phosphorus < 4.5 mg/dL, and tubular reabsorption of phosphate (TRP) > 95%. Fracture was suspected in neonates presenting with localized swelling, tenderness, decreased spontaneous movement of the affected extremity, or unexplained irritability. The diagnosis was confirmed by direct radiographs.

Infants were excluded from the study if they had any congenital anomaly, metabolic or genetic disease, showed any syndromic characteristic, had any gastrointestinal pathologies, kidney or bone diseases, or had gestational age of > 32 weeks or birth weight of > 1500 g.

Data on demographic characteristics, clinical parameters, laboratory findings, duration of mechanical ventilation, and mortality were retrospectively collected from the patients’ medical records.

Intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) was assessed according to the Volpe classification [13], and its presence was confirmed by a neonatologist certified in transfontanelle ultrasonography. Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) was diagnosed based on the modified Bell staging criteria [14]. Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) was evaluated as “present” or “absent” by a pediatric cardiology specialist. Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) was defined using the widely accepted criteria proposed by Jobe and Bancalari in 2001, as recommended by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Workshop [15]. Cholestasis was defined as a direct bilirubin level exceeding 20% of the total bilirubin, or a direct bilirubin ≥ 1 mg/dL when the total bilirubin was < 5 mg/dL [16].

Nutritional management

Enteral and parenteral nutrition practices were conducted in accordance with the Turkish Neonatal Society guidelines for nutrition in preterm infants. In clinically stable preterm infants, minimal enteral (trophic) feeding was initiated within the first 24–48 h of life, using mother’s own milk as the first choice and preterm formula when human milk was unavailable. Enteral feeding was advanced at a rate of 20–30 mL/kg/day, depending on gestational age, clinical condition, and feeding tolerance. Feed advancement was temporarily slowed or discontinued in the presence of signs of feeding intolerance or suspected necrotizing enterocolitis.

In infants unable to achieve adequate enteral intake, parenteral nutrition was initiated on the first day of life. Amino acids were started at 3 g/kg/day and gradually increased to 3.5–4 g/kg/day as tolerated. Lipid emulsions were initiated at 1 g/kg/day and advanced to a maximum of 3 g/kg/day as tolerated. The glucose infusion rate was started at 4 mg/kg/min and increased up to 12 mg/kg/min to maintain euglycemia. Electrolytes, as well as calcium and phosphorus, were adjusted according to daily biochemical monitoring. Parenteral nutrition was gradually tapered as enteral feeding volumes increased and was discontinued once full enteral feeding (approximately 150–180 mL/kg/day) was achieved.

Ethics

All study procedures were in compliance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration and Good Clinical Practice, and the study was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of Bursa Yüksek Ihtisas Training and Research Hospital (decision no: 2024-TBEK 2024/11 − 08, dated:06.11.2024).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences). The distributional characteristics of continuous variables were assessed using normality tests. Continuous variables that did not follow a normal distribution were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR). Comparisons of continuous variables between two independent groups were performed using the Mann Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and group comparisons were performed using the Chi-square test. Group analyses were performed to compare the demographic, perinatal, clinical, and laboratory characteristics of cases with and without biochemical MBD. Subgroup analyses among patients who developed biochemical MBD were also performed according to the presence of fractures; the Mann Whitney U test was used for continuous variables and the Chi-square test for categorical variables. Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent risk factors associated with the development of biochemical MBD. Variables considered clinically relevant were included in the model. The regression results were reported as regression coefficient (B), standard error (SE), Wald statistic, odds ratio (OR), and 95% confidence interval (CI). The overall significance of the model was assessed using the chi-square test. The explanatory power of the model was reported using the Cox & Snell R² and Nagelkerke R² coefficients. The assumption of multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values (VIF < 5 and tolerance > 0.20 were considered acceptable). Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer Lemeshow Goodness-of-fit test. The discriminative performance of the logistic regression model was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis based on the predicted probabilities derived from the model, and the area under the curve (AUC), standard error, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Additionally, the discriminative performance of parenteral nutrition (PN) duration in identifying biochemical MBD was evaluated using ROC curve analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

During the study period, a total of 4,555 patients were admitted to the NICU. Of these, 915 were identified as preterm, and 87 were excluded due to incomplete data. Ultimately, 413 preterm infants with a gestational age of less than 32 weeks and a birth weight below 1,500 g were included in the analysis. The participants were stratified according to the presence of biochemical MBD into an Group 1 (n = 82) and an Group 2 (n = 331). Within the Group 1, 22 infants experienced bone fractures, whereas 60 infants did not. Among the 413 very low birth weight infants (≤ 32 weeks of gestation, ≤ 1500 g) included in the study, biochemical MBD was identified in 82 (19.8%) and fractures were identified in 22 (5.3%). No fractures were observed in the Group 2 (Fig. 1).

Flowchart for the study

The premature infants that developed biochemical MBD (Group 1) were determined to be significantly more immature and showed a more severe clinical course than the premature infants that did not develop biochemical MBD (Group 2). In Group 1, the gestational age at birth and birth weight were statistically significantly lower (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, respectively). Male gender, (p = 0.002), a low 5-min APGAR score (p = 0.003), and a greater need for resuscitation (p = 0.013) were found to be associated with biochemical MBD. Statistically significantly higher rates of caffeine consumption (p < 0.001), bronchopulmonary dysplasia (p = 0.005), postnatal steroid treatment (p = 0.006), patent ductus arteriosus (p = 0.046), and cholestasis (p = 0.017) were observed in Group 1. In infants with biochemical MBD, the time to full enteral feeding (p < 0.001) and the duration of parenteral feeding (p < 0.001) were significantly longer and the need for erythrocyte transfusion at least three times was significantly greater (p < 0.001). The durations of oxygen requirement (p < 0.001), mechanical ventilation (p < 0.001), and hospital stay (p < 0.001) were significantly longer in Group 1 (Table 1).

Discussion

This study, which examined the relationship between biochemical MBD development in very low birth weight premature infants and biochemical markers and the risk of fracture, is an important cohort analysis in terms of the large sample and from a clinical perspective.

The study results demonstrated that gestational age and birth weight were significantly lower in Group 1, and risk factors such as male gender, low 5-min APGAR score, the need for resuscitation, caffeine consumption, BPD, PDA, postnatal steroid treatment, cholestasis, delayed transition to full enteral feeding and a longer duration of parenteral nutrition, increased need for erythrocyte transfusion, and prolonged duration of oxygen/ventilation/ICU stay were determined to be risk factors associated with biochemical MBD.

Premature infants who developed biochemical MBD in this study were significantly more immature and exhibited higher clinical morbidity. Low gestational age and birth weight were determined to be the strongest risk factors for MBD, and this finding was consistent with the literature [7, 17]. Male gender, low 5-min APGAR score, and the need for resuscitation were more frequent in the MBD group, suggesting that perinatal stress could negatively affect bone mineralisation [7, 18]. In line with previous findings in the literature, MBD was also determined more frequently in male infants in this study [11].

The increasing prevalence of BPD has been associated with prolonged duration of mechanical ventilation and persistent oxygen requirement, which are recognized as important risk factors. In addition, low birth weight is a key determinant in the development of BPD. Moreover, erythrocyte transfusion has been found to be independently associated with high risk of MBD, and the need for further research to investigate the underlying mechanisms of this relationship has been emphasized [17, 19]. Daily and cumulative caffeine doses and lower gestational age at birth have been determined to be associated with MBD development, and there has been strongly stated to be a need for randomised controlled clinical studies to determine the safest and most effective caffeine ranges in the treatment of apnea of premature newborns and to confirm these findings. These results are in agreement with recent studies that have reported the negative effects of cumulative caffeine doses on bone mineralisation [20].

Cholestasis, PDA, delayed transition to enteral feeding, lengthy parenteral nutrition and postnatal steroid treatment were determined at a higher frequency in the MBD group of the current study, and these findings were consistent with the literature [7, 17].

The current study findings showed that premature infants who developed biochemical MBD were more immature and had a higher clinical morbidity burden. In particular, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, a long period of parenteral nutrition, delayed transition to enteral feeding, and the need for repeated blood transfusions were seen to significantly increase the risk of biochemical MBD. It was also thought that perinatal stress symptoms and concomitant systemic complications contributed to the development of biochemical MBD. When all these data were evaluated together, biochemical MBD was seen to be a multifactorial condition closely associated with immaturity in preterm infants, insufficient nutrition, and clinical instability. However, it was also determined that antenatal steroid use had a protective effect in respect of bone fracture development. In a study by Erol et al., antenatal steroid administration was evaluated using biochemical markers in the four-week postnatal period, and the diagnosis of biochemical MBD was reported not to have been significantly affected. While these findings emphasize the determining role of birth weight in the MBD risk profile, they also showed that antenatal steroids were not a contributing risk factor [21]. In contrast, the current study results determined that antenatal steroid use was associated with a protective effect against biochemical MBD development, and it was thought that this effect could have occurred because of increased postnatal clinical stability due to fetal lung maturation, and thereby improved feeding tolerance. Our study found that antenatal steroids protect against bone fractures, but prospective studies are needed due to conflicting evidence in the literature.

Fractures were suspected in neonates presenting with localized swelling, tenderness, decreased spontaneous movement of the affected limb, or unexplained irritability, and diagnoses were confirmed by direct radiographs. To minimize radiation exposure, routine radiographic screening was not performed in preterm or immature infants; imaging was reserved for cases with clinical suspicion. This approach may have led to missed asymptomatic fractures, particularly in immature or clinically stable neonates, potentially resulting in an underestimation of the true fracture prevalence.

The data presented in Table 3 indicate that infants with fractures appeared clinically healthier compared with those with biochemical MBD without fractures. Specifically, infants with fractures had a higher median gestational age (28.5 vs. 26 weeks, p = 0.002) and lower rates of BPD, RDS, and PDA (31.8% vs. 65%, 50% vs. 93.3%, 22.7% vs. 70%, respectively). These findings contrast with previous literature, where more clinically vulnerable infants are generally expected to require prolonged support and have a higher risk of fractures. These paradoxical observations may be attributable to the fracture detection strategy used in our study. As fractures were identified solely based on clinical suspicion, asymptomatic or mild fractures may have been missed; this may also have influenced the nutritional status of the infants, as we observed lower calcium levels in the fracture Group 1.

The findings in Table 3, which in some aspects contrast with previous literature, also highlight the multifactorial nature of fracture development in very preterm infants with metabolic bone disease. Infants who developed fractures exhibited more severe and prolonged clinical courses, including higher rates of respiratory morbidity, increased requirements for red blood cell transfusions, longer durations of parenteral nutrition, and delayed transition to full enteral feeding. These results suggest that fracture risk cannot be explained solely by isolated perinatal factors; rather, it is closely associated with cumulative disease burden, the length and complexity of intensive care, and nutritional challenges. Therefore, strategies aimed at preventing fractures in metabolic bone disease should consider not only prenatal and perinatal risk factors but also complications occurring during intensive care and the management of nutrition.

The high ALP levels observed in the current study infants who developed biochemical MBD indicate the bone mineralisation disorder often seen in premature newborns. As has been reported in the literature, elevated ALP is accepted as one of the early biochemical markers of MBD, and the fact that serum calcium levels usually remain within normal limits shows that in MBD calcium homeostasis is protected by endogenous mechanisms [22]. In the current study, the increase in ALP levels of biochemical MBD group occurred at mean 39.33 days postnatally, and the mean maximum ALP value was 691.41 IU/L. It has been reported in multivariate analysis that parenteral feeding with supportive ventilation is an independent risk factor for biochemical MBD development [17]. The current study results showed that ALP levels had high differentiating power in predicting biochemical MBD. The significantly low phosphorus levels in Group 1 are supported by findings in literature as one of the earliest and most specific biochemical markers of insufficient bone mineralisation. The detection of high TRP has shown that low phosphorus levels are not caused by renal loss, but rather by a protective mechanism aimed at retaining phosphorus [23].

These findings suggest that the Group 1 infants were at higher risk of biochemical MBD, and emphasize the importance of regular biochemical monitoring with early nutrition interventions, especially in premature infants.

The primary risk factors for biochemical MBD include low gestational age, low birth weight, prolonged total parenteral nutrition, late (> 7 days) transition to full enteral feeding, cholestasis, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, and anaemia requiring blood transfusion, and logistic regression analysis results have shown that late transition to full enteral feeding is a significant predictor for the development of MBD [24]. It has been reported in the literature that serial monitoring of ALP and phosphorus levels is critical in respect of early diagnosis and prevention, and these parameters could optimise early follow-up and nutrition strategies. In addition, the importance of interpretation of these findings together with clinical and radiological evaluations has been emphasized in respect of preserving the long-term bone health of premature infants [11, 24, 25].

In the current study, no significant differences were observed between infants radiologically classified as with and without fracture in respect of ALP levels (701 vs. 698 U/L), serum phosphate levels (3.60 vs. 3.68 mg/dL), and tubular reabsorption of phosphate (97% for both groups). Although not statistically significant, the biochemical MBD group with fractures had classically high ALP and low phosphorus levels. In addition, the serum calcium levels were determined to be lower in the group with fractures (9.1 mg/dL vs. 9.5 mg/dL, p = 0.022). These findings differ from the classically defined biochemical MBD high ALP and low phosphorus profile in premature infants. It has been emphasized in the literature that a single biochemical marker is not sufficient to make a diagnosis of biochemical MBD. Therefore, it has also been highlighted in the literature that fracture risk cannot be predicted with only ALP, phosphorus, and tubular reabsorption of phosphate parameters, but that there is a need for serial biochemical monitoring, Vitamin D/PTH measurements, nutrition evaluation, and if necessary, radiological or bone densitometry follow-up in the determination of infants at risk [4, 26, 27]. In our study, although ALP, phosphorus, and calcium values alone may appear insufficient for diagnosing biochemical MBD, biochemical markers demonstrated high predictive value for biochemical MBD, as no fractures were observed in the group without biochemical MBD.

Prolonged parenteral nutrition (PN) and elevated serum ALP levels are significant factors associated with an increased risk of biochemical MBD in premature infants. In the present study, infants receiving PN for more than 19.5 days had a significantly higher likelihood of developing biochemical MBD (AUC: 0.769, sensitivity 68%, specificity 63%; p < 0.001). The association between prolonged PN and biochemical MBD has been previously reported, with one study indicating that extended PN in premature infants with a birth weight ≤ 1500 g and gestational age ≤ 32 weeks increases the risk of MBD [28]. It has also been recommended in many studies that elevated ALP be used as a marker of impaired bone mineralisation [17, 29].

Limitations and strengths of the study

There were some limitations to this study. The retrospective design prevented the establishment of causal relationships, and the use of record-based data could have led to some missing clinical and laboratory data. The fact that serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D and PTH levels were not available in a significant proportion of the cases limited the detailed evaluation of calcium-phosphorus metabolism. The DEXA method, which is accepted as the gold standard in bone mineral density evaluation, was not performed, which restricted objective evaluation. Another important factor limiting generalisability to different NICUs was that the findings were obtained from a single centre.Another important limitation affecting the generalisability of the findings to different neonatal intensive care units is that the study was conducted at a single centre.

Routine radiographic screening was not performed in immature infants to minimize radiation exposure; imaging was only conducted in cases with clinical suspicion. This approach may have led to missed asymptomatic fractures, particularly in immature or clinically unstable infants, potentially resulting in an underestimation of the true fracture prevalence. Radiographic evaluation was also not routinely performed in all infants, and fractures were identified only when clinically suspected.

However, this study simultaneously evaluated clinical, biochemical, and fracture risk factors associated with biochemical biochemical MBD in very early premature infants, and presented a comprehensive cohort analysis of a large sample. The 5-year observation period and high number of patients monitored in a fourth-level neonatal ICU increase the clinical reliability of the findings obtained. The diagnostic accuracy was strengthened by the use of an approach based on pathophysiology and many parameters including serum ALP, phosphorus level, and tubular reabsorption of phosphate in the definition of biochemical MBD. Determining cutoff values for PN duration that can be applied in clinical practice using ROC analysis increases the practical value of the study. Evaluation of the cases with biochemical MBD by separating them into subgroups according to the development of fracture, and analysis together with clinical, nutritional, transfusion needs, and respiratory morbidities fully revealed the multifactorial nature of the disease.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that biochemical MBD in very low birth weight preterm infants is a multifactorial condition closely associated with immaturity, clinical morbidities, and nutritional factors. Prolonged total parenteral nutrition, delayed transition to full enteral feeding, high serum ALP, and low phosphorus levels significantly increase the risk of biochemical MBD. Male sex, prolonged PN duration (> 19.5 days), and repeated erythrocyte transfusions were identified as independent predictors. Early biochemical monitoring, timely nutritional interventions, and careful management of systemic complications are essential to prevent biochemical MBD and its associated fracture risk in this vulnerable population. These findings highlight the importance of a comprehensive and individualized approach to bone health in preterm infants.
