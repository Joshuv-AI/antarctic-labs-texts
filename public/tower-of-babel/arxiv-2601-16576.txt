Solving Regularized Multifacility Location Problems with Unknown Number of Centers via Difference-of-Convex Optimization
W. Geremew, V. S. T. Long , N. M. Nam, A. Solano-Herrera

Abstract
In this paper, we develop optimization methods for a new model of multifacility location problems defined by a Minkowski gauge with Laplace-type regularization terms. The model is analyzed from both theoretical and numerical perspectives. In particular, we establish the existence of optimal solutions and study qualitative properties of global minimizers. By combining Nesterov’s smoothing technique with recent advances in difference-of-convex optimization, following the pioneering work of P. D. Tao and L. T. H. An and others, we propose efficient numerical algorithms for minimizing the objective function of this model. As an application, our approach provides an effective method for determining the number of centers in gauge-based multifacility location and clustering problems. Our results extend and complement recent developments by L. T. H. An in [ 1 ] .



Keywords:
Multifacility location; clustering; Laplace-type regularization; Minkowski gauge; DC programming; DCA.

Introduction

The multifacility location problem aims to place several facilities in optimal locations to minimize the total distance to a set of demand points. These problems are closely related to clustering, which plays a central role in data analysis, signal processing, and machine learning. Given a finite collection of data points, the goal of a clustering problem is to identify a prescribed number of representative centers that capture the underlying structure of the data. Classical formulations, such as the
k
-means algorithm, typically rely on squared Euclidean distances and lead to nonconvex and nonsmooth optimization models. Their theoretical analysis and numerical solution have been extensively studied in the literature; see, for instance,

Let
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a fixed compact convex set containing the origin in its interior and let
\rho_{F}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be its associated Minkowski gauge function defined by

\rho_{F}(x)=\inf\{\lambda\geq 0\mid x\in\lambda F\}.

(1)

In this paper, we propose a new model of multifacility location and clustering based on generalized distances defined by Minkowski gauges and Laplace-type regularization terms. Consider a finite number of distinct data points

\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}}\Biggl\{f(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min_{1\leq\ell\leq k}\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})+\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}-x_{t}\|^{2}\Biggr\},

(2)

centers.
where

A new model of gauge multifacility location and clustering:

The Existence of Solutions

We begin this section by recalling some basic notions and definitions from convex analysis that will be employed throughout the paper.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a nonempty subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(a)

The
distance function
associated with
\Omega
is given by

d(x;\Omega)=\inf\left\{\|x-w\|\mid w\in\Omega\right\},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(b)

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
Euclidean projection
of
x
onto
\Omega
is given as the set

P(x;\Omega)=\{\omega\in\Omega\mid\|x-\omega\|=d(x;\Omega)\}.

The
polar set

F^{\circ}
of a subset
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined by

F^{\circ}=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\langle v,x\rangle\leq 1\text{ for all }x\in F\}.

To simplify our estimates, we denote the maximum norms associated with the set
F
and its polar
F^{\circ}
as:

\|F\|=\sup\{\|x\|\mid x\in F\}\quad\text{and}\quad\|F^{\circ}\|=\sup\{\|v\|\mid v\in F^{\circ}\}.

We will use the following properties concerning the Minkowski function in our subsequent analysis. These results can be found in
[
13
, Proposition 2.1]
,
[
19
, Lemma 4.1]
, and
[
25
, Theorem 6.14 and Proposition 6.18]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\rho
be the Minkowski function defined in (
1
). Then the following properties hold:

(a)

\rho(\alpha x)=\alpha\rho(x)
for all
\alpha\geq 0
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(b)

\rho(x_{1}+x_{2})\leq\rho(x_{1})+\rho(x_{2})
for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(c)

\rho(x)=0\;\text{ if and only if }\;x=0.

(d)

\rho
is
\|F^{\circ}\|
-Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(e)

x\in{\rm bd}(F)
if and only if
\rho(x)=1,
where
{\rm bd}(F)
denotes the boundary of
F
.

(f)

\rho(x)=\max_{v\in F^{\circ}}\langle v,x\rangle
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(g)

\frac{\rho(x)}{\|F^{\circ}\|}\leq\|x\|\leq\|F\|\rho(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(h)

\rho(x)\leq\|F\|\|F^{\circ}\|\rho(-x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.1
Global Optimal Solutions

In this subsection, we establish the existence and compactness of the global optimal solution set for problem (
2
), followed by illustrative examples.

Theorem 2.3
.

The global optimal solution set
S
of problem (
2
) is nonempty and compact.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.2
(d), the Minkowski gauge function
\rho
is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Thus,
f
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
. We first show that
f
is coercive, i.e.,

\|X\|_{F}\to\infty\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(X)\to\infty,

(3)

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm on
\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
.
Let
\{X_{m}\}_{m}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
such that

\|X_{m}\|_{F}\to\infty\text{\; as }\;m\to\infty,

where
X_{m}=(x_{1,m},\dots,x_{k,m})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
. Then there exists an index
t_{0}\in\{1,\dots,k\}
such that

\|x_{t_{0},m}\|\to\infty\;\text{ as }\;m\to\infty.

We now distinguish two cases:

Case A: There exists
s_{0}\neq t_{0}
such that the sequence
\{\|x_{t_{0},m}-x_{s_{0},m}\|\}_{m}
is unbounded.

By passing to a subsequence if necessary, we may assume that

\|x_{t_{0},m}-x_{s_{0},m}\|\to\infty\;\text{ as }\;m\to\infty.

In this case, we consider the fusion term of
f
. Let
(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
be a pair of indices such that
\{s^{\prime},t^{\prime}\}=\{s_{0},t_{0}\}
with
s^{\prime}<t^{\prime}
. Then we have

\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s,m}-x_{t,m}\|^{2}\geq\frac{\lambda n}{2}\|x_{s^{\prime},m}-x_{t^{\prime},m}\|^{2}=\frac{\lambda n}{2}\|x_{t_{0},m}-x_{s_{0},m}\|^{2}\to\infty,

which implies that

f(X_{m})\to\infty\;\text{ as }\;m\to\infty.

Case B: For every
\ell=1,\dots,k
, the sequence
\{\|x_{t_{0},m}-x_{\ell,m}\|\}_{m}
is bounded.

Since
\|x_{t_{0},m}\|\to\infty
, it follows from the triangle inequality that for all
\ell=1,\dots,k
,

\|x_{\ell,m}\|\geq\|x_{t_{0},m}\|-\|x_{t_{0},m}-x_{\ell,m}\|\to\infty\;\text{ as }\;m\to\infty.

Fix any index
i_{0}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. For every
\ell=1,\dots,k
, we have

\|x_{\ell,m}-a_{i_{0}}\|\geq\|x_{\ell,m}\|-\|a_{i_{0}}\|\to\infty\quad\text{as }m\to\infty,

because
\|a_{i_{0}}\|
is a fixed constant.
Applying the lower bound property of the gauge function from Lemma
2.2
(g), for every
\ell=1,\dots,k
, we have

\rho(x_{\ell,m}-a_{i_{0}})\geq\frac{1}{\|F\|}\|x_{\ell,m}-a_{i_{0}}\|\to\infty\;\text{ as }\;m\to\infty.

Hence, the minimum over
\ell
also diverges, i.e.,

\min_{1\leq\ell\leq k}\rho(x_{\ell,m}-a_{i_{0}})\to\infty\;\text{ as }\;m\to\infty.

Consequently, the data-fidelity term satisfies

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min_{1\leq\ell\leq k}\rho(x_{\ell,m}-a_{i})\geq\min_{1\leq\ell\leq k}\rho(x_{\ell,m}-a_{i_{0}})\to\infty\;\text{ as }\;m\to\infty.

As the fusion term is non-negative, we again conclude that
f(X_{m})\to\infty
.

Combining Cases A and B, we conclude that
f
is coercive. Since
f
is continuous and coercive on the finite-dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
, the set of global optimal solutions
S
is nonempty and compact (see, e.g.,
[
31
, Theorem 1.9]
), which completes the proof.
∎

The following example shows that in the case
\lambda=0
, one cannot use coercivity
arguments to prove the existence and compactness of the global optimal
solution set to problem (
2
).

Example 2.4
.

Consider the simplest one-dimensional setting
d=1
and let
F=[-1,1]\subset\mathbb{R}.

Then the associated Minkowski gauge is the absolute value,

\rho(x)=|x|,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Take two data points
a_{1}=0,\;a_{2}=1,

and
k=2
centers. If we remove the penalty term (i.e. set
\lambda=0
),
problem (
2
) reduces to

\min_{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Big\{f(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\min_{\ell=1,2}|x_{\ell}-a_{i}|\Big\}.

Take the sequence

X_{m}=(x_{1,m},x_{2,m})=(0,m),\qquad m\in\mathbb{N}.

Then the Frobenius norm of
X_{m}
satisfies

\|X_{m}\|_{F}=\sqrt{(0)^{2}+m^{2}}=m\;\to\;\infty\;\text{ as }\;m\to\infty.

However, for each
m\geq 2
we have

f(X_{m})=\min\big(|0-0|,|m-0|\big)+\min\big(|0-1|,|m-1|\big)=0+1=1,

since the first center
x_{1,m}=0
fits
a_{1}=0
perfectly and remains the closest
center to
a_{2}=1
as soon as
m
is large.
Thus we obtain a sequence
\{X_{m}\}
with

\|X_{m}\|_{F}\to\infty\quad\text{but}\quad f(X_{m})=1\ \text{ for all }m\geq 2,

which shows that
f
is
not
coercive when
\lambda=0
.

The next example provides a simple one-dimensional illustration of Theorem
2.3
.

Example 2.5
.

Let
\{a_{1},a_{2}\}=\{0,1\}
,
k=3
centers,
F=[-1,1]
, and
\lambda=1
. Then
problem (
2
) takes the form

\min_{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Big\{f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\min_{1\leq\ell\leq 3}|x_{\ell}-a_{i}|+\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq 3}|x_{s}-x_{t}|^{2}\Big\},

that is,

\min_{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Big\{\min(|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|,|x_{3}|)+\min(|x_{1}-1|,|x_{2}-1|,|x_{3}-1|)+\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq 3}|x_{s}-x_{t}|^{2}\Big\}.

Step 1: Reduction to the interval
[0,1]
and ordered centers.

Since the objective is symmetric in
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
, we may assume without loss
of generality that

x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq x_{3}.

If
x_{1}<0
, we can move
x_{1}
to
0
, then
\min(|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|,|x_{3}|)
and all
pairwise distances involving
x_{1}
decrease (or stay the same), while

\min(|x_{1}-1|,|x_{2}-1|,|x_{3}-1|)
does not increase. A similar argument shows
that if
x_{3}>1
we can move
x_{3}
to
1
without increasing the objective.
Hence any global minimizer must satisfy

0\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq x_{3}\leq 1.

(4)

Step 2: Explicit form of the objective on
[0,1]
.

For
0\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq x_{3}\leq 1
, we have

\min(|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|,|x_{3}|)=x_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\min(|x_{1}-1|,|x_{2}-1|,|x_{3}-1|)=1-x_{3},

and thus

\sum_{i=1}^{2}\min_{1\leq\ell\leq 3}|x_{\ell}-a_{i}|=x_{1}+(1-x_{3}).

The penalty term becomes

\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq 3}|x_{s}-x_{t}|^{2}=(x_{2}-x_{1})^{2}+(x_{3}-x_{1})^{2}+(x_{3}-x_{2})^{2}.

Therefore, for any
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
satisfying (
4
), we have

f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=x_{1}+(1-x_{3})+(x_{2}-x_{1})^{2}+(x_{3}-x_{1})^{2}+(x_{3}-x_{2})^{2}.

(5)

Step 3: Minimization in terms of the gaps between centers.

Letting

u=x_{2}-x_{1}\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad v=x_{3}-x_{2}\geq 0,

we obtain

x_{2}=x_{1}+u\quad\text{and}\quad x_{3}=x_{2}+v=x_{1}+u+v.

Then the constraint
x_{3}\leq 1
becomes

x_{1}+u+v\leq 1,\qquad x_{1}\geq 0,\quad u\geq 0,\quad v\geq 0.

Substituting into (
5
) yields

\displaystyle f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})

\displaystyle=x_{1}+\bigl(1-(x_{1}+u+v)\bigr)+u^{2}+(u+v)^{2}+v^{2}

\displaystyle=1-u-v+\bigl(u^{2}+(u+v)^{2}+v^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=1-u-v+2(u^{2}+uv+v^{2}).

Thus
f
depends only on
(u,v)
, and we can write

g(u,v)=1-u-v+2(u^{2}+uv+v^{2}),\qquad u,v\geq 0,\ u+v\leq 1.

Step 4: Minimizing
g(u,v)
.

The function
g
is strictly convex quadratic in
(u,v)
, since its Hessian matrix

H_{g}(u,v)=\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\
2&4\end{pmatrix}

is constant and positive definite. Hence
g
has a unique global minimizer in

\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and any critical point in the interior of the feasible region

\{(u,v)\mid u,v\geq 0,\ u+v\leq 1\}
is the global minimizer.
Solving
\nabla g(u,v)=0
gives

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\partial g}{\partial u}=-1+4u+2v=0\\[3.99994pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\partial g}{\partial v}=-1+2u+4v=0,\end{cases}

whose unique solution is

u^{\star}=v^{\star}=\frac{1}{6}.

Since
u^{\star}+v^{\star}=\frac{1}{3}\leq 1
, this point lies in the interior of
the feasible region. Substituting into
g
yields

g(u^{\star},v^{\star})=1-\frac{1}{6}-\frac{1}{6}+2\Bigl(\Bigl(\frac{1}{6}\Bigr)^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{1}{6}+\Bigl(\frac{1}{6}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr)=\frac{5}{6}.

Step 5: Description of the optimal centers.

We have shown that any global minimizer must satisfy

u=x_{2}-x_{1}=\frac{1}{6},\qquad v=x_{3}-x_{2}=\frac{1}{6},

that is,

x_{2}=x_{1}+\frac{1}{6},\qquad x_{3}=x_{1}+\frac{1}{3}.

The constraints
0\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq x_{3}\leq 1
become

0\leq x_{1}\leq x_{1}+\frac{1}{6}\leq x_{1}+\frac{1}{3}\leq 1,

which implies

0\leq x_{1}\leq\frac{2}{3}.

Thus the set of global minimizers is the line segment

S=\Bigl\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\ \Big|\ 0\leq x_{1}\leq\frac{2}{3},\ \ x_{2}=x_{1}+\frac{1}{6},\ \ x_{3}=x_{1}+\frac{1}{3}\Bigr\},

and the minimal value is
f=\frac{5}{6}
. The solution set
S
is
clearly compact, in agreement with Theorem
2.3
.

For comparison, when
\lambda=0
(and
k^{\star}=3
) the optimal value is
0
, attained
for example by
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(0,0,1)
or
(0,1,1)
, since each demand point
can be matched exactly by at least one center. With
\lambda=1
, the penalty
term enforces repulsion among the centers and yields a nontrivial trade-off
between approximation quality and separation: in an optimal solution, the
three centers are equally spaced with gap
\frac{1}{6}
between consecutive
centers and total span
\frac{1}{3}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Consider problem (

X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}

\widehat{x}_{1},\dots,\widehat{x}_{q}
be the distinct values among

.
Then the following properties hold:

(i)

For every

(ii)

If we discard empty sets among

\{A_{s}(X)\}_{s\in J}

In particular, the number of

(i) Fix any

\ell\in\{1,\dots,k\}

\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})=\min_{r=1,\dots,k}\rho(x_{r}-a_{i}),

.
On the other hand, each index

.
Since

(ii) By the definition of

\widehat{x}_{s}\neq\widehat{x}_{s^{\prime}}
, so the
indices in
C_{s}
and
C_{s^{\prime}}
correspond to different center locations.
Thus, the
family
\{A_{s}(X)\}_{s\in I}
provides a partition of
A
associated with the
distinct centers
\{\widehat{x}_{s}\}_{s\in J}
, and hence defines a natural
clustering of
A
.

Finally, by construction we have
q\leq k
, so
|J|\leq q\leq k
. On the other
hand, the sets
A_{s}(X)
for
s\in J
are nonempty and are all subsets of
A
,
which implies
|J|\leq n
. Combining these inequalities yields

|J|\leq\min\{k,n\},

which completes the proof.
∎

We illustrate Proposition
2.7
by the following example.

Example 2.8
.

In the setting of Example
2.5
, we illustrate Proposition
2.7
by considering

X=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(0,\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}).

The three centers take only two distinct values
\widehat{x}_{1}=0
and
\widehat{x}_{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}.

Thus, we obtain
q=2
,

C_{1}=\{\ell\in\{1,2,3\}\mid x_{\ell}=\widehat{x}_{1}\}=\{1\}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{2}=\{\ell\in\{1,2,3\}\mid x_{\ell}=\widehat{x}_{2}\}=\{2,3\}.

We compute
A_{\ell}[X]
for
\ell=1,2,3
explicitly:

•

For
a_{1}=0
, we have

|x_{1}-0|=|0-0|=0,\quad|x_{2}-0|=\Bigl|\tfrac{1}{2}-0\Bigr|=\tfrac{1}{2},\quad|x_{3}-0|=\Bigl|\tfrac{1}{2}-0\Bigr|=\tfrac{1}{2},

hence
a_{1}\in A_{1}(X)
and
a_{1}\notin A_{2}(X)\cup A_{3}(X).

•

For
a_{2}=1
, we have

|x_{1}-1|=|0-1|=1,\quad|x_{2}-1|=\Bigl|\tfrac{1}{2}-1\Bigr|=\tfrac{1}{2},\quad|x_{3}-1|=\tfrac{1}{2},

so the minimum is attained at both
\ell=2
and
\ell=3
, and hence

a_{2}\in A_{2}(X)\cap A_{3}(X),\qquad a_{2}\notin A_{1}(X).

Thus

A_{1}(X)=\{a_{1}\},\qquad A_{2}(X)=\{a_{2}\},\qquad A_{3}(X)=\{a_{2}\}.

Now we regroup according to the distinct locations
\widehat{x}_{1},\widehat{x}_{2}

as in Proposition
2.7
:

A_{1}(X)=\bigcup_{\ell\in C_{1}}A_{\ell}(X)=A_{1}(X)=\{a_{1}\}\quad\text{and}\quad A_{2}(X)=\bigcup_{\ell\in C_{2}}A_{\ell}(X)=A_{2}(X)\cup A_{3}(X)=\{a_{2}\}.

Hence

A=A_{1}(X)\cup A_{2}(X)=\{a_{1},a_{2}\},

while both
A_{1}(X)
and
A_{2}(X)
are nonempty. In the notation of
Proposition
2.7
, we have

J=\{s\in\{1,2\}\mid A_{s}(X)\neq\emptyset\}=\{1,2\},

so the number of effective clusters is

|J|=2\leq\min\{k,n\}=\min\{3,2\}=2.

This example shows how several centers (here
x_{2}
and
x_{3}
) can coincide at
the same location
\widehat{x}_{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}
while still giving rise to only
one effective cluster
A_{2}(X)=\{a_{2}\}
. The family

\{A_{s}(X)\}_{s\in I}=\bigl\{\{a_{1}\},\{a_{2}\}\bigr\}
is a natural clustering of

A
associated with the distinct center locations
\widehat{x}_{1}=0
and

\widehat{x}_{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

2.2
Local Optimal Solutions

This subsection is devoted to studying local optimal solutions of problem (
2
).

For
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
and
\ell\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, set

I_{\ell}(X)=\{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\mid a_{i}\in A_{\ell}(X)\},

where
A_{\ell}(X)
is defined as in (
6
).
Then we have

A_{\ell}(X)=\{a_{i}\mid i\in I_{\ell}(X)\}.

Definition 2.9
.

An element
\overline{X}=(\bar{x}_{1},\dots,\bar{x}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
is called a

local optimal solution
of problem (
2
) if there exists

\varepsilon>0
such that

f(\overline{X})\;\leq\;f(X)\quad\text{for all }X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}\text{ with }\|x_{\ell}-\bar{x}_{\ell}\|<\varepsilon\text{ for all }\ell.

The set of all local optimal solutions will be denoted by
S_{loc}
.

As in
[
21
]
, it is convenient to encode the assignment of the cluster of each demand point in terms of an index set.

Definition 2.10
.

For
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
and
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
we set

L_{i}(X)=\bigl\{\ell\in\{1,\dots,k\}\mid a_{i}\in A_{\ell}(X)\bigr\}.

Then we obtain

\ell\in L_{i}(X)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})=\min_{r=1,\dots,k}\rho(x_{r}-a_{i}).

Thus
L_{i}(X)
is the set of indices of all nearest centers to
a_{i}
. In particular, we have

i\in I_{\ell}(X)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\ell\in L_{i}(X).

(7)

The next lemma shows that, when all nearest centers are uniquely defined, the clustering is locally stable under small perturbations of the centers.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\overline{X}=(\bar{x}_{1},\dots,\bar{x}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
be such that

L_{i}(\overline{X})
is a singleton for every
i=1,\dots,n
. Then there exists

\varepsilon>0
such that for every

X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
satisfying

\|x_{\ell}-\bar{x}_{\ell}\|<\varepsilon
for all
\ell=1,\dots,k
, we have:

(a)

L_{i}(X)=L_{i}(\overline{X})\quad\text{for all }i=1,\dots,n.

(b)

I_{\ell}(X)=I_{\ell}(\overline{X})\quad\text{and}\quad A_{\ell}(X)=A_{\ell}(\overline{X})\quad\text{for all }\ell=1,\dots,k.

Proof.

(a) For each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let
\ell(i)
be the unique element of

L_{i}(\overline{X})
. By Definition
2.6
and
2.10
, we have

\rho(\bar{x}_{\ell(i)}-a_{i})<\rho(\bar{x}_{\ell}-a_{i})\qquad\text{for all }\ell\in\{1,\dots,k\}\setminus\{\ell(i)\}.

Using the continuity of
\rho
, for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
we can choose

\varepsilon_{i}>0
such that

\rho(x_{\ell(i)}-a_{i})<\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})\qquad\text{for all }\ell\in\{1,\dots,k\}\setminus\{\ell(i)\},

whenever
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
satisfies

\|x_{\ell}-\bar{x}_{\ell}\|<\varepsilon_{i}
for all
\ell=1,\dots,k
.
For such
X
, we then have

L_{i}(X)=\{\ell(i)\}=L_{i}(\overline{X}).

Now take
\varepsilon=\min\{\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}\}
and fix any

X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
with
\|x_{\ell}-\bar{x}_{\ell}\|<\varepsilon
for all
\ell
.
From the previous paragraph we obtain
L_{i}(X)=L_{i}(\overline{X})
for all
i
,
which proves the first assertion.

(b) Fix any
\ell\in\{1,\dots,k\}
. Then it follows from (
7
) that

i\in I_{\ell}(\overline{X})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\ell\in L_{i}(\overline{X})\quad\text{and}\quad i\in I_{\ell}(X)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\ell\in L_{i}(X).

Since
L_{i}(X)=L_{i}(\overline{X})
for all
i
, the two index sets coincide:

I_{\ell}(X)=I_{\ell}(\overline{X})
. Hence we also obtain

A_{\ell}(X)=\{a_{i}\mid i\in I_{\ell}(X)\}=\{a_{i}\mid i\in I_{\ell}(\overline{X})\}=A_{\ell}(\overline{X}).

The proof is complete.
∎

We now derive a necessary optimality condition for local optimal solutions of
problem (
2
).

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
\overline{X}=(\bar{x}_{1},\dots,\bar{x}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
be such that
L_{i}(\overline{X})
is a singleton for every
i=1,\dots,n
. If
\overline{X}
is a local optimal solution of problem (
2
), then for each
\ell\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, the point
\bar{x}_{\ell}
is a global minimizer of the problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Biggl\{\phi_{\ell}(x)=\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}(\overline{X})}\rho(x-a_{i})+\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{j=1,\;j\neq\ell}^{k}\|x-\bar{x}_{j}\|^{2}\Biggr\}.

(8)

Proof.

By Lemma
2.11
, the assumption that
L_{i}(\overline{X})
is a singleton
for every
i
implies the following stability property, i.e., there exists

\varepsilon>0
such that, for all
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
with

\|x_{\ell}-\bar{x}_{\ell}\|<\varepsilon\qquad\text{for all }\ell=1,\dots,k,

(9)

we have

L_{i}(X)=L_{i}(\overline{X})\qquad\text{for all }i=1,\dots,n.

(10)

In particular, in the neighbourhood defined by (
9
), we have

I_{\ell}(X)=I_{\ell}(\overline{X})\quad\text{and}\quad A_{\ell}(X)=A_{\ell}(\overline{X})\qquad\text{for all }\ell=1,\dots,k.

(11)

Suppose now that
\overline{X}
is a local optimal solution of (
2
).
By Definition
2.9
, there exists
\delta>0
such that

f(\overline{X})\;\leq\;f(X)\quad\text{for all }X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}\text{ with }\|x_{\ell}-\bar{x}_{\ell}\|<\delta\text{ for all }\ell.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be as in (
9
), and set
\gamma=\min\{\varepsilon,\delta\}.

For a fixed index
\ell
, take any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\|x-\bar{x}_{\ell}\|<\gamma
and define

\widehat{x}_{\ell}=x\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{x}_{r}=\bar{x}_{r}\ \text{ for }r\neq\ell.

Then
\widehat{X}
satisfies (
9
), and by local optimality we have

f(\overline{X})\leq f(\widehat{X}).

(12)

To compare these two values, write

f(X)=D(X)+C(X),

(13)

where

D(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min_{1\leq\ell\leq k}\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad C(X)=\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}-x_{t}\|^{2}.

By (
10
) and (
11
), we have

D(\overline{X})=\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}(\overline{X})}\rho(\bar{x}_{\ell}-a_{i})+\sum_{i\notin I_{\ell}(\overline{X})}\min_{1\leq r\leq k}\rho(\bar{x}_{r}-a_{i}),

and

\displaystyle D(\widehat{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}(\widehat{X})}\rho(x-a_{i})+\sum_{i\notin I_{\ell}(\widehat{X})}\min_{1\leq r\leq k}\rho(\bar{x}_{r}-a_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}(\overline{X})}\rho(x-a_{i})+\sum_{i\notin I_{\ell}(\overline{X})}\min_{1\leq r\leq k}\rho(\bar{x}_{r}-a_{i}),

which implies that

D(\widehat{X})-D(\overline{X})=\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}(\overline{X})}\rho(x-a_{i})-\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}(\overline{X})}\rho(\bar{x}_{\ell}-a_{i}).

(14)

For the fusion term, we can group as follows:

C(\overline{X})=\frac{\lambda n}{2}\left(\sum_{j=1,\;j\neq\ell}^{k}\|\bar{x}_{\ell}-\bar{x}_{j}\|^{2}+\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k,\;s,t\neq\ell}\|\bar{x}_{s}-\bar{x}_{t}\|^{2}\right).

(15)

Similarly for
\widehat{X}
, we have

C(\widehat{X})=\frac{\lambda n}{2}\left(\sum_{j=1,\;j\neq\ell}^{k}\|x-\bar{x}_{j}\|^{2}+\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k,\;s,t\neq\ell}\|\bar{x}_{s}-\bar{x}_{t}\|^{2}\right).

(16)

Subtracting (
15
) from (
16
) yields

C(\widehat{X})-C(\overline{X})=\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{j=1,\;j\neq\ell}^{k}\left(\|x-\bar{x}_{j}\|^{2}-\|\bar{x}_{\ell}-\bar{x}_{j}\|^{2}\right).

Combining this with (
13
) and
(
14
), we obtain

f(\widehat{X})-f(\overline{X})=\phi_{\ell}(x)-\phi_{\ell}(\bar{x}_{\ell}),

(17)

where
\phi_{\ell}
is defined in (
8
). It follows from (
12
) that

\phi_{\ell}(\bar{x}_{\ell})\leq\phi_{\ell}(x)
for all
x
in a neighborhood of
\bar{x}_{\ell}
.
Since
\phi_{\ell}
is convex,
every local minimizer is a global minimizer. Hence

\bar{x}_{\ell}
is a global solution of (
8
) for every
\ell
,
which proves the theorem.
∎

Remark 2.13
.

When
\lambda=0
, the penalty term
C(X)
vanishes and the functions

\phi_{\ell}
in (
8
) reduce to

\phi_{\ell}(x)=\sum_{i\in I_{\ell}(\overline{X})}\rho(x-a_{i}),

so Theorem
2.12
recovers the necessary optimality condition for the
generalized multi-source Weber problem obtained in
[
21
, Theorem 3.11]
.
For
\lambda>0
, we do not claim that the converse implication holds in general.

The following example shows that the condition in Theorem
2.12
is genuinely necessary.

Example 2.14
.

Consider two data points

a_{1}=0,

a_{2}=2,

k=2
centers and
\rho(x)=|x|
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. For
X=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 1}
, the
objective function in (
2
) with
\lambda=\tfrac{1}{2}
reads

f(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\min_{\ell=1,2}|x_{\ell}-a_{i}|+\frac{1}{2}\,|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{2}.

For
\overline{X}=(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2})=(0,2)
,
we have

\min_{\ell=1,2}|\bar{x}_{\ell}-a_{1}|=\min\{|0-0|,\;|2-0|\}=0

and

\min_{\ell=1,2}|\bar{x}_{\ell}-a_{2}|=\min\{|0-2|,\;|2-2|\}=0.

Thus we obtain

f(\overline{X})=0+0+\frac{1}{2}\,|0-2|^{2}=2,

L_{1}(\overline{X})=\{1\}\quad\text{and}\quad L_{2}(\overline{X})=\{2\},

so the assumption in Theorem
2.12
(each
L_{i}(\overline{X})
is a singleton)
is satisfied.

Next, we compute the functions
\phi_{\ell}
in (
8
) at
\overline{X}
.
Since
n=2
and
k=2
, for
\ell=1
we have

\phi_{1}(x)=\sum_{i\in I_{1}(\overline{X})}\rho(x-a_{i})+\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{j=2}^{2}\rho^{2}(x-\bar{x}_{j})=|x|+\frac{1}{2}(x-2)^{2},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Similarly, for
\ell=2
we have

\phi_{2}(x)=\sum_{i\in I_{2}(\overline{X})}\rho(x-a_{i})+\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{r=1}^{1}\rho^{2}(\bar{X}_{r}-x)=|x-2|+\frac{1}{2}x^{2},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Clearly,

\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are convex on
\mathbb{R}
.
A direct computation shows that

\arg\min\phi_{1}=\{1\}\quad\text{and}\quad\arg\min\phi_{2}=\{1\},

while the centers at
\overline{X}
are

\bar{x}_{1}=0
and
\bar{x}_{2}=2.

Thus neither
\bar{x}_{1}
nor
\bar{x}_{2}
is a global minimizer of the corresponding
convex problem (
8
).
By Theorem
2.12
,
\overline{X}=(0,2)

cannot
be a local optimal solution of
problem (
2
) when
\lambda=\tfrac{1}{2}
, even though each

L_{i}(\overline{X})
is a singleton. This illustrates the
necessity
of the
condition in Theorem
2.12
.

Direct verification that
\overline{X}
is not a local minimizer for
\lambda=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Now perturb only the first center and keep the second one fixed.
For
t\in(0,1)
, consider
X(t)=(x_{1},x_{2})=(t,2).

For the demand point
a_{1}=0
, we have

|x_{1}-a_{1}|=|t-0|=t\quad\text{and}\quad|x_{2}-a_{1}|=|2-0|=2,

and hence

\min_{\ell=1,2}|x_{\ell}-a_{1}|=t.

For the demand point
a_{2}=2
, we have

|x_{1}-a_{2}|=|t-2|=2-t\quad\text{and}\quad|x_{2}-a_{2}|=|2-2|=0,

and hence

\min_{\ell=1,2}|x_{\ell}-a_{2}|=0.

The penalty term is

\frac{1}{2}\,|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\,|t-2|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\,(2-t)^{2}.

Consequently,

f(X(t))=t+\frac{1}{2}\,(2-t)^{2}=2-t+\frac{1}{2}\,t^{2}.

We compare this value with
f(\overline{X})=2
:

f(X(t))-f(\overline{X})=\bigl(2-t+\tfrac{1}{2}t^{2}\bigr)-2=-t\Bigl(1-\frac{t}{2}\Bigr).

For
t\in(0,1)
, we have
1-\tfrac{t}{2}>0
, and hence

f(X(t))-f(\overline{X})<0.

In other words,

f(X(t))<f(\overline{X})\qquad\text{for all }t\in(0,1).

Moreover,
X(t)\to\overline{X}
as
t\to 0^{+}
, so there are points arbitrarily
close to
\overline{X}
with strictly smaller objective value. Therefore,

\overline{X}=(0,2)
is
not
a local optimal solution of problem (
2
) when
\lambda=\tfrac{1}{2}
.
This direct verification is consistent with Theorem
2.12
: in this
example
L_{i}(\overline{X})
is a singleton for each
i
, but the centers
\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2}
do not minimize the corresponding convex functions

\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
, and hence
\overline{X}
cannot be locally optimal.

We conclude this subsection with an example that illustrates Theorem
2.12

and shows that the set of global optimal solutions is strictly contained in the
set of local optimal solutions.

Example 2.15
.

We work in
\mathbb{R}
and take

\rho(x)=|x|
. Consider the data set
A=\{0,3,4\}

and
k=2
centers. For
X=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 1}
, the objective function
in (
2
) reads

f(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\min_{\ell=1,2}|x_{\ell}-a_{i}|+\frac{3\lambda}{2}\,|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{2}.

We show that for small
\lambda>0
, there exists a point which is a
local, but not global, minimizer of
f
, that is,

S\subsetneq S_{loc}.

Step 1:

Let
\overline{X}=(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2})=(3,4).

Then a direct computation shows that

I_{1}(\overline{X})=\{1,2\}\quad\text{and}\quad I_{2}(\overline{X})=\{3\}.

By the
continuity of the absolute value, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that, whenever

|x_{1}-3|<\epsilon\quad\text{and}\quad|x_{2}-4|<\epsilon,

the same cluster assignment is preserved, i.e.,

I_{1}(X)=\{1,2\}\quad\text{and}\quad I_{2}(X)=\{3\}.

Moreover, shrinking
\epsilon
if necessary, we may assume that
x_{1}>0
whenever

|x_{1}-3|<\epsilon
. This ensures that
|x_{1}|=x_{1}
in this neighbourhood.
For any
X=(x_{1},x_{2})
satisfying
|x_{1}-3|<\epsilon
and
|x_{2}-4|<\epsilon
, we have

\min_{\ell=1,2}|x_{\ell}-a_{1}|=|x_{1}-0|=|x_{1}|,\quad\min_{\ell=1,2}|x_{\ell}-a_{2}|=|x_{1}-3|,\quad\min_{\ell=1,2}|x_{\ell}-a_{3}|=|x_{2}-4|.

Hence, in this neighbourhood,

f(x_{1},x_{2})=|x_{1}|+|x_{1}-3|+|x_{2}-4|+\frac{3\lambda}{2}(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}.

(18)

Moreover, we obtain

f(\overline{X})=|3|+|3-3|+|4-4|+\frac{3\lambda}{2}(3-4)^{2}=3+\frac{3\lambda}{2}.

To study local optimality, write

x_{1}=3+\delta_{1},\qquad x_{2}=4+\delta_{2},

with
|\delta_{1}|<\epsilon
and
|\delta_{2}|<\epsilon
. Since
x_{1}>0
, in this neighbourhood
we have
|x_{1}|=3+\delta_{1}
. Then it follows from (
18
) that

\displaystyle f(3+\delta_{1},4+\delta_{2})

\displaystyle=(3+\delta_{1})+|\delta_{1}|+|\delta_{2}|+\frac{3\lambda}{2}\bigl[(3+\delta_{1})-(4+\delta_{2})\bigr]^{2}

\displaystyle=3+\delta_{1}+|\delta_{1}|+|\delta_{2}|+\frac{3\lambda}{2}\,(-1+\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})^{2}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\Delta(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})

\displaystyle=f(3+\delta_{1},4+\delta_{2})-f(3,4)

\displaystyle=\delta_{1}+|\delta_{1}|+|\delta_{2}|+\frac{3\lambda}{2}\Bigl[(-1+\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})^{2}-1\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\delta_{1}+|\delta_{1}|+|\delta_{2}|-3\lambda(\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})+\frac{3\lambda}{2}(\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-3\lambda)\delta_{1}+3\lambda\delta_{2}+|\delta_{1}|+|\delta_{2}|+\frac{3\lambda}{2}(\delta_{1}-\delta_{2})^{2}.

Note that we always have

(1-3\lambda)\delta_{1}+3\lambda\delta_{2}\geq-(1-3\lambda)|\delta_{1}|-3\lambda|\delta_{2}|.

Substituting into the expression for
\Delta
and discarding the nonnegative
quadratic term, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})

\displaystyle\geq-(1-3\lambda)|\delta_{1}|-3\lambda|\delta_{2}|+|\delta_{1}|+|\delta_{2}|

\displaystyle=3\lambda|\delta_{1}|+(1-3\lambda)|\delta_{2}|.

If
0<\lambda<\tfrac{1}{3}
, then
3\lambda>0
and
1-3\lambda>0
. Therefore,
for any
(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\neq(0,0)
we have

\Delta(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})>0,

which yields that

f(3+\delta_{1},4+\delta_{2})>f(3,4)\qquad\text{for all }(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\neq(0,0)\text{ with }|\delta_{1}|,|\delta_{2}|<\epsilon.

In other words, for every
\lambda\in(0,\tfrac{1}{3})
, the point
\overline{X}=(3,4)

is a
strict local minimizer
of
f
.

Step 2:

We now compare
\overline{X}
with
X_{0}=(0,3)
. We first have

f(X_{0})=1+13.5\,\lambda.

Therefore,

f(X_{0})<f(\overline{X})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 1+13.5\lambda<3+1.5\lambda\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lambda<\frac{1}{6}.

Conclusion.

Combining Step 1 and Step 2, we see that for every
\lambda\in(0,\tfrac{1}{6})
,
the point
\overline{X}=(3,4)
satisfies:

•

\overline{X}
is a strict local minimizer of
f
(by the direct
computation in Step 1);

•

\overline{X}
is not a global minimizer of
f
, since

f(X_{0})<f(\overline{X})
(Step 2).

Hence, for any
\lambda\in(0,\tfrac{1}{6})
, we have

S\subsetneq S_{loc}.

3
Stability of the Optimal Value Function and the Solution Mapping

In this section, we study the dependence of the optimal value on the data set

A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For convenience, we identify the data set
A
with the data matrix
\mathbf{A}=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. We also equip both
\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and

\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
with the Frobenius norm

\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|a_{i}\|^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\|X\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{r=1}^{k}\|X_{r}\|^{2}}.

To emphasize the role of the data matrix
\mathbf{A}
, we write the objective function of (
2
) as

f(X;\mathbf{A})=D(X;\mathbf{A})+C(X)\quad\text{for }X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}

where

D(X;\mathbf{A})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min_{1\leq\ell\leq k}\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad C(X)=\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}-x_{t}\|^{2}.

Note that the penalty term
C(X)
does not depend on
\mathbf{A}
.

Definition 3.1
.

The
optimal value function

\mathcal{V}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{A})=\inf_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}}f(X;\mathbf{A}).

(19)

Furthermore, the
global optimal solution mapping

S\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
, which assigns each data matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
to the set of its global minimizers for problem (
2
), is given by

S(\mathbf{A})=\left\{\overline{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}\mid f(\overline{X};\mathbf{A})=\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{A})\right\}.

(20)

We first establish a Lipschitz estimate in the data for the data-fitting part

D(X;\mathbf{A})
with
X
fixed.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
be fixed. Then the mapping

\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\ni\mathbf{A}\ \longmapsto\ D(X;\mathbf{A})

is globally Lipschitz continuous with

|D(X;\mathbf{A})-D(X;\mathbf{B})|\;\leq\;\|F^{\circ}\|\sqrt{n}\,\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{F}\qquad\text{for all }\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}.

In particular, the Lipschitz constant does not depend on
X
.

Proof.

Consider two arbitrary data matrices

\mathbf{A}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
,
\mathbf{B}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. For
each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we define

\psi_{i}(\mathbf{A})=\min_{1\leq\ell\leq k}\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad\psi_{i}(\mathbf{B})=\min_{1\leq\ell\leq k}\rho(x_{\ell}-b_{i}).

Then

D(X;\mathbf{A})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{A})\quad\text{and}\quad D(X;\mathbf{B})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{B}),

which implies that

\bigl|D(X;\mathbf{A})-D(X;\mathbf{B})\bigr|=\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{A})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{B})\Bigr|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\psi_{i}(\mathbf{A})-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{B})|.

Fix
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Since
\psi_{i}
is the minimum of finitely many

\|F^{\circ}\|
–Lipschitz functions in
a_{i}
, we have

|\psi_{i}(\mathbf{A})-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{B})|\leq\max_{1\leq\ell\leq k}\bigl|\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})-\rho(x_{\ell}-b_{i})\bigr|.

Using Lemma
2.2
(b) and (g), we obtain

\bigl|\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})-\rho(x_{\ell}-b_{i})\bigr|\leq\rho(a_{i}-b_{i})\leq\|F^{\circ}\|\|a_{i}-b_{i}\|

for all
\ell=1,\dots,k
. Thus, it follows that

|\psi_{i}(\mathbf{A})-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{B})|\leq\|F^{\circ}\|\|a_{i}-b_{i}\|.

Summing over
i=1,\dots,n
and applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have

|D(X;\mathbf{A})-D(X;\mathbf{B})|\leq\|F^{\circ}\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|a_{i}-b_{i}\|\leq\|F^{\circ}\|\sqrt{n}\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{F}.

This completes
the proof.
∎

We now establish a global Lipschitz property for the optimal value function

\mathcal{V}
defined in (
19
).

Theorem 3.3
.

The optimal value function
\mathcal{V}(\cdot)
of problem (
2
)
is globally Lipschitz continuous with respect to the Frobenius norm. More
precisely,

|\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{A})-\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{B})|\;\leq\;\|F^{\circ}\|\sqrt{n}\,\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{F}\qquad\text{for all }\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and fix
\varepsilon>0
. By the definition of
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{A})
,
there exists
X_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
such that

f(X_{\varepsilon};\mathbf{A})\leq\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{A})+\varepsilon.

Then we have

\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{B})\leq f(X_{\varepsilon};\mathbf{B})=D(X_{\varepsilon};\mathbf{B})+C(X_{\varepsilon})

and

\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{A})\leq f(X_{\varepsilon};\mathbf{A})=D(X_{\varepsilon};\mathbf{A})+C(X_{\varepsilon})\leq\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{A})+\varepsilon.

Subtracting, we obtain

\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{B})-\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{A})\leq D(X_{\varepsilon};\mathbf{B})-D(X_{\varepsilon};\mathbf{A})+\varepsilon.

Meanwhile, it follows from Lemma
3.2
that

D(X_{\varepsilon};\mathbf{B})-D(X_{\varepsilon};\mathbf{A})\leq\|F^{\circ}\|\sqrt{n}\,\|\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}\|_{F},

and so

\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{B})-\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{A})\leq\|F^{\circ}\|\sqrt{n}\,\|\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}\|_{F}+\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, this yields

\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{B})-\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{A})\leq\|F^{\circ}\|\sqrt{n}\,\|\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}\|_{F}.

Interchanging the roles of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, we also obtain

\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{A})-\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{B})\leq\|F^{\circ}\|\sqrt{n}\,\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{F},

and hence

|\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{A})-\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{B})|\leq\|F^{\circ}\|\sqrt{n}\,\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{F}.

This proves the theorem.
∎

Our next goal is to investigate the semicontinuity properties of the global optimal solution mapping (
20
).
We begin by recalling the definition of upper semicontinuity for set-valued mappings, see
[
10
, p. 109]
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
G:\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
be a set-valued mapping. We say that
G
is
upper semicontinuous
at
\mathbf{\overline{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
if for every open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
such that
G(\mathbf{\overline{A}})\subset U
, there exists an open neighborhood
V
of
\mathbf{\overline{A}}
such that

G(\mathbf{A})\subset U\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{A}\in V.

The mapping
G
is said to be upper semicontinuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
if it is upper semicontinuous at every point
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
.

Theorem 3.5
.

The global optimal solution mapping
S:\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
defined in (
20
) is upper semicontinuous.

Proof.

Fix any
\overline{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. Suppose on the contrary that
S
is not upper semicontinuous at
\overline{\mathbf{A}}
. Then there exists an open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
with
S(\overline{\mathbf{A}})\subset U
such that for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, one can find
\mathbf{A}_{m}\in B(\overline{\mathbf{A}},1/m)
and
X_{m}\in S(\mathbf{A}_{m})
with

X_{m}\notin U.

Since
\mathbf{A}_{m}\to\overline{\mathbf{A}}
and the optimal value function
\mathcal{V}
is continuous by Theorem
3.3
, we have

f(X_{m};\mathbf{A}_{m})=\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{A}_{m})\longrightarrow\mathcal{V}(\bar{\mathbf{A}}),

so the sequence
\{f(X_{m};\mathbf{A}_{m})\}_{m}
is bounded.
We now show that the sequence
\{X_{m}\}_{m}
is bounded. Assume on the contrary that it is unbounded. Then there exists a subsequence (not relabelled) such that

\|X_{m}\|_{F}\to\infty.

By Lemma
3.2
, there is a constant
L>0
such that

|f(X_{m};\mathbf{A}_{m})-f(X_{m};\bar{\mathbf{A}})|\leq L\|\mathbf{A}_{m}-\overline{\mathbf{A}}\|_{F}\longrightarrow 0,

which implies that
\{f(X_{m};\overline{\mathbf{A}})\}_{m}
is also bounded. On the other hand, for the fixed data matrix
\overline{\mathbf{A}}
, the proof in Theorem
2.3
shows that
f(\cdot;\overline{\mathbf{A}})
is coercive in
X
, so
\|X_{m}\|_{F}\to\infty
forces
f(X_{m};\overline{\mathbf{A}})\to\infty
, which is a contradiction. Therefore,
\{X_{m}\}_{m}
must be bounded. By the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, there exists a subsequence (still denoted by
\{X_{m}\}
) and some
\overline{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
such that
X_{m}\to\overline{X}
. Since each
X_{m}\notin U
and the complement of
U
is closed, we have

\bar{X}\notin U.

(21)

Because
f
is continuous in both variables, we obtain

f(X_{m};\mathbf{A}_{m})\longrightarrow f(\overline{X};\overline{\mathbf{A}}).

Recalling that
f(X_{m};\mathbf{A}_{m})=\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{A}_{m})
and
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{A}_{m})\to\mathcal{V}(\overline{\mathbf{A}})
, we conclude that

f(\overline{X};\overline{\mathbf{A}})=\mathcal{V}(\overline{\mathbf{A}}),

which means
\overline{X}\in S(\overline{\mathbf{A}})\subset U
. This contradicts (
21
). Hence,
S
is upper semicontinuous at
\overline{\mathbf{A}}
. Since
\overline{\mathbf{A}}
was arbitrary, the mapping
S
is upper semicontinuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
.
∎

4
Computational Framework via Smoothing and DC Programming

In this section, we develop a DC-based algorithmic framework for minimizing the nonsmooth, nonconvex objective function
f
. The approach is built on an exact DC decomposition, Nesterov-type smoothing, and efficient computation of gradients and subgradients of the DC components.
Then we use this model to determine the optimal number of centers in multifacility location problems.

4.1
DC Decomposition and Nesterov Smoothing

We begin by expressing the objective function of (
2
) in the form of a
difference-of-convex (DC) decomposition.
Observe first that for each
i=1,\ldots,n
, the assignment term can be written as

\min_{1\leq\ell\leq k}\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})-\max_{r=1,\ldots,k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1,\;\ell\neq r\end{subarray}}^{k}\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i}).

(22)

Substituting (
22
) into (
2
) yields

\displaystyle f(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})-\max_{r=1,\ldots,k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1,\;\ell\neq r\end{subarray}}^{k}\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})\right]+\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}-x_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})+\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}-x_{t}\|^{2}\Bigg)-\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{r=1,\ldots,k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1,\;\ell\neq r\end{subarray}}^{k}\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})\Bigg),

that is,

f(X)=g(X)-h(X),

with

\displaystyle g(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})+\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}-x_{t}\|^{2},

\displaystyle h(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{r=1,\ldots,k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1,\;\ell\neq r\end{subarray}}^{k}\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i}).

Clearly, both
g
and
h
are convex, so
f
is a DC function.

Remark 4.1
.

Direct DC algorithms based on convex conjugates would require an explicit expression for the Fenchel conjugate of
\rho
, which is generally unavailable. To obtain a practical scheme, we instead smooth the gauge
\rho
and work with a differentiable approximation while preserving the DC structure.

Recall from Lemma
2.2
(f) that

\rho(z)=\max_{y\in F^{\circ}}\langle z,y\rangle.

Following the Nesterov smoothing technique introduced in
[
29
]
and developed in
[
27
,
26
]
, for a parameter
\mu>0
, a smooth approximation of the Minkowski gauge function is given by

\rho_{\mu}(z)=\max_{y\in F^{\circ}}\left\{\langle z,y\rangle-\frac{\mu}{2}\|y\|^{2}\right\}=\frac{1}{2\mu}\|z\|^{2}-\frac{\mu}{2}\left[d\!\left(\frac{z}{\mu};F^{\circ}\right)\right]^{2}.

(23)

Next, we define a smoothed objective function by replacing the gauge function
\rho
with its smooth approximation
\rho_{\mu}
as follows:

f_{\mu}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\rho_{\mu}(x_{\ell}-a_{i})+\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}-x_{t}\|^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{r=1,\ldots,k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1,\;\ell\neq r\end{subarray}}^{k}\rho_{\mu}(x_{\ell}-a_{i}).

(24)

Using (
23
), we obtain a DC decomposition
f_{\mu}=g_{\mu}-h_{\mu}
with

g_{\mu}(X)=\frac{1}{2\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\|x_{\ell}-a_{i}\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}-x_{t}\|^{2}

(25)

and

h_{\mu}(X)=\frac{\mu}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\left[d\!\left(\frac{x_{\ell}-a_{i}}{\mu};F^{\circ}\right)\right]^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{r=1,\dots,k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1,\;\ell\neq r\end{subarray}}^{k}\rho_{\mu}(x_{\ell}-a_{i}).

(26)

Here, both
g_{\mu}
and
h_{\mu}
are convex functions. Moreover, since
\rho_{\mu}(z)\to\rho(z)
as
\mu\downarrow 0
, it follows that
f_{\mu}(X)\to f(X)
pointwise for any
X\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
.

4.2
Subgradient of the Concave Part and DCA Step

A crucial step in each iteration of the DCA and BDCA schemes is the computation of a subgradient

Y(X)\in\partial h_{\mu}(X)
. For computational convenience, we decompose

h_{\mu}
as
h_{\mu}=h^{(1)}_{\mu}+h^{(2)}
, where

h^{(1)}_{\mu}(X)=\frac{\mu}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\left[d\!\left(\frac{x_{\ell}-a_{i}}{\mu};F^{\circ}\right)\right]^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad h^{(2)}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{r=1,\dots,k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
\ell\neq r\end{subarray}}^{k}\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i}).

Gradient of
h^{(1)}_{\mu}
.

The function
h^{(1)}_{\mu}
is continuously differentiable
(see, e.g.,
[
27
, Proposition 3.1]
), and its gradient with respect to

x_{p}
is given by

\nabla_{x_{p}}h^{(1)}_{\mu}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{x_{p}-a_{i}}{\mu}-P\!\left(\frac{x_{p}-a_{i}}{\mu};F^{\circ}\right)\right).

Subgradients of
h^{(2)}
.

For each
i=1,\ldots,n
, define

Q_{i,r}(X)=\sum_{\ell=1,\ \ell\neq r}^{k}\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i}),\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{i}(X)=\max_{r=1,\ldots,k}Q_{i,r}(X).

By construction,

h^{(2)}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}(X).

Since
h^{(2)}
is a finite sum of convex functions, its subdifferential satisfies

\partial h^{(2)}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial Q_{i}(X).

Therefore, it suffices to compute a subgradient

V_{i}\in\partial Q_{i}(X)
for each
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Fix
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. To compute
V_{i}
, we proceed as follows:

\circ

Choose any active index

r_{i}\in\arg\max_{r=1,\ldots,k}Q_{i,r}(X).

\circ

Define

V_{i}=(v_{i1},\ldots,v_{ik})

with components given by

–

v_{ir_{i}}=0
, since
x_{r_{i}}
does not appear in
Q_{i,r_{i}}(X)
;

–

for each
\ell\neq r_{i}
,

v_{i\ell}\in\partial\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i}).

Then
V_{i}\in\partial Q_{i}(X)
, and consequently

\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}\in\partial h^{(2)}(X).

A subgradient
Y(X)\in\partial h_{\mu}(X)
is obtained by summing the subgradients of these two components:

Y=Y^{1}+Y^{2},

where
Y^{1}\in\partial h^{1}_{\mu}(X)
and
Y^{2}\in\partial h^{(2)}(X)
.

4.3
DCA Iterations and Solution of the Subproblem

For a fixed smoothing parameter
\mu>0
, we consider the smoothed DC decomposition

f_{\mu}(X)=g_{\mu}(X)-h_{\mu}(X).

Given the current iterate
X^{(t)}
, following the standard DCA framework
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
, the update is defined by

Y^{(t)}\in\partial h_{\mu}(X^{(t)}),\qquad X^{(t+1)}\in\arg\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}}\{g_{\mu}(X)-\langle Y^{(t)},X\rangle\}.

(27)

Since
g_{\mu}
is strongly convex in our formulation, the minimizer
X^{(t+1)}
is unique and can be characterized by the first-order optimality condition

\nabla g_{\mu}(X^{(t+1)})=Y^{(t)}.

(28)

The gradient of
g_{\mu}
.

In our specific case with the squared Euclidean fusion penalty, the convex component is given by

g_{\mu}(X)=\frac{1}{2\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\|x_{\ell}-a_{i}\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}-x_{t}\|^{2}.

Differentiating
g_{\mu}
with respect to each prototype
x_{p}
yields

\nabla_{x_{p}}g_{\mu}(X)=\frac{n}{\mu}(x_{p}-\bar{\mathbf{A}})+\lambda n\sum_{j\neq p}(x_{p}-x_{j}),

(29)

where
\bar{\mathbf{A}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
is the centroid of the data points.

DCA update via linear systems.

Substituting (
29
) into the optimality condition (
28
), the DCA subproblem reduces to solving a coupled linear system for
p=1,\dots,k
:

\left(\frac{1}{\mu}+\lambda(k-1)\right)n\,x_{p}^{(t+1)}-\lambda n\sum_{j\neq p}x_{j}^{(t+1)}=y_{p}^{(t)}+\frac{n}{\mu}\bar{\mathbf{A}}.

(30)

Set

B_{p}=y_{p}^{(t)}+\frac{n}{\mu}\bar{\mathbf{A}}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma^{(t+1)}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}^{(t+1)}.

Summing (
30
) over all
p
yields
\sigma^{(t+1)}=\frac{\mu}{n}\sum_{p=1}^{k}B_{p}
. Consequently, each prototype can be updated by

x_{p}^{(t+1)}=\frac{B_{p}+\lambda n\sigma^{(t+1)}}{n(\mu^{-1}+\lambda k)},\quad p=1,\dots,k.

(31)

This formulation avoids the need for matrix inversion or iterative solvers within the DCA loop, reducing the update cost to
\mathcal{O}(kd)
.

6
Convergence Analysis

In this section, we establish the fundamental convergence properties of the DCA-type schemes for the smoothed Minkowski clustering model. Throughout the analysis, we fix the parameters
\lambda>0
and
\mu>0
, and consider the DC decomposition
f_{\mu}=g_{\mu}-h_{\mu}
derived in Section
4
.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex set. A function
g:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be strongly convex with parameter
\gamma>0
if for all
x,y\in\Omega
and any
\alpha\in[0,1]
, the following inequality holds:

g(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y)\leq\alpha g(x)+(1-\alpha)g(y)-\frac{\gamma}{2}\alpha(1-\alpha)|x-y|^{2}.

Remark 6.2
.

As stated in
[
12
, p. 459]
or in
[
28
, Definition 2.1.2 and Theorem 2.1.11]
, we have

(a)

a continuously differentiable function
g
is strongly convex with parameter
\gamma>0
if it satisfies the following quadratic lower bound:

g(y)\geq g(x)+\langle\nabla g(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\quad\text{for all }x,y\in\Omega

(b)

if
g
is twice continuously differentiable, then
g

is strongly convex with parameter
\gamma>0

if and only if its Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}g(x)
satisfies

\nabla^{2}g(x)\succeq\gamma I_{d},\quad\forall x\in\Omega,

where
I_{d}
is the identity matrix and
\succeq
denotes the Loewner partial order (i.e.,
\nabla^{2}g(x)-\gamma I_{d}
is positive semidefinite). Since the Hessian matrix is symmetric, this condition is equivalent to requiring that all eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}g(x)
are bounded below by
\gamma
.

In the following theorem, we establish the fundamental analytical properties of the function
g_{\mu}
.

Theorem 6.3
.

The function
g_{\mu}\colon\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by (
25
) is twice continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
and possesses the following properties:

(a)

The Hessian matrix of
g_{\mu}
is given by

\nabla^{2}g_{\mu}(X)=\frac{n}{\mu}I_{kd}+\lambda n(L_{G}\otimes I_{d}),\quad\forall X\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}.

(32)

(b)

g_{\mu}
is strongly convex with parameter
\gamma=\frac{n}{\mu}
. That is, for all
X,X^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
, one has

g_{\mu}(X^{\prime})\geq g_{\mu}(X)+\langle\nabla g_{\mu}(X),X^{\prime}-X\rangle+\frac{n}{2\mu}\|X^{\prime}-X\|_{F}^{2}.

(c)

The gradient
\nabla g_{\mu}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L=\frac{n}{\mu}+\lambda nk
. That is, for all
X,X^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
, one has

\|\nabla g_{\mu}(X^{\prime})-\nabla g_{\mu}(X)\|_{F}\leq\left(\frac{n}{\mu}+\lambda nk\right)\|X^{\prime}-X\|_{F}.

Proof.

It follows from (
25
) that
g_{\mu}
is decomposed as

g_{\mu}(X)=g_{\mu}^{(1)}(X)+g_{\mu}^{(2)}(X),

where

g_{\mu}^{(1)}(X)=\frac{1}{2\mu}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{\ell}-a_{i}\|^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad g_{\mu}^{(2)}(X)=\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}-x_{t}\|^{2}.

Since both
g_{\mu}^{(1)}
and
g_{\mu}^{(2)}
are quadratic forms in
X
,
g_{\mu}
is twice continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
.

(a) We prove this statement in three steps.

Step 1: The Hessian matrix of
g_{\mu}^{(1)}
.

Write the prototype matrix as

X=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}^{\top}\\
x_{2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
x_{k}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d},

so that
X
can be viewed as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{kd}
. Moreover, we have

g_{\mu}^{(1)}(X)=\frac{1}{2\mu}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{\ell}-a_{i}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2\mu}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\|x_{\ell}\|^{2}-2\langle x_{\ell},a_{i}\rangle+\|a_{i}\|^{2}\big).

Note that

\frac{1}{2\mu}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{\ell}\|^{2}=\frac{n}{2\mu}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\|x_{\ell}\|^{2}.

Thus, for each
\ell=1,\dots,k
, the gradient and Hessian with respect to
x_{\ell}
are

\nabla_{x_{\ell}}g_{\mu}^{(1)}(X)=\frac{n}{\mu}x_{\ell}-\frac{1}{\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla_{x_{\ell}}^{2}g_{\mu}^{(1)}(X)=\frac{n}{\mu}I_{d},

where
I_{d}
is the identity matrix.
Moreover,

\nabla_{x_{\ell}x_{j}}^{2}g_{\mu}^{(1)}(X)=0\quad\text{for }\ell\neq j.

Therefore, the full Hessian of
g_{\mu}^{(1)}
in block-matrix form is

\nabla^{2}g_{\mu}^{(1)}(X)=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{n}{\mu}I_{d}&\mathbf{0}&\dots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\frac{n}{\mu}I_{d}&\dots&\mathbf{0}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\dots&\frac{n}{\mu}I_{d}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{n}{\mu}I_{kd}.

(33)

All eigenvalues of this block diagonal matrix are equal to
\frac{n}{\mu}
, so
g_{\mu}^{(1)}
is strongly convex with parameter
\frac{n}{\mu}
.

Step 2: The Hessian matrix of
g_{\mu}^{(2)}
.

We now rewrite the fusion term
g_{\mu}^{(2)}
in matrix form. Recall the Laplacian matrix of the complete graph
K_{k}
:

L_{G}=kI_{k}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}k-1&-1&\cdots&-1\\
-1&k-1&\cdots&-1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-1&-1&\cdots&k-1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},

where
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is the vector of ones.
Next, we show that

\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}-x_{t}\|^{2}=\mathrm{tr}(X^{\top}L_{G}X).

(34)

First, we compute
L_{G}X
explicitly. Using the block structure,

L_{G}X=\begin{bmatrix}k-1&-1&\cdots&-1\\
-1&k-1&\cdots&-1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-1&-1&\cdots&k-1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}^{\top}\\
x_{2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
x_{k}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}^{\top}\\
y_{2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
y_{k}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},

where each row
y_{s}^{\top}
is given by

y_{s}=(k-1)x_{s}-\sum_{t\neq s}x_{t},\quad s=1,\dots,k.

Then we obtain

X^{\top}L_{G}X\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(X^{\top}L_{G}X)=\sum_{s=1}^{k}x_{s}^{\top}y_{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{k}x_{s}^{\top}\left((k-1)x_{s}-\sum_{t\neq s}x_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=(k-1)\sum_{s=1}^{k}\|x_{s}\|^{2}-\sum_{s=1}^{k}\sum_{t\neq s}\langle x_{s},x_{t}\rangle.

On the other hand, by expanding the sum of pairwise squared distances, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}-x_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\big(\|x_{s}\|^{2}+\|x_{t}\|^{2}-2\langle x_{s},x_{t}\rangle\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}\|^{2}+\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{t}\|^{2}-2\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\langle x_{s},x_{t}\rangle.

Since each
\|x_{s}\|^{2}
appears exactly
k-1
times in the first two sums, we have

\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}\|^{2}+\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{t}\|^{2}=(k-1)\sum_{s=1}^{k}\|x_{s}\|^{2}.

Moreover,

2\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\langle x_{s},x_{t}\rangle=\sum_{s\neq t}\langle x_{s},x_{t}\rangle.

Therefore,

\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}-x_{t}\|^{2}=(k-1)\sum_{s=1}^{k}\|x_{s}\|^{2}-\sum_{s\neq t}\langle x_{s},x_{t}\rangle.

So we obtain

\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}-x_{t}\|^{2}=\mathrm{tr}(X^{\top}L_{G}X),

which proves (
34
), and thus

g_{\mu}^{(2)}(X)=\frac{\lambda n}{2}\sum_{1\leq s<t\leq k}\|x_{s}-x_{t}\|^{2}=\frac{\lambda n}{2}\mathrm{tr}(X^{\top}L_{G}X).

Now, the Hessian of
g_{\mu}^{(2)}
can be written in block form as

\nabla^{2}g_{\mu}^{(2)}(X)=n\lambda\,(L_{G}\otimes I_{d})=n\lambda\begin{bmatrix}(k-1)I_{d}&-I_{d}&\dots&-I_{d}\\
-I_{d}&(k-1)I_{d}&\dots&-I_{d}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-I_{d}&-I_{d}&\dots&(k-1)I_{d}\end{bmatrix}.

(35)

It is well known that for the complete graph
K_{k}
, the Laplacian
L_{G}
is positive semidefinite with eigenvalues

\{0,k,k,\dots,k\}.

The eigenvalues of the Kronecker product
L_{G}\otimes I_{d}
are all eigenvalues of
L_{G}
repeated
d
times. Hence, the eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}g_{\mu}^{(2)}(X)=n\lambda(L_{G}\otimes I_{d})
are

\{0,n\lambda k,n\lambda k,\dots,n\lambda k\},

so
\nabla^{2}g_{\mu}^{(2)}(X)
is positive semidefinite.

Step 3: The full Hessian of
g_{\mu}
.

Combining the two Hessian matrices (
33
) and (
35
), we obtain

\nabla^{2}g_{\mu}(X)=\nabla^{2}g_{\mu}^{(1)}(X)+\nabla^{2}g_{\mu}^{(2)}(X)=\frac{n}{\mu}I_{kd}+n\lambda(L_{G}\otimes I_{d}),

(36)

which proves statement (a).

(b) It follows from (
36
) that

\nabla^{2}g_{\mu}(X)=\frac{n}{\mu}I_{kd}+n\lambda(L_{G}\otimes I_{d})\succeq\frac{n}{\mu}I_{kd}.

By Remark
6.2
(a),
g_{\mu}
is
\gamma
-strongly convex with
\gamma=\frac{n}{\mu}
in the sense of the Loewner ordering.
Moreover, it follows from Remark
6.2
(b) that for all
X,X^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
,

g_{\mu}(X^{\prime})\geq g_{\mu}(X)+\langle\nabla g_{\mu}(X),X^{\prime}-X\rangle+\frac{n}{2\mu}\|X^{\prime}-X\|_{F}^{2},

which is exactly statement (b).

(c) The gradient
\nabla g_{\mu}
is an affine mapping (specifically, it is linear in its quadratic components). In the space of matrices
\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
equipped with the Frobenius norm, the Jacobian of the gradient corresponds to the constant Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}g_{\mu}
. It is well-known that the Lipschitz constant
L
of the gradient
\nabla g_{\mu}
is given by the spectral norm of its Hessian matrix (see, e.g.,
[
12
]
), namely,

L=\|\nabla^{2}g_{\mu}\|_{2}=\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}g_{\mu}),

where
\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}g_{\mu})
denotes the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian.
Recall from Statement (a) that the Hessian possesses a structured block form:

\nabla^{2}g_{\mu}=\frac{n}{\mu}I_{kd}+n\lambda(L_{G}\otimes I_{d}).

(37)

By substituting the explicit form of the complete graph Laplacian
L_{G}=kI_{k}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}
, the second term in (
37
) reveals the following block-matrix structure:

n\lambda(L_{G}\otimes I_{d})=n\lambda\begin{bmatrix}(k-1)I_{d}&-I_{d}&\dots&-I_{d}\\
-I_{d}&(k-1)I_{d}&\dots&-I_{d}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-I_{d}&-I_{d}&\dots&(k-1)I_{d}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{kd\times kd}.

To compute
\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}g_{\mu})
, we utilize the properties of the Kronecker product. The eigenvalues of
L_{G}\otimes I_{d}
are exactly the eigenvalues of
L_{G}
, each repeated
d
times. Since
L_{G}
is the Laplacian of a complete graph
K_{k}
, its spectrum is known to be
\sigma(L_{G})=\{0,k,k,\dots,k\}
. Consequently, the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian is

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}g_{\mu})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{n}{\mu}I_{kd}+n\lambda(L_{G}\otimes I_{d})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{\mu}+n\lambda\cdot\lambda_{\max}(L_{G})

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{\mu}+\lambda nk.

Thus, we obtain
L=\frac{n}{\mu}+\lambda nk
and for all
X,X^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
, the following Lipschitz inequality holds

\|\nabla g_{\mu}(X^{\prime})-\nabla g_{\mu}(X)\|_{F}\leq\left(\frac{n}{\mu}+\lambda nk\right)\|X^{\prime}-X\|_{F},

which completes the proof of Statement (c).
∎

The following proposition summarizes basic convexity and regularity properties of the smoothed function
h_{\mu}.

Proposition 6.4
.

The function
h_{\mu}
is convex and locally Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
.

Proof.

By the definition of the smoothed model (
26
), the function
h_{\mu}
is decomposed as

h_{\mu}(X)=h_{\mu}^{(1)}(X)+h_{\mu}^{(2)}(X),

where

h_{\mu}^{(1)}(X)=\frac{1}{2\mu}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{2}(x_{\ell}-a_{i};F^{\circ})

and

h^{(2)}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{r=1,\dots,k}\left\{\sum_{\ell=1,\ell\neq r}^{k}\rho(x_{\ell}-a_{i})\right\}.

Therefore, it is not difficult to verify that
h_{\mu}
is convex. Moreover, since
h_{\mu}
is finite, it is locally Lipschitz continuous (see, e.g.,
[
25
, Corollary 1.63]
).
∎

We now provide the main convergence result for the sequence generated by the proposed DCA scheme.

Theorem 6.5

(Monotone Descent and Stationarity)
.

Let
\{X^{(t)}\}_{t\geq 0}
be the sequence of iterates generated by Algorithm
1

for the DC decomposition
f_{\mu}=g_{\mu}-h_{\mu}
. Then:

(a)

The sequence
\{X^{(t)}\}
is bounded and satisfies

f_{\mu}(X^{(t)})-f_{\mu}(X^{(t+1)})\;\geq\;\frac{n}{2\mu}\,\|X^{(t+1)}-X^{(t)}\|_{F}^{2}\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

(b)

The sequence of objective values
\{f_{\mu}(X^{(t)})\}
is nonincreasing and converges to a finite value.

(c)

Every accumulation point
X^{\star}
of
\{X^{(t)}\}
is a critical point of
f_{\mu}
, i.e.,

\nabla g_{\mu}(X^{\star})\in\partial h_{\mu}(X^{\star}).

Proof.

At each step
t
,
X^{(t+1)}
is obtained by solving the linear system (
30
),
which is the optimality condition for the subproblem

\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}}\{g_{\mu}(X)-\langle Y^{(t)},X\rangle\},\qquad Y^{(t)}\in\partial h_{\mu}(X^{(t)}).

It follows from Theorem
6.3
(a) that
g_{\mu}
is
(n/\mu)
-strongly convex
and

g_{\mu}(X)\;\geq\;g_{\mu}(Z)+\langle\nabla g_{\mu}(Z),X-Z\rangle+\frac{n}{2\mu}\,\|X-Z\|_{F}^{2}\quad\text{for all }X,Z\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}.

Applying this inequality with
X=X^{(t)}
and
Z=X^{(t+1)}
and using the optimality condition
(
28
), we obtain

g_{\mu}(X^{(t)})\;\geq\;g_{\mu}(X^{(t+1)})+\langle Y^{(t)},X^{(t)}-X^{(t+1)}\rangle+\frac{n}{2\mu}\,\|X^{(t)}-X^{(t+1)}\|_{F}^{2}.

On the other hand, by convexity of
h_{\mu}
and
Y^{(t)}\in\partial h_{\mu}(X^{(t)})
, we have

h_{\mu}(X^{(t+1)})\;\geq\;h_{\mu}(X^{(t)})+\langle Y^{(t)},X^{(t+1)}-X^{(t)}\rangle.

Adding these inequalities yields

f_{\mu}(X^{(t)})-f_{\mu}(X^{(t+1)})\;\geq\;\frac{n}{2\mu}\,\|X^{(t+1)}-X^{(t)}\|_{F}^{2},

which proves the descent inequality in (a). In particular,
\{f_{\mu}(X^{(t)})\}
is nonincreasing.

From the representation of
\rho_{\mu}
in (
23
) and the compactness of
F^{\circ}
,
there exist constants
c_{1}>0
and
c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\rho_{\mu}(z)\geq c_{1}\|z\|-c_{2}\qquad\text{for all }z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Using the same arguments as in the proof of Theorem
2.3
, it follows that
f_{\mu}
is coercive and hence all its sublevel sets

\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid f_{\mu}(X)\leq f_{\mu}(X^{(0)})\}

are bounded. Since
\{X^{(t)}\}
remains in the initial
sublevel set by monotonicity, the sequence
\{X^{(t)}\}
is bounded. Furthermore,
f_{\mu}
is
bounded below, so
\{f_{\mu}(X^{(t)})\}
converges to a finite value, which proves (b).

Let
X^{\star}
be an accumulation point of
\{X^{(t)}\}
, and let
\{t_{j}\}
be such that

X^{(t_{j})}\to X^{\star}
. Summing the inequality in (a) over
t
shows that

\sum_{t}\|X^{(t+1)}-X^{(t)}\|_{F}^{2}<\infty,

which implies that

\|X^{(t_{j}+1)}-X^{(t_{j})}\|_{F}\to 0.

Thus,
X^{(t_{j}+1)}\to X^{\star}
as well.
By Theorem
6.3
(c),
\nabla g_{\mu}
is continuous, and hence we have

Y^{(t_{j})}=\nabla g_{\mu}(X^{(t_{j}+1)})\longrightarrow\nabla g_{\mu}(X^{\star}).

Since
Y^{(t_{j})}\in\partial h_{\mu}(X^{(t_{j})})
and the graph of
\partial h_{\mu}
is closed (see, e.g.,
[
30
, Theorem 24.4]
),
we obtain

\nabla g_{\mu}(X^{\star})\in\partial h_{\mu}(X^{\star}),

which shows that
X^{\star}
is a critical point of
f_{\mu}
and proves (c).
∎

7
Numerical Experiments

This section presents numerical experiments designed to evaluate the behavior of LDCA‑K across a range of synthetic and real datasets exhibiting diverse geometric properties. All computations were performed on a MacBook Air equipped with an Apple M4 processor and 16 GB of unified memory. The implementation was written in Python using standard numerical libraries for linear algebra and optimization.

7.1
Test Datasets

We consider four datasets—one real and three synthetic—chosen to represent increasing levels of geometric complexity and cluster ambiguity. The ground-truth number of clusters is denoted by
k^{\star}
. Across four datasets—three synthetic with known ground truth and the
Fisher–Iris dataset—the method reliably recovers the correct cluster count in
all settings. These results demonstrate that the fusion mechanism provides effective and automatic model complexity control with minimal tuning.

Fisher Iris dataset.

The Iris dataset consists of
n=150
observations in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, corresponding to sepal and petal measurements from three species. The ground-truth number of clusters is
k^{\star}=3
. One class (
setosa
) is well separated, while the remaining two (
versicolor
and
virginica
) exhibit substantial overlap, making this a classical benchmark for testing clustering algorithms under weak separation.

Synthetic Laplace data 1 (
k^{\star}=3
).

This dataset comprises three well-separated clusters in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, each generated from a Laplace distribution with identical scale parameters. Each cluster contains
150
observations. The geometry is symmetric and strongly separated, aligning closely with the noise model assumed by LDCA-K.

Synthetic Laplace data 2 (
k^{\star}=4
).

This dataset extends the previous construction to four Laplace-distributed clusters in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, each containing
100
observations. All clusters remain well separated and balanced, but the additional cluster introduces more complex interactions between prototype fusion and deletion dynamics.

Synthetic Gaussian data (
k^{\star}=4
).

This dataset consists of four Gaussian clusters in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with 200 samples per cluster and moderate inter‑cluster separation. To increase difficulty, the fourth cluster is placed at the centroid of the remaining three, thereby creating overlapping attraction basins. Compared to the Laplace datasets, this setting is intentionally adversarial for LDCA‑K, which—by design—draws prototypes toward their mean and can therefore obscure the correct model order when clusters are partially co-located.

7.2
Empirical Behavior and Practical Parameter Selection

A central challenge in prototype–based clustering models with both fusion
(
\lambda
) and smoothing (
\mu
) penalties is choosing regularization
strengths that yield stable, interpretable solutions. Our aim here is
not to optimize hyperparameters exhaustively, but to document a simple,
repeatable procedure that behaves consistently across heterogeneous datasets.
We report what was done, what was observed, and where the evidence should be
interpreted with caution.

Regularization path construction (warm starts).

For each dataset, we initialized the model with a modestly over–specified set
of prototypes (
k_{0}=10
). We then constructed a paired, one–dimensional
trajectory of regularization parameters

\bigl\{(\lambda_{t},\mu_{t})\bigr\}_{t=1}^{N},\qquad\lambda_{t}\in\mathrm{GeomSpace}\!\left(10^{-2},\,2.0,\,N\right),\quad\mu_{t}\in\mathrm{GeomSpace}\!\left(2.0,\,10^{-4},\,N\right),

with
N=100
. The path is traversed sequentially so that
\lambda_{t}

monotonically increases while
\mu_{t}
monotonically decreases. Critically, we
use
warm starts
: the surviving prototypes obtained at step
t
serve as
the initialization for step
t{+}1
. This produces a coherent, piecewise
continuous trajectory of solutions that reflects how the model simplifies the
prototype representation as fusion pressure strengthens (larger
\lambda
) and
smoothing weakens (smaller
\mu
).

Per–step procedure and logged quantities.

At each
(\lambda_{t},\mu_{t})
along the path we:
(i) run the DCA solver to convergence starting from the warm–started
prototypes; (ii) prune empty prototypes; and (iii) record
summary statistics of the resulting partition: the number of surviving
prototypes, and the minimum, maximum, mean, and standard deviation of cluster
sizes after nearest–prototype assignment. This yields a complete “regularization
path” of prototype counts and size statistics as functions of
t
(and thus
implicitly of
(\lambda,\mu)
).

Observed behavior.

Across all four datasets we observed a consistent pattern along the warm–started
path:

1.

Rapid reduction from
k_{0}
to the data–driven regime.

After a short initial segment at small
\lambda
(large
\mu
), the number
of prototypes drops quickly from the over–specified
k_{0}{=}10
to a
smaller, stable range.

2.

Stabilization at the known cluster count.

On the three synthetic datasets, the path stabilizes at the ground–truth
number of clusters (
k{=}3
or
k{=}4
) and remains there over the vast
majority of the trajectory. On
Iris
, the path stabilizes at

k{=}3
, consistent with the three species.

3.

Interpretable size statistics.

Once stabilized, the cluster–size statistics remain steady and
dataset–appropriate: nearly uniform on balanced synthetic data; mildly
imbalanced but stable on
Iris
, reflecting the known overlap between

versicolor
and
virginica
. As
\lambda
grows further (with

\mu
shrinking), boundaries may shift and size variability can increase,
yet the prototype count typically remains constant within a broad region.

4.

Continuity induced by warm starts.

Warm starts yield piecewise continuous changes in both prototype locations
and counts, avoiding erratic jumps that can arise from cold–starting each
grid point independently. This facilitates interpretation of the entire
path as a data–adaptive simplification process.

Interpretation.

The empirical evidence suggests that a simple paired geometric schedule for

(\lambda,\mu)
, when combined with warm starts, yields a robust and
interpretable regularization path. In all tested cases, the method quickly
discovers a plausible cluster structure and maintains it across a wide portion
of the path. The stability of the prototype count, together with steady
cluster–size statistics, indicates qualitative robustness in practice.

Caveats and scope.

Warm starts introduce path dependence: the final solution at a given

(\lambda,\mu)
can depend on the trajectory taken through parameter space.
Our goal here is not to claim trajectory independence or universal optimality,
but to document a practical, reproducible procedure that behaves consistently on
diverse datasets. Broader studies would help to delineate the limits and generality of these observations. Within the scope tested, however, the warm–started
path offers a clear and stable view of how fusion and smoothing jointly govern
the emergence of cluster structure.

7.3
Discussion

The empirical results indicate that LDCA–K provides a coherent and effective
framework for simultaneous clustering and model selection across a range of
geometric conditions. Beginning from intentionally overparameterized prototype
sets, the method quickly removes redundant components through the combined
action of fusion and pruning, yielding compact cluster representations without
requiring delicate initialization or manual tuning. Prototype fusion is not an
auxiliary post–processing step but an intrinsic property of the objective: as
the fusion penalty grows, closely positioned prototypes naturally coalesce,
thereby performing model order reduction in a data–driven manner.

In datasets with clear separation, LDCA–K reliably identifies the ground–truth
number of clusters and maintains this structure across a wide region of the
regularization path. This behavior is particularly notable given the use of
warm starts: despite the inherent path dependence, the number of surviving
prototypes stabilizes early and remains unchanged even as the regularization
parameters continue to vary. Such stability suggests that the method is
capturing persistent geometric structure rather than transient artifacts of the
optimization trajectory.

In more ambiguous settings—for example, when clusters overlap or exhibit
anisotropic shapes—the method displays a principled sensitivity to the
regularization parameters. Instead of producing fragmented or unstable
solutions, LDCA–K tends to favor simpler and more interpretable clusterings
that are consistent with the underlying data geometry. This reflects a desirable
property: the effective number of clusters emerges from a balance between the
fusion pressure, the smoothing penalty, and the intrinsic separation in the
data, rather than from brittle thresholding or hand-tuned heuristics.

Overall, the experiments demonstrate that the number of clusters recovered by
LDCA–K is driven primarily by geometric structure and the regularization path,
not by initialization randomness or numerical instability. While a broader set
of datasets would be needed to characterize the full scope of this behavior,
the evidence presented here supports the view that LDCA–K offers a stable,
interpretable, and practically robust mechanism for clustering with automatic
model complexity control.
