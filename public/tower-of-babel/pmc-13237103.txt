Bypassing the VTA: Thalamic modulation of striatal dopamine prioritizes safety
Authors: Rachel E. Clarke, James M. Otis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Avoiding danger even when competing goals are imminent supports survival, yet the mechanisms orchestrating this behavioral override remain unclear. Macdonald and colleagues1 describe a thalamostriatal microcircuit that modulates terminal dopamine release, bypassing canonical midbrain dopamine circuitry to prioritize safety.

Survival in a dynamic environment demands a constant balancing act, wherein reward-directed actions must be rapidly overridden in favor of avoidance of risk and pursuit of safety. While midbrain dopamine neurons encode prediction errors that enable extrinsic rewards to shape goal-directed behavior, this canonical view of reinforcement learning does not explain how internal goals such as safety are prioritized, or how striatal dopamine release could be selectively modulated to support this process.

The paraventricular nucleus of the thalamus (PVT) has emerged as a central node capable of orchestrating the shift from reward-directed to safety-directed actions. The PVT is a unique thalamic hub where internal signals converge with contextual and cognitive information to generate powerful top-down regulation of behavior. Compelling in vivo data demonstrates that PVT activity is highly state-dependent, as PVT neurons are robustly activated by stressors and aversive events, yet their activity is profoundly inhibited during active goal-directed reward seeking and consumption2–9. Multiple studies have now established that the PVT powerfully suppresses reward seeking via projections to the nucleus accumbens (NAc)3,4; however, this same pathway is implicated in driving safety-directed action5, suggesting that PVT→NAc projection neurons can both suppress and facilitate behaviors relevant for survival.

Here, Macdonald and colleagues1 provide a critical missing link, demonstrating that PVT→NAc neurons support the avoidance of risk and pursuit of safety in a manner that is far more sophisticated than previously understood. While research has shown that the PVT can suppress dangerous or inappropriate goal-directed actions by activating accumbal parvalbumin interneurons (PV-INs)3, the present study reveals that PVT can also shift behavior toward risk avoidance and safety learning via recruitment of accumbal cholinergic interneurons (CINs)1. Impressively, the authors find that PVT projection neurons recruit accumbal CINs, which in turn drive terminal dopamine release in a manner that is divorced from somatic activity in VTA cell bodies.

Overall, these findings suggest that PVT acts as a highly efficient, dual-channel survival mechanism (Fig. 1). The PVT receives dense cortical and subcortical inputs encoding environmental and internal state demands. Rather than distributing this integrated signal across parallel networks, the PVT leverages distinct striatal microcircuits to simultaneously suppress goal-directed action (through PV-INs) and drive avoidance and safety learning (via CINs). Though these outputs may appear opposing, they are better understood as complementary arms of the behavioral strategy: survival. Critically, this output architecture affords the PVT unusually strong top-down control. Interestingly, PVT also sends direct inputs to accumbal dopamine D1 receptor (D1) and dopamine D2 receptor (D2) expressing MSNs3,4,7,10–12. However, how the PVT modulates behavior through NAc MSNs is not yet well understood. There is growing evidence that opioid use potentiates the PVT→NAcD2-MSN circuit7,11, driving the aversive state associated with withdrawal11 and promoting relapse via increased inhibition of hypothalamic-projecting D1-MSNs10. Adding further complexity, NAcD2-MSNs are reported to retrogradely modulate activity in PVT→NAc terminals through endocannabinoid signaling, implicating this circuit in both the initiation and the termination of reward seeking, and further revealing the diverse range of neuromodulators that can regulate this pathway12.

The molecular architecture of the PVT renders it a critical site of action for neuromodulatory systems13. Specifically, the PVT→NAc projection is enriched in mu-opioid receptors7, D2 receptors7, and cannabinoid receptors12; contains corticotropin releasing hormone (CRH) expressing neurons4; and orexin signaling in the PVT can modulate NAc activity14. This rich neuromodulatory influence positions the PVT→NAc projection as a critical mediator of adaptive and survival-motivated behaviors but also makes it unusually susceptible to the actions of drugs of abuse. For example, acute or chronic exposure to opioids weakens synaptic innervation of accumbal PV-INs via mu opioid receptors3,7, impairing behavioral inhibition, while simultaneously potentiating inputs to accumbal D2-MSNs7,11, promoting aversive withdrawal symptoms and relapse11. Whether opioids or other drugs of abuse could similarly dysregulate PVT→NAcCIN synapses, leading to deficits in safety learning and risk avoidance, is just one of many compelling questions raised by the present study.

The findings described here1 reframe how we think about the way PVT modulates behavior and raise numerous compelling questions related to PVT→NAc circuitry. For example, do different stimuli engage PVT→NAcCIN (avoidance, safety) and PVT→NAcPV-IN (inhibition of reward seeking) circuits or are they recruited simultaneously? Do the same PVT neurons project to both PV-INs and CINs in the accumbens to guide behavior away from inappropriate reward seeking and towards avoidance? Or do non-overlapping circuits exist in parallel? One possibility is that the distinct PVT→NAc neuronal ensembles, defined based on unique computational dynamics during reward seeking and avoidance/safety learning, have synaptic bias for the distinct downstream targets (i.e., PV-INs versus CINs). While such questions are challenging to address, those computationally distinct PVT→NAc ensembles may overlap with transcriptomically unique cell-types; and indeed, there is evidence to support divergent encoding of behavior within the PVT based on gene expression6,8. Thus, given the critical role the PVT→NAc microcircuits play in adaptive behavioral strategies, they represent a promising target in the treatment of neuropsychiatric diseases, such as substance use disorder, where behavioral suppression and risk avoidance are profoundly impaired.
