Prevalence and Correlates of Test Anxiety Among Medical Students: Systematic Review
Authors: Marwa Mahmoud Mady, Mayssah El Nayal, Ahmed M Abdel-Khalek, Nelofar Sami Khan, Sara Osman, Mona G Sayegh, Hiba A E Hajomer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTest anxiety is common among medical students, impacting their performance, well-being, and growth. While interest is rising, evidence on its prevalence and factors remains scattered.ObjectiveThis systematic review aimed to synthesize recent evidence on the prevalence of test anxiety and identify psychological, academic, and demographic correlates among undergraduate medical students.MethodsA systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Electronic databases, including PubMed, MEDLINE, Scopus, ERIC, EBSCOhost, and PsycINFO, were searched for studies published between January 2019 and July 2025. Eligible studies included cross-sectional research involving undergraduate medical students and using validated instruments to measure test anxiety. Methodological quality was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT). A narrative synthesis was performed due to methodological heterogeneity across studies.ResultsThirteen cross-sectional studies from diverse geographic regions, including India, Ethiopia, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Iran, Germany, Bangladesh, and China, were included. Sample sizes ranged from 82 to 735 participants. The prevalence of moderate to high test anxiety ranged from 13.5% to 72%. Higher anxiety levels were consistently associated with female gender, early academic years, academic workload, poor coping strategies, and limited social support. Psychological correlates included emotional distress, low self-efficacy, maladaptive coping, and burnout, while academic factors included examination format, heavy curriculum demands, and poor academic performance. Considerable heterogeneity in measurement tools and study settings limited quantitative synthesis.ConclusionTest anxiety is common among medical students worldwide, influenced by psychological, academic, and demographic factors. Early detection and targeted support, Effective study strategies, and stress management can enhance well-being and academic success.Keywords: test anxiety, medical students, prevalence, correlates, systematic review, medical education

Introduction

Test anxiety refers to tension and apprehension related to examinations, which can often lead to decreased performance.1 It is a situational type of anxiety manifested by cognitive, emotional, behavioral, and physiological reactions before, during, or after tests, such as worry, fear of failure, helplessness, rapid heartbeat, and muscle tension.2–4 In medical education, test anxiety is especially significant because assessments are frequent, high-stakes, and closely linked to academic advancement and professional identity.

Prevalence of Test Anxiety

Associated Factors and Correlates

Across the literature, test anxiety among medical students is consistently associated with academic, psychological, and contextual factors, although the nature and strength of these relationships vary across settings. Academic stress, heavy workloads, repeated assessments, and poorer academic performance have been identified as important correlates in demanding medical training environments. Psychological correlates include poor concentration, procrastination, burnout, emotional exhaustion, sleep disturbance, depressive symptoms, reduced self-efficacy, maladaptive coping, and impaired attentional control, showing that test anxiety is closely linked to both learning and mental well-being.5 It may also affect high-achieving students, especially those with perfectionistic tendencies, fear of failure, or high self-imposed standards.6

Recent studies from the Middle East and South Asia provide important contextual insights. In Saudi Arabia, test anxiety has been associated with psychosocial factors such as social support and examination-related stressors.7 In India, it remains common in educational settings characterized by frequent exams and high performance expectations.8 Broader evidence from Iraq also indicates connections with coping behaviors and medication use during exam periods.9 Overall, the evidence shows that test anxiety among medical students results from an interaction between demanding academic environments and individual psychological vulnerabilities, with regional differences influenced by assessment culture, family expectations, stigma, and available coping resources.5,7,8

This review aims to address the following research question: What insights does recent literature (2019–2025) provide regarding the prevalence and psychological, academic, and demographic factors linked to test anxiety among undergraduate medical students, assessed through validated quantitative tools? The objectives are: (1) to analyze the prevalence and reported test anxiety scores among medical students, and (2) to identify the psychological, academic, and demographic factors associated with test anxiety.

Theoretical Frameworks

Various theoretical models examine how factors like cognition, attention, emotional arousal, and self-beliefs contribute to the development and persistence of test anxiety. Spielberger’s model is key in distinguishing between state anxiety, a temporary reaction to exams, and trait anxiety, a more consistent tendency to respond with anxiety across different situations, thus explaining individual differences in exam stress.10,11

Sarason’s Test Anxiety Model identifies worry, self-preoccupation, and negative self-evaluation as main factors impairing performance in evaluative settings.12 Similarly, Cognitive Interference Theory proposes that intrusive thoughts deplete working memory, diminishing focus during tests.3 Eysenck’s Attentional Control and Processing Efficiency Theories show that anxiety disrupts goal-directed attention and increases mental effort, leading to inefficiency, fatigue, and burnout.13 Other perspectives, such as Self-Focus and Evaluation Apprehension, highlight the fear of negative judgment, particularly in oral, clinical, and performance assessments.14

Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory emphasizes self-efficacy, suggesting that students with low confidence are more likely to experience anxiety and avoidance.15 Control-Value Theory links anxiety to high task importance paired with low perceived control.16 The Transactional Model of Stress and Coping describes anxiety as arising when perceived demands exceed coping resources.17–19 Together, these models reveal how worry, attentional disruptions, low perceived control, and weak self-efficacy foster test anxiety and inform interventions such as cognitive-behavioral therapy and attentional training.4,20

Research Methodologies and Measurement Approaches

The existing research on test anxiety among medical students predominantly consists of cross-sectional quantitative studies that estimate prevalence and examine associated factors. Although these studies are beneficial for identifying patterns, they offer limited insights into causality or the evolution of anxiety over time. There are relatively fewer studies employing longitudinal, mixed-methods, or intervention designs; consequently, the evidence base primarily supports associations rather than elucidating temporal dynamics. Recent reviews also highlight that many studies conceptualize test anxiety as a continuous variable rather than utilizing standardized severity thresholds, which may impede comparability across studies.21 Although less prevalent, qualitative research provides valuable insights into students’ lived experiences, coping strategies, stigma, and the sociocultural context surrounding anxiety in medical education.7

Several validated tools assess test anxiety. The State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) differentiates between situational anxiety and general anxiety predispositions.10,11 The Westside Test Anxiety Scale (WTAS) provides a quick screening method suitable for educational contexts.22 The Cognitive Test Anxiety Scale (CTAS) emphasizes worry, intrusive thoughts, and cognitive interference, aligning closely with cognitive-behavioral models.23 Other common measures include the Test Anxiety Inventory (TAI), the German Test Anxiety Inventory (PAF), the Sarason Test Anxiety Scale (TAS), the Reactions to Tests Scale (RTT), the Nist and Diehl Test Anxiety Questionnaire, and occasionally the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) in clinical or experimental settings.24–30

A significant methodological concern is that some studies assess overall anxiety rather than exam-specific anxiety, using broader tools like the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS). While these are useful for measuring overall distress, they are less precise for capturing the cognitive and situational aspects of test anxiety. Overall, the CTAS, STAI, WTAS, and TAI are particularly relevant in research with medical students because they combine reliable psychometric properties with a focus on worry, attentional disruption, and emotional arousal.11,22,23

While test anxiety is increasingly recognized as a significant factor affecting medical students’ academic performance and mental health, most intervention studies involve broader student populations or mixed health disciplines, which limits their relevance to medical education. Additionally, few systematic reviews have specifically examined medical students using validated psychometric tools. This review addresses that gap by analysing studies from 2019 to 2025 that focus exclusively on medical students, utilize validated outcome measures, and employ MMAT appraisal to identify effective and reproducible interventions for test anxiety in medical training.

Methods

This systematic review followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines to synthesize recent evidence on the prevalence of test anxiety and its correlates among undergraduate medical students, using validated quantitative measures. The review protocol has been registered on the Open Science Framework. The registration DOI is 10.17605/OSF.IO/V6US3. The authors clearly defined the review question, eligibility criteria, search strategy, screening process, data extraction, and quality assessment methods in advance to ensure transparency and consistency.

Methodological Framework

The review was guided by the PECO framework (Population, Exposure, Comparison, Outcome), which is appropriate for systematic reviews addressing prevalence and associated factors. The population comprised undergraduate medical students. The exposures included psychological, academic, and demographic factors associated with test anxiety, as well as examination-related stressors. The comparison referred to students without these exposures or, where applicable, to those with lower versus higher levels of the examined factors. The outcome was the prevalence, level, or reported scores of test anxiety measured using validated quantitative instruments. This framework informed the development of the review question, eligibility criteria, data extraction, and synthesis of findings.

Information Sources

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in the following electronic databases: PubMed, MEDLINE, Scopus, ERIC, EBSCOhost, and PsycINFO. The search was restricted to studies published between 1 January 2019 and 31 July 2025.

This period was selected to collect current evidence on test anxiety in medical education, which is marked by recent changes in curriculum delivery, assessment methods, student support, and the increased use of validated psychometric tools in research. It also coincides with a time of significant global educational and psychosocial disruption, making recent studies more relevant to today’s medical training environment. Although earlier research laid the groundwork and theoretical framework, it was excluded from the main systematic analysis to focus on recent, methodologically consistent evidence.

The database search was conducted from 31 July 2025 to 31 August 2025, and supplementary manual searching of reference lists and Google Scholar were completed on 5 September 2025.

Search Strategy

The search strategy was developed iteratively using database-specific subject headings. The strategy aimed to identify studies that addressed test anxiety among undergraduate medical students and reported prevalence or associated psychological, academic, or demographic factors.

The PubMed search was conducted on 15 July 2025 and updated on 31 July 2025. The full Boolean search strategy used in PubMed was as follows:

(“test anxiety” OR “exam anxiety” OR “examination anxiety” OR “performance anxiety” OR “assessment-related anxiety”) AND (“medical students” OR “undergraduate medical students” OR “medical education” OR “medicine students”) AND (prevalence OR correlates OR predictors OR “associated factors”).

Filters applied were: English language and publication dates from 1 January 2019 to 31 July 2025. Database-specific adaptations of this strategy are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Reference management, duplicate removal, and blinded screening were conducted Rayyan systematic review software.

Justification for Restriction to Studies Published Between 2019 and 2025

The restriction was set based on methodological and contextual reasons rather than to update an existing systematic review. The review aimed to gather the latest evidence on test anxiety among medical students, especially considering evolving curricula, assessment formats, increased focus on student mental health, and the rising use of validated psychometric tools in recent studies. Additionally, test anxiety gained greater visibility during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, when disruptions in teaching, uncertainty, online assessments, and heightened psychological stress exacerbated concerns about student anxiety and academic performance. This review timeframe also coincides with major global and regional conflicts and crises that have impacted students’ mental health, stability, and learning environments, possibly affecting the experience and severity of test anxiety. While earlier studies are still valuable for providing conceptual and theoretical context, they were excluded to ensure a focused and methodologically consistent review.

Justification for Excluding Longitudinal and Interventional Studies

Longitudinal and interventional studies were excluded because this review aimed to synthesize evidence on the prevalence and correlates of test anxiety at specific points or periods of measurement using comparable observational data. Prevalence is best estimated through cross-sectional designs, while longitudinal studies mainly explore changes over time, incidence, trajectories, or prediction. Interventional studies, on the other hand, focus on the effectiveness of strategies to reduce anxiety rather than its natural course and related factors. Including these study types would have introduced significant methodological heterogeneity and hindered comparability among the studies.

Study Selection

All database search records were exported for reference management, with duplicates removed before screening. The remaining records were imported into Rayyan for review. Study selection involved two stages: initially, titles and abstracts were screened against predefined eligibility criteria. Then, the full texts of potentially eligible articles were assessed for final inclusion. Both stages were independently performed by two reviewers. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus, involving the wider author team if necessary. The entire study selection process—covering the number of records identified, duplicates removed, records screened, full texts assessed, exclusions, and final included studies—was documented using a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.

Data Extraction

Data were collected using a standardized Microsoft Excel template created by the review team. This extraction form was designed to promote consistency and thoroughness, and it was refined through team discussions before the full data collection. From each study included, the following information was extracted: authors, publication year, country, study aim, design, setting, sample size, participant characteristics, academic year or stage, measurement tool for test anxiety, validity and reliability evidence if available, prevalence or mean test anxiety scores, psychological, academic, and demographic correlates, statistical methods, main findings, and conclusions. Data extraction was carried out by the first two authors, with the remaining authors verifying the completeness and accuracy of the collected data. Any disagreements were handled through discussion and consensus.

Quality Appraisal

Methodological quality was evaluated using the 2018 version of the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT).31 This tool was chosen because it offers a clear, structured way to assess the quality of empirical studies across various methodological approaches, making it particularly appropriate for reviews that include quantitative descriptive research, as in this study. The MMAT emphasizes key methodological areas such as proper sampling, measurement validity, risk of nonresponse bias, and the appropriateness of statistical analysis.

The quality assessment was carried out independently by the first two authors and then reviewed by the other authors. Any disagreements were settled through discussion and consensus. Studies were rated as moderate to high quality, with only those meeting the predetermined methodological standards included in the final analysis.

Data Synthesis

A narrative synthesis was undertaken to integrate findings across the included studies. A meta-analysis was not performed because of substantial heterogeneity in study settings, sampling approaches, test anxiety instruments, scoring systems, cut-off values, and reported correlates.

The synthesis focused on summarizing the characteristics of the included studies, reported prevalence or levels of test anxiety, and the psychological, academic, and demographic correlates identified across studies. Quantitative findings were summarized descriptively using frequencies, ranges, and tabulated study characteristics where appropriate. This approach enabled comparison across diverse educational and cultural contexts while preserving important methodological differences between studies.

Results

Study Selection and Characteristics

A total of 1914 records were identified through database searching. After removal of 383 duplicates and 909 records deemed ineligible before screening, 622 titles and abstracts were screened. Of these,

8 records were excluded, and 154 full-text reports were sought for retrieval. Eighty-seven reports could not be retrieved, leaving 67 articles for full-text eligibility assessment. Following full-text review, 54 articles were excluded due to mixed populations (n = 21), longitudinal designs (n = 3), low methodological quality (MMAT < 3) (n = 2), purely qualitative studies (n = 3), interventional designs (n = 6), and analyses not specific to test anxiety (n = 19). The included studies were conducted across India, Ethiopia, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Iran, Germany, Bangladesh, and China, with sample sizes ranging from 82 to 735 participants. Most studies employed validated self-report tools, including the Westside Test Anxiety Inventory (WTAI), Westside Test Anxiety Scale (WTAS), Test Anxiety Inventory (TAI), State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), Prüfungsangstfragebogen (PAF), Nist and Diehl Test Anxiety Questionnaire, Sarason Test Anxiety Scale, and Visual Analogue Scale (VAS).10,34,39,41

Across studies, methodological quality was generally moderate to high. All studies used cross-sectional designs, while seven studies used inferential analyses and five applied multivariable regression models to identify associated factors with test anxiety. Below is a detailed synthesis of the findings regarding test anxiety from the included studies:

Prevalence of Test Anxiety Among Medical Students

The prevalence of test anxiety varied substantially across studies, ranging from 13.5% to 72%, reflecting heterogeneity in study populations, measurement tools, and examination formats. The highest prevalence was reported by Mastour et al35 in Iran, where 72% of students experienced moderate to severe anxiety, followed by Bonna et al38 in Bangladesh reporting 70% moderate to severe anxiety. These high prevalence rates were primarily associated with examination pressure, academic workload, and oral examination formats.

Moderate prevalence levels were reported in several studies. Tsegay et al10 reported 52.3% problematic anxiety among Ethiopian medical students, with significantly higher anxiety among females (79.75% vs. 33.62%, p <0.001). Similarly, Sharma et al39 and Tata et al40 both reported 49.6% high anxiety among Indian medical students.

Studies conducted in Saudi Arabia and India also demonstrated substantial anxiety levels. Memon et al36 and Patel et al8 reported approximately 60% moderate to high anxiety, particularly among early-year students and those experiencing academic stress Almutairi et al7 reported 215% high anxiety, 33.2% moderate anxiety, and 45.4% low anxiety, suggesting moderate levels of anxiety among Saudi medical students. Poor social support significantly increased the likelihood of high anxiety (OR = 2.56, 95% CI [1.03, 6.38], p =0.042), while frequent examinations also increased anxiety risk (OR = 10.58, 95% CI [1.86, 60.29], p =0.009).7

Temporal variation in anxiety was observed in Shaheen et al,37 where high anxiety decreased from 230% pre-assessment to 13.5% post-assessment (p <0.001), suggesting examination-related anticipatory anxiety.

Bischofsberger et al34 found that 50% of students showed signs of anxiety in at least one area, with worry being the most common. Ding and Zhang41 identified four distinct anxiety profiles through latent profile analysis: 17.14% had high anxiety, 20.82% moderate, and 40.54% low, highlighting the diversity in anxiety experiences. Overall, these results indicate that test anxiety is widespread among medical students worldwide, with its prevalence affected by the type of exam, academic level, and contextual factors.

Psychological Correlates of Test Anxiety

Psychological factors emerged as key correlates of test anxiety. Patel et al8 reported a significant positive correlation between psychological distress and test anxiety (r =47, p <0.01), indicating that students experiencing emotional distress were more likely to report higher anxiety levels.

Similarly, Memon et al36 identified low self-esteem, avoidant coping strategies, and emotional dysregulation as significant associated factors with test anxiety (p <0.05). Tata et al40 further reported that students relying on emotion-focused coping strategies experienced significantly higher anxiety levels (p <0.01).

Ding and Zhang41 also identified distinct psychological profiles, with high-anxiety groups demonstrating poorer emotional regulation and increased stress levels. These findings collectively suggest that maladaptive coping strategies and psychological distress contribute significantly to test anxiety among medical students.

Academic Correlates of Test Anxiety

Academic-related factors were consistently associated with test anxiety across studies. Arain et al33 reported significantly higher anxiety among students undergoing OSCE examinations compared to written examinations (p <0.05). Similarly, Tsegay et al10 reported a significant association between lower GPA and increased anxiety (p <0.05).

Sharma et al39 reported that students experiencing academic stress and heavy course loads had significantly higher anxiety levels (p <0.01). Patel et al8 also identified poor time management, procrastination, and inadequate revision strategies as associated factors with anxiety.

Academic year was another important factor. Memon et al36 reported higher anxiety among early-year students, likely due to unfamiliarity with examination formats and academic transition.

These findings indicate that academic demands and performance pressure contribute significantly to test anxiety.

Demographic Correlates of Test Anxiety

Gender emerged as the most consistent demographic associated factor. Female students reported significantly higher anxiety levels in most studies.7,10,36 For example, Tsegay et al10 reported significantly higher anxiety among females (p <0.001). Almutairi et al7 also reported higher odds among female students (OR = 1905, 95% CI [3.12, 116.02], p =0.002).

Age was also associated with anxiety levels. Mastour et al35 reported significantly higher anxiety among students older than 20 years (p <0.05). Additionally, Almutairi et al7 reported that poor social support significantly increased anxiety levels (OR = 256, 95% CI [1.03, 6.38], p =0.042).

Overall Synthesis of Findings

Across the included studies, test anxiety among medical students was influenced by psychological, academic, and demographic factors. Female gender, early academic years, academic workload, poor coping strategies, and limited social support were consistently associated with higher test anxiety levels. Despite methodological differences across studies, findings were generally consistent, suggesting that test anxiety represents a significant global concern in medical education.

Discussion

This systematic review compiled quantitative data from 13 cross-sectional studies to explore the prevalence, psychological and academic factors, and associated factors of test anxiety among undergraduate medical students. The results consistently show that test anxiety is a common and complex issue with significant effects on academic achievement, mental health, and professional growth. Throughout the studies, test anxiety appeared as a sustained problem in medical education, emphasizing the importance of implementing organized institutional strategies to bolster student mental health and academic performance.3,10,53

The prevalence of moderate to high test anxiety varied widely across studies, ranging from 13.5% to 72%, reflecting differences in populations, measurement tools, and assessment contexts. Higher rates were seen in studies involving high-stakes exams such as oral exams and OSCEs, as well as among first-year students adjusting to academic pressures.33,35,38 These findings match recent research showing that medical education environments tend to cause increased stress and psychological strain on students.6,42 The continued presence of high anxiety levels across different settings indicates that test anxiety is a common challenge in medical education.

A consistent trend observed across the included studies was a higher prevalence of test anxiety among female students. This finding aligns with recent research showing that female students often report greater levels of academic anxiety and stress compared to males.7,10,36 A recent study conducted in the United Arab Emirates also reported significantly higher test anxiety among female university students.9 Biological differences in stress reactivity, along with psychological and sociocultural factors, may contribute to this disparity.43 Furthermore, personality-related vulnerability, including higher neuroticism levels, may increase susceptibility to anxiety among female students.44

These findings emphasize the need for gender-sensitive approaches to promote student well-being. Psychological factors were consistently highlighted across multiple studies, with high distress, low self-esteem, and maladaptive coping linked to higher test anxiety.8,36,40 Contemporary cognitive models support these results, noting that anxiety often stems from perceived threats, fear of failure, and reduced self-efficacy in evaluative settings.4,6 Students employing emotion-focused coping were more prone to heightened anxiety, while problem-focused coping correlated with lower levels. Additionally, academic factors played a significant role; poor academic performance, low GPA, heavy workloads, and ineffective study habits were repeatedly associated with increased anxiety.8,10,39

Additionally, examination format was an important determinant. Students undergoing practical assessments such as OSCEs reported higher anxiety levels compared to written exams.33 This finding is consistent with recent literature showing that performance-based assessments increase anxiety due to time pressure and perceived evaluation intensity.45 Early-year students also reported higher anxiety levels, likely due to academic transition and unfamiliarity with exam formats.36 This is supported by recent research indicating that first-year medical students experience greater stress and anxiety compared to senior students.6,7 Although several studies used regression analyses to identify associated factors with of test anxiety, the explanatory power of these models was modest, with R2 values ranging from 18.5% to 30%.8,36 This suggests that additional factors such as personality traits, resilience, institutional climate, and cultural expectations may play a role in test anxiety. Recent literature emphasizes the importance of the learning environment and psychological resilience in moderating anxiety among medical students.32,46,47 Overall, this review suggests that test anxiety among medical students is influenced by a complex interaction of psychological, academic, and demographic factors. The consistency of findings across various geographic settings strengthens the generalizability of these results. These findings highlight the need for early identification of at-risk students and the implementation of targeted interventions like stress management programs, academic skills training, and supportive learning environments to reduce test anxiety and improve academic outcomes.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations. All included studies used cross-sectional designs, limiting causal inference. Most studies relied on self-reported questionnaires, which may introduce response bias. The use of different measurement tools contributed to heterogeneity and limited comparability across studies. Additionally, convenience sampling and single-institution settings reduced the generalizability of findings. Variability in statistical reporting limited the possibility of conducting a meta-analysis. The review also excluded longitudinal, pure qualitative, and interventional studies, potentially overlooking important contextual insights and intervention evidence. Furthermore, publication bias and geographical imbalance among included studies may affect the overall generalizability of the findings. In addition, some databases, such as Web of Science, were not accessed, and studies published in languages other than English were excluded, which may have resulted in the omission of relevant studies.

Conclusion

Test anxiety is highly prevalent among undergraduate medical students and is influenced by psychological, academic, and demographic factors, particularly female gender, early academic years, and academic workload. These findings highlight the need for medical schools to implement early identification and supportive strategies to improve student wellbeing and academic performance. Future research should focus on longitudinal and intervention studies to better understand causal relationships and evaluate effective approaches to reducing test anxiety.

Recommendations

In light of these findings, several recommendations are proposed:

Early screening for test anxiety during preclinical years helps identify at-risk students early. Gender-responsive interventions, like stress management workshops and psychological support tailored for female students, could be particularly helpful due to their higher risk. Curriculum reform should focus on reducing dependence on high-stakes exams and include alternative, formative assessments. Faculty development programs need to train educators to recognize and address signs of test anxiety. Longitudinal and experimental studies are essential to explore causality, monitor anxiety over time, and assess the impact of targeted interventions. Including clinical psychology courses in medical curricula can help reduce the rising anxiety levels among students by teaching effective coping strategies and also foster the development of more empathetic and psychologically aware future doctors.
