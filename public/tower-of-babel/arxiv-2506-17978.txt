Hybridizable Discontinuous Galerkin Methods for Thermo-Poroelastic Systems
Salim Meddahi

Abstract
We propose a high-order hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) formulation for the fully dynamic, linear thermo-poroelasticity problem. The governing equations are formulated as a first-order hyperbolic system incorporating solid and fluid velocities, heat flux, effective stress, pore pressure, and temperature as state variables. We establish well-posedness of the continuous problem using semigroup theory and develop an energy-consistent HDG discretization. The method exploits computational advantages of HDG—including locality and static condensation—while maintaining energy conservation for the coupled system. We establish an h ​ p hp -convergence analysis and support it with comprehensive numerical experiments, confirming the theoretical rates and showcasing the method’s effectiveness for thermo-poroelastic wave propagation in heterogeneous media.

1.  Introduction

The coupled interaction of mechanical deformation, pore-fluid flow, and heat transport governs numerous engineering and geophysical applications, including geothermal energy extraction, nuclear waste storage, hydraulic fracturing, and enhanced oil recovery
[
11
,
13
,
27
]
. Accurate prediction of these phenomena presents significant challenges because the governing thermo-poroelastic system involves multiple time scales and sharp solution gradients arising from material heterogeneity or localized sources.

Classical computational methods such as finite difference
[
15
]
, finite element
[
29
]
, boundary element
[
8
]
, finite volume
[
19
]
, and mixed methods
[
18
]
typically require extremely fine meshes to resolve these features adequately. This computational burden has motivated the development of high-order methods, including spectral techniques
[
24
]
and high-order space-time Galerkin formulations
[
3
]
. In particular, advanced polytopal discontinuous Galerkin schemes developed in
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
6
]
provide local conservation, geometric flexibility, and natural
hp
-adaptivity. However, their large computational stencils may result in a high cost per degree of freedom.

Hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) methods
[
9
]
address this computational challenge by statically condensing element unknowns onto faces, thereby producing smaller global systems while preserving the accuracy of standard DG methods. HDG has demonstrated remarkable effectiveness for standalone and coupled elastodynamic problems
[
25
,
10
,
17
,
20
,
14
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
. The present work extends the HDG framework proposed in
[
22
]
for poroelasticity to the more complex case of thermo-poroelasticity. To our knowledge, no HDG strategy for thermo-poroelasticity has been developed previously.

Our point of departure is the linear, fully dynamic thermo-poroelasticity model considered in
[
7
,
6
]
, which couples solid mechanics, Darcy flow, and finite-speed heat transport. In this model, heat conduction follows the Maxwell-Cattaneo law, eliminating the non-physical infinite propagation velocity inherent in classical Fourier diffusion
[
4
]
. In contrast to
[
7
,
6
]
, where the problem is formulated as a second-order PDE in time, we formulate the governing equations as a first-order hyperbolic system. This reformulation facilitates the application of semigroup theory for establishing well-posedness of the continuous problem. It is worth noting that
[
28
]
establishes well-posedness through energy estimates and a classical Galerkin procedure, in contrast to our semigroup-based analysis.

Following
[
21
]
, we develop an energy-consistent HDG discretization on general simplicial meshes and present an
hp
-convergence analysis. Comprehensive numerical experiments validate the theoretical convergence rates and demonstrate the method’s effectiveness for wave propagation phenomena in heterogeneous media, including comparisons with purely poroelastic models that highlight the significance of thermal coupling effects.

2.  The Dynamic Linear Thermo-Poroelastic Model

This section presents a dynamic linear thermo-poroelastic model describing the coupled mechanical, thermal, and hydraulic behavior of a fluid-saturated porous medium subjected to time-varying loads and temperature changes, cf.
[
7
,
6
]
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, be a bounded Lipschitz domain with boundary
\Gamma
and outward unit normal
\boldsymbol{n}
. Let
T>0
denote a fixed final time. Throughout, an overdot denotes the partial derivative with respect to time, i.e.,
\dot{v}:=\partial_{t}v
. The model’s behavior is characterized by the primary variables

(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\sigma},p,\theta):\Omega\times(0,T]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R},

where
\boldsymbol{u}
represents the velocity of the solid matrix,
\boldsymbol{q}
the Darcy velocity of the fluid phase,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
the effective Cauchy stress tensor,
p
the pore pressure of the fluid,
\theta
the absolute temperature, and
\boldsymbol{r}
the heat flux.

With these variables, the linear first-order thermo-poroelastic system reads:

(1a)

\displaystyle\rho\,\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}+\rho_{f}\,\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}-\operatorname{\mathbf{div}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-(\alpha p+\beta\theta)I_{d}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}_{s},

(solid momentum)

(1b)

\displaystyle\rho_{f}\,\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}+\rho_{w}\,\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}+\frac{\eta}{\kappa}\,\boldsymbol{q}+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}_{f},

(fluid momentum)

(1c)

\displaystyle c_{0}\,\dot{p}-b_{0}\,\dot{\theta}+\alpha\,\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{u}+\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{q}

\displaystyle=0,

(mass balance)

(1d)

\displaystyle a_{0}\,\dot{\theta}-b_{0}\,\dot{p}+\beta\,\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{u}+\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{r}

\displaystyle=g,

(energy balance)

(1e)

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\mathcal{C}\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

(elastic law)

(1f)

\displaystyle\frac{\tau}{\chi}\,\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}+\frac{1}{\chi}\,\boldsymbol{r}+\nabla\theta

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

(Cattaneo law)

Here,
I_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the identity in the space of real matrices
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\frac{1}{2}\left[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u})^{\mathtt{t}}\right]:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}}
is the linearized strain tensor, where
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}}\coloneq\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\mid\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\mathtt{t}}\}
. Additionally,
\operatorname{\mathbf{div}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}:=\bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{j}}\sigma_{ij}\bigr)_{i=1}^{d}
is the divergence operator, and
\boldsymbol{f}_{s}
,
\boldsymbol{f}_{f}
, and
g
represent body forces, fluid supply, and a heat source/sink term, respectively. The material parameters appearing in the equations are detailed in Table
1
.

The system is supplemented with initial conditions:

(2)

\boldsymbol{u}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}^{0},\quad\boldsymbol{q}(0)=\boldsymbol{q}^{0},\quad\boldsymbol{r}(0)=\boldsymbol{r}^{0},\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}(0)=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0},\quad p(0)=p^{0},\quad\theta(0)=\theta^{0}\qquad\text{in }\Omega,

and boundary conditions defined on complementary parts of the boundary
\partial\Omega
. Specifically,
\Gamma=\Gamma_{D}^{S}\cup\Gamma_{N}^{S}=\Gamma_{D}^{F}\cup\Gamma_{N}^{F}=\Gamma_{D}^{H}\cup\Gamma_{N}^{H}
, where
\Gamma^{\star}_{D}
denotes Dirichlet boundaries and

\text{meas}(\Gamma_{D}^{S})
,

,

to avoid rigid body motions or ill-posedness:

(3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{u}&=\mathbf{0}&&\qquad\text{on }\Gamma_{D}^{S}\times(0,T],&(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\alpha pI_{d}-\beta\theta I_{d})\boldsymbol{n}&=\mathbf{0}&&\qquad\text{on }\Gamma_{N}^{S}\times(0,T],\\

The homogeneous boundary conditions presented here are for simplicity. The term
(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\alpha pI_{d}-\beta\theta I_{d})\boldsymbol{n}
represents the total traction on the solid phase boundary.

Remark 1
.

Several key properties of system (
1
) are worth noting. First, it constitutes a linear, first-order-in-time system that admits a non-increasing energy functional under appropriate positivity assumptions on the material parameters (detailed below). This energy structure is fundamental for establishing well-posedness in the classical
L^{2}
-framework. Second, the system reduces to Biot’s poroelasticity when thermal effects are neglected, and to linear thermoelasticity with finite-speed heat conduction when poroelastic effects are absent. Third, the first-order formulation is particularly well-suited for energy-conserving high-order time integrators and hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin discretizations.

The elasticity tensor
\mathcal{C}
is assumed to be symmetric and positive definite, meaning there exist constants
c^{+}>c^{-}>0
such that

(4)

c^{-}\boldsymbol{\zeta}:\boldsymbol{\zeta}\leq\mathcal{C}\boldsymbol{\zeta}:\boldsymbol{\zeta}\leq c^{+}\boldsymbol{\zeta}:\boldsymbol{\zeta}\quad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}}.

For subsequent analysis, it is convenient to define the following matrices based on the material parameters:

(5)

R:=\begin{pmatrix}\rho&\rho_{f}&0\\
\rho_{f}&\rho_{w}&0\\
0&0&\frac{\tau}{\chi}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad Q:=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}&-b_{0}\\
-b_{0}&a_{0}\end{pmatrix}.

The matrix
R
groups coefficients of the time derivatives of velocities and
Q
relates to storage and coupling in the mass and energy balance equations.

Under the physical assumptions that the tortuosity
\nu>1
(which implies
\rho\rho_{w}-\rho_{f}^{2}>0
) and that the matrix
Q
is positive definite (i.e.
a_{0}>0
,
c_{0}>0
, and
a_{0}c_{0}-b_{0}^{2}>0
) (ensuring positive definiteness of
R
and
Q
), we can establish the following coercivity and boundedness inequalities for
R
and
Q
:

(6)

\displaystyle\rho^{-}\boldsymbol{\eta}:\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle\leq\boldsymbol{\eta}R:\boldsymbol{\eta}\leq\rho^{+}\boldsymbol{\eta}:\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle\text{for all }\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 3},

(7)

\displaystyle c^{-}\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle\leq Q\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}\leq c^{+}\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle\text{for all }\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

where
\rho^{+}>\rho^{-}>0
and
c^{+}>c^{-}>0
are constants depending on the material parameters.

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}-\mathcal{C}\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}.

and let

The coupled system of partial differential equations (

The system (

(14)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}\\
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\end{pmatrix}+\mathbb{A}\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}\\
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\end{pmatrix}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\boldsymbol{f}}R^{-1}\\
\boldsymbol{G}\end{pmatrix},\quad\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}(0)\\

\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}\coloneq\left[\boldsymbol{u}^{0}\mid\boldsymbol{q}^{0}\mid\boldsymbol{r}^{0}\right]

\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{0}\coloneq(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0},\left[p^{0}\mathrel{;}\theta^{0}\right])

\mathbb{A}\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}\\

\boldsymbol{\Theta}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}R^{-1}\\

Theorem 1
.

For all
\underline{\boldsymbol{f}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{[0,T]}(L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d\times 3}))
,

\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{1}
, and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{2}
, there exist unique
\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\cap\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{X}_{1})
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{H}_{2})\cap\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{X}_{2})
solutions to the initial boundary value problem (
14
). Moreover, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(15)

\max_{[0,T]}\lVert\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\max_{[0,T]}\lVert\boldsymbol{\Theta}(t)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\leq C\left(\max_{[0,T]}\lVert\underline{\boldsymbol{f}}(t)\rVert_{0,\Omega}+\max_{[0,T]}\lVert g(t)\rVert_{0,\Omega}\right)+\lVert\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\lVert\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}.

Proof.

By definition, we have that

\left(\mathbb{A}\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}\\
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}\\
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\end{pmatrix}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2}}=\left(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}},\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}\right)_{\Omega}\geq 0\quad\forall\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}\\
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2},

which proves that
\mathbb{A}
is monotone.

It remains to show that the operator
\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{A}:\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2}\to\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2}
is surjective, where
\mathbb{I}
represents the identity operator in
\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2}
. Given an arbitrary
\left(\underline{\boldsymbol{f}},\boldsymbol{G}\right)\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2}
, we seek
\left(\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{*}\right)\in\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2}
satisfying

\left(\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{A}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}\\
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{*}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\boldsymbol{f}}\\
\boldsymbol{G}\end{pmatrix}.

Writing out the components,

(16)

\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}+\mathfrak{L}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{*}+\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}R^{-1}=\underline{\boldsymbol{f}},\quad\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{*}-\mathfrak{L}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}=\boldsymbol{G},

and substituting
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{*}=\boldsymbol{G}+\mathfrak{L}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}
into the first equation, we obtain the variational problem: find
\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}_{1}
such that

(17)

\left(\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)_{\mathcal{X}_{1}}+\left(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}R^{-1},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}=\left(\underline{\boldsymbol{f}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}-\left(\boldsymbol{G},\mathfrak{L}\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\quad\forall\,\underline{v}\in\mathcal{X}_{1}.

By the Lax–Milgram lemma, there exists a unique solution
\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}_{1}
to this problem.

To ensure that
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{*}=\boldsymbol{G}+\mathfrak{L}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
actually lies in
\mathcal{X}_{2}
, we employ a dual procedure to the first step and substitute
\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}=-\mathfrak{L}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{*}-\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}R^{-1}+\underline{\boldsymbol{f}}
into the second equation of (
16
) to deduce that
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}_{2}
is the unique solution of the variational problem

(18)

\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{*},\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)_{\mathcal{X}_{2}}=\left(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*}R^{-1}-\underline{\boldsymbol{f}},\mathfrak{L}^{H}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\left(\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\Phi}\in\mathcal{X}_{2}.

We have shown that solving problems (
17
) and (
18
) successively provides an inverse image
(\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{*})\in\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2}
of
(\underline{\boldsymbol{F}},\boldsymbol{G})\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\mathcal{H}_{2}
under
\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{A}
.

Since
\mathbb{A}
is maximal monotone, the result follows from the Lumer-Phillips variant of the Hille-Yosida theorem (cf.
[
12
, Theorem 76.7]
).
∎

We define the bounded bilinear form
B:\mathcal{X}_{1}\times\mathcal{X}_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
as

B(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{\Phi})\coloneq(\operatorname{\mathbf{div}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}-(\alpha z+\beta\varphi)I_{d},\boldsymbol{v})_{\Omega}-(\nabla z,\boldsymbol{q})_{\Omega}-(\nabla\varphi,\boldsymbol{r})_{\Omega}.

In the following sections, we will develop a finite element discretization method based on the following weak form of (
14
): Find
\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}=\left[\boldsymbol{u}\mid\boldsymbol{q}\mid\boldsymbol{r}\right]\in\mathcal{C}_{[0,T]}^{1}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\cap\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{X}_{1})
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=(\boldsymbol{\sigma},[p\mathrel{;}\theta])\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{H}_{2})\cap\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{X}_{2})
satisfying

(19)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\left(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)_{\Omega}-B(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{\Theta})&=\left(\underline{\boldsymbol{f}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)_{\Omega}\\
(\dot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}},\boldsymbol{\Phi})_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+B(\underline{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\Phi})&=\left(g,\varphi\right)_{\Omega},\end{split}

for all
\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}=\left[\boldsymbol{v}\mid\boldsymbol{w}\mid\boldsymbol{s}\right]\in\mathcal{X}_{1}
and all
\boldsymbol{\Phi}=(\boldsymbol{\tau},[z\mathrel{;}\varphi])\in\mathcal{X}_{2}
, and subject to the initial conditions specified in (
14
).

4.  The semi-discrete problem

In this section, we present the HDG semi-discrete method for the poroelasticity problem (
19
). We establish the well-posedness of the resulting algebraic-differential equations and prove the method’s consistency with the continuous formulation.

For the sake of simplicity, from now on we assume that
\Gamma^{S}_{D}=\Gamma^{F}_{D}=\Gamma^{H}_{D}=\Gamma
. As a result, the boundary conditions (
3
) become

(20)

\boldsymbol{u}=\mathbf{0},\quad p=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\theta=0\quad\text{on $\Gamma\times(0,T]$}.

4.1.  Broken Sobolev Spaces

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a shape-regular partition of the computational domain closure
\bar{\Omega}
into elements. These elements are tetrahedra or parallelepipeds when the spatial dimension
d=3
, and triangles or quadrilaterals when
d=2
. The partition
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is permitted to have hanging nodes. For each element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we denote its diameter by
h_{K}
. The mesh size parameter
h
is defined as the maximum diameter among all elements, i.e.,
h:=\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\{h_{K}\}
.

A closed subset
F\subset\overline{\Omega}
is classified as an interior edge or face if it possesses a positive
(d-1)
-dimensional measure and can be expressed as the intersection of the closures of two distinct elements
K
and
K^{\prime}
, formally
F=\bar{K}\cap\bar{K}^{\prime}
. Conversely,
F\subset\overline{\Omega}
is a boundary edge or face if there exists an element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
such that
F
is an edge or face of
K
, and
F=\bar{K}\cap\partial\Omega
. We denote the set of all interior edges/faces by
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}
and the set of all boundary edges/faces by
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}
. The union of these sets constitutes the set of all mesh faces,
\mathcal{F}_{h}=\mathcal{F}_{h}^{0}\cup\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}
. For each face
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
,
h_{F}
represents its diameter. The mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is assumed to be locally quasi-uniform with a constant
\gamma>0
. This condition implies that for all
h
and for every element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, the following relationship holds:

(21)

h_{F}\leq h_{K}\leq\gamma h_{F}\quad\forall F\in\mathcal{F}(K),

where
\mathcal{F}(K)
denotes the set of all faces comprising the boundary of element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
. This local quasi-uniformity is a prerequisite for the proof of Lemma
5
.

For any
s\geq 0
, the broken Sobolev space with respect to the partition
\mathcal{T}_{h}
of
\bar{\Omega}
is defined as the space

H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}):=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})\mid\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}\in H^{s}(K,\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})\quad\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\}.

Following standard conventions, we simplify notation where the codomain is
\mathbb{R}
, writing
H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R})=H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
, and note that for
s=0
,
H^{0}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})=L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
. We introduce an inner product on
L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
as the sum of element-wise inner products:

(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\varphi})_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}:=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\varphi})_{K}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}).

The corresponding norm is denoted by
\lVert\boldsymbol{\psi}\rVert^{2}_{0,\mathcal{T}_{h}}:=(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\psi})_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
.
Furthermore, we define
\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}:=\{\partial K\mid K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}
as the collection of all element boundaries. The space
L^{2}(\partial\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
comprises
m\times n
matrix-valued functions that are square-integrable on each
\partial K\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}
. We define the following inner product and induced norm on this space:

\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}:=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle_{\partial K},\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}:=\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in L^{2}(\partial\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}),

where the element boundary integral is given by
\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\rangle_{\partial K}:=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}(K)}\int_{F}\boldsymbol{\psi}:\boldsymbol{\varphi}
.
Additionally, the space
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
is equipped with the inner product:

(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\varphi})_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}:=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\int_{F}\boldsymbol{\psi}:\boldsymbol{\varphi}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}),

with the associated norm denoted by
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varphi}\right\rVert^{2}_{0,\mathcal{F}_{h}}:=(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\varphi})_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}
.

It is crucial to distinguish between functions defined on
L^{2}(\partial\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
versus
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
. Functions in
L^{2}(\partial\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
can possess two distinct values on each interior face
F
, corresponding to the trace from each adjacent element. In contrast, functions in
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
are single-valued on each face
F
.

To formulate the HDG method, we need to introduce product spaces that accommodate both volume and trace variables. For
r>\frac{1}{2}
, we define:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{U}&\coloneq\left\{\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}=[\boldsymbol{v}\mid\boldsymbol{w}\mid\boldsymbol{s}]\mid\boldsymbol{v}\in H^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{s}\in H(\operatorname{div},\mathcal{T}_{h})\cap H^{r}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\right\},\\
\hat{\mathcal{U}}&\coloneq\left\{\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}}=[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}]\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \hat{\boldsymbol{w}},\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\right\},\end{split}

where
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneq\left\{\boldsymbol{\phi}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid\boldsymbol{\phi}|_{F}=\mathbf{0},\ \forall F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}\right\}
.
We endow the product space
\mathcal{U}\times\hat{\mathcal{U}}
with the semi-norm defined for all
(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}})\in\mathcal{U}\times\hat{\mathcal{U}}
by:

(22)

\left\lvert(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}})\right\rvert^{2}_{\mathcal{U}\times\hat{\mathcal{U}}}=\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\rVert^{2}_{0,\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{w}\rVert^{2}_{0,\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{s}\rVert^{2}_{0,\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\frac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\frac{1}{2}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}-\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}})\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}},

where
h_{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\mathcal{F}_{h})
is a piecewise constant function defined such that
h_{\mathcal{F}}|_{F}:=h_{F}
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
.

4.2.  Piecewise-polynomial Spaces

Hereafter,
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(D)
denotes the space of polynomials. Specifically, if
D
is a triangle or tetrahedron,
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(D)
consists of polynomials of total degree at most
\ell\geq 0
. If
D
is a quadrilateral or parallelepiped,
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(D)
comprises polynomials of degree at most
\ell
in each variable. The space of
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
-valued functions whose components belong to
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(D)
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(D,\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
. In particular,
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(D,\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}})
refers to symmetric
d\times d
matrices with components in
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(D)
. We introduce the space of piecewise-polynomial functions defined over the mesh partition
\mathcal{T}_{h}
as:

\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathcal{T}_{h}):=\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega):\ v|_{K}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(K),\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

Similarly, the space of piecewise-polynomial functions defined on the mesh skeleton
\mathcal{F}_{h}
is given by:

\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathcal{F}_{h}):=\{\hat{v}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h}):\ \hat{v}|_{F}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(F),\ \forall F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}\}.

The subspace of
L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
with components in
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
is written
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
. Analogously,
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
represents the subspace of
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
with components in
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathcal{F}_{h})
. Finally, we consider the space of piecewise-polynomial functions on the element boundaries:

\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\partial\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}):=\left\{\phi\in L^{2}(\partial\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n});\ \phi|_{\partial K}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\partial K,\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}),\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\},

where
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\partial K,\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
is defined as the product space
\prod_{F\in\mathcal{F}(K)}\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(F,\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
.

We define
\boldsymbol{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\partial\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})
as the piecewise constant unit normal vector field, where
\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial K}=\boldsymbol{n}_{K}
is the unit normal vector of
\partial K
oriented outwards from
K
. It is important to note that if
F=K\cap K^{\prime}
is an interior face of
\mathcal{F}_{h}
, then the normal vectors are oppositely oriented, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{n}_{K}=-\boldsymbol{n}_{K^{\prime}}
on
F
.

4.3.  The HDG Method

For a given polynomial degree
k\geq 0
, we introduce the finite-dimensional subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{1,h}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2,h}
approximating
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
, respectively:

\mathcal{H}_{1,h}\coloneq\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d\times 3})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{H}_{2,h}\coloneq\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}})\times[\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})]^{2}.

Additionally, for
k\geq 0
, we define the finite-dimensional subspace
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1,h}
as:

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1,h}\coloneq\left\{[\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}]\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \hat{\boldsymbol{w}},\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\right\}\subset\hat{\mathcal{U}},

where
\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\{\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{F}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid\boldsymbol{\phi}|_{F}=\mathbf{0},\ \forall F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}\}
.

We propose the following HDG spatial discretization method for problem (
19
): find
(\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{h})\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{H}_{1,h}\times\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1,h})
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{h}=(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},[p_{h}\mathrel{;}\theta_{h}])\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{H}_{2,h})
satisfying:

(23)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{h},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+(\dot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\Phi})_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}&+(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\Omega}+B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{h},(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}))-B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},(\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{h}))\\
&\quad+\langle\tfrac{(k+1)^{2}}{h_{\mathcal{F}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{h}),\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}-\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}=(\underline{\boldsymbol{f}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\Omega}+(g,\varphi)_{\Omega},\end{split}

for all
(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}})\in\mathcal{H}_{1,h}\times\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1,h}
and
\boldsymbol{\Phi}=(\boldsymbol{\tau},[z\mathrel{;}\varphi])\in\mathcal{H}_{2,h}
. The bilinear form
B_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
is given by:

\displaystyle\begin{split}B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}))&:=\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}-(\alpha z+\beta\theta)I_{d},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}-\left(z,\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{w}\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}-\left(\varphi,\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{s}\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\\
&\qquad-\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\tau}-(\alpha z+\beta\theta)I_{d})\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{v}-\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\left\langle z\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{w}-\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\left\langle\varphi\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{s}-\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}.\end{split}

Problem (
23
) is initialized with the following conditions:

(24)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(0)&=\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{T}}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0},\quad\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{h}(0)=\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{F}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}|_{\partial\mathcal{F}_{h}}),\\
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}(0)&=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0},\quad p_{h}(0)=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}p^{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{h}(0)=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}\theta^{0},\end{split}

where the
L^{2}
-projection operators
\Pi^{k}_{\mathcal{T}}
and
\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{F}}
are defined in Appendix
A
.

The bilinear form
B_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
possesses the following boundedness property.

Proposition 1
.

There exists a constant
C>0
independent of
h
and
k
such that:

(25)

|B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h},(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}))|\leq C\lVert\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\lvert(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}})\rvert_{\mathcal{U}\times\hat{\mathcal{U}}}\quad\text{for all}\ \boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h}\in\mathcal{H}_{2,h}\ \text{and}\ (\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}})\in\mathcal{U}\times\hat{\mathcal{U}}.

Proof.

We recall that if
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
, with
s>\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, then
\boldsymbol{\varphi}|_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\in L^{2}(\partial\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{m\times n})
is well-defined due to the trace theorem. Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and (
11
), we deduce that:

(26)

|B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}))|\lesssim(\lVert\boldsymbol{\Phi}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\boldsymbol{\tau}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}z\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\varphi\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lvert(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}})\rvert_{\mathcal{U}\times\hat{\mathcal{U}}},

for all
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
such that
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}})
and
z,\varphi\in H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
with
s\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, and for all
(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}})\in\mathcal{U}\times\hat{\mathcal{U}}
. The result follows from applying the discrete trace inequality (
44
).
∎

Proposition 2
.

Problem (
23
)-(
24
) admits a unique solution.

Proof.

We introduce the linear operator
\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\cdot\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}:\mathcal{H}_{1,h}\to\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1,h}
defined by
\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}=[\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}\mid\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\boldsymbol{w}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}\mid\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\boldsymbol{s}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}]
, where for an interior face
F=K\cap K^{\prime}
, the mean value is
\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}|_{F}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{v}|_{K}+\boldsymbol{v}|_{K^{\prime}})|_{F}
. For a boundary face
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\partial}
,
\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}|_{F}=\mathbf{0}
due to the Dirichlet boundary condition for
\boldsymbol{u}
, while
\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\boldsymbol{w}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}|_{F}=\boldsymbol{w}|_{F}
and
\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\boldsymbol{s}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}|_{F}=\boldsymbol{s}|_{F}
because
p=0
and
\theta=0
on
\Gamma
, respectively, which implies natural boundary conditions for the fluxes
\boldsymbol{q}
and
\boldsymbol{r}
.

The algebraic differential equation (
23
) can be decomposed into the following system of equations:

(27)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{h},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}&+(\dot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\Phi})_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\Omega}+B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{h},(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\mathbf{0}))-B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},(\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{h}))\\
&\quad+\langle\tfrac{(k+1)^{2}}{h\mathcal{F}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{h}),\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}=\left(\underline{\boldsymbol{f}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)_{\Omega}+(g,\varphi)_{\Omega},\quad\forall(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{\Phi})\in\mathcal{H}_{1,h}\times\mathcal{H}_{2,h}\\
&B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{h},(\mathbf{0},\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}}))+\langle\tfrac{(k+1)^{2}}{h\mathcal{F}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{h}),-\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}=0\quad\forall\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}}\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1,h}.\end{split}

From the second equation in (
27
), we deduce that the numerical flux
\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{h}\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1,h}
satisfies:

\langle\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{h},\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rangle_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}=\langle\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}-\tfrac{h\mathcal{F}}{(k+1)^{2}}\left[\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-(\alpha p_{h}+\beta\theta_{h})I_{d})\boldsymbol{n}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}\mid\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{p_{h}\boldsymbol{n}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}\mid\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\theta_{h}\boldsymbol{n}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}\right],\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rangle_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}\quad\forall\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}}\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1,h}.

In other words,
\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{h}
is the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection of the expression

\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}-\tfrac{h\mathcal{F}}{(k+1)^{2}}\left[\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-(\alpha p_{h}+\beta\theta_{h})I_{d})\boldsymbol{n}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}\mid\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{p_{h}\boldsymbol{n}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}\mid\left\{\kern-4.30554pt\left\{\theta_{h}\boldsymbol{n}\right\}\kern-4.30554pt\right\}\right]

onto
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1,h}
. Substituting this expression for
\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{h}
into the first equation of (
27
) yields a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) with unknowns
\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}{[0,T]}(\mathcal{H}_{1,h})
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{h}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}{[0,T]}(\mathcal{H}_{2,h})
, along with the initial conditions:

(28)

\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(0)=\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{T}}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0},\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}(0)=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{0},\quad p_{h}(0)=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}p^{0},\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{h}(0)=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}\theta^{0}.

The well-posedness of this resulting first-order ODE system follows directly from the fact that
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}
and
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}
are inner products on the finite-dimensional function spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1,h}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2,h}
, respectively.
∎

We now proceed to verify the consistency of the HDG scheme (
23
) with the continuous problem (
19
).

Proposition 3
.

Let
\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}=[\boldsymbol{u}\mid\boldsymbol{q}\mid\boldsymbol{r}]\in\mathcal{C}_{[0,T]}^{1}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\cap\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{X}_{1})
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=(\boldsymbol{\sigma},[p\mathrel{;}\theta])\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{H}_{2})\cap\mathcal{C}^{0}_{[0,T]}(\mathcal{X}_{2})
be the solutions of (
19
). Assume that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}-(\alpha p+\beta\theta)I_{d}\in\mathcal{C}_{[0,T]}^{0}(H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}}))
and
\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathcal{C}_{[0,T]}^{0}(H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d\times 3}))
, with
s>\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
. Then, the following consistency equation holds:

(29)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left(\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+(\dot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}},\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h})_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})_{\Omega}+B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\Theta},(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{h}))\\
&\quad-B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h},(\underline{\boldsymbol{u}},\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}|_{\partial\mathcal{F}_{h}}))+\langle\tfrac{(k+1)^{2}}{h_{\mathcal{F}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}-\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}|_{\partial\mathcal{F}_{h}}),\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}-\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{h}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}=\left(\underline{\boldsymbol{f}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\right)_{\Omega}+(g,\varphi_{h})_{\Omega},\end{split}

for all
(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{h})\in\mathcal{H}_{1,h}\times\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1,h}
and
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h}=(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},[z_{h}\mathrel{;}\varphi_{h}])\in\mathcal{H}_{2,h}
.

Proof.

The continuity of the normal components of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}-(\alpha p+\beta\theta)I_{d}
,
pI_{d}
, and
\theta I_{d}
across element interfaces in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, coupled with the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\Gamma
, yields:

\displaystyle\begin{split}B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\Theta},(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{h}))&=\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-(\alpha p+\beta\theta)I_{d},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}-\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-(\alpha p+\beta\theta)I_{d})\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\\
&\qquad-\left(p,\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\left\langle p\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{w}_{h}-\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{h}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\\
&\qquad-\left(\theta,\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{s}_{h}\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\left\langle\theta\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}-\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{h}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\\
&=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\Big(\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-(\alpha p+\beta\theta)I_{d},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\right)_{K}-\left(p,\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\right)_{K}-\left(\theta,\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{s}_{h}\right)_{K}\\
&\qquad-\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-(\alpha p+\beta\theta)I_{d})\boldsymbol{n}_{K},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\right\rangle_{F_{K}}+\left\langle p\boldsymbol{n}_{K},\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\right\rangle_{F_{K}}+\left\langle\theta\boldsymbol{n}_{K},\boldsymbol{s}_{h}\right\rangle_{F_{K}}\Big).\end{split}

Applying element-wise integration by parts to the right-hand side of the previous identity, and utilizing the identities from the continuous problem (
1a
), (
1b
), and (
1f
) in the form

\left[-\operatorname{\mathbf{div}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-(\alpha p+\beta\theta)I_{d}\bigr)\mid\nabla p\mid\nabla\theta\right]=\underline{\boldsymbol{f}}-\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}-\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}}R,

we obtain:

(30)

\displaystyle\begin{split}B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\Theta},(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}_{h}))&=\left(-\operatorname{\mathbf{div}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-(\alpha p+\beta\theta)I_{d}\bigr),\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\left(\nabla p,\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\left(\nabla\theta,\boldsymbol{s}_{h}\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\\
&=\left(\underline{\boldsymbol{f}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\right)_{\Omega}-\left(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\right)_{\Omega}-\left(\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}}R,\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\right)_{\Omega}.\end{split}

On the other hand, we have that:

(31)

B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h},(\underline{\boldsymbol{u}},\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}|_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}))=\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}-(\alpha z_{h}+\beta\varphi_{h})I_{d},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}-\left(z_{h},\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{q}\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}-\left(\varphi_{h},\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{r}\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h}\in\mathcal{H}_{2,h}.

Substituting (
30
) and (
31
) into the consistency equation (
29
) gives:

(32)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}&+(\dot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}},\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h})_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h})_{\Omega}+\left(\left(\underline{\boldsymbol{f}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\right)_{\Omega}-\left(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{u}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\right)_{\Omega}-\left(\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}}R,\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\right)_{\Omega}\right)\\
&\quad-\left(\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}-(\alpha z_{h}+\beta\varphi_{h})I_{d},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}-\left(z_{h},\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{q}\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}-\left(\varphi_{h},\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{r}\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\right)\\
&=\left(\underline{\boldsymbol{f}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\right)_{\Omega}+(g,\varphi_{h})_{\Omega}.\end{split}

Simplifying terms and recalling that
\left(\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}}R,\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\right)_{\Omega}=\left(\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{u}}},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}
, the equation becomes:

(33)

\displaystyle(\dot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}},\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{h})_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}

\displaystyle-\left(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}-(\alpha z_{h}+\beta\varphi_{h})I_{d},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\left(z_{h},\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{q}\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\left(\varphi_{h},\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{r}\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}=(g,\varphi_{h})_{\Omega}.

Now, we point out that applying Green’s formula to the second equation of (
19
) yields:

(\dot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}},\boldsymbol{\Phi})_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}-(\boldsymbol{\tau}-(\alpha z+\beta\varphi)I_{d},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))_{\Omega}+(z,\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{q})_{\Omega}+(\varphi,\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{r})_{\Omega}=(g,\varphi)_{\Omega},

for all
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\in\mathcal{X}_{2}
. Hence, the density of the embedding
\mathcal{X}_{2}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{2}
ensures that equation (
33
) holds true, thereby verifying consistency.
∎

5.  Convergence analysis of the HDG method

The convergence analysis of the HDG method (
23
) follows standard procedures. Using the stability of the HDG method and the consistency result (
29
), we prove that the projected errors

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{e}_{u,h}(t)&:=\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},&\quad\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{u},h}(t)&:=\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{u}|_{\mathcal{F}_{h}})-\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\\
\boldsymbol{e}_{q,h}(t)&:=\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{q}-\boldsymbol{q}_{h},&\quad\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{q},h}(t)&:=\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{q}|_{\mathcal{F}_{h}})-\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{h},\\
\boldsymbol{e}_{r,h}(t)&:=\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}_{h},&\quad\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{r},h}(t)&:=\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{r}|_{\mathcal{F}_{h}})-\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}_{h},\\
\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma,h}(t)&:=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},&\quad e_{p,h}(t)&:=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}p-p_{h},\quad e_{\theta,h}(t):=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}\theta-\theta_{h}.\end{aligned}

can be estimated in terms of the approximation errors

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\chi}_{u}(t)&:=\boldsymbol{u}-\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{u},&\quad\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\hat{u}}(t)&:=\boldsymbol{u}|_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}-\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{u}|_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}),\\
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{q}(t)&:=\boldsymbol{q}-\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{q},&\quad\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\hat{q}}(t)&:=\boldsymbol{q}|_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}-\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{q}|_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}),\\
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{r}(t)&:=\boldsymbol{r}-\Pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{r},&\quad\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\hat{r}}(t)&:=\boldsymbol{r}|_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}-\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{r}|_{\mathcal{F}_{h}}),\\
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma}(t)&:=\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}\boldsymbol{\sigma},&\quad\chi_{p}(t)&:=p-\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}p,\quad\chi_{\theta}(t):=\theta-\Pi_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}\theta.\end{aligned}

As before, we concatenate the error terms corresponding to velocities and heat flux by defining

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}(t)&:=\left[\boldsymbol{e}_{u,h}\mid\boldsymbol{e}_{q,h}\mid\boldsymbol{e}_{r,h}\right],&\quad\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{u},h}(t)&:=\left[\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{u},h}\mid\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{q},h}\mid\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{r},h}\right],\\
\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u}(t)&:=\left[\boldsymbol{\chi}_{u}\mid\boldsymbol{\chi}_{q}\mid\boldsymbol{\chi}_{r}\right],&\quad\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{\hat{u}}(t)&:=\left[\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\hat{u}}\mid\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\hat{q}}\mid\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\hat{r}}\right].\end{aligned}

It will also be useful to introduce the notations
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{\Theta,h}\coloneq(\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma,h},[e_{p,h}\mathrel{;}e_{\theta,h}])
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{\Theta}\coloneq(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma},[\chi_{p}\mathrel{;}\chi_{\theta}])
.

Lemma 2
.

Under the conditions of Proposition 4.3, there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
h
and
k
such that

(34)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\max_{[0,T]}\lVert\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\max_{[0,T]}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{\Theta,h}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+\int_{0}^{T}(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}(s),\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}(s))_{\Omega}\,\text{d}s+\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}-\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{u},h})\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\,\text{d}s\\
&\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\lvert(\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u},\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{\hat{u}})\rvert^{2}_{\mathcal{U}\times\hat{\mathcal{U}}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\chi_{p}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\chi_{\theta}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\Big)\,\text{d}t.\end{split}

Proof.

By virtue of the consistency result (
29
), it is straightforward that the projected errors satisfy the following orthogonality property:

(35)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left(\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}}_{u,h},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\left(\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}_{\Theta,h},\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\Omega}+B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{\Theta,h},(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}))\\
&\qquad\qquad-B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{u},h}))+\langle\tfrac{(k+1)^{2}}{h_{\mathcal{F}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}-\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{u},h}),\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}-\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\\
&=-\left(\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}}_{u},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}-\left(\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}_{\Theta},\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}-(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\Omega}-B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{\Theta},(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}))\\
&\qquad\qquad+B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},(\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u},\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{\hat{u}}))-\langle\tfrac{(k+1)^{2}}{h_{\mathcal{F}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u}-\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{\hat{u}}),\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}-\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\end{split}

for all
\boldsymbol{\Phi}=(\boldsymbol{\tau},[z\mathrel{;}\varphi])\in\mathcal{H}_{2,h}
and
(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}})\in\mathcal{H}_{1,h}\times\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1,h}
.

The second line of (
35
) simplifies using the
L^{2}
-orthogonality of the projectors:

\displaystyle(\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}}_{u},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u},\underline{\boldsymbol{v}})_{\Omega}=0\quad\forall\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{H}_{1,h},\quad\text{and}\quad\left(\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}_{\Theta},\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}=0\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\Phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{2,h}.

Furthermore, for all
(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}})\in\mathcal{H}_{1,h}\times\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{1,h}
, the following identity holds:

B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{\Theta},(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\underline{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}))=-\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma}-(\alpha\chi_{p}+\beta\chi_{\theta})I_{d})\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{v}-\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\left\langle\chi_{p}\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{w}-\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\left\langle\chi_{\theta}\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{s}-\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}.

This is a direct consequence of the definition of
B_{h}
and the fact that
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d}))\subset\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}})
and
\operatorname{div}(\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{R}^{d}))\subset\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
.

The choices
\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{\Theta,h}
and
(\underline{\boldsymbol{v}},\hat{\underline{\boldsymbol{v}}})=(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{u},h})
in (
35
) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality together with (
25
) yield

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}

\displaystyle\Big\{\lVert\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{\Theta,h}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\Big\}+(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h},\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h})_{\Omega}+\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}-\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{u},h})\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma}-(\alpha\chi_{p}+\beta\chi_{\theta})I_{d})\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{e}_{u,h}-\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{u}_{h}}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}-\left\langle\chi_{p}\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{e}_{q,h}-\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{q},h}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}-\left\langle\chi_{\theta}\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{e}_{r,h}-\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{r},h}\right\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}

\displaystyle\quad+B_{h}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{\Theta,h},(\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u},\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{\hat{u}}))-\langle\tfrac{(k+1)^{2}}{h_{\mathcal{F}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u}-\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{\hat{u}}),\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}-\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{u},h}\rangle_{\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma}-(\alpha\chi_{p}+\beta\chi_{\theta})I_{d})\boldsymbol{n}\rVert_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}(\boldsymbol{e}_{u,h}-\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{u}_{h}})\rVert_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}

\displaystyle\quad+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\chi_{p}\boldsymbol{n}\rVert_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}(\boldsymbol{e}_{q,h}-\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{q},h})\rVert_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\chi_{\theta}\boldsymbol{n}\rVert_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}(\boldsymbol{e}_{r,h}-\boldsymbol{e}_{\hat{r},h})\rVert_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}

\displaystyle\quad+C\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{\Theta,h}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\lvert(\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u},\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{\hat{u}})\rvert_{\mathcal{U}\times\hat{\mathcal{U}}}+\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u}-\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{\hat{u}})\rVert_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}-\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{u},h})\rVert_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}.

We notice that, because of assumption (
24
), the projected errors satisfy vanishing initial conditions, namely,
\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma,h}(0)=\mathbf{0}
,
e_{p,h}(0)=0
,
e_{\theta,h}(0)=0
and
(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}(0),\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{u},h}(0))=(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})
. Hence, integrating over
t\in(0,T]
and using again the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we deduce that

\displaystyle\lVert\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{\Theta,h}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+\int_{0}^{T}(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}(s),\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}(s))_{\Omega}\,\text{d}s+\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}-\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{u},h})\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\,\text{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma}-(\alpha\chi_{p}+\beta\chi_{\theta})I_{d})\boldsymbol{n}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\chi_{p}\boldsymbol{n}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\chi_{\theta}\boldsymbol{n}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\Big)\text{d}t\Big)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}-\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{u},h})\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\ \text{d}t\Big)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{\Theta,h}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\text{d}t\Big)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\lvert(\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u},\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{\hat{u}})\rvert^{2}_{\mathcal{U}\times\hat{\mathcal{U}}}\text{d}t\Big)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},\quad\forall t\in(0,T].

Finally, a simple application of Young’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\max_{[0,T]}\lVert\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\max_{[0,T]}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{\Theta,h}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+\int_{0}^{T}(\mathfrak{I}\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}(s),\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}(s))_{\Omega}\,\text{d}s+\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\tfrac{k+1}{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}(\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{u,h}-\underline{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\hat{u},h})\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}\,\text{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\sigma}-(\alpha\chi_{p}+\beta\chi_{\theta})I_{d})\boldsymbol{n}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\chi_{p}\boldsymbol{n}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lVert\tfrac{h_{\mathcal{F}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k+1}\chi_{\theta}\boldsymbol{n}\rVert^{2}_{0,\partial\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\lvert(\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u},\underline{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{\hat{u}})\rvert^{2}_{\mathcal{U}\times\hat{\mathcal{U}}}\Big)\,\text{d}t,

and the result follows.
∎

7.  Conclusions

This study presented a high-order hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) formulation for fully dynamic thermo-poroelasticity. We established the well-posedness of the continuous problem using semigroup theory and demonstrated the method’s optimal
hp
-error estimates. Numerical experiments, including manufactured solution tests and wave propagation benchmarks in heterogeneous media, confirmed the predicted convergence rates and validated the scheme’s accuracy and efficiency in capturing coupled phenomena.

This HDG framework provides a robust and computationally effective tool for multiphysics simulations in geomechanics, biomechanics, and materials science. It accurately models wave propagation, including
P
-,
S
-, and thermal waves, making it valuable for predictive simulations.

Future work will explore extensions to non-linear fully-coupled thermo-poroelastic problems, specifically considering systems that include a non-linear convective transport term in the energy equation.
