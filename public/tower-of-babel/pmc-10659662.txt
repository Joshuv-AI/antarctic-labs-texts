The impact of two dietary patterns on hyperuricemia in adults: A meta analysis of observational studies
Authors: Can Liu, Xin Kuang, Mingxiao Gu, Xuancheng Liu, Jiao Lu, Haiqing Yu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Hyperuricemia (HUA) is a crucial factor contributing to some chronic diseases among adults. In past observational literatures, scholars have debated the effectiveness of dietary pattern on HUA and inconsistencies exist. Given this condition, the study aimed to provide a comparative assessment of the relation between dietary pattern and HUA risk and offer implications to policy makers.Methods:A systematic research was undertaken in PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane, Embase, Medicine, ScienceDirect and Medline to identify observational studies examining the effect of dietary pattern on HUA, and search period was from past to January 2022. Meta analysis was applied by using the Stata version 11.0 software.Results:A total of 34,583 adults from 8 observational studies, 45,525 adults from 6 observational studies were included to examine the effectiveness of “healthy” and “meat/western” dietary patterns on HUA risk respectively. The findings suggested that “healthy” dietary pattern significantly decreased the HUA risk (OR = 0.73; 95% CI: 0.61–0.88) both in Eastern countries (OR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.64–0.98) and Western countries (OR = 0.53; 95% CI: 0.30–0.92) while the “Meat/Western” pattern increased the HUA possibilities (OR = 1.26; 95% CI: 1.17–1.37). Stratified analysis exhibited that “healthy” pattern reduced HUA risk in adults was more effective in cohort study (OR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.72–0.86).Conclusions:This study’s findings highlighted the potential benefit of healthy dietary pattern in decreasing HUA risk. Accordingly, implementing policy makers of countries should enhance to appeal adults to keep a healthy diet, offer financial support to low-income staff, or provide guidelines for adult’s dietary behavior changes.Trial registration number:INPLASY: INPLASY202290034.

1. Introduction

Hyperuricemia (HUA) is a metabolic disease caused by purine metabolic disorder. With the growth of economy and the change of lifestyles, the prevalence of HUA was increasing rapidly worldwide in recent years.[1,2] Previous studies have demonstrated that HUA was an important risk factor for some chronic diseases, such as hypertension,[3] diabetes,[4] metabolic syndrome,[5] chronic kidney disease[6] and cardiovascular disease.[7] Besides, numerous studies in this field were indicative of the fact that factors including genetic, environment and alcohol consumption, especially diet might contribute to the risk for developing HUA.[8–11] Additionally, researchers also illustrated that the level of uric acid in serum was susceptible to diet which is rich in purine, such as meats, seafood or fructose-rich vegetables and animal protein, diet and nutrients were critical to the management of HUA.[3] Due to the complicated correlation of diet and nutrients, it might be difficult to identify the specific effect of each aspect of diet because the effect of one ingredient could be partially confounded by the effect of others.[12]

Nutritional epidemiology studies which were mainly paying attention to individual foods and nutrients[12,13] have reported the association between diet and HUA risk. In this context, dietary patterns as a representative of comprehensive dietary variables, examined the diet in relation to chronic diseases and assessed the effect of overall diet.[12] A cohort study showed that adherence to the Mediterranean diet decreased the risk of HUA,[14] and a cross-sectional study indicated that adherence to the “animal products and fried foods pattern” and “soyabean products and fruit pattern” were obviously associated with HUA risk increasement and decreasement respectively.[15] Whereas, a null significant association was found between vegetable and fruit pattern and serum uric acid level in another study among the Taiwanese.[16] Considering the inconsistencies of these results, the effect of dietary patterns on HUA is still controversial, especially in adults. Furthermore, systematic reviews which conclude these researches are also not reported. Therefore, we conduct this paper to identify the association between dietary patterns and HUA risk in adult population.

2. Methods

2.1. Overview

In this analysis of effectiveness of “healthy” dietary pattern and “meat or western” dietary pattern on HUA risk in adults, we conducted the article research to identify satisfactory observational studies from 7 databases such as PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane, Embase, Medicine, ScienceDirect and Medline. The time period was from past to 31 January 2022. By dealing with these records according to the MOOSE (Meta-analysis of observational Epidemiological Studies) guidelines[17] and the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) statement,[18] 8 and 6 researches were included in final analysis for “healthy” and “meat/western” dietary patterns respectively. The quality of selected studies was assessed by judging 3 fields, the selection of the study groups, the comparability of the groups, and the determination of the outcome of interest according to the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. The scale allowed a range of points from 0 to 9, with higher scores indicating the better quality.

No separate ethical approval was demanded for this paper. We followed the guidelines for Meta-analysis of Observational Epidemiological Studies (MOOSE), the average of the logarithm of the observed OR (approximated to RR, if necessary) was estimated for each dietary pattern. The random-effects model was computed the summary OR and 95% CIs by using Stata version 11.0 software (StataCorp, TX).

2.2. Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search, without restrictions, was conducted to determine all the original papers on the correlation of dietary patterns with HUA risk among adults. The following search medical subject headings (MeSH) and key words were included in the literature search: (“Hyperuricemia” OR HUA OR “Serum uric acid”) AND (“Dietary pattern” OR “Food pattern” OR “Diet pattern” OR “Nutrient pattern” OR “Dietary score” OR “Food score” OR “Diet score” OR “Dietary index” OR “Diet index” OR “Dietary habits” OR “Food diversity” OR “Diet diversity” OR “Diet variety” OR “Dietary factors”) AND (“Factor analysis” OR “Principal component analysis” OR “Cluster analysis” OR “Exploratory factor analysis” OR “Reduced rank regression” OR “Principal component factor analysis” OR “Data-driven approach”).

The reference lists of selected articles and recent relevant reviews to determine possible other correlative publications were manually examined by us.

2.3. Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: evaluated the association between dietary patterns and HUA risk in adults; observational study; provided relevant data, including odds ratio (OR), relative risk (RR) or hazard ratio estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs); and published in English.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: no measure of whole diet (e.g., dietary screeners or individual questions); and low-quality studies, duplicate studies, individual case reports and studies without available data.

Two researchers independently selected, extracted, and assessed the quality of each potentially included study. Inconsistent views were resolved through discussion or in consultation with the third author. Although background information was useful, reviews and meta-analysis were excluded. No studies were excluded because of weak design or data quality.

2.4. Data extraction

We extracted the following information for each selected study: first author’s last name, year of publication, country, study design, sample size (if possible, number of cases and controls; cohort size and event cases), population characteristics (gender, age), follow-up time of cohort studies, HUA assessment methods, dietary assessment and dietary patterns recognition methods (FA, PCA, and PCFA), the methods for evaluating the dietary characteristics, the names and characteristics of dietary patterns, cutoff points for adherence to the different categories of the dietary patterns (tertile, quartile and quintile), 95% CIs risk estimates for the different categories of adherence, and confounders adjustment. When multiple estimates were mentioned in the paper, we excluded data which were adjusted for the most confounders.

2.5. Dietary patterns

To classify the dietary patterns, we relied on definition data of published studies. “Healthy” dietary pattern was characterized by a high intake of vegetables and fruit, poultry, fish, and whole grains.[19] Generally, the Mediterranean diet was also defined as a healthy eating pattern.[20] The selected articles marked this pattern as “Mediterranean,”[14,21,22] “Vegetable and fruit,”[23] “Vegetable,”[24,25] “Traditional Chinese,”[26] “Plant-based,”[27] “Soybean products and fruit.”[15] The second dietary pattern, named “Meat/Western,” had a high intaking of red meat, processed meat, animal fat, eggs and sweets.[19] The included articles marked this pattern as “Western,”[15] “Animal food,”[24,25] “Meat food,”[26] “Animal products,”[27] “ultra-processed foods.”[28]

2.6. The effect of dietary patterns on HUA risk

HUA risk was estimated through calculating the average of the logarithm of the observed OR or RR. We also applied the Cochran’s Q statistic based on chi-square test and the I2 statistic to assess heterogeneity in results across studies.[29] The I2 statistic yields results ranged from 0% to 100% (I2 = 0–25%, no heterogeneity; I2 = 25–50%, moderate heterogeneity; I2 = 50–75%, large heterogeneity; and I2 = 75–100%, extreme heterogeneity).[30] If the probability of publication depends on the results of the study, the meta-analysis results may be biased. The methods of Begg and Mazumdar[31] and Egger et al[32] were used to examine publication bias. If a potential bias was detected, a sensitivity analysis was further performed in order to evaluate the robustness of combined effect estimates, computer the possible effect of the bias, and to correct the bias. Besides, a sensitivity analysis was also done to investigate the impact of a single study on the overall risk estimate by omitting one study per round. In addition, the cumulative meta-analysis was conducted by updating the pooled estimate of the treatment effect each time trial results with a more recent publication date were added. A correlation P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The results of the meta-analysis were displayed in the form of a graph with forest plots representing the effect size in OR and the corresponding 95% CIs.

2.7. Role of the funding source

The funder of this paper has contributions in article screening and data extraction.

3. Results

3.1. Literature search

The primary literature search through PubMed (n = 2126), Web of Science (n = 340), Embase (n = 162), Medline (n = 84), Medicine (n = 16), Cochrane (n = 3), and ScienceDirect (n = 2) databases identified a total of 2733 articles. Of which, 852 duplicates were excluded, 1726 articles were unrelated the correlation between dietary patterns and the finding of interest. 133 studies were excluded after reading through the article titles and abstracts (Fig. 1). Subsequently, 11 papers were removed, including reviews (n = 4), Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (n = 1), animal studies (n = 2), adolescents (n = 1), and studies that did not use food frequency questionnaire (FFQ)/24 h recall questionnaire (n = 3). Therefore, 10 studies were included to identify different dietary patterns in the systematic review and meta-analysis at the end of the selection process.[14,15,21–28]

PRISMA flowchart for search strategy.

3.2. Study description

Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K664 shows the general characteristics of the 10 studies that assessed the correlation between adherence to the dietary patterns with HUA risk in adults.

These studies were published between 2011 and 2021. Seven studies were performed in China,[15,23–28] two in Greece,[21,22] and one each in Spain.[14] Three were cohort studies,[14,25,28] two were case-control study[15,24] and all others were cross-sectional studies.[21–23,26,27] All studies assessed the risk of HUA on women and men together and used a FFQ (22–168 items)[14,15,21–28] to collect dietary information. Additionally, one study also used an EPIC-Greek FFQ.[21] One study[22] derived dietary patterns through MedDietScore, another study[23] used a “Maximum likelihood method,” while other studies used a posteriori method (PCA, PCFA, EFA, and FA) to derive dietary patterns. Six studies[15,23–27] reported the relation between HUA risk and 3 different dietary patterns and 4 studies[14,21,22,28] only considered one dietary pattern.

3.3. The effect of dietary patterns on HUA risk

The overall analysis showed a significant reduction in the risk of HUA associated with the adherence to the “Healthy” pattern (OR = 0.73; 95% CI: 0.61–0.88) and a significant increase in the risk of HUA associated with the adherence to the “Meat/Western” pattern in adults (OR = 1.26; 95% CI: 1.17–1.37) (Fig. 2).

Forest plots of the association between “Healthy” A and “Meat/Western” B dietary patterns and HUA risk. HUA = hyperuricemia.

3.4. Subgroup analysis

In the “Healthy” pattern meta-analysis, the stratification by study design revealed a significantly decreased HUA risk in the cohort studies only. Stratifying the analysis by geographic location, the risk of HUA reduced significantly in Eastern countries (OR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.64–0.98) and Western countries (OR = 0.53; 95% CI: 0.30–0.92). Furthermore, similar results stratified by geographic area were also displayed in Asia and Europe. However, in the “Meat/Western” pattern meta-analysis, when stratifying the analysis by study design the HUA risk significantly increased in cohort (OR = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.12–1.29), cross-sectional (OR = 1.39; 95% CI: 1.15–1.68), and case control (OR = 1.50; 95% CI: 1.21–1.84) studies (Table 1).

3.5. Cumulative meta-analysis

The cumulative meta-analysis of studies reported since 2011 showed the expected progressive accuracy of CI had improved with the addition of the new studies (Fig. 3). The result demonstrated that the “Healthy” pattern negatively associated with HUA risk while “Meat/Western” pattern positively associated with HUA risk after inclusion of the first studies, and it was worth mentioning that the related effects were significant. Notably, there was no significant difference in reducing HUA risk associated with adhering to the “Healthy” pattern via including 2018 (Liu et al) study. With the inclusion of further studies reported from 2018 (Xia et al) to 2021(OR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.72–0.87), the cumulative meta-analysis indicated a slight reduction in HUA risk associated with adhering to the “Healthy” pattern. Nevertheless, there was a significant augmentation in HUA risk associated with adhering to the “Meat/Western” pattern when the study reported in 2018 (Xia et al) (OR = 1.27; 95% CI: 1.16–1.38) was included.

Cumulative meta-analysis according to the publication year of “Healthy” A and “Meat or Western” B dietary patterns.

3.6. Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

All the sensitivity analyses indicated the stability of our results and there had been no material modification of the estimates in any single study. When outlier study by Zhang et al[15] (OR = 0.32; 95% CI: 0.19–0.57) was removed from the “Healthy” pattern analysis, the risk estimates changed slightly. Additionally, small change was also observed in the risk estimates when outlier study by Zhang et al[28] (OR = 1.16; 95% CI: 1.05–1.28) was removed from the “Meat/Western” pattern analysis.

No publication bias was detected by the Egger’s test in the overall analysis in the meta-analysis on the “Healthy” pattern (P = .162) and “Meat/Western” pattern (P = .072).

4. Discussion

Our systematic review and meta-analysis explored the influence of dietary patterns on HUA risk. According to literature, several different health results were related to western and healthy dietary patterns.[19] Especially, the western dietary pattern increased the risk of chronic diseases, such as obesity,[33] chronic kidney disease,[34] coronary artery disease[35] and related metabolic diseases.[33] The healthy dietary pattern was associated with lower risk of the major diet-related chronic diseases, including diabetes,[36,37] cardiovascular disease,[38] some cancers[39] and cognitive decline and dementia.[40] Furthermore, past researchers have found that adults with diabetes and chronic kidney disease were associated with 4 times increased likelihood of developing HUA,[41,42] it suggested that meat/western might be more susceptible to lead to diabetes or kidney disease because of taking more animal and sweet foods, so this type dietary pattern were more accessible to HUA.

Considering the 8 included studies, 2 main dietary patterns (i.e. “Healthy” dietary pattern and “Meat/Western” pattern) were identified in adult population. The main finding of this observational study was that the “Healthy” dietary pattern showed a protective effect on HUA risk in the cohort study, and in Eastern and Western countries, and the “Meat/Western” pattern was associated with an elevated risk of HUA and the relationship still exist in the stratified analysis by study design. Similarly, several studies by He et al,[26] Liu et al,[27] Xia et al[24] showed that a positive association between meat dietary pattern and HUA risk, whereas a healthy dietary pattern, such as Mediterranean diet, was associated with lower the risk of HUA. In this study, “Meat/Western” dietary pattern significantly increased HUA risk by 26%, by 48% in the study by He et al,[26] by 34% in the study by Liu et al[27] and by 50% in the study by Xia et al[24] Moreover, HUA risk reduced by 27% through “Healthy” dietary pattern, while it decreased by 18% in the study by He et al[26] and by 21% in the study by Zhang et al[25] The “Meat/Western” dietary pattern was associated with elevated HUA risk of 20% in the cohort study, 39% in cross-sectional study and 50% in case control study. “Healthy” dietary pattern significantly reduced HUA risk of 21% in the cohort study, of 21% in Eastern countries and of 47% in Western countries.

The differences were likely to be influenced by different cultures of nations.[43,44] Besides, it also might be due to the different levels of cadmium which food contained in the adults, food can be contaminated through industries or agricultures, cadmium as the nephron toxic heavy metal, leading to kidney damage by tubular dysfunction and affecting resorptive ability for nutrients.[45] Moreover, geographical area[46–48] play a crucial role in developing HUA, other pre-defined typical dietary patterns around the world, such as the Northern Europe dietary pattern is characterized by high intake of fruit, vegetables, egg, fatty fish, low-fat dairy, legumes and oats[47]); the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension diet is characterized by high intake of vegetables, fruit, dairy, beans and whole grains[49]; and the Iranian diet is characterized by high intakes of dairy product, animal fat, sweets and organ meat.[50] Our study mainly combined dietary patterns from Asian and Europe with widely vary eating habits, particularly, traditional dietary patterns from China,[15,23–27] from Greece[21,22] and from Spain.[14] In fact, the traditional dietary pattern in China is characterized by high intake of fruits, vegetables, mushroom, algae food, legumes, nuts, rice and rice products, coarse grains,[15,23–27] and in Greece and Spain by full-fat dairy products, non-refined cereals, potatoes, red meat and products, fruit, vegetables, fish, poultry and legumes.[21,22]

Our research also found that cumulative meta-analysis effectively presented the trend of 2 dietary patterns on HUA risk with the accumulation of data over time. A large number of observational studies have been conducted to address efficacy questions that previous trials had already clearly answered through cumulative meta-analysis.[48] For example, as reported by Zeng et al[50] the association between trimethylamine N-oxide concentrations and kidney function was estimated by using cumulative meta-analysis.[51]

The “Meat/Western” pattern, characterized by high intake of sweets, fried foods, red and processed meat, carbonated beverages, and canned food, significantly increased HUA risk by 26%. To some extent, these foods may influence the development of HUA risk, especially foods with pro-inflammatory properties, including red and processed meats, fried foods, and canned food which can increase inflammatory cytokines.[52] In reality, although no significant correlation between the most pro-inflammatory diet and HUA was found,[53] inflammatory factors may connect with insulin resistance and lipid disorders.[48]

In addition, this study demonstrated that the “Healthy” pattern significantly reduced HUA risk by 27%. The healthy patterns tended to include the consumption of foods with high intake of olive oils, chicken and fish, fruits, vegetables and n-3 fatty acids, which could contribute to explain the positively association between the “Healthy” pattern and HUA risk. Indeed, adherence to healthy dietary patterns was significant inversely associated with the risk of MetS, obesity, hypertension, diabetes, inflammation, insulin resistance.[50,54] There was an article showed that the adverse effect of “Healthy” pattern on obesity was possibly counteracted through inverse relationship with diabetes and chronic kidney disease and a lower level of cadmium intake.[45] The beneficial effect of “Healthy” dietary pattern might be attributed to higher amounts of antioxidants including vitamin C, vitamin E, flavonoids, some plant proteins, carotenoids, isoflavone, monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids.[50,55,56]

4.1. Strengths and novelty

This study has a few strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first study in adult population to assess the relationship between dietary patterns and HUA risk. The result of this review can offer an implication for future related researches. A total of 34,583 adults from 8 observational studies, 45,525 adults from 6 studies were included to examine the association between dietary patterns and HUA risk, a relatively large number of adults can reduce sampling error to a large extent and allowing a greater likelihood to draw reasonable conclusions. The ORs adjusted for the most confounders were extracted to make the results more reliable. Besides, we applied cumulative meta-analysis, it illustrated effectively the trend of 2 dietary patterns on HUA risk with the accumulated data over time.

4.2. Limitations

Nevertheless, our study also has several limitations. As mentioned above in discussion, the developing of HUA risk may be related to other dietary patterns, but the studies the paper included were mainly from Asian and Europe, so the generation of the effectiveness was limited. If we included other researches from other nations, the result may be more accurate? We did an analysis of observational studies, the nature of the cross-sectional has a limitation to make a causal inference. Furthermore, the number of analyzed articles were just 8 and 6 respectively, the differences of unmodifiable factors, such as age, sex, genetic materials of different adult populations were possibly influencing the result. The inconsistent findings on the relationship between dietary patterns and HUA risk might be partly because of the cadmium in the food.[45] However, few studies until now have been conducted incorporated cadmium intake and no studies have been included in this review, therefore, the mechanism linking between them was still unclear. Our analysis only included the OR of the highest and the lowest quantile of healthy or western dietary patterns, limiting the assessment of the existence of any trends.

5. Conclusion

The study demonstrated that the “Healthy” pattern defined by high consumption of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, low and nonfat dairy and lean protein, could decrease the HUA risk while the “Meat/Western” pattern increased the HUA risk. Stratified analysis exhibited that “healthy” pattern reduced HUA risk in adults was more effective in cohort study (OR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.72–0.86).

Adults, especially these who have diabetes and chronic kidney disease, should take limited red meat, processed meat, seafood, sugar-sweetened drinks, and eat more fruits or other lower-fat-containing foods in order to reduce the HUA risk. The policy makers should pay attention to public health and concern HUA developing, it is worth appealing adults to keep a healthy diet, offering financial support to low-income staff. Additionally, providing guidance for change of dietary behaviors by food recommend guidelines, encouraging adults to have a balanced dietary pattern.

Further prospective studies with high quality, large number in adults and comparison dietary pattern and HUA risk are necessarily required. If it is possible, cadmium in the food also should be observed. In addition, balanced gender, grace in the samples, and the narrow age gap of participants between selected studies are also necessary to minimize the possible confounding effect.
