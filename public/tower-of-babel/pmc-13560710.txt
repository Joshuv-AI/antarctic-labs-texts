No link between polymetallic nodules and anomalous oxygen concentration increases in benthic experiments from the Clarion Clipperton Zone
Authors: Alexander P. Webber, Patrick Downes, Joaquim Bento, Leigh Marsh, Felipe S. Freitas, Michael Clarke, Sabine Kasten, Klaas Meyn, Clare E. Reimers, Alexander Webber, Klaas Meyn, Alexander Webber, Walter Geibert, Alexander Webber, Huaiming Li, Alexander Webber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract*BackgroundPolymetallic nodules in the deep sea of the Clarion Clipperton zone (CCZ) have been implicated in the abiotic production of oxygen during benthic chamber respirometer experiments. The hypothesis, termed “dark oxygen”, was supported with a correlation between average nodule surface area and oxygen production rates, given as a Spearman’s correlation coefficient of ⍴ = 0.664, p = 0.031, n = 11, suggesting that polymetallic nodules were responsible for the oxygen concentration rises and that larger polymetallic nodules cause more oxygen production. However, this correlation and the dark oxygen hypothesis appears to be incompatible with the observation that experiments in the western CCZ and a control experiment recorded increasing oxygen with no nodules present.MethodsTo investigate the dark oxygen hypothesis, we repeated the surface area analysis using image segmentation, and expanded the dataset with three additional chamber images, the weight of the nodules recovered from the chambers and a further three experiments for which nodule weight data are available.ResultsWe find no significant correlations between total nodule area, average area, total nodule weight or average nodule weight and net oxygen concentrations, increase in the chambers. The inclusion of the three additional chamber images in the analysis reduces the significance of the previously stated correlation to ⍴ = 0.433, p = 0.122, with confidence intervals that cross zero (−0.13, 0.78).ConclusionOn further scrutiny the correlation between oxygen production and nodule surface area is spurious, and consequently, there is no established causal link between polymetallic nodules and increasing oxygen concentration in the benthic chamber respirometer experiments. Since oxygen concentration increased in experiments regardless of the size, abundance, or presence of polymetallic nodules, we find it likely that polymetallic nodules are not related to the rising oxygen concentration in any of these experiments.

Introduction

Polymetallic nodules, which are manganese- and iron-oxide concretions found at the surface of certain parts of the seafloor, have been linked to the potential production of oxygen in benthic chamber respirometer experiments by Sweetman et al. (2024) in a hypothesis called “dark oxygen production”. 1 These experiments were conducted in the Clarion Clipperton Zone (CCZ), an area known for abundant polymetallic nodules. Benthic chamber respirometers incubate a small area of the seafloor in-situ, and typically record a net decrease in dissolved oxygen concentration over the course of the incubation. 2 This decline reflects both aerobic respiration by benthic fauna and microbes and the reoxidation of reduced inorganic compounds generated during anaerobic degradation of organic matter. However, some of the experiments reported in Sweetman et al. (2024) recorded an increase of dissolved oxygen instead of, or prior to, the expected declining oxygen concentration. The observation of increasing oxygen concentration contrasts with the results of other studies, which consistently measured declining oxygen concentration, not oxygen production, in similar settings with polymetallic nodules present. 3 – 7 As such, the results and conclusions of Sweetman et al. (2024) are anomalous in the context of scientific consensus on the topic, and the work has attracted criticism from the scientific community, detailing methodological concerns and issues relating to experimental design, reporting and interpretation of the data, and suggested significance of the findings. 8 – 15 In Sweetman et al. (2024), the rising oxygen concentrations were specifically attributed to polymetallic nodules on the basis of an ex-situ incubation of polymetallic nodules. This causal link was supported with a statistically significant but low sample size correlation between average nodule surface area and the amount of oxygen produced in the benthic chambers ( ⍴ = 0.664, p = 0.031, n = 11), which served to link polymetallic nodules to the oxygen concentration increases in the benthic chambers, and suggested that larger nodules correspond to more oxygen production. Sweetman et al. (2024) reported the total weight of nodules collected in each experiment, however, this data was not interrogated in relation to oxygen production. We also note that multiple chambers from the western CCZ recorded increasing oxygen when no nodules were present, 16 , 17 and a control experiment also recorded increasing oxygen concentration without nodules or sediment of any kind, as it incubated only seawater. 8 These observations cast considerable doubt on the hypothesis that the oxygen increases can be attributed to the nodules and appear to be incompatible with the stated correlation with average nodule surface area. We therefore suggest that the correlation between nodule surface area and oxygen production may be spurious.

Here, we aim to determine if any link can be established between polymetallic nodules and the rise in oxygen concentration in the benthic chamber respirometer experiments using all the available data. To that end, we re-analyse the photos that were used to estimate nodule surface area, and expand the dataset by including three additional chambers that were omitted from the analysis of Sweetman et al. (2024). We outline a method of image segmentation to extract 2D surface area values, and use these to interrogate the previously stated correlation between nodule surface area and the oxygen concentration increases. We also examine any relationship between nodule weight, which constitutes a direct measurement of the size and abundance of nodules present during the incubations, and oxygen concentration increases, using the weight of nodules collected at the surface of the chambers and buried within the sediment. These datasets allow the previously stated correlation between nodule size and oxygen concentration increases to be tested, and more broadly any link between the abundance and size of polymetallic nodules and increasing oxygen concentration in these experiments.

Methods

Benthic chamber respirometer experiments were conducted on Nauru Ocean Resources Inc. (NORI) research cruises 5D, 5E and 7A to the NORI-D contract area in the eastern CCZ. 1 These took place in April to June 2021, November to December 2021 and August to September 2022, respectively. The experiments used a KUM benthic chamber respirometer lander system comprising three incubation chambers (KUM, Kiel). Details of the benthic chamber respirometer deployments, oxygen optode data and experimental procedures are described fully in Sweetman et al. (2024); here we summarise only those aspects relevant to the nodule surface area re-analysis. Photographs were taken of the top surface of benthic chambers after recovery, showing the seafloor with nodules sitting on the sediment surface. After photographs were taken of the chambers, the sediment, fauna and nodules in the chambers were processed.

The chamber photographs were not intended to be used for quantitative analysis and consequently are not ideally composed for the task of image segmentation. They are taken at an oblique angle, often with poor lighting, with undesirable shadows and highlights that make segmentation difficult. Additionally, some photos were taken with water or fogging present on the camera lens. Nevertheless, the photographs can be improved by correcting the geometry and enhancing the contrast between nodules and the sediment. The workflow for image preparation is outlined as follows: 1.Use image processing software to bring the four corners of the chamber box to squareness.2.Crop the image to the four corners of the box, where the sediment surface meets the edges of the box. The image should now be proportional to the dimensions of the chamber, which is 22x22 cm.3.Adjust the brightness and contrast of the image, if necessary, to ensure the best colour separation between the nodules and the surrounding sediment.4.Use the paint tools to remove artifacts on the nodules, such as reflection highlights and mobilised sediment that has washed onto the nodule surface during sampling.

In some cases, steps three and four were applied iteratively to optimise segmentation performance, and some images required extensive editing to outline the nodules. After image preparation, the photos were accessed using Python, and image segmentation was applied with k-means clustering in HSV colour space, implemented using the Scikit-learn library. 18 The number of clusters was tuned for each image to best represent the nodules, and the nodule clusters were assigned. Alternative, more sophisticated segmentation algorithms were explored, but the variation in image quality resulted in the best outcome using k-means clustering, which is easily tuned to each image. Pixels classified as nodules were counted, and the known chamber dimensions (22 x 22 cm) were used to convert pixel counts to area, yielding the 2D nodule-covered area for each image ( Figure 1). Average nodule surface area was obtained by dividing the nodule surface area by the number of nodules in the image, following Sweetman et al. (2024).

In our analysis we include three additional chambers for which photographs and oxygen data are available: AKS279 chamber 3 and AKS316 chamber 1 and chamber 3. Although these were reported as showing increases in oxygen concentration, and the optode data and total oxygen production were published and used to support the hypothesis of oxygen production, 1 they were not included in the surface area analysis of Sweetman et al. (2024). A gap in the optode data for AKS279 chamber 3 was noted, but this does not appear to exclude the part of the timeseries where the oxygen concentration reaches a maximum ( Figure 2). AKS316 chamber 1 and chamber 3 record optode data for the full 47 hour period. We can see no reason to exclude these chambers from the analysis; the top-shot photographs are present, nodule weight and water volume data are available, and there are no comments in the sample logs regarding chamber failure.

The three additional chambers, which were published in Sweetman et al. (2024) but not included in the surface area analysis, record the full incubation period and the maximum oxygen concentration reached during that period.

We publish data for the weight of nodules collected from the benthic chambers after recovery. The nodules were collected and the wet weight was recorded from two depth intervals, the uppermost (0–2 cm) layer and the lower (2–5 cm) layer. Total nodule weight is obtained by adding these together and average nodule weight is obtained by dividing the weight of nodules in the 0–2 cm layer, which represents the nodules visible in the photograph, by the number of nodules counted in the photograph. These data are available for 17 chambers with corresponding oxygen concentration data, including three chambers for which photographs are not available. We therefore have a sample size of n = 17 for nodule weight analysis, compared to n = 14 for the nodule surface area analysis. Nodule weight and oxygen data is available for AKS331 chamber 2, but this was excluded from the analysis due to an incomplete oxygen optode data timeseries. AKS282 chamber 3 was also excluded from the analysis due to missing the 0–2 cm nodule weight value, which was not recorded in the offshore logs, despite the chamber photograph showing many nodules on the sediment surface.

We also report oxygen concentration increases from three benthic chamber lander deployments (AKS254, AKS257 and AKS261) from cruise KM1808 of the R/V Kilo Moana in 2018. 16 Although O 2 flux and total O 2 production in these chambers was not previously published, we have used available data 16 and the same method applied by Sweetman et al. (2024); taking the difference between the T 0 O 2 concentration and maximum O 2 concentration reached during the experiment, and using the calculated volume of water above the level of the sediment in the chamber to reach a total moles of O 2 that has apparently been produced during the experiment. The average nodule area, total nodule area and weight of nodules are all zero for these experiments, since no nodules were observed. 16, 17

We find no correlation between total nodule surface area and oxygen production (Spearman’s ⍴ = −0.451, p = 0.106) ( Figure 4A, B) and no correlation between nodule weight in the upper layer (0–2 cm) of the chamber and oxygen production (Spearman’s ⍴ = 0.115, p = 0.660), nor total nodule weight and oxygen production (Spearman’s ⍴ = 0.145, p = 0.58) ( Figure 4C, D). Average nodule weight also does not show any correlation (Spearman’s ⍴ = 0.47, p = 0.11, or ⍴ = 0.42, p = 0.17 with an outlier value removed) ( Figure 5). In contrast to the published correlation in Sweetman et al. (2024), we do not observe a statistically significant correlation between average nodule surface area and oxygen production ( Figure 6). Instead, we calculate a Spearman’s correlation of ⍴ = 0.433, p = 0.122, with 95% confidence intervals that cross zero (−0.13, 0.78). Therefore, these data do not corroborate the finding that oxygen concentration increases in the chambers are correlated to average nodule surface area. We note that our correlation becomes significant (Spearman’s ⍴ = 0.609, p = 0.047) when excluding AKS279 chamber 3 and AKS316 chamber 1 and chamber 3, which were omitted from the analysis in Sweetman et al. (2024). Furthermore, AKS273 chamber 3 is a univariate outlier in the dataset of Sweetman et al. (2024), defined by median absolute deviation 21 ( Figure 6A). When this outlier is excluded from their dataset, the correlation becomes insignificant (Spearman’s ⍴ = 0.5758, p = 0.0816, 95% CI = −0.08, 0.88). In addition, the chambers from deployments AKS254, AKS257 and AKS261 in the western CCZ recorded oxygen increases that are well within the range of the rest of the data (429.8, 769.7 and 287.2 μmol O 2) with no polymetallic nodules present ( Table 1).

Discussion

In Sweetman et al. (2024), rising bottom water oxygen concentrations were observed in benthic chambers deployed on the seafloor of the CCZ. During the incubations, the chambers contained many things: sediment composed of various minerals and organic matter, sediment porewater, seawater, polymetallic nodules in some cases, and all of the various forms of life present in the water, sediment and nodules. In order to link the rising oxygen concentrations specifically to polymetallic nodules, the correlation between average nodule surface area (albeit a 2D, pseudo-surface area) and oxygen production rates was stated as a Spearman’s correlation coefficient with ⍴ = 0.664, p = 0.031, and the number of observations was 11 ( Figure 6A). The correlation was used to suggest that larger nodules results in more oxygen production. The average nodule surface area was obtained by dividing the total surface area of nodules in a chamber by the number of nodules in that chamber, it thus describes the mean average surface area of any one nodule in the chamber. Using the average surface area alone is problematic because any information about the size distribution, number of nodules or total amount of nodules is lost when this ratio is calculated. For any given value of a ratio, the magnitude of the numerator and denominator can be large or small, as long as the two are varied in tandem. In other words, a chamber with very few nodules could have the same average surface area as a chamber with a great many nodules ( Figure 7). The chambers in this study display considerable variation in both total nodule area and number of nodules ( Table 1). Consequently, the average surface area should be accompanied by some measure of nodule abundance — for example total surface area, or nodule weight. Therefore, correlating oxygen production against this average surface area alone is unlikely to demonstrate the relationship that was claimed in Sweetman et al. (2024), at least without a greater number of samples to better represent the natural range of nodule abundance. It is more likely that the low number of samples allowed a spurious correlation to emerge. We have demonstrated that including all the available data in the analysis results in a statistically insignificant correlation ( Figure 6B), confirming that the published correlation was spurious, and raises questions about the unexplained omission of chambers from the analysis of Sweetman et al. (2024).

These two chambers have the same average nodule surface area, but very different total surface areas and number of nodules.

Instead of average surface area, at least two alternative datasets are available that could be used to link polymetallic nodules to rising oxygen concentrations. The total surface area of nodules in each chamber could be used, however, there is no such correlation. The data presented in Sweetman et al. (2024) has a non-significant Spearman’s correlation coefficient of ⍴ = −0.136, p = 0.69 ( Figure 4A). After applying our segmentation method to these data, together with the three additional chambers, we also find no correlation ( Figure 4B). The weight of nodules collected from the chambers could have been used, which is a direct measurement of the size and quantity of nodules present in the chamber whilst also having a higher statistical power due to the greater number of samples (n = 17). Neither the weight of nodules in the upper layer, nor the total weight of nodules, nor the average weight of nodules show a correlation ( Figure 4C, D, Figure 5). The lack of correlation with average nodule weight is particularly revealing, since it represents a similar metric to the average surface area in that it describes the size of the nodules present, but is more robust because it uses directly obtained empirical data rather than a derived and somewhat problematic 2D pseudo-surface area. Other methods of measuring surface area of polymetallic nodules are available, such as N 2 gas adsorption, 22 and these could be considered for future studies. However, the currently available data are unable to establish a relationship between the size or abundance of nodules in the chambers and the oxygen concentration increases, and therefore they do not support the assertion made in Sweetman et al. (2024) that the oxygen concentration increases can be attributed to the polymetallic nodules in the benthic chamber respirometers.

It is highly pertinent to the discussion to note that three chambers from deployments AKS254, AKS257 and AKS261, collected in the western CCZ on cruise KM1808 of the R/V Kilo Moana either did not contain polymetallic nodules or only contained manganese oxide granules. 16, 17 These three deployments recorded increases in oxygen concentration of a similar magnitude to the experiments that included abundant polymetallic nodules ( Table 1). AKS254, AKS257 and AKS261 were conducted in Areas of Particular Environmental Interest (APEI) 7, 4 and 1, respectively, and were 600 to 1,500 kilometres from each other. Photographs and descriptions of the sediments at these sites are available. 23– 27 Nodule abundance in APEIs 1 and 4 is described as sparse, patchy, and with areas of no nodules. No nodules at all were observed or collected in APEI 7. 26, 27 Grainsize analysis of the sediment at all three locations show a peak at 8 μm and a peak at 80 μm, and all measured particles were less than around half a millimetre in size. 26 Photographs of the sediment surface at these areas show a muddy brown seafloor typical of abyssal clay sediments. 23– 26 Photos of the sediment surface at APEIs 1 and 4 show some sparse nodules, whereas photos taken at APEI 7 are clear of any polymetallic nodules. Abundant manganese oxide particles, granules or micro-nodules are not apparent in any of the published photographs from these areas. The lack of nodules precludes the possibility of nodules being responsible for the oxygen concentration increases in these experiments. Manganese oxide granules or micro-nodules are not considered in Sweetman et al. (2024) and were not accounted for in their experimental design or conclusions. We suggest that if manganese oxide granules are to be implicated in oxygen production instead, or in addition to centimetre-scale nodules, then this would implicate oxic deep sea sediments more widely, since manganese oxide is a common constituent of such sediments worldwide. 28, 29 In particular, the experiment in APEI 7 would seem to serve as a control site for nodule-free oxic abyssal sediment, found in many areas of abyssal seafloor in all the world’s oceans. 30 It is, therefore, also pertinent that oxygen production has not been reported in any other locations, whether in the presence of nodules or not. 1

In addition to the chamber experiments from the western CCZ conducted without nodules, a control experiment – known as a closed bottom chamber experiment – was conducted above the seafloor and incubated only seawater without nodules, sediment, or manganese oxide granules, and recorded rising oxygen concentrations in two out of three chambers. 8 This control experiment suggests that the equipment itself is capable of causing rising oxygen concentrations in the chambers, which would constitute an experimental artefact. There are several lines of evidence that support the hypothesis that experimental artefact may have caused the rising oxygen concentrations in the other deployments as well, many of which have been discussed elsewhere, 8 – 13 , 15 and most notably the fact that the results of Sweetman et al. (2024) are anomalous compared to other studies in similar areas of the seafloor. 3 – 7 These other experiments, which effectively stand as proactive repetitions of the experiments of Sweetman et al. (2024), did not repeat the finding, thus undermining the dark oxygen hypothesis from the start. Even in isolation, the work of Sweetman et al. (2024) is confounding: they found rising oxygen concentrations inconsistently between chambers on the same lander, with and without manganese nodules or granules present, with just normal abyssal sediment present, and even with seawater alone. The correlation between nodule size and the magnitude of oxygen concentration increase was the sole piece of evidence that linked nodules in particular to the oxygen rises in the benthic chamber experiments, and this has now been invalidated under further scrutiny.

Aside from the benthic chamber experiments, there is one remaining piece of evidence that links polymetallic nodules to rising oxygen concentrations. This is a single ex-situ incubation performed with one or more polymetallic nodule immersed in seawater. 1 However, there is little detail about this experiment, including how the nodule(s) were stored prior to the incubation commencing, or the composition of the gas that was produced during the incubation. A simple analysis of the gas would determine if it was atmospheric in composition, which would be consistent with the release of trapped air rather than in situ oxygen production. We note that in the time since Sweetman et al. (2024) was published, no study has replicated this result, despite nodules being readily available at various institutions around the world. Such a finding—that polymetallic nodules can spontaneously produce oxygen when submerged in seawater—would be extraordinary, and likely violate the established laws of thermodynamics. 8, 10 Furthermore, this experiment is at odds with the data from the western CCZ 16, 17 where oxygen concentrations rose without any polymetallic nodules present at all, and the control experiment conducted without nodules or sediment. 8

Conclusion

Re-analysis of the data presented by Sweetman et al. (2024), together with additional datasets, demonstrates no relationship between polymetallic nodules size or abundance and rising oxygen concentrations measured in benthic chambers. Combined with the experiments conducted in the western CCZ that recorded rising oxygen concentration in the absence of nodules, 1, 16, 17 the nodule-free control experiment, 8, 9 and the prior work showing only oxygen consumption in nodule-rich areas, 3– 7 these findings collectively reject the hypothesis that polymetallic nodules caused the observed oxygen concentration increases. There is now a substantial body of evidence 8– 15, 31 that challenges the observations and claims reported in Sweetman et al. (2024).

Ethical considerations

Not applicable.

I have examined the manuscript submitted by Webber et al. as well as the 4 reviews already available for this submission. I agree with many of the comments and arguments given in these 4 evaluations – in particular those given by Walter Geibert and Clare Reimers. I therefore do not want to repeat all of them here in detail – but rather mention and echo selected issues and aspects that are most important from my point of view and add a few additional comments.

The manuscript submitted by Webber et al. has re-examined original data presented by Sweetman et al. (2024) and included three additional sites that were not part of the initial statistical analyses by Sweetman et al. The peer-reviewed article by Sweetman et al. (2024) that was published in Nature Geoscience reported a positive correlation between nodule surface area - determined based on an image analysis approach – and the increase in oxygen concentrations in benthic chamber

experiments/deployments/incubations. They hypothesized that seawater electrolysis may contribute to this dark oxygen production (DOP). The article by Sweetman et al. (2024) has received a lot of attention in the scientific community and has provoked a very controversial debate. So any follow-up studies to re-evaluate these findings and in particular to advance our understanding of this observation are highly welcome and of profound scientific interest.

Based on their re-evaluation of the data of Sweetman et al. (2024), Webber et al. report that they have not found a significant positive correlation between the nodule surface area determined based on an image analysis approach and the amount of oxygen produced in the benthic chamber experiments. They do not doubt or question the dark production of oxygen in these abyssal environments. However, unfortunately, they also do not further discuss potential processes or conditions most likely driving the increase in oxygen concentrations observed in the lander experiments and thus do not significantly contribute to advancing our understanding of this „observation“. Together with the fact that all 6 authors are (one was) employed at „The Metals Company“, this submission – as already stated by Clare Reimers – gives the strong impression that the prime objective is to prove the results and conclusions of Sweetman et al. (2024) wrong by demonstrating that there is no relationship between the abundance or surface area of polymetallic nodules and the production of oxygen in this dark deep-sea setting. This is not surprising as „The Metals Company“ of course has a strong (economic) interest that any further scientific observations adding to the wealth of findings that argue against potential deep-sea mining, are rather refuted or at least weakened.

The fact that the authors state that oxygen is obviously also produced in sediments where no (large or visible) polymetallic concretions are found at the sediment surface makes this observation even more interesting and demonstrates - as already stated by Sweetman et al. - that the process/es potentially generating oxygen in these abyssal environments is/are far from being understood. This may be due to the fact that the approaches applied to determine the surface area of the nodules (and other polymetallic precipitates) present at or within the surface sediments are either inappropriate and/or that other or additional factors and conditions determining the rate of this process are not taken under consideration. As already pointed out by Clare Reimers, I am also very sceptical about the way the surface area of the Mn nodules located at the seafloor has been determined – both by Sweetman et al. (2024) and by Webber et al. – namely by means of an image analyis approach. This is certainly not the appropriate approach to determine the specific surface area of these mineral precipitates. Based on the wealth of literature on shape, size, surface texture and mineralogy of Mn nodules as well as more than 15 years of own research and numerous cruises in the area of the CCZ, nodules in the CCZ were show to display a broad spectrum of surface structure/texture (e.g. smooth to botryoidal), shape, size and mineralogy. The approach chosen here is definitely not capable to take this natural variability into account. Moreover, the (undisturbed) surface sediments of the CCZ are known to typically show high enrichments of authigenic Mn precipitates that appear finely dispersed within the sediment as well as abundant micro-nodules. To me it appears plausible that besides the abundance of large nodules the high specific surface area of these fine-grained Mn prepicitates (invisible to image analysis software) together with the mineralogy of the precipitates will determine the overall/total specific surface area and control the (bulk) reactivity of the polymetallic precipitates present.

These considerations may underpin the broadly accepted insight that our understanding of conditions prevailing and processes acting in these remote abyssal seafloor environments is still very limited. However, the knowledge collected over more than 7 or 8 decades of research in the CCZ is sufficient to know with a high degree of certainty that any future mining activities removing nodules and heavily disturbing, removing and suspending the surface sediments will heavily impact and destroy these unique abyssal environments and ecosystems. Polymetallic nodules found in the CCZ and in other deep-sea environments have been shown to grow over time scales of several millions of years. So, targeting these precipitates during any potential future mining activities will irretrievably remove them from the deep-sea environment in conjunction with the unique ecosystems that rely on polymetallic nodules as hard grounds/substrate and the sessile organisms attached to these. Also the impacts of disturbance, removal and resuspension of the surface sediments – which typically goes along with any know (potential) technique collecting nodules from the ocean floor – will be profound and manifold. The sediments in the CCZ accumulate at extremely low rates - typically well below 0.5 cm/1000 years. So any natural recovery of the surface sediment habitat will take several tens of thousands of years. In addition, the organic carbon contents in these surface sediments are surprisingly high for the prevailing water depths – mostly due to the extended mid-depth oxygen minimum zone found in the equatorial Pacific Ocean. As a consequence, suspending these organic-carbon containing fine-grained sediments would enhance the mineralisation/oxidation of organic carbon in the water column, releasing CO 2 as a consequence and consuming oxygen in the lower water column. At the same time the intense re-suspension induced by any mining operation will significantly weaken the natural longterm carbon storage capacity of theses surface sediments.

So, to conclude I find it very interesting to see that the re-evaluation of the data suggests that oxygen concentrations do not only rise in benthic chamber incubations of seafloor areas covered by large polymetallic nodules but also in seafloor habitats barren of large/visible nodules. The findings of both Sweetman et al. (2024) and Webber et al. do not question dark oxygen production in these settings. However, as already mentioned above the work submitted by Webber et al. does not discuss, present or offer any novel scientific insight or aspects to advance the understanding of the observed increase in oxygen concentrations in the benthic chamber experiments. Besides, the numerous formal aspects (e.g. interpretation of the results of the statistical analysis, referencing) that are for example outlined in great detail in the review of Walter Geibert, I think there is a need to include and carefully discuss additional relevant scientific aspects, conditions and parameters that might be capable of explaining their observations, before I can approve this submission.

General comments

The manuscript “Anomalous oxygen concentration increases in benthic experiments from the Clarion Clipperton Zone are not related to polymetallic nodules” by Webber et al. To give an example: Even if fifteen measurements of oxygen production/consumption vs. tree number in a forest might yield a wide range of positive and negative numbers (trees produce, but also consume oxygen, and some oxygen might be produced/consumed by moss), a poor correlation would not mean that trees are not related to oxygen production. (Vice versa, a correlation alone would also not prove the relation). Correlation is not a proof for causality, the absence of correlation is not a proof for the absence of causality.

In response to your questions, the growing body of evidence and reanalysis of the results presented in Sweetman et al. strongly suggest that the reported "dark oxygen" process does not represent a valid natural process of oxygen production. Rather, the signal is more plausibly explained as an experimental artefact, with the oxygen signal originating from the equipment itself rather than the nodules. This is covered in detail in the references we cite in the Conclusion section, including Tengberg et al. (2024), Cuesta and Jaspars (2025), Trellevik et al. (2024), Downes et al. (2024), Nakamura (2024), and the synthesis presented in Downes et al. (2025).

We hope this clarification addresses your questions. We will revise the main text to make clearer how this contribution complements the existing body of evidence and further supports the experimental artefact explanation.

Thank you again for your constructive review.
