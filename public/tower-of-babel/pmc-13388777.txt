Functional hydrogel biomaterials for urological diagnosis and treatment—design strategies and application progress: a review
Authors: Kuntai Li, Huichong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In urology, the treatment of diseases such as bladder cancer, urethral stricture, and benign prostatic hyperplasia faces challenges such as short drug retention time, unstable therapeutic effects, and drug resistance. Traditional treatments, such as surgery, systemic drug therapy, and local drug instillation, although controlling the condition to some extent, still have many limitations, necessitating new localized treatment and precise diagnostic strategies. Hydrogels have made significant progress in urology, especially in the fields of drug delivery, disease diagnosis, and tissue repair. The biocompatibility, tunable physicochemical properties, and highly hydrated three-dimensional network structure of hydrogels give them broad application potential in the treatment of the urinary system. This narrative review highlights representative studies on hydrogel applications in anti-infection, anti-fibrosis, tissue repair, and cancer treatment, and discusses the composition, design strategies, and functional development of hydrogels. By analyzing material characteristics, response mechanisms, and therapeutic strategies, this article aims to provide guidance for future clinical applications of hydrogels in urology and serve as a reference for researchers in related fields.

Introduction

Urinary system diseases are common health issues worldwide, severely impacting the quality of life of individuals [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most common bacterial infections globally, with a lifetime risk of over 50% [2]. Bladder cancer, one of the most prevalent malignant tumors in the urinary system, can be controlled to some extent through transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) and intravesical drug instillation. However, it is associated with a high recurrence rate, long follow-up periods, and an increasing issue of drug resistance [3]. A 2019 study reported that the 5-year recurrence rate of bladder cancer is approximately 70% [4]. Additionally, the treatment of structural diseases, such as urethral stricture and benign prostatic hyperplasia, also faces challenges such as short drug retention time and unstable therapeutic effects. These diseases urgently require novel localized treatment and diagnostic strategies [5, 6]. Traditional treatment for urinary system diseases mainly relies on surgical intervention, systemic or local drug therapy, and imaging or endoscopic-assisted diagnosis [7]. Although these treatments control the disease to some extent, they still face several limitations, including the short retention time of drugs in luminal organs, making it difficult to maintain effective concentrations [8]; in diagnostics, traditional imaging and endoscopic examinations have certain limitations in identifying early lesions and locating small foci [9]; in drug delivery, frequent dosing, non-specific distribution, and systemic side effects of drugs further limit the repeatability of treatment outcomes and patient compliance [10]. Therefore, developing a new localized treatment, precise diagnostic, and effective drug delivery strategy has become an urgent issue to address in the field of urology.

Hydrogels, as highly hydrated three-dimensional network materials, possess significant biocompatibility, soft mechanical properties, and tunable physical and chemical characteristics, thus demonstrating broad application potential in urology. By controlling the composition and crosslinking of the material, hydrogels can achieve different biological properties and physical performances [11]. Thermosensitive hydrogels have been widely used in intravesical drug instillation therapy. By adjusting the gel’s temperature sensitivity, it can quickly transition from a solution state to a gel state at body temperature, thereby prolonging the drug retention time and enhancing its therapeutic effect [12, 13]. One study developed a thermosensitive hydrogel-based local treatment system for bladder cancer, which can continuously release anticancer drugs within the bladder, significantly increasing drug concentration and reducing systemic toxicity [13]. Adhesive hydrogels enhance the interaction with the urinary system’s mucosa, ensuring sustained drug release in conditions like urethral stricture or postoperative wounds. For example, Zheng et al. used chitosan and hyaluronic acid composite hydrogels for the treatment of urethral stricture, achieving a good local retention effect and significantly reducing postoperative recurrence rates [14]. Furthermore, functionalized hydrogels loaded with imaging probes have been used for visual identification and intraoperative localization of bladder tumors. Dong et al. designed a hydrogel system based on tumor microenvironment (TME) pH change probes, capable of real-time monitoring of drug release and dynamic tumor changes [15].

Responsive hydrogels can regulate drug release behavior in response to changes in pH, temperature, or enzymatic environments, demonstrating potential application value in urinary system tumors and infectious diseases [16]. Qu et al. developed a pH-responsive hydrogel system based on N-carboxyethyl chitosan (CEC) and Polyethylene Glycol (PEG) crosslinking for carrying doxorubicin (DOX). In an acidic environment, the material degrades and accelerates drug release, achieving targeted delivery to tumor regions and improving local anticancer effects [17]. This study provides a new approach for the application of hydrogels in the treatment of urinary system tumors, particularly in targeted therapy for tumor sites. Although existing research has made progress in various applications of hydrogels, current studies often focus on single diseases or specific functions, lacking a systematic summary and lateral comparison of the material composition, structural parameters, and functional implementation pathways of different hydrogel systems [18]. Existing research models are typically application-driven and overlook the unified design principles of hydrogel technology in urology [19]. Especially in the urinary system, which has a dynamic urine environment and complex mucosal interfaces, the biocompatibility, mechanical matching, retention mechanisms, and functional integration of hydrogels are often interrelated. Optimizing a single property may not fully reflect their value in practical applications [20].

However, compared with the existing reviews of hydrogels in general drug delivery or tissue engineering, a systematic analysis specifically focused on urological applications remains insufficient. The unique physiological features of the urinary system, including dynamic urine flow, urine-induced dilution and washout, the multilayered barrier structure of the bladder mucosa, and interfacial constraints associated with catheters or intravesical devices, profoundly affect the retention and functional performance of locally administered therapeutic and diagnostic materials [14]. In intravesical instillation therapy, conventional water-soluble drugs are readily washed away by urine, resulting in short local exposure times and difficulty in maintaining effective therapeutic concentrations; in contrast, hydrogel-based carriers can adhere to the bladder mucosa and act as local drug reservoirs, thereby alleviating these limitations, increasing local drug concentrations, and prolonging residence time, as demonstrated in experimental studies [21]. Furthermore, to overcome the penetration barriers imposed by the bladder mucus layer and tightly apposed urothelial cell layers, adhesive–penetrative hydrogel systems have been developed that can maintain close contact with the bladder wall in vivo while enabling deeper drug penetration, thereby enhancing antitumor efficacy [22]. On the surfaces of urological devices such as urinary catheters, antibacterial adhesive hydrogel coatings have also been designed to reduce the risk of catheter-associated infections while improving mechanical stability, underscoring the specific requirements for adhesion and lubricity in materials intended for the urinary tract lumen [23]. Collectively, these studies suggest that conventional hydrogel systems must undergo tailored design and functional optimization to address the specific constraints of the urinary system, such as urinary washout, mucosal barriers, and device–tissue interfaces; nevertheless, existing reviews have largely focused on individual disease entities or general drug-delivery mechanisms, with limited in-depth comparison and integration of the physiological and physical challenges unique to urological applications.

Therefore, this article provides a narrative review of the research progress of hydrogels in urology from the perspective of biomaterial design and functional implementation. Rather than aiming for exhaustive systematic coverage, it focuses on the key biological and physicochemical properties required for hydrogel applications in the urinary system, and discusses how different material compositions, crosslinking modes, and design strategies are matched to specific functional demands. Representative application patterns of hydrogels in anti-infection, anti-fibrosis, tissue repair, and regeneration are summarized, covering not only lower urinary tract disorders but also emerging renal-related applications, particularly kidney-targeted therapy and renal injury repair. In addition, this review highlights functional hydrogel design approaches for diagnostic platforms and tumor drug delivery in urinary system diseases, while comparing the advantages and limitations of different strategies in addressing the unique physiological challenges of the urinary environment. To further clarify the translational status of this field, we also examined relevant clinical trial databases and found that clinically advanced hydrogel-based urological applications are currently concentrated in only a limited number of areas, particularly thermosensitive intraluminal chemoablative systems for urothelial carcinoma and injectable hydrogel bulking agents for stress urinary incontinence, whereas many approaches for renal injury repair, urinary tract regeneration, stem cell or extracellular vesicle delivery, and other multifunctional reparative applications remain largely at the preclinical stage. Taken together, this review aims to provide a design-oriented reference for the functional development and clinical translation of hydrogels in urology, while also highlighting that future progress will depend not only on biomaterial innovation but also on clinical feasibility, delivery compatibility, retention under urinary conditions, manufacturability, and long-term safety evaluation (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Representative applications of hydrogel materials in urological diseases

Hydrogel material properties related to urinary system applications

Biocompatibility and biological safety

Hydrogels for the urinary system must have reliable biocompatibility and biological safety, meaning they should not cause significant immune rejection, inflammatory reactions, or systemic toxicity after implantation or tissue contact [24]. Due to the sensitivity of urinary epithelium to external stimuli, material incompatibility can induce local inflammation and fibrosis, potentially increasing the risk of stone formation, which can affect the structure and function of luminal organs (Table 1) [25]. Therefore, ideal materials should ideally have low immunogenicity, maintain chemical stability under direct contact with urine, and avoid releasing harmful degradation products [26] (Fig. 2A). From a material system perspective, natural polymer hydrogels (collagen, gelatin, alginates, and hyaluronic acid) typically exhibit better cell compatibility due to their composition being similar to extracellular matrix, and they can be gradually degraded and absorbed in the body [19]. Decellularized matrix hydrogels or scaffolds rich in collagen and proteoglycans, after complete removal of cells, nucleic acids, and other immune-related residues, can reduce immunogenicity and support urinary tissue regeneration. However, their preparation quality control directly determines the immune safety after implantation [28, 29]. Synthetic polymer hydrogels, owing to their high hydration and chemical inertness, typically exhibit lower tissue reactions and better long-term stability. In contrast, early particle-based urethral injection materials were reported to cause granuloma formation and migration complications, prompting researchers to turn toward continuous network, particle-free hydrogel systems. Polyacrylamide hydrogel (PAHG) can represent the design direction for long-term implantation materials in the urinary system [30]. For example, Bulkamid® with its high water content provides good tissue compliance and contains no solid particles, reducing migration risks [31]. Clinical follow-up studies suggest that it has good local tolerance, maintaining relative inertness and stability in tissues over the long term, thus providing more lasting effects [32]. In addition to injection applications, hydrophilic hydrogel coatings on urological devices are also used to improve material-mucosa interface compatibility, reducing friction and minimizing mucosal damage, which helps reduce the incidence of catheter-related complications. Overall, the functional design of urinary system hydrogels should be based on clear immunological safety, urine contact stability, and controllable long-term tissue responses.

Injectability and in situ gelation properties

Injectability and in situ gelation ability are key physicochemical properties for hydrogels to achieve minimally invasive delivery and local shaping in the urinary system. For urological applications involving the bladder and other lumen-containing structures, such as the urethra and renal pelvis, the hydrogel precursor should maintain appropriate viscosity and flowability during administration, enabling smooth delivery through a fine needle or catheter while preserving system stability under shear conditions. After reaching the target area, it should rapidly gel within a controllable time window, forming a stable three-dimensional network to achieve local retention and sustained action [33, 34]. The coordinated regulation of rheological behavior and gelation kinetics is central to the design of injectable hydrogels. In urological applications, these materials must retain appropriate flowability and viscosity during administration to allow smooth delivery through catheters or fine needles, while also resisting shear-induced structural disruption. Thermoresponsive hydrogels remain in a liquid state at lower temperatures and undergo a sol-gel transition at physiological temperature after injection, thereby forming a stable three-dimensional network that enables local retention and sustained drug release. The mechanical properties of such systems are typically evaluated by oscillatory rheology, including measurements of storage modulus, loss modulus, and the viscoelastic ratio, with frequency sweeps spanning low to high frequencies to capture network responses under dynamic stress. These measurements are generally performed under fully hydrated conditions and at 37 °C with moisture saturation maintained to better simulate the physiological environment of the urinary tract. Chemically crosslinked hydrogels form covalent networks in situ through reactions such as Michael addition or Schiff base formation, and their strength, stiffness, and resistance to washout can be quantitatively characterized by shear rheology or uniaxial compression testing; measurements obtained under hydrated conditions are particularly informative for predicting structural stability after injection in the setting of urinary washout and repeated bladder filling-emptying cycles. Shear-thinning and self-healing are hallmark properties of dynamic networks based on reversible covalent or noncovalent interactions, enabling the hydrogel to flow during injection and then rapidly recover its network architecture once shear is removed, thereby ensuring predictable in situ shape retention and resistance to washout. [35, 36]. In addition to temperature, pH, or ionic strength-responsive systems can also utilize the urinary tract microenvironment for gelation, although the stability of gelation and adaptability to individual differences still need systematic evaluation [37, 38]. Chemical crosslinking in situ gelation typically forms a covalent network in the body through mild reactions, such as Michael addition, Schiff base reactions, or enzyme-catalyzed crosslinking, which can significantly enhance the gel strength and resistance to washout. However, careful control of the biological safety and completeness of the reaction components is necessary to reduce the risks posed by residual small molecules [39–41]. Furthermore, shear-thinning and self-healing hydrogels, based on dynamic covalent bonds or reversible non-covalent interactions, can temporarily flow under the shear force during injection. Once the shear force is removed, the network structure recovers, which is advantageous for maintaining the in situ shape after delivery through long catheters and improving washout resistance stability (Fig. 2B) [42–44]. Overall, injectable hydrogels for the urinary system need to strike a balance between injectability, gelation rate, and mechanical stability after gelation, ensuring repeatable and predictable in vivo formation and retention, under the premise of biological safety.

Adhesion, retention, and stability in the urinary environment

The continuous generation and periodic expulsion of urine create a dilution washout and mechanical shear that impose higher retention and stability requirements for hydrogels in the urinary system [45, 46]. If a material fails to adhere effectively to the mucosal surface or maintain structural integrity, it is easily washed out with urination or rapidly diluted, resulting in insufficient local drug exposure, shortened treatment duration, or failure of implantation and localization. Therefore, the adhesion strength, resistance to washout, and deformation adaptability of the material collectively determine its effectiveness in the urinary environment (Fig. 2C) [47]. The adhesion of hydrogels mainly relies on molecular interactions between the material and the urinary mucosa or the surface mucous layer. The mucous layer is rich in mucins and negatively charged groups, and cationic polymers can enhance adhesion through electrostatic interactions. Chitosan and its derivatives are frequently used in this regard [37]. Thiol functionalization can further enhance mucoadhesive stability under hydrated conditions by enabling disulfide bond formation with mucins in PBS at pH 7.4 and 37 °C. The incorporation of functional groups such as catechol can provide multiple hydrogen-bonding and coordination interactions in aqueous environments, thereby strengthening adhesion to tissue surfaces and improving hydrogel residence under simulated urinary washout conditions in PBS buffer at pH 7.4. [48]. The effectiveness of these adhesion strategies typically needs to be validated through both wet adhesion mechanical testing and in vivo retention experiments. In addition to interfacial adhesion, the mechanical stability and tissue deformation adaptability of the gel are also crucial. The bladder wall undergoes repeated expansion and contraction during filling and voiding, requiring hydrogels to possess a certain degree of extensibility, toughness, and fatigue resistance to avoid cracking, peeling, or structural instability. Methods such as dual-network structures, optimization of crosslinking density, and nanoparticle reinforcement can enhance the gel’s strength and toughness, thereby improving its stability in the urine dynamic environment. Conversely, variations in ionic strength and pH within the urinary environment can markedly affect the stability of certain crosslinking modes, with networks primarily stabilized by hydrogen bonding or electrostatic interactions being particularly susceptible. Equilibrium swelling tests of PA and PE hydrogels have therefore been conducted in water baths across a salt concentration range of 1 μM to 1 M and a pH range of 3-12 to simulate the effects of urinary ionic and acid-base conditions on network swelling behavior and crosslink stability. Accordingly, the design of hydrogels for urological applications should incorporate these parameters into the integrated optimization of swelling properties, crosslinking stability, and degradation behavior. [43, 49].

Although the preceding discussion highlights the general requirements for adhesion, retention, and mechanical stability, the marked differences in urinary stability among hydrogels with distinct crosslinking mechanisms critically influence their long-term performance in the urological tract. Current evidence suggests that chemically covalently crosslinked networks, particularly those built on covalent backbones generated by photo-initiated acrylate free-radical polymerization, are more likely to maintain structural integrity under conditions of high ionic strength and pH fluctuation, and are generally more resistant to swelling and mechanical disintegration than purely physically crosslinked systems. By preventing rapid chain dissociation through permanent covalent bonding, chemical crosslinking confers greater network stability; for example, clinically relevant drug-delivery gels such as PEG/acrylamide interpenetrating polymer network systems have been shown to preserve their mesh architecture across varying pH and salt concentrations, thereby minimizing the impact of swelling changes on release kinetics [50]. In contrast, supramolecular or ionically crosslinked networks dominated by noncovalent interactions, such as alginate-Ca2+ structures or hydrogen bond-rich systems, often undergo ion exchange or bond dissociation when exposed to the elevated ionic strength of urine, leading to network softening, sharply increased swelling, and even structural collapse. For instance, classical physically crosslinked polymer/gelatin networks governed primarily by hydrogen bonding have been shown in experiments using 8% urea solution to undergo rapid water uptake, pronounced swelling, and eventual loss of shape, directly illustrating the susceptibility of hydrogen bond-mediated crosslinking to urea interference [51]. Dynamic covalent networks, including those based on Schiff base or imine bonds, generally exhibit greater adaptability across a broader pH range than purely physical networks, but they may still undergo bond rearrangement under extreme pH conditions or in the presence of chelating agents; although such reversibility may help accommodate cyclic shear stress and deformation, an unfavorable dynamic balance may reduce network density and thereby compromise resistance to urinary washout. Double-network architectures, which integrate a densely and permanently crosslinked network with a reconfigurable physical or dynamic network, can simultaneously provide mechanical robustness and environmental responsiveness in the urinary milieu, thereby substantially reducing the risks of excessive swelling and network destabilization under high ionic strength and urea-rich conditions [52, 53]. Taken together, the failure modes of different hydrogel network classes in urine follow a broadly recognizable pattern: chemically covalent networks display superior resistance to swelling and structural loss, supramolecular and ionic networks are more sensitive to changes in ionic strength and pH, whereas dynamic covalent and double-network strategies offer a tunable balance between robustness and adaptability through rational design.

Material degradability

The degradability of hydrogels must be designed to match the specific application goals in the urinary system. For tissue engineering and regenerative repair purposes, hydrogels are typically expected to degrade gradually in vivo and be replaced by new tissue matrix. The degradation rate should align with the process of urinary tissue regeneration and remodeling, providing necessary support and a cell-adhesive environment in the early stages, and then gradually allowing space for mature tissue formation [54, 55]. Common degradable materials include natural systems such as collagen, gelatin, and fibrin, as well as hydrolyzable synthetic polymers like poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) and polyglycolic acid (PGA), whose degradation occurs through enzymatic or hydrolytic processes [56–58]. By controlling crosslinking density, introducing hydrolyzable bonds or enzyme-sensitive sequences, the degradation curve can be programmed within a certain range to adapt to different tissue repair windows. For localized drug delivery and instillation therapy, the degradation behavior of materials needs to match the drug administration cycle. Short-residence systems should dissolve or disintegrate promptly after drug release and be expelled with urine, minimizing the risk of irritation and complications associated with long-term foreign body retention [36, 59]. For implantation scenarios that require sustained mechanical support or a long-lasting volumizing effect, inert hydrogels with high water content that are nondegradable or only very slowly degradable, such as polyacrylamide hydrogel (PAAG), are commonly used because their pronounced viscoelasticity and water-exchange properties enable durable volume retention and stable structural support. Experimental studies in porcine models and human tissues have shown that PAAG injection can maintain tissue bulking effects for 12–14 months, while eliciting only limited long-term tissue reactions, which are primarily characterized by the gradual formation of a thin fibrous network composed of macrophages and granulation-associated cells surrounding the hydrogel, without the development of a thick fibrous capsule or tissue induration. Although the material itself appears to have a favorable safety profile, potential long-term risks, including chronic inflammation, granulomatous reactions, and mild infection, still warrant careful surveillance in prolonged clinical applications [60]. Regardless of the degradation strategy employed, the safety and clearance of degradation products are essential prerequisites. Ideally, degradation should produce soluble small molecules or metabolites that can be excreted by the kidneys or further metabolized and eliminated, avoiding the formation of insoluble residues that could lead to deposition, blockages, or stone-related risks. Degradation assessments typically involve in vitro urine simulation soaking, tracking changes in mass and mechanical properties over time, analyzing degradation products, and in vivo imaging and histological follow-up to confirm the controllability and safe clearance of the material in the urinary system environment. Overall, the degradation design of urinary system hydrogels should achieve a synergistic optimization between the treatment window, tissue response, and excretion clearance to enhance clinical translational potential and long-term safety.

Hydrogel composition and design strategies

Natural polymer hydrogels

Natural polymers are derived from biological organisms or their derivatives, typically possessing good biocompatibility and degradability, and carrying structural features that promote cell adhesion and tissue regeneration. As such, they are widely used in hydrogels related to the urinary system [61, 62]. The main limitations of natural polymers include relatively insufficient mechanical strength and structural stability, difficulty in precisely controlling the degradation rate, and batch-to-batch variability caused by raw material sources and purification processes [63, 64]. Given the characteristics of the urinary system, such as repeated urine washout, local infection risks, and pH fluctuations, natural polymer hydrogels often need to be enhanced through chemical modifications, composite reinforcement, or multiple crosslinking strategies to improve stability and functional sustainability.

Chitosan, a typical cationic polysaccharide, is soluble under acidic conditions and can form solution or gel systems. Its positive charge makes it easy to interact electrostatically with the negatively charged mucin layer on the urinary mucosa, thereby enhancing adhesion and local retention capabilities. This property is valuable for bladder instillation and local urethral treatments [65]. Chitosan also exhibits certain antimicrobial activity, which helps provide protective support in urinary tract infections or mucosal damage scenarios. In terms of gelation, chitosan can form in situ gels through ionic interactions, thermosensitive systems, or dynamic covalent bonds, making it suitable for catheter instillation or injection delivery needs. However, its solubility and network stability in neutral urine environments still need optimization through derivatization or composite strategies [66–68]. One study used chitosan methacrylate/silk fibroin methacrylate hydrogel adhesives to promote the healing of cyclophosphamide-induced hemorrhagic cystitis (Fig. 3A), showing rapid hemostasis and muscle tissue repair promotion through angiogenesis and inflammation improvement (Fig. 3B) [69]. Gelatin and collagen are commonly used natural protein-based materials. Collagen, as a major component of the extracellular matrix, has good biocompatibility and cell-supporting capabilities. However, pure collagen gels often face the issues of limited mechanical strength and rapid degradation in vivo [55]. Gelatin retains functional fragments that are beneficial for cell adhesion and has better processability. It can achieve rapid gelation through temperature-induced physical gelation. In urinary system tissue engineering, gelatin/collagen systems are often used as bioactive components for cell-loaded scaffolds or repair materials. They are commonly combined with decellularized matrices and other structural materials to balance bioactivity with overall mechanical support [71, 72]. It is important to note that collagen injection fillers have been used in clinical treatments for diseases such as urinary incontinence, but their degradation and absorption, along with potential immune-related issues, limit their long-term efficacy and application scope. In recent years, more stable synthetic systems have gradually replaced them for some indications. Hyaluronic acid, an important component of the glycosaminoglycan layer in the urinary tract mucosa, plays roles in lubrication and barrier protection. Non-crosslinked hyaluronic acid is relatively soft and easily degraded, typically requiring chemical crosslinking or combination with other polymers to improve its stability and retention in the urine environment [73]. Crosslinked hyaluronic acid and its composite gels have been clinically applied in certain urinary-related indications, such as as an injectable filler for the treatment of vesicoureteral reflux and as a bladder instillation agent for the adjunctive treatment of mucosal barrier repair diseases. Hyaluronic acid is also commonly used as a composite component, combined with chitosan or thermosensitive polymers, to construct adhesive and injectable systems, improving gel strength and local retention [74] .

In addition to the materials mentioned above, alginates, fibrin, and silk fibroin are also commonly used in urinary system hydrogel design. Alginates can be rapidly ionically crosslinked in the presence of divalent ions, making them suitable for in situ gelation and cell encapsulation. However, their stability under varying ionic conditions needs to be evaluated in the context of urine ion composition and optimized crosslinking conditions. Fibrin systems, widely used as biological adhesives in clinical practice, are suitable for wound adhesion, hemostasis, and tissue repair. However, they degrade quickly and provide limited mechanical support, often being combined with other networks. Silk fibroin has good mechanical potential and processability, and after modification, such as methacrylation, it can form stable networks through photopolymerization. When combined with chitosan, silk fibroin can offer better wet-state adhesion, hemostasis, and barrier repair abilities, making it suitable for scenarios such as hemorrhagic cystitis or mucosal injuries. Overall, natural polymer hydrogels provide a strong bioactive foundation in the urinary system, but their clinical translational potential often relies on systematic design that enhances stability, controls gelation, and integrates functionality.

Synthetic polymer hydrogels

Synthetic polymer hydrogels offer significant advantages, including strong designability of structure, controllable composition and purity, and high batch-to-batch stability, making them suitable for urinary system applications that have specific requirements for mechanical properties, degradation behavior, and gelation kinetics [75]. By adjusting parameters such as molecular weight, functional group density, copolymer composition, and crosslinking methods, the gel strength, swelling behavior, adhesion properties, and drug release profiles can be finely tuned [76]. However, the main limitation is that most synthetic scaffolds lack cell recognition signals and require the introduction of bioactive fragments or blending with natural polymers to enhance cell interactions and tissue integration capabilities.

PEG is a commonly used hydrophilic and inert polymer, with a low tendency for nonspecific protein adsorption, making it a suitable material for constructing biocompatible neutral hydrogel matrices [77]. One study used thermosensitive PLEL hydrogel material, which undergoes a sol-gel transition at body temperature, significantly extending the residence time of drugs in the bladder and reducing side effects (Fig. 3C). In a mouse bladder cancer model, drug instillation therapy using this hydrogel system resulted in increased local drug concentration and significantly enhanced tumor suppression (Fig. 3D) [12]. Additionally, another study utilized polyacrylamide (PAM) hydrogel coatings combined with functionalized nanoparticles. In a rabbit urethral stricture model, this material was observed to slowly release antifibrotic drugs, significantly reducing the incidence of postoperative urethral stricture and improving tissue repair. These studies demonstrate that synthetic hydrogels have significant advantages in local treatment and drug release control for urinary system diseases [26]. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) based hydrogel coatings have mature applications in urological devices, where they form a lubricating surface after absorbing water, reducing urethral friction and mucosal irritation, and helping to reduce related complications. The drawback of PVA is its non-biodegradability, so when used as a long-term implant material in vivo, it requires careful evaluation or improvement in biocompatibility through blending with degradable polymers or introducing hydrolyzable bonds [78, 79]. Polyacrylamide hydrogels have representative applications in urology, such as crosslinked polyacrylamide hydrogels used as urethral surrounding injection fillers for female stress urinary incontinence. These applications highlight the advantages of synthetic hydrogels in long-term in vivo stability and low immunogenicity [80, 81]. Such materials emphasize chemical inertness and long-term shape stability to maintain tissue support and functional improvement, but when used as permanent or semi-permanent implants, they still require long-term follow-up to verify chronic tissue reactions and long-term safety.

In terms of injectability and in situ gelation, the block copolymer strategy can impart thermosensitive phase transition properties to synthetic polymers, allowing the material to remain flowable at low or room temperatures and rapidly gel at body temperature. This enhances local retention and supports sustained release [82, 83]. Thermosensitive gels, used in clinical formulations for upper urinary tract tumor instillation therapy, exemplify this approach. They extend the contact time of drugs within the renal pelvis and ureter system, thereby improving local treatment efficacy [84]. In addition to thermosensitive systems, synthetic polymers can also achieve rapid crosslinking under mild conditions through click chemistry, Michael addition, or enzyme-catalyzed crosslinking. By introducing polyester segments or other degradable structures, these systems allow for controlled clearance after treatment. To address catheter-related infections and biofilm issues, synthetic hydrogels can be surface-functionalized by incorporating anti-adhesion groups, antimicrobial components, or anti-inflammatory drugs. However, it is essential to balance release rates, durability, and potential cytotoxicity through systematic evaluation [19]. Overall, synthetic polymer hydrogels are more suitable for serving as scaffolds with controllable structure and performance. Through bioactivation and compositing, they can achieve the required adhesion, antimicrobial properties, controlled release, and tissue compatibility needed for urinary system applications.

Composite and functionalized hydrogels

To meet the comprehensive needs of the urinary system for adhesion retention, washout stability, anti-infection, and local regeneration, composite and functionalized hydrogels are an important direction in hydrogel design [85]. By integrating polymer networks with nanomaterials, exosomes, bioactive molecules, or imaging/therapeutic functional components, these hydrogels can maintain the advantages of local retention and controlled release while offering enhanced targeting, stimulus responsiveness, and tissue-regulating abilities (Fig. 4A) [86]. Nanomaterial-composite hydrogels are often used to achieve physical stimulus responsiveness and enhanced delivery efficiency. Magnetic nanoparticles can be combined with drug-loaded hydrogels, enabling local enrichment and directional delivery under an external magnetic field. This improves drug exposure at the target site and reduces off-target tissue burden [87, 88]. Metal or metal oxide nanoparticles can also impart antimicrobial, imaging, or photothermal-related functions. However, the particle size, release rate, and biodistribution must be strictly controlled to avoid potential tissue accumulation and toxicity risks [89]. For the bladder mucosal barrier and the dense microenvironment of tumor tissues, charged nanogels or surface-functionalized nanoparticles can improve mucosal adhesion and tissue penetration, thereby increasing local drug concentrations and treatment efficacy. However, their impact on the urinary epithelium barrier function and inflammatory response still requires validation through systematic experiments. Exosome-composite hydrogels have seen rapid advancements in regenerative repair research [90, 91]. Exosomes carry various active components such as miRNAs and proteins. Their endocytosis triggers the formation of early endosomes, which subsequently encapsulate selected cargo, including nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids, into multivesicular bodies. These multivesicular bodies fuse with the plasma membrane and release exosomes into the extracellular space. Exosomes, which carry proteins, mRNA, miRNA, and DNA, are taken up by target cells through three main mechanisms: direct fusion with the cell membrane, receptor interactions, and endocytosis (Fig. 4B) [92]. Exosomes have potential for immune modulation, angiogenesis, and tissue regeneration, but free exosomes can diffuse or be cleared in vivo, leading to insufficient local bioavailability. Loading exosomes into hydrogel networks can improve their retention at the injury site and enable sustained release, enhancing anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects, and promoting the repair of urological tissues, such as renal damage and sphincter injuries [93, 94]. The key to such systems lies in the protection of exosome activity, release kinetics, and batch consistency control, as well as clarifying the mechanisms of action and effective dose windows for different disease scenarios. Drug and functional molecule composites are one of the most common functionalization pathways for urinary system hydrogels. Anticancer drugs, antibiotics, hemostatic, and anti-inflammatory molecules can be integrated into the gel network through physical encapsulation or chemical bonding, enabling local sustained release and reducing systemic exposure. For catheter-related infections, hydrogel coatings can integrate antimicrobial metal ions, antibiotics, or anti-biofilm components to inhibit bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation, while balancing antimicrobial effective concentration and cytotoxicity risks [16, 95]. For bladder mucosal injury repair, growth factors or pro-regenerative signaling molecules can be released in a controlled manner to promote angiogenesis, epithelial repair, and barrier reconstruction, although care must be taken to avoid inactivation of factors and adverse reactions caused by non-physiological high doses [96]. The integrated diagnostic and therapeutic approach can incorporate photothermal agents, photosensitizers, or imaging probes into hydrogels to combine local treatment and process monitoring. However, clinical translation of this approach still requires further solutions for light/energy delivery conditions, tissue penetration depth, and safety boundaries.

Practical considerations for hydrogel administration in urology

The application of hydrogels in the urinary system depends not only on their intrinsic composition, crosslinking strategy, and functional design, but also critically on the specific route by which they are deployed. Compared with other organ systems, the urinary tract is characterized by continuous fluid washout, the complex chemical composition of urine, cyclic expansion and contraction of local organs, and the fact that many interventions rely on endoscopic or catheter-based access. Accordingly, whether a hydrogel can be delivered to the lesion through an appropriate route, achieve stable retention at the target site, and maintain its structure and function in the urinary environment must all be considered when evaluating its translational potential. Existing reviews have noted that the use of hydrogels in urology has expanded from early intraluminal drug delivery alone to a broader range of applications, including coatings for urinary catheters and ureteral stents, local injection therapy for urinary incontinence, and bladder and urethral tissue repair, indicating that research in this field has moved beyond a narrow focus on biocompatibility and drug-loading capacity toward a greater emphasis on the alignment between material properties and specific clinical modes of application. The summary by Wierzbicka et al. likewise suggests that the mode of administration or implantation required in different disease settings effectively determines the subsequent material requirements in terms of rheological behavior, gelation characteristics, adhesive performance, and degradation profile[19]. Among current approaches, local instillation via catheter or endoscopic delivery remains the most common and clinically translatable mode of hydrogel application in the urinary system, particularly for maintaining local drug exposure after bladder tumor resection and for intracavitary treatment of upper tract urothelial carcinoma. The principal advantage of this route lies in its close compatibility with established urological procedures; however, the major limitation to efficacy is not whether the material can be introduced into the bladder or urinary tract, but whether it can maintain sufficient local contact time after administration despite continuous urinary dilution and periodic voiding. In response to this challenge, reverse thermosensitive hydrogels and mucoadhesive hydrogel systems have become major focuses of investigation. For example, mitomycin-containing reverse thermal hydrogels based on in situ gelation strategies have been shown to significantly prolong drug residence within the urinary lumen and improve local therapeutic efficacy. This highlights that, in the urinary system, the mode of application is not merely a matter of choosing a delivery route, but is directly linked to the central translational challenge of local retention. For such systems, injectability or instillability is only the baseline requirement, whereas rapid in vivo gelation, local confinement, and effective adhesion to the mucosal surface are the factors that more fundamentally determine therapeutic performance [21].

This requirement for local retention is not limited to intravesical drug delivery, but is equally important in local injection-based therapies and device-surface functionalization. In applications such as urinary incontinence, stricture prevention, or local antifibrotic treatment, hydrogels must maintain appropriate flowability during injection to allow smooth delivery through fine needles or catheters, yet rapidly form a relatively stable network upon reaching the target site, thereby minimizing diffusion and migration while prolonging the local activity of the drug or bioactive molecules. Previous studies have applied mitomycin-containing gels after internal urethrotomy to prevent recurrence and have shown some potential in reducing stricture relapse, suggesting that the value of locally injected hydrogels lies not only in the pharmacological agent they carry, but also in their ability to establish a spatially confined distribution and enable sustained release within the postoperative wound microenvironment [12, 97]. By contrast, in device-related applications involving ureteral stents and urinary catheters, hydrogels are more often used as interfacial modification layers, where the emphasis is no longer on forming a free-standing gel reservoir, but on achieving stable attachment to the device surface so as to improve lubricity, reduce protein deposition and bacterial adhesion, and, when necessary, provide sustained drug release to mitigate device-associated complications. Tian et al. developed a drug-eluting hydrogel coating for ureteral stents that exhibited only approximately 45% mass loss after 3 months of immersion in artificial urine while largely preserving its surface morphology and interfacial integrity over time, indicating that for urological devices, the ability of a hydrogel to remain stable under prolonged urinary exposure and continuous friction is directly related to whether its antistenotic and antifouling functions can be effectively maintained [26]. Overall, whether used for instillation, injection, or coating, the diverse application modes of hydrogels in the urinary system ultimately converge on the same fundamental requirement: the material must maintain sufficient local confinement and sustained functionality within a dynamic wet environment.

In this context, the influence of the urinary environment on hydrogel behavior should not be regarded merely as an external source of interference, but rather as a defining determinant of translational feasibility. Within the urinary system, urine not only shortens material residence time through continuous washout, but also affects hydrogel swelling, network stability, and degradation through its urea content, ionic composition, pH fluctuations, and the mechanical perturbations associated with cyclic filling and voiding. Systems that rely predominantly on hydrogen bonding or ionic interactions for structural integrity are particularly susceptible to network relaxation, volumetric expansion, and mechanical weakening under these conditions; accordingly, stability demonstrated in conventional buffer solutions cannot be assumed to reflect performance in the actual urinary milieu. Chen et al. reported that a tea polyphenol-enhanced silk fibroin hydrogel was able to maintain favorable adhesive properties in urine and, in a cyclophosphamide-induced hemorrhagic cystitis model, promoted hemostasis, alleviated inflammation, and supported neovascularization and smooth muscle repair; importantly, the study also showed that urea directly influenced hydrogen-bonding interactions within the system as well as its degradation behavior [43]. These findings indicate that hydrogel design for urological applications cannot remain limited to the conventional requirements of biocompatibility and mild degradability that are often emphasized in general tissue engineering, but must also account for the specific interactions between the chemical environment of urine and the material network. In other words, the establishment of effective local retention depends not only on initial adhesion and rapid gelation, but also on the ability of the material to preserve network stability within urine.

When the therapeutic objective extends beyond local drug delivery to urinary tract tissue regeneration, these challenges become even more pronounced. Unlike drug-delivery applications, hydrogels used in regenerative settings must serve not only as carriers for bioactive factors, but also, over a defined period, as interfaces for tissue adhesion, space-filling matrices, guides for cellular organization, and even provisional structural support during early tissue repair [57, 98]. However, the use of hydrogels alone for urinary tract reconstruction is often associated with substantial limitations, particularly in tissues such as the bladder and urethra, which exist in a persistently wet environment while also needing to maintain luminal architecture; under these conditions, conventional hydrogels generally cannot simultaneously meet the requirements for suture fixation, tear resistance, and durable mechanical support [99]. A single soft hydrogel is therefore rarely sufficient to accomplish complex urinary tract repair independently, largely because its inherently compliant and loosely organized structure provides limited load-bearing capacity, making it prone to tearing or interfacial instability when directly sutured to native tissue, while also impairing its ability to preserve the intended shape during prolonged urine exposure and repeated organ deformation [100]. Accordingly, recent strategies have increasingly avoided the use of hydrogels as stand-alone scaffolds and instead combined them with fibrous membranes, decellularized matrices, printable supporting frameworks, or multilayer composite patches, allowing the hydrogel component to function primarily in bioactive regulation, cell delivery, and interfacial microenvironmental modulation, while complementary structural materials provide the necessary mechanical reinforcement and surgical handling properties. From this perspective, the value of hydrogels in urinary tract regeneration lies not in replacing all structural biomaterials, but in working synergistically with supportive substrates to create repair systems better suited to the anatomical and functional demands of the urinary tract.

Overall, the mode of hydrogel application in the urinary system should not be viewed simply as the route by which a material is introduced into the body, but rather as a critical interface linking material design with clinical implementation. For intraluminal instillation systems, the central requirements are in situ gelation and sustained mucosal retention; for local injection systems, the key considerations are the delivery window, confinement of local distribution, and prolonged drug release; for device-surface coatings, the primary focus is interfacial adhesion stability together with long-term structural integrity under artificial urine exposure and frictional stress; and for tissue-regenerative applications, additional attention must be given to structural preservation in the urinary environment, fixation to host tissue, and the limitations imposed by the insufficient mechanical support of hydrogels alone. Accordingly, future development of hydrogels for urological applications should move beyond isolated optimization of individual material properties and instead place greater emphasis on their systematic alignment with the intended mode of delivery, the microenvironment of the target site, and the specific demands of tissue repair.

Hydrogels in urology treatment strategies

Hydrogels have shown broad therapeutic potential across multiple urological conditions, with current treatment-oriented research mainly focusing on antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory intervention, antifibrotic and anti-stricture regulation, tissue repair and regeneration, as well as renal injury repair and kidney-targeted therapy. These application directions reflect the versatility of hydrogels as functional biomaterials in addressing the distinct pathological and physiological challenges of the urinary system. Antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory hydrogels

UTIs are one of the most common bacterial infections in clinical settings, with catheter-associated infections being a prominent contributor to hospital-acquired infections. The key pathological event involves bacterial adhesion to the device surface, colonization, and biofilm formation, leading to persistent infection, recurrent episodes, and decreased efficacy of antimicrobial agents [101]. Traditional strategies primarily rely on systemic antibiotics or antimicrobial surface treatments of medical devices. However, systemic drug administration can lead to antibiotic resistance and systemic side effects, while simple antimicrobial surfaces often fail to sustain biofilm prevention under the complex urinary environment and prolonged indwelling conditions[102–104]. Therefore, hydrogels, with their high water content, excellent lubricity, and functionalizable features, have been widely used for modifying the surface of devices such as catheters and for local drug delivery in the urinary tract to reduce bacterial adhesion and provide local antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory effects.

As device coatings, hydrogels can create a stable, highly hydrated layer on the surface, reducing protein adsorption and bacterial adhesion, thus weakening biofilm formation at the source [105, 106]. To enhance antimicrobial properties, hydrogels are often combined with antimicrobial components such as silver, copper, or antimicrobial polymers to maintain a sustained local antimicrobial environment [107]. Some clinical and preclinical studies suggest that silver alloy or nanosilver-modified hydrogel coatings can reduce urinary culture positivity rates and the incidence of catheter-associated infections, indicating the feasibility of combining anti-adhesion base layers with local antimicrobial components. It is important to emphasize that metal ion-based systems should establish a clear dosage window between antimicrobial effectiveness and cytotoxicity, and evaluate their release behavior and safety boundaries under long-term indwelling conditions, urine ion environments, and inflammation [108]. In addition to passive anti-adhesion, hydrogels can also serve as active controlled release carriers to locally release antibiotics, antimicrobial peptides, or anti-biofilm molecules, maintaining effective concentrations while reducing systemic exposure (Fig. 5A) [109]. A study employing an on-demand antibacterial, self-adaptive anti-fouling, and self-healing lubricating hydrogel coating for catheters showed significant inhibition of Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Fig. 5B) [109]. Controlled release designs for infections often rely on the urinary infection microenvironment characteristics, such as bacterial enzyme activity, pH changes, or the elevation of inflammatory mediators. These triggers release the drugs early during bacterial colonization, inhibiting biofilm maturation and reducing the risk of resistance formation [110, 111]. Recent composite delivery strategies emphasize synergistic mechanisms, such as co-loading antimicrobial peptides and antibiotics or utilizing nanocarriers to enhance drug stability and local enrichment, thus achieving stronger antimicrobial effects under biofilm and resistant bacteria conditions [95, 112]. In terms of anti-inflammation, long-term indwelling catheters can cause mechanical irritation and local inflammatory responses in the urinary mucosa, manifesting as mucosal edema, pain, and bladder irritation symptoms. Some patients may experience overactive bladder symptoms related to detrusor hyperactivity [19, 113]. Hydrogels can reduce mechanical damage through material lubrication and friction reduction, while serving as local carriers for sustained release of anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, or mucosal protective drugs, thereby alleviating the inflammatory cascade and promoting mucosal barrier repair [114]. Studies have reported that adhesive gelatin or hyaluronic acid-based hydrogels can provide sustained release of anti-inflammatory molecules in cystitis models, reducing oxidative stress and inflammation factor levels, and promoting angiogenesis and mucosal re-epithelialization [115]. Overall, the clinical value of antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory hydrogels lies in their ability to cover the continuous process of inhibiting bacterial adhesion, blocking biofilm formation, locally controlling the release of bactericidal agents, and alleviating device-induced inflammatory irritation. However, their translational potential depends on long-term indwelling safety, stable release behavior, and consistency in large-scale preparation.

Antifibrotic and anti-stricture hydrogels

The common pathological basis of diseases like urethral stricture and ureteral stricture is the activation of fibroblasts, excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM), and an imbalance in tissue remodeling during the injury repair process, ultimately leading to lumen contraction and decreased elasticity [116, 117]. Clinical treatments, such as internal urethrotomy or balloon dilation, can restore patency in the short term but are associated with high recurrence rates. Repeated injuries further aggravate scarring, forming a refractory cycle. The core issue in this field lies in how to maintain effective antifibrotic intervention locally in the early postoperative period and inhibit abnormal collagen deposition and smooth muscle layer disorganization during the tissue reconstruction window [118].

Traditional local medications, such as corticosteroids or mitomycin C, are rapidly cleared by urine after a single wash or injection, making it difficult to maintain a sustained effective concentration. Hydrogels, through their in situ gelation and sustained release capabilities, provide a platform for long-term local drug delivery. They can anchor antifibrotic drugs at the site of stricture or surrounding damaged tissue after surgery, extending the drug’s action time and reducing systemic exposure. Research on thermosensitive or chemically crosslinked hydrogels for delivering mitomycin C suggests that this strategy may reduce the tendency for recurrence under controlled safety conditions and improve local drug tolerance [119, 120]. In addition to mitomycin C, molecules such as rapamycin, pirfenidone, and paclitaxel, which inhibit fibroblast proliferation and matrix synthesis, have also been used in urinary stricture prevention research. However, their clinical potential depends on whether they can maintain a release cycle that aligns with the tissue remodeling timeline while avoiding inhibition of urinary epithelial regeneration [121, 122]. In terms of construction strategies, drugs are often pre-encapsulated in nanoparticle or microparticle carriers before being embedded in the hydrogel network, which increases drug loading, reduces burst release, and allows for more stable, long-term release. Some animal studies show that these nano-drug-library hydrogel coating/embedding systems can provide relatively stable release over weeks to months, reducing local inflammation and fibrosis and promoting mucosal regeneration [26]. For stricture prevention related to stents, hydrogels can serve as both drug reservoirs on the stent surface and as coatings that reduce secondary injury caused by device-induced irritation through surface lubrication and anti-adhesion effects.

Beyond drug inhibition pathways, hydrogels can also act as degradable temporary support materials, providing short-term mechanical support to the lumen and maintaining patency during the critical healing phase, thus reducing the risk of lumen narrowing caused by scar contraction (Fig. 5C, D) [123]. These hydrogel scaffolds need to strike a balance between flexibility and resistance to compression collapse, ensuring that degradation does not produce fragments or deposits that would obstruct urinary tract patency. Further strategies emphasize concurrent regeneration and antifibrosis, through the material’s microstructure and surface bioactivity, regulating epithelial and smooth muscle regeneration to reduce abnormal collagen deposition and improve the quality of tissue layer reconstruction [124]. Overall, the key evaluation endpoints for antifibrotic and anti-stricture hydrogels include not only the maintenance of patency and recurrence rate but also histological aspects such as smooth muscle layer reconstruction, collagen proportion and arrangement, inflammatory cell infiltration, and long-term functional indicators. These factors are critical to support the transition of such therapies from animal studies to clinical applications.

Hydrogels for promoting tissue repair and regeneration

Bladder and urethral tissue engineering reconstruction requires meeting multiple demands, including mechanical compliance, barrier function, smooth muscle contraction, and neural regulation. Traditional autologous tissue grafts can be used for defect repair but are limited by donor availability and complications such as stones, infections, and mucus secretion. As a result, biomaterial-based regenerative strategies have received continued attention [125]. Hydrogels, with their high water content, soft elasticity, and ability to three-dimensionally encapsulate cells and growth factors, can provide a near-physiological microenvironment for urinary tract soft tissues, supporting cell survival, migration, and tissue remodeling [126, 127]. However, relying solely on a single hydrogel system is often insufficient to meet the mechanical strength and multi-layer structural guidance required for large volume defect repair. As a result, composite and multi-signal regulation strategies are the mainstream approaches.

Hydrogels derived from natural decellularized matrices can retain tissue-specific matrix components and some endogenous signals, promoting angiogenesis and tissue reconstruction. Bladder decellularized matrix can be used as a sheet for patching or prepared as a hydrogel for cell loading or defect filling [128]. Studies have shown that decellularized matrix hydrogels have advantages in promoting vascularization and supporting endothelial and smooth muscle-related cell phenotypes. However, their performance highly depends on the decellularization quality and batch consistency. Furthermore, when used alone, they have limited mechanical support and often need to be combined with fibrous scaffolds or porous frameworks to improve resistance to leakage and deformation [129]. Furthermore, integrating exosomes or other cell-derived factors into composite hydrogel systems can provide immune regulation and regenerative signals in acellular conditions, improving vascularization and smooth muscle layer reconstruction quality [130]. However, their mechanisms, dose standardization, and long-term safety still require systematic evidence for validation. Synthetic polymer hydrogels offer higher controllability in terms of injectability and signal delivery. By combining with electrospun fibers, degradable porous scaffolds, and other structural materials, hydrogels can act as carrier layers for biological signals and cells, while the outer or intermediate scaffold layer provides mechanical support and shape stability. This approach better matches the dynamic loading of bladder filling and voiding [131]. To address insufficient endogenous repair, functional peptides or growth factors can be released in a controlled manner to promote cell recruitment and angiogenesis. For example, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) can reduce fibrosis tendencies to some extent [132]. Encapsulating growth factors in degradable micro- or nanocarriers, followed by embedding in thermosensitive or chemically crosslinked hydrogels, can extend the release period and improve factor stability. Animal studies suggest that this approach can promote urinary epithelium coverage, detrusor layer differentiation, and structural reconstruction while reducing graft contraction [133]. It is important to note that in post-tumor surgery or scenarios with potential residual tumor risks, the use of angiogenesis or proliferation-promoting signals should be strictly adapted and safety evaluated. In urethral and other tubular tissue repairs, hydrogels’ shaping ability and manufacturing customizability provide significant advantages. Three-dimensional shaping technologies based on crosslinkable hydrogel inks have made it possible to match individualized defects. By controlling the material system, it is possible to regulate the porosity connectivity and mechanical properties of the printed structure, supporting epithelial cell migration and the formation of the lumen barrier. The key challenge in these strategies is maintaining long-term patency, synergizing infection resistance and antifibrosis, and matching the degradation rate with tissue remodeling timing [134].

Overall, the role of hydrogels in urinary system tissue repair and regeneration can be summarized as providing a microenvironment for cell and signal delivery, constructing defect filling and scaffold interface layers, and supporting tissue remodeling through controllable degradation and mechanical compliance. The translational bottlenecks primarily focus on long-term structural stability in dynamic mechanical environments, tolerance and infection resistance in the urinary environment, reproducibility in multi-layer tissue functional reconstruction, and long term functional evaluation in large animal and clinical stages. Materials designed for clinical applications need to integrate mechanical support, barrier reconstruction, angiogenesis, and antifibrosis regulation into a unified framework, with standardized preparation and stringent safety verification as prerequisites for translation.

Hydrogels for renal injury repair and kidney targeted therapy

Although current research on hydrogels in the urinary system has focused predominantly on the bladder, urethra, and local intracavitary drug delivery, a growing body of recent evidence indicates that hydrogels also hold substantial promise in kidney-related diseases, particularly in acute kidney injury repair, intervention in renal interstitial fibrosis, and localized renal drug delivery. Compared with systemic administration, renal pathology is often characterized by local inflammation, oxidative stress, microcirculatory impairment, and subsequent fibrotic progression, while bioactive agents such as growth factors, anti-inflammatory molecules, cells, and extracellular vesicles typically have short in vivo half-lives and are rapidly cleared, thereby limiting their effective exposure within renal tissue [135]. By forming local drug depots through direct injection or subcapsular delivery, hydrogels can prolong the residence time of therapeutic payloads and improve release kinetics, and have therefore emerged as increasingly attractive platforms for renal regenerative therapy. In a unilateral ureteral obstruction model, Soranno et al. injected an injectable IL-10-loaded hydrogel beneath the renal capsule and showed that this system markedly reduced macrophage infiltration and apoptosis, while also attenuating subsequent fibrosis; further studies demonstrated that in models of ischemic acute kidney injury, similar localized hydrogel systems also decreased serum IL-6 levels and type III collagen expression in renal tissue, suggesting that beyond serving as passive carriers, these materials may also contribute to renoprotection through modulation of the local microenvironment [136, 137].

In the repair of acute kidney injury, the value of hydrogels lies not merely in the sustained release of a single bioactive factor, but more importantly in their capacity to orchestrate the coordinated regulation of multiple signals throughout the repair process. Liu et al. developed a self-assembling peptide/heparin hydrogel for the codelivery of an anti-TNF-α antibody and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), and in a renal ischemia-reperfusion model showed that this system was more effective than free-drug administration in reducing serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, tubular cell apoptosis, inflammatory cytokine expression, and macrophage infiltration, while simultaneously promoting tubular cell proliferation and attenuating subsequent chronic fibrosis, suggesting that renal injury repair may benefit more from hydrogel-based platforms that integrate anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative signaling [138]. Zhou et al. used an MMP-2-responsive self-assembling peptide hydrogel to deliver mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs), and found in mice with renal ischemia-reperfusion injury that this system outperformed EVs alone or blank hydrogel in improving renal function, reducing tubular apoptosis, inflammatory cytokine expression, and macrophage infiltration, while promoting endothelial cell proliferation and angiogenesis, ultimately mitigating chronic-stage fibrosis [139]. Zhang et al. subsequently introduced an RGD motif to enhance integrin-mediated interactions between EVs and the hydrogel matrix, thereby improving intrarenal EV retention and stability and yielding superior renoprotective and antifibrotic effects in both the acute and chronic phases [140]. Collectively, these studies indicate that in the renal injury microenvironment, hydrogels function not only as carriers of bioactive agents, but also as active therapeutic platforms that enhance local treatment efficacy through interactions between the material and its cargo. Beyond EV delivery, hydrogels have also been used to improve the retention and survival of cell-based therapies in the kidney. Cho et al. co-encapsulated mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and α-lipoic acid within an injectable collagen/polyglutamic acid hydrogel, and in a model of renal dysfunction demonstrated significant reductions in blood urea nitrogen and serum creatinine, with the therapeutic advantage attributed to improved MSC retention at the injury site together with sustained release of the antioxidant agent [141]. Similarly, the functionalized β sheet peptide hydrogel reported by Wang et al. provided a more supportive microenvironment for human placenta-derived MSCs and improved renal function in an acute kidney injury model by enhancing cell survival and angiogenesis [142]. These findings suggest that for organs such as the kidney, which are highly vascularized and prone to rapid loss of locally delivered bioactive agents, hydrogels have substantial value as supportive matrices for improving therapeutic retention and efficacy.

In studies related to chronic kidney disease, hydrogels have likewise demonstrated considerable therapeutic promise. Qin et al. developed an injectable micelle-hybrid hydrogel capable of sustaining local release within the mouse kidney for approximately 3 weeks; after a single administration, whether loaded with the small-molecule agent celastrol or an anti-TGF-β antibody, the system attenuated renal interstitial fibrosis by suppressing local inflammation or neutralizing TGF-β1, respectively, without causing evident local or systemic toxicity [143]. These findings suggest that for therapeutic strategies requiring prolonged inhibition of profibrotic signaling while minimizing systemic adverse effects, hydrogels offer a more rational mode of local intervention than conventional repeated dosing. Quinteira et al. prepared an injectable, thermoresponsive kidney extracellular matrix hydrogel derived from decellularized porcine renal tissue, which retained a high proportion of native ECM components, supported improved survival and proliferation of renal progenitor cells, and induced the expression of markers associated with tubular cells and podocytes, thereby demonstrating potential as a scaffold for renal regeneration [144]. Compared with generic collagen-based or synthetic materials, such tissue-derived hydrogels more closely recapitulate the native renal microenvironment and may therefore play a greater role in future renal regeneration and precision delivery strategies. Notably, the use of localized renal delivery platforms is not restricted to non-neoplastic diseases. In renal cancer research, Fujii et al. developed a cyclodextrin-based nanogel for the delivery of siVEGF and achieved effective intratumoral silencing of VEGF in a renal cell carcinoma model through direct tumor injection, ultimately suppressing tumor angiogenesis and growth [145]. Together, these studies indicate that gel- or nanogel-based strategies for local retention and sustained release may also provide a promising therapeutic framework for renal malignancies, with the shared advantage of enhancing the local exposure of active agents within the kidney while reducing systemic toxicity.

Hydrogels in urological diagnostics

Imaging-functionalized hydrogels

By integrating imaging probes into the hydrogel network, a visual diagnostic and intraoperative navigation platform can be constructed for urinary system tumors. The high-water content three-dimensional network of hydrogels can provide a stable carrier for imaging molecules, and through mechanisms such as adhesion, in situ gelation, or sustained release, it enhances the retention and local enrichment of probes at the lesion surface, improving the signal-to-noise ratio and imaging stability [146]. In bladder cancer research, imaging strategies based on tumor stroma rather than tumor cell receptors can reduce the risk of missed diagnoses caused by cell heterogeneity [147]. Guo et al. reported a collagen-adhesive probe (CA-P) that utilizes a polymer capable of undergoing sol-gel transition and combining with near-infrared (NIR) fluorescent groups. After recognizing tumor-associated collagen, the probe rapidly gelates and adheres to the tumor surface, providing more stable local signals [148]. Animal models and ex vivo specimen results suggest that such hydrogel probes can more clearly delineate tumor boundaries, providing real-time support for transurethral resection of bladder tumors and potentially reducing the recurrence risk due to residual lesions. Near-infrared region II (NIR-II) imaging is well-suited to enhance contrast for intraluminal imaging due to its lower background autofluorescence and smaller tissue scattering [149]. One study proposed a non-invasive treatment for bladder cancer using engineered nanosensors that, under X-ray activation, enable real-time NIR imaging and fractionated photodynamic therapy (f-PDT) (Fig. 6A). These nanosensors, enhanced by tumor-targeting peptides, promoted tumor regression and reduced recurrence. In a mouse model, the system significantly increased survival and restored immune homeostasis (Fig. 6B) [150]. Similarly, loading nanoparticles with high photoacoustic conversion efficiency into hydrogels can prolong material retention at the lesion site, enhance photoacoustic contrast, and support the detection and localization of deeper regions of the bladder wall [151]. In addition to optical imaging, hydrogels can also be combined with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and other modalities to achieve more reliable anatomical localization and long-term follow-up. Since conventional hydrogels have limited contrast with surrounding soft tissues on MRI, their visibility can be enhanced by incorporating paramagnetic or superparamagnetic components [152, 153]. Existing work has combined superparamagnetic nanoparticles, such as Fe₃O₄, with hydrogels to form bladder submucosal injection materials that can be tracked long-term in vivo, enabling the evaluation of material location, volume changes, and potential complications through MRI post-surgery [154]. Furthermore, introducing iodinated contrast agents or high atomic number nanomaterials into hydrogels can impart X-ray/CT visualization capability, facilitating intraoperative localization or postoperative follow-up. However, careful evaluation of contrast agent dosage, long-term residue, and urinary safety is necessary [155, 156]. Overall, the complementary advantages of different imaging modes offer multiple pathways for hydrogel diagnostic platforms. In the future, integrating multimodal probes, designing signal stabilization, and adapting to endoscopic workflows are expected to enhance the detection, localization, and intraoperative decision-making ability for urinary system tumors.

Biosensing and responsive hydrogels

Responsive hydrogels can serve as platform materials for the detection of urinary biomarkers, enabling non-invasive, rapid early screening and dynamic monitoring by embedding recognition elements and signal transduction mechanisms within the hydrogel network [157]. The hydrated microenvironment within the hydrogel is conducive to the immobilization and activity maintenance of bio-recognition components such as antibodies, aptamers, nucleic acid probes, or enzyme substrates. At the same time, their designable crosslinking structures and functional groups provide the material basis for various readout methods, including volume changes, color changes, fluorescence variations, or electrochemical responses [158–160]. By introducing pH-sensitive groups, enzyme-sensitive bonds, molecular imprinting sites, or dissociable nucleic acid structures, the presence of target molecules can be converted into a change in the hydrogel network state, enabling selective detection and signal amplification [161, 162]. For urinary tract infection detection, hydrogel-based colorimetric sensors have the advantage of simplified operation and fast readout. Studies have fixed specific enzyme substrates in chitosan and other matrices, where bacterial-secreted enzymes specifically cleave the substrates to trigger a color change, enabling rapid screening for pathogenic bacteria with corresponding enzymatic activity [163]. Furthermore, integrating multiple substrates within the same hydrogel system can create a multi-channel colorimetric mode, improving the ability to distinguish complex samples and reducing false positives. The key to such platforms lies in the specificity of substrate selection, inhibition of potential interfering substances in urine, and the stability of the detection threshold across different bacterial loads.

For urological tumors, enzyme activity and specific proteins in urine can serve as accessible targets for detection. Taking hyaluronidase as an example, research has used hyaluronic acid-related networks to construct enzyme-degradable hydrogels, encapsulating reporter molecules. When the target enzyme is present in the sample, the hydrogel matrix is selectively degraded, releasing the reporter molecules, which can then be detected through corresponding immunochromatographic readout for visual detection, and semi-quantitative analysis can be performed via image capture [164]. This strategy embodies the enzyme-triggered release signal amplification—rapid readout design concept and is suitable for on-site detection of low-abundance or dynamically changing biomarkers in urine. In addition to colorimetric readouts, hydrogels can also be combined with fluorescence and electrochemical sensors for higher sensitivity. Aptamers or nucleic acid structures can act as highly programmable recognition units. Upon target binding, these structures can induce strand displacement, denaturation, or hydrogel disintegration, which subsequently releases fluorescence signals or alters quenching states, enabling rapid response detection. In the electrochemical field, hydrogels can form a stable hydrated interface on electrode surfaces, reducing nonspecific protein adsorption and enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio in complex samples [165, 166]. Once the target binds to immobilized antibodies or aptamers, quantitative detection can be achieved through impedance or current changes. Further research is exploring the incorporation of nucleic acid amplification reactions and CRISPR/Cas recognition systems into the hydrogel framework, utilizing cascading amplification and highly specific cutting reactions for rapid detection of trace nucleic acid biomarkers [167, 168], indicating their potential in early tumor molecular screening.

Overall, the competitive advantage of biosensing and responsive hydrogels lies in their material programmability and sample adaptability, enabling integrated design for recognition, enrichment, amplification, and readout on the same platform. For real-world applications in the urinary system, further systematic optimization is needed to address issues such as fluctuations in urine composition, viscosity and ionic strength interference, long-term storage stability, batch-to-batch consistency, and standard calibration systems. Additionally, they must be engineered to integrate with microfluidics, portable readout terminals, and clinical workflows to transition from proof of concept to widely applicable non-invasive diagnostic tools

Hydrogels for drug delivery in urinary system tumors

Intravesical drug delivery hydrogels

Intravesical instillation therapy is a key element in the post-surgical adjuvant and recurrence management of non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). However, traditional aqueous formulations often struggle to maintain stable and sufficient effective contact time in the bladder due to periodic filling and emptying cycles, continuous dilution by urine, and the combined action of the mucous layer and urothelial barrier. This results in significant fluctuations in local drug exposure, making it difficult to sustain the necessary therapeutic window, and increasing the risk of irritation symptoms and infections caused by repeated catheterization [169, 170]. The primary goal of hydrogel-based drug delivery systems in the bladder is to improve the stability of drug residence and mucosal contact without significantly increasing the complexity of the procedure. This system aims to achieve controlled release kinetics and more predictable local drug effects while reducing peak systemic absorption and local irritation [38, 46]. Current research and translation are advancing along several key directions, including in situ gelation for extended residence time, enhanced adhesion for resistance to washout, drug loading and penetration enhancement, and multi-mechanism collaborative optimization.

Thermosensitive gels are one of the most well-established intravesical delivery strategies with the clearest clinical evidence and translation pathway. These systems remain in a solution state at lower temperatures, facilitating catheter instillation, and rapidly transition into gel form at physiological temperatures once inside the body, forming a relatively stable local drug reservoir in the bladder. This significantly prolongs the contact time of the drug with the urothelial surface and reduces rapid loss due to urination. [62, 171]. Chemotherapeutic ablation strategies using thermosensitive mitomycin C gels have shown promising results in clinical trials. The key findings from the ENVISION phase III study demonstrated that patients with recurrent low-grade, intermediate-risk non-muscle invasive bladder cancer had a complete remission rate of 79.6% at three months post-treatment, with a high level of remission maintenance at 12 months for those who achieved complete remission [172]. Importantly, this strategy has a clear regulatory pathway, as the Food and Drug Administration(FDA) approved the mitomycin C intravesical solution, Zusduri, for the treatment of adult recurrent low-grade intermediate-risk non-muscle invasive bladder cancer, scheduled for approval in June 2025. This approval provides strong support for the delivery logic of thermosensitive gelation to extend exposure time from both clinical and regulatory perspectives. Mechanistically, the value of thermosensitive gels lies not only in prolonging retention but also in converting intravesical drug delivery from an instantaneous washout exposure to a more stable local exposure curve, reducing the adverse effects of fluctuations between peak and trough concentrations on efficacy and tolerability. This is one reason why this strategy has more evident advantages in the local delivery of chemotherapeutic agents [173, 174].

In addition to thermosensitive gels, adhesive hydrogels offer another key solution for improving effective contact time [175]. Since the bladder mucous layer is overall negatively charged, introducing cationic groups can enhance electrostatic interactions with the mucous layer, increasing the material’s retention on the urothelial surface and its resistance to washout (Fig. 7A, B) [176]. Based on this approach, cationic materials such as chitosan have been widely explored in intravesical delivery research [177, 178] (Fig. 7C). Cationic peptide-based nanogels with enhanced penetration ability have shown potential in prolonging drug release and improving local antitumor effects both in vitro and in vivo. These systems can sustain drug release for over 10 days, demonstrating superior local tumor inhibition and acceptable safety in animal experiments (Fig. 7D). Further enhancement of adhesion is often achieved by incorporating catechols or dopamine, which provide stronger interface interactions in wet environments and improve material adhesion stability under urine-covered conditions [43]. Recent studies have introduced hydrogel systems that maintain adhesion even under urine-covered conditions, offering new material paths for sustained residence and local treatment in the bladder [28]. At the same time, dynamic covalent bond networks and self-healing networks are used to improve material structural integrity under bladder wall deformation, shear, and washout conditions, making the gel less prone to rapid failure due to microcracks or local peeling. It is important to emphasize that merely extending residence time is not enough to ensure improved efficacy. The mucosal barrier and the tight connections between urothelial cells limit the effective delivery of many drugs, especially hydrophobic and macromolecular drugs, into tissues. As a result, an increasing number of intravesical hydrogel systems are adopting composite structures of hydrogels and nanoparticle carriers. On one hand, nanoparticle carriers enhance the drug loading capacity of hydrophobic drugs and improve their dispersion stability. On the other hand, surface charges and mucosal interactions can switch the design or trigger mechanisms, further enhancing drug penetration through the mucous layer and improving drug flux across the urothelial cells after adhesion and retention [179]. Some studies have used penetrative, transformable nanoparticle systems combined with hydrogel platforms, exemplifying the design trend of balancing residence time and tissue delivery by switching functions at different stages [13]. These composite systems are widely considered to be one of the key directions to break through the current bottlenecks in intravesical drug delivery, both in reviews and methodological frameworks. In addition to the mainstream strategies mentioned above, buoyancy hydrogels represent an engineered strategy with urinary system specificity that helps reduce the risk of urinary tract obstruction and optimize the spatial distribution of materials in the bladder [180]. Thermosensitive systems like Poloxamer, for instance, may face challenges such as blockage or aggregation when applied in the bladder. Buoyancy designs introduce components like bicarbonate to generate gas microbubbles and reduce the apparent density of the system, making the gel more likely to float within the bladder. This reduces the risk of obstruction and extends the residence time to some extent. Some studies have focused on developing buoyant thermosensitive gels based on Poloxamer, providing more systematic formulation and performance data for this direction [181].

Overall, the development trend of intravesical drug delivery hydrogels has shifted from simply extending retention time to more complex optimization that emphasizes the stability of retention, tissue delivery efficiency, predictability of release kinetics, and the synergy between tolerability and these factors. The clinical and regulatory progress represented by thermosensitive mitomycin C gel systems offers a replicable development paradigm for the field. Adhesion-enhancing and composite delivery frameworks represent research directions for further improving efficacy. Future work will focus on establishing quantifiable correlations between local pharmacokinetics indicators and clinical endpoints, defining the boundary of how different material parameters contribute to efficacy and toxicity, and completing scalable consistency verification under real instillation procedures and patient compliance constraints.

Stimuli-responsive antitumor hydrogels

Stimuli-responsive hydrogels enable controlled drug release in response to endogenous signals from the lesion or external physical signals, achieving higher levels of temporal control and spatial selectivity in the local treatment of urinary system tumors [179]. The most suitable triggering mechanism for intraluminal infusion remains temperature-responsive hydrogels. This approach not only offers compatibility with drug delivery processes and catheter operations but also allows the hydrogel to quickly form a gel phase in the body that resists dilution and washout, thereby enhancing the stability of local exposure and reducing fluctuations in systemic absorption [182]. In one study, a composite thermosensitive hydrogel containing methoxy poly(ethylene glycol)-poly(lactic acid) (mPEG-PDLLA) and Pluronic F127 was developed to load a dual-light-absorbing compound (T1) and pyropheophorbide a (PPa) for photodynamic therapy. After 72 hours, the cumulative release rates of T1 and PPa were below 40%, whereas the release rate of the micelle solution was 100%. In addition to temperature triggers, tumor-associated endogenous stimuli can be used to enhance the selectivity of local drug release and improve therapeutic indices. Bladder tumors and surrounding tissues often exhibit changes in protease profiles, elevated oxidative stress, and altered metabolic microenvironments. These characteristics provide a basis for designing networks sensitive to enzymes, pH, and redox conditions [13]. By incorporating peptide crosslinking bonds that can be cleaved by specific enzymes, constructing pH-sensitive dynamic bonds in the network, or introducing structures sensitive to reactive oxygen species (ROS), the materials can remain stable in relatively normal urine environments while undergoing swelling, degradation, or crosslink rearrangement in the tumor microenvironment. This increases the effective release ratio in the tumor region and forms a more substantial local concentration gradient [181, 184]. The key challenge in these systems is establishing reproducible triggering windows, balancing structural stability under urine washout conditions with response rates in the lesion environment, while minimizing non-specific release to avoid instability in efficacy or increased local toxicity, thus maintaining their translational potential.

External stimuli triggers offer stronger process control and expanded strategy space for local treatments. Coupling magnetic, photothermal, or photoresponsive units with hydrogel networks allows for changes in the local temperature field or network microstructure upon applying an alternating magnetic field or specific wavelength light, enabling pulsed drug release, or combining these effects with photothermal or magnetic thermal effects for synergistic treatment [180]. For intraluminally accessible organs like the bladder and upper urinary tract, the delivery of external energy is operable in engineering terms. It can be integrated with endoscopic fibers, localized temperature control, or infusion processes, forming a treatment mode triggered after drug administration[185, 186]. At present, this direction has shown good adjustability and synergistic potential in animal models and early-stage research, but clinical advancement still depends on the systematic validation of several key factors, including the uniformity of energy distribution in the intraluminal environment, safety thresholds for normal urothelial tissue, compatibility with existing devices and procedures, and the stability and consistency of material composition and performance under large-scale preparation conditions.

Integrated diagnostic and therapeutic hydrogel platforms

Integrated diagnostic and therapeutic hydrogels refer to hydrogel systems that simultaneously incorporate therapeutic payloads and detectable signals within the same matrix. These materials enable local treatment while providing visual or quantifiable feedback on distribution, retention, release processes, and early therapeutic responses [187]. For urinary system tumors, intraluminal drug delivery and endoscopic procedures play a critical role. Treatment efficacy relies on the material’s ability to maintain adequate residence and effective contact under conditions of continuous dilution by urine and periodic organ deformation, making real-time or phase-based evaluation of the treatment process directly clinically relevant [188, 189]. Unlike materials used solely for imaging localization, integrated diagnostic and therapeutic hydrogels emphasize the connection between imaging signals and the therapeutic process. The goal is to improve the controllability and assessability of local treatments and provide a basis for personalized drug delivery strategies.

In the field of optical imaging, combining fluorescent probes with hydrogels can enhance the visualization and identification of intraluminal tumors, especially useful for delineating tumor boundaries and highlighting residual lesions during cystoscopy [190]. Hydrogels can prolong probe retention on the lesion surface through adhesion or in situ gelation mechanisms, reducing signal attenuation caused by urine washout and expanding the operable imaging window [191]. Studies have used tumor matrix-related components as binding targets, for example, probes targeting collagen, which undergo sol-gel transition upon binding with tumor matrix, making the fluorescent signal more stable and persistent locally. This approach improves imaging reliability in cases of high tumor cell heterogeneity [192]. More importantly, when fluorescence tracking is combined with a local drug reservoir, hydrogels not only provide real-time intraoperative visualization but also form a more stable local drug exposure in the same area, thus translating diagnostic information into therapeutic advantage [193, 194], reducing the uncertainty of efficacy due to short-term contact. In imaging modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), introducing paramagnetic or superparamagnetic nanoparticles into the hydrogel network allows for non-invasive monitoring of gel distribution, volume changes, and degradation processes in the bladder or upper urinary tract [195]. This capability is valuable for assessing the dosimetry of local delivery, as the effectiveness of infusion treatments depends not only on the drug dose but also on whether the material covers the lesion area, remains in place for the preset duration, and maintains the appropriate release rate within the therapeutic window [196]. Imaging tracking can further support individualized adjustments of infusion volume, retention time, and re-dosing intervals, and provide a reference for process consistency and in vivo behavior across different hydrogel formulations[197, 198]. If a reproducible correlation between tracking signals and drug release processes can be established, with the tracer molecules and drugs exhibiting similar diffusion behaviors or the imaging signal showing a quantifiable response to network structural changes, it will be possible to indirectly assess the release state, transitioning follow-up assessments from merely detecting the presence of the material to evaluating whether the expected release kinetics have been achieved. The further development of integrated diagnostic and therapeutic hydrogels in the therapeutic domain focuses on combining with local energy treatments and providing early feedback on treatment responses. By integrating photothermal agents, magnetic materials, or sonodynamic sensitizing components with chemotherapeutic drugs within hydrogels, local sensitization and combined killing can be achieved under endoscopic or external energy input conditions, while the hydrogel maintains continuous controlled release to sustain the inhibitory effects [199]. For intraluminal organs like the bladder, energy delivery methods such as endoscopic fibers are feasible engineering solutions, enabling the combination of image-guided local energy interventions with hydrogel-controlled release in the same treatment workflow. Additionally, if the imaging signal can reflect changes in local temperature, tissue microenvironment alterations, or drug release rates, it could be used to guide adjustments in energy input intensity and duration, thereby reducing the risk of normal mucosal damage and improving treatment consistency [200]. The key challenges in this direction involve clarifying safety boundaries and standardizing processes, including energy distribution uniformity, heat damage thresholds for normal urothelial tissue, long-term biological safety of imaging and therapeutic components, and achieving stable, reproducible material performance under large-scale production conditions.

Overall, the research focus of integrated diagnostic and therapeutic hydrogels is shifting from simple visual marking to the quantifiable evaluation and feedback regulation of the local treatment process. Clinical translation will require establishing systematic evidence for the safety of imaging agents, signal stability, production consistency, and device compatibility. Moreover, it will need clear clinical endpoint verification of the actual benefits in improving resection completeness, extending local exposure, reducing recurrence risk, or reducing the number of treatments.

Summary and outlook

This review provides a comprehensive discussion of the advancements in hydrogel applications in urology, covering their biocompatibility, structural design, functional development, and diverse therapeutic applications. Hydrogels, with their unique physicochemical properties, show broad potential in drug delivery, disease diagnosis, and tissue repair. We analyzed their fundamental properties, including high hydration, tunable mechanical strength, and good biocompatibility, and highlighted their significant role in urological interventions such as bladder tumor treatment, urethral stricture management, intravesical drug delivery, and anti-infection therapies. Functionalized hydrogel designs thermosensitive, adhesive, and responsive can prolong drug residence time, improve delivery efficiency, and enhance therapeutic effects. Composite and multifunctional hydrogels integrating nanomaterials, exosomes, and antimicrobial agents further enable multi-mechanism collaboration and targeted therapy. Despite these advances, challenges remain, particularly regarding hydrogel stability in urine, long-term effectiveness, and compatibility with existing medical devices. To facilitate translation into clinical practice, future research should focus on the following specific directions:

1. Standardized in vitro testing using artificial urine and flow models to evaluate hydrogel stability, swelling, and degradation under physiologically relevant conditions.

2. Residence time benchmarking protocols to systematically quantify how long hydrogels remain at target sites within the urinary tract.

3. Long term catheter encrustation studies to assess hydrogel performance in preventing or reducing mineral deposition and biofilm formation.

4. Comparative in vivo retention imaging using multimodal techniques to evaluate hydrogel localization, retention, and distribution in animal models.

5. Unified reporting of gelation kinetics and mechanical windows to enable better correlation of hydrogel properties with urologic tissue requirements.

6. Integration with smart therapeutic modalities, such as local energy delivery, stimuli-responsive release, and multimodal drug combinations for enhanced efficacy.

7. Clinical alignment and reproducibility, focusing on scalable fabrication, batch-to-batch consistency, and regulatory compliance to accelerate translation to patient care.

By addressing these targeted research directions, hydrogels are expected to evolve into more robust, multifunctional, and clinically applicable platforms, offering safer and more effective therapeutic options for urinary tract infections, urological tumors, and urethral strictures. Continuous optimization of material design, functionalization, and testing protocols will be essential to realize their full potential in personalized urological treatments.
