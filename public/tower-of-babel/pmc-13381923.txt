Cascade of magnetic-field-induced quantum spin states in a spin-1 honeycomb magnet
Authors: Kaixin Tang, Zhao-Yang Dong, Yanyan Shangguan, Zhao Gong, Ye Yang, Song Bao, Houpu Li, Nan Zhang, Hongyu Li, Jian-Xin Li, Jinsheng Wen, Ziji Xiang, Xianhui Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuantum fluctuations endow spin systems with surprisingly enriched magnetic phase diagrams. In frustrated magnets, strong quantum fluctuations boosted by either geometrical incompatibility or competitive exchange interactions stabilize cascades of unusual phases of matter. Here we reveal the presence of multiple quantum phases in the honeycomb antiferromagnet Na3Ni2BiO6, both inside and beyond its field-induced one-third magnetization plateau. Comprehensive measurements of thermodynamic quantities demonstrate that the one-third plateau comprises at least three distinct spin states with nearly-degenerate net magnetization, separated by first-order transitions that likely involve sequential spin reconfiguration. Upon further increasing the magnetic field, the system evolves across a myriad of peculiar phases before reaching full polarization; these intermediate phases possess copious low-energy excitations, manifested by anomalous upturns of specific heat at ultralow temperatures — probably hinting at the development of “hidden” ordered ground states. The complex magnetic phase diagram of Na3Ni2BiO6 underlines the preponderant impact of quantum fluctuations on a honeycomb spin lattice with strong exchange frustration.Subject terms: Magnetic properties and materials, Phase transitions and critical phenomena

Introduction

Frustrated magnets offer a blue-print to realize fascinating quantum states and emergent phenomena. The frustrations arising from magnetic lattice geometry (i.e., the geometric frustration)1,2 or from competing magnetic interactions (i.e., the exchange or bond frustration)3 prevail in different types of spin systems, yet both explicitly enhance the quantum fluctuation in the spin degree of freedom4. Whilst abundance of quantum fluctuations potentially leads to the spin liquid state that remains disordered down to zero temperature (T)5,6, in most cases the spin systems establish long-range orders at low T and, with the assistance of quantum fluctuations, develops a plethora of unconventional quantum phases such as fractional magnetization plateaus7–11, longitudinal spin-density waves12, supersolid phase13–15, spin-nematic state16,17, etc, under the application of magnetic fields (H)—some of those are classically unexpected4,10. Among the frustrated magnets, the quasi-two-dimensional (2D) triangular lattice antiferromagnets are of special interest because their geometric frustration promises (i) strong quantum fluctuations, which stabilize a unique up-up-down ground state manifested by a magnetization plateau at one-third of the saturation value (the 1/3-plateau phase hereafter) in a certain range of H4,7,18,19; (ii) unusual magnon condensates that are analogue to the supersolid state emerging from hard-core bosons on a triangular lattice13,19,20. In principle, the density of bosonic spin excitations in the magnetically ordered states (i.e., magnons) can be effectively manipulated by the external H-field through the Zeeman-type coupling21; subsequently, the H-field drives the system through a quantum critical point (QCP) marking an analogous of the Bose-Einstein condensation (BEC) of magnons22 and triggers intriguing critical behavior in its vicinity23,24. Latest experimental results have revealed that in quasi-2D triangular lattice antiferromagnets Na2BaCo(PO4)2 and Na2BaNi(PO4)2, a BEC-QCP occurs beyond the endpoint of the 1/3-plateau phase in the magnetic phase diagram; in the former, the BEC leads to spin-lattice supersolidity characterized by remarkable low-T entropy stemming from gapless Goldstone modes (corresponding to the the U(1) phase fluctuations)15, whereas the latter, successfully described by a spin-1 model, develops a two-magnon BEC that gives rise to a spin-nematic state17.

In quasi-2D honeycomb spin lattices, the above-mentioned exotic magnetically ordered phases are not inherent, because the spin frustration can hardly be established without involving complex intersite exchange interactions. Nevertheless, a spin-1 honeycomb lattice antiferromagnet Na3Ni2BiO6 has recently been identified as a notable exception25. This compound crystallizes in a layered monoclinic structure (space group C2/m) comprising stacks of alternating Ni-Bi-O honeycomb layers and Na atom layers along the perpendicular c* axis; the Ni atoms adopt a honeycomb geometry in the ab plane (see Supplementary Fig. 1a)25,26. The antiferromagnetic (AFM) order sets in at the Néel temperature TN ≈ 10 K26, characterized by an in-plane zigzag configuration of the Ni2+ spins (S = 1) which are aligned along the monoclinic c axis (~19∘ away from the perpendicular direction c*)25,27. With the application of H along c*, a 1/3-magnetization plateau emerges at low T between μ0H ≈ 5.2 and 7.4 T, and a spin-polarized state is realized at higher H (8.3 T at 2 K)25. It has been proposed that the bond-anisotropic Kitaev interaction is an essential prerequisite for the occurrence of 1/3-magnetization plateau25: the inclusion of Kitaev interaction creates pronounced exchange frustrations and, consequently, strong quantum fluctuations. The underlying mechanism of the 1/3-plateau phase in Na3Ni2BiO6 can be different from the order-by-disorder scenario (that quantum fluctuations select one of the classically-degenerate spin configurations in a finite field range)25 that is frequently referred to the cases of geometrically frustrated magnets, e.g., triangular or kagome lattices4,7,8,28. However, the nature of this intriguing phase remains elusive.

Here, we investigate the field-induced quantum phase transitions and spin states in Na3Ni2BiO6 from the thermodynamic perspective using four complementary experimental probes: ac magnetostriction, dc magnetization, torque magnetometry and specific heat. We track the evolution of thermodynamic properties under magnetic fields up to 14 T down to an ultralow temperature of 0.1 K and map the magnetic phase diagram, which appears to be surprisingly complicated. The 1/3-magnetization plateau is determined to be composed of three distinct phases down to ~0.35 K and probably more at even lower T, instead of being a single phase; such phenomenon has seldom been observed in geometrically frustrated spin lattices, suggesting the highly unusual nature of the fractional plateau states herein. Above the critical H-field marking the endpoint of 1/3 magnetization plateau, successive quantum phase transitions occur before the realization of full magnetization saturation. The corresponding quantum spin phases, termed intermediate phases in this work, exhibit abnormal increases of specific heat Cp(T) upon cooling at the lowest T: Cp(T)/T ∝ T−α(α ≃ 2.4 − 3.0), which cannot be attributed to the conventional Schottky anomalies; this inverse-power-law rise contradicts the theoretical expectations for gapless magnetic excitations and most likely implies incipient signature of a transition/crossover towards a “hidden” ground state (residing below 100 mK) stemming from the in-plane component of Ni spins. The overall phase diagram bears some degree of resemblance to certain quasi-2D triangular lattice antiferromagnets15,17, yet the quantum spin states can be completely different from their counterparts in essence. The segmented 1/3-plateau phases and the intermediate phases with masked ground states both emerge uniquely from the strong quantum fluctuations prompted by exchange frustration. These results highlight the fundamental role of Kitaev physics in promoting unusual quantum phenomena in honeycomb magnets.

Results

Magnetic-field-induced quantum phase transitions

We explored the field-induced phase transitions in Na3Ni2BiO6 by the method of ac magnetostriction measurement29–31. This technique directly probes the linear magnetostriction coefficient λ(H) = (1/L0)∂ΔL(H)/∂μ0H (here L0 is the sample length at zero H and ΔL is the variation of sample length under H) by converting it to an ac electrical voltage signal in a magnetoelectric (ME) composite configuration, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. More information of the measurement is presented in Methods. Figure 1b displays the H-dependent real part of the ME voltage coefficient, αx(H), a parameter that is proportional to λ(H)29,31, measured with H∥c* (perpendicular to the ab plane); such a configuration detects the in-plane linear magnetostriction (αx(H) ∝ λab(H), see Methods). Successive magnetic phase transitions occur with increasing H, manifest as peaks in αx(H). At T = 1.8 K, we identify totally seven transitions up to μ0H = 9 T. The peaks at μ0Hc1 = 4.9 T and μ0Hc2 = 7.4 T correspond to the transitions entering and exiting the 1/3 magnetization plateau (see magnetization isotherms M(H) in Fig. 1c), respectively, in agreement with previous results25. Surprisingly, two salient anomalies appear within the field range of the 1/3-plateau phase, with the maxima occurring at μ0H1* = 5.7 T and μ0H2* = 6.3 T, respectively; these features divide the plateau into three phases (phases I, II and III in Fig. 1b), an unusual phenomenon to be discussed in more detail below. Above μ0Hc2, the system undergoes three more transitions at Hc3, Hc4 and Hc5, implying the presence of a remarkable cascade of intermediate phases before eventual establishment of spin saturation slightly above μ0Hc5 = 8.2 T. As shown in Fig. 1d, Hc3-Hc5 are also manifested as distinctive features in dM/dH; intriguingly, the dip of dM/dH between Hc3 and Hc4 (Hc4 and Hc5) occurs where M exhibits kinks at approximately 1/2 (2/3) of its saturation value Ms, implying that the corresponding intermediate phases may also be fractional quantum states without developing a stable plateau. Such behavior is reminiscent of the multiple plateau states at higher fractions observed in triangular lattice antiferromagnets10,32,33 and the Shastry-Sutherland lattice magnet SrCu2(BO3)234,35, underlining the perplexing spin frustration in Na3Ni2BiO6.

With increasing T, the transition fields delineating the boundaries of the 1/3 magnetization plateau show different evolution tendencies: Hc1 is almost T independent, whereas Hc2 gradually decreases; the 1/3 magnetization plateau thus becomes narrower upon warming and is not well-defined above 6 K (Fig. 1c). The transitions separating the intermediate phases (Hc3-Hc5) shift to lower fields before merging and turning indiscernible above ≃4 K (Fig. 1b). The critical fields H1* and H2* are largely independent of T and smear out above ≃5 K and ≃4 K, respectively (Fig. 1b). A H − T phase diagram of Na3Ni2BiO6 for T > 1.8 K is presented in Fig. 1e, which is deduced from the ac magnetostriction data, M(H), torque magnetometry (Supplementary Fig. 2) and specific heat (Supplementary Fig. 3). The field-induced transitions Hc1 − Hc5 are of first-order nature (Supplementary Section I); in Fig. 1e, we only plot the data for the up-sweep measurements. We remark that the vertical transition lines Hc1, H1* and H2* strongly imply that the complicated phase diagram of Na3Ni2BiO6 is predominantly controlled by the zero-T quantum fluctuations, instead of the entropies—such phenomenon has been recognized as a distinctive characteristic of frustrated magnets10,36. This scenario is also supported by the fact that most transition lines (with the exceptions of Hc1 and Hc5) smear out at temperatures below TN, suggesting that the corresponding quantum phases may become unstable against thermal fluctuation before the destruction of magnetic order. Moreover, measurement of specific heat Cp(T) reveals a shoulder-like feature slightly below the sharp peak at TN in the field range 5.1–6.8 T (Supplementary Fig. 3). This feature is indicative of an additional phase transition at T * < TN within the magnetically ordered state (the olive shaded region in Fig. 1e); the 1/3-plateau phases evolve from the state developed below T* upon cooling (Supplementary Fig. 5a).

The exotic 1/3-plateau phases

Now we further look into the multi-phase 1/3 magnetization plateau segmented by transitions H1* and H2* (navy shaded region in Fig. 1e). Whilst the 1/3 magnetization plateau has been observed in a number of triangular-lattice frustrated magnets possessing both quantum10,11,17,23 and classical37 spins, in such cases it is attributed to an up-up-down spin configuration4,7,9 and the occurrence of multiple phases inside the plateau regime, as we observed here in Na3Ni2BiO6, is exceptionally rare. We mention that although the transition features at H1* and H2* are most clearly captured by the ac magnetostriction measurement (Fig. 1b) above 1.8 K, thanks to its extreme sensitivity to field-induced phase transitions, corresponding signatures can also be seen in the H-derivatives of magnetic torque τ(H) and M(H). As demonstrated in Fig. 2a, the features in αx, d(τ/H)/dH and dM/dH (measured on three different samples) are generally consistent in their positions, implying that the presence of phases I, II and III in the plateau regime is intrinsic to Na3Ni2BiO6. Small mismatches (0.1–0.2 T) may be due to unintentional sample misalignment with respect to H. These features are also reversible during up and down sweeps of H (solid and dashed lines in Fig. 2a). Hence, they are unlikely to originate from evolution of random domain structures in the 1/3 plateau phase (see Supplementary Sections I and II of Supplementary Information for more details).

To clarify the nature of these exotic plateau phases, we measured M(H) and τ(H) down to T = 0.4 K and 0.15 K, respectively. Low-T data reveal several additional interesting results. First, the phases I, II and III show M/Ms ≃ 0.30, (0.33–0.37) and (0.38–0.40) at 0.4 K, respectively (Fig. 2b); they are thus ‘approximate’ 1/3-plateau phases with either higher fraction or incommensurate magnetization. Second, despite that H1* and H2* appear to be independent of the H-sweep direction (Fig. 2a, b), they are likely to be weak first-order transitions, as evidenced by the notable variation in the magnitude of M for plateau phases I and II between up and down sweeps (Fig. 2b), as well as the absence of signatures at H1* and H2* in Cp(H) (Supplementary Fig. 3d, see Supplementary Section I). We note that the double-peak structure of the anomalies at H1* and H2* in αx(H) (Figs. 1b and 2a) is possibly caused by the domain formation in the vicinity of these first-order transitions. Third, a small jump occurs between H1* and H2* in τ(H) for T ≤ 0.2 K (red triangles in Fig. 2c), an indication of an additional phase transition further separating phase II into IIa and IIb at the lowest T. The emergence of extra features upon cooling strongly challenges the conventional order-by-disorder scenario for plateau formation4,7,8,28, in which quantum fluctuation only select one of the highly-degenerate classical spin states instead of creating new phases.

The unique properties of the 1/3-plateau phases in Na3Ni2BiO6 are further unveiled by contrasting the M(T) and Cp(T) data at the lowest T. As shown in Fig. 2d, the plateau phases exhibit distinct behaviors of M(T) below T ≃ 3 K: In phase I (μ0H = 5.3 and 5.5 T), M(T) first drops upon cooling, developing a rounded knee-shape feature before leveling off, whereas M(T) in phase II (5.7–6.2 T) and phase III (6.4–6.8 T) show continuous decrease and nearly T-independent behavior, respectively. The disparity of the slope dM/dT hints at different field evolution of magnetic entropy S(H, T). According to the Maxwell relation, ∂M/∂T = − ∂S/∂(μ0H), the H-derivative of magnetic entropy is weak in phases I and III but appreciable in phase II; this inference is strengthened by the divergent magnetic Grüneisen parameter ΓH(T)=−1Cp∂M∂T observed only in phase II (Fig. 2e). ΓH is related to the entropy as ΔS = ∫ CpΓHd(μ0H) and its divergence is usually assigned to field-induced instabilities of the system, such as quantum criticality38. Nevertheless, we point out that although S may change notably across phase II, fits of Cp(T) suggest gapped magnetic excitation spectrum in all three plateau phases (Supplementary Fig. 6), which is consistent with nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)27 results. Hence, abundance of low-T magnetic entropy is unexpected for the entire (approximate) 1/3 magnetization plateau region.

Low-T specific heat anomaly occurring between Hc2 and full polarization

The specific heat data of Na3Ni2BiO6 measured down to 0.1 K for different μ0H from 0 to 14 T are summarized in Fig. 3a (see Supplementary Fig. 7 for more data). Cp(T) can be satisfyingly described by a sum of the contributions from gapped magnons (Cm) and phonons (Cph) for fields below 7.5 T (Supplementary Fig. 6); Cph is negligibly small below 1 K (Supplementary Section III). Therefore, we conclude that the ground state of spin system in both the low-H zigzag AFM phase and the 1/3-plateau phases (as mentioned above) are gapped, with a spin excitation gap decreasing with increasing H (Supplementary Fig. 6d). Such observations are qualitatively in agreement with a previous NMR study27. The situation, however, becomes totally different when the system exits the 1/3 magnetization plateau as H is further increased: whereas the NMR results suggest a gap closing point at μ0H ≈ 8.3 T, our specific heat measurements reveal an anomalous rise of Cp(T) at the lowest T, which occurs in an extended field regime from 7.5 to ~11 T (Fig. 3a). This is more clearly displayed in Fig. 3b; the divergent behavior of Cp(T)/T is completely absent at μ0H = 7.3 T (in plateau phase III), yet abruptly emerges once μ0H > μ0Hc2 (≈7.4 T) and becomes the strongest at μ0H ≃ 8.5 T. With further increase of H, the divergent anomaly is suppressed above 10 T, with Cp(T)/T at 10.5 and 10.7 T developing a broad peak at Tpeak before dropping to a very low value. Above μ0H = 11.5 T, the anomalous low-T upturn completely vanishes (Supplementary Fig. 7) and Cp(T) retains the form consisting of gapped magnon and phonon terms (Supplementary Fig. 6c). We stress that the upturn persists to a field far beyond μ0Hc5 (≈8.2 T, Figs. 1e and 2b), that is, it not only exists in the intermediate phases but also apparently survives into the high-H polarized state (inset of Fig. 3b) in the phase diagram presented in Fig. 1e. A few more intriguing characteristics of this specific heat anomaly are to be noted: (i) It only accounts for a small fraction (4–5%) of the total spin entropy for a spin-1 ground state, Rln3 (R is the universal gas constant), between 0.1 and 1.0 K (Fig. 3c); (ii) the resulted excessive specific heat Cp(H) in the corresponding range of H appears to be reversible upon up and down sweeps of H above Hc5 and becomes hysteretic below it (Supplementary Fig. 4e); (iii) its T dependence is best described by an inverse-power-law relation, Cp(T)/T ~ T−α, α changes from 3.0 at 7.5 T to 2.1 at 10.7 T (where the fit becomes less compelling) and the most prominent anomalies are characterized by α ≃ 2.6–2.7, as shown in Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 12. The peculiar field evolution and the specific values of exponent α exclude the nuclear or impurity Schottky contributions as the origin of this low-T anomaly (Supplementary Section III, Supplementary Figs. 12–14).

In Fig. 4a we append the value of Cp/T onto the low-T magnetic phase diagram determined by torque magnetometry (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 2). The color contour map unambiguously corroborates that the Cp anomaly happens immediately above Hc2 at the lowest T. Thereby, the transition from the 1/3 plateau phase III to the first intermediate phase promptly triggers the emergence of abundant low-energy collective excitations, giving rise to the remarkable enhancement of low-T specific heat. On the high-H side, the red region representing high Cp/T exhibits an abnormal S-shaped boundary, at which it is cut off by an evidently gapped state with marginal Cp/T (blue region) below ≃0.5 K (Tpeak in Fig. 4a). More interestingly, we find that such cut-off boundary for Cp/T closely tracks the crossover line Hp(T) to the full spin polarization in this region of phase diagram; the latter is determined from τ(H) (Fig. 4b) as the onset of high-H linear behavior (suggestive of H-independent magnetization, see Supplementary Section IV). The coincidence between Hp and the disappearance of Cp anomaly, as illustrated in Fig. 4c, provides valuable information: the unusual low-T upturn of Cp is closely linked to the incomplete polarization in the ‘nominally’ polarized state above Hc5, and can be erased once all Ni spins are fully polarized by the H-field.

Discussion

A power-law T dependence of the specific heat, Cm(T) ~ Tγ (γ ≲ 1), has been frequently cited as evidence for the presence of low-lying fermionic spin excitations (i.e., spinons) in frustrated quantum magnets — which points towards the realization of gapless spin-liquid states24,39–41. In particular, γ = 1 (Cm ∝ T) implies that the coherent quasiparticles form a spinon Fermi surface that is resistive against gauge field fluctuations42, whereas γ = 2/3 is expected when the quasiparticle mass renormalization caused by such fluctuations is considered43,44. T-linear specific heat can also emerge from gapless excitations in insulating magnets when H induces a QCP in transverse Ising chain magnets45 or a BEC-QCP in the 2D limit15,24. Alternatively, a sublinear Cm = Tγ with γ < 1 may also stem from bond randomness in disordered frustrated magnets46,47. However, none of these scenarios interpret the inverse-power-law behavior Cp(T)/T ~ T−α (α > 2) established between Hc2 and Hp in Na3Ni2BiO6 (Fig. 4a); all predictions for gapless excitations yield a much weaker divergence, Cp(T)/T ~ T−α (0 ≤ α < 1).

Thereby, we propose that the enigmatically divergent Cp(T)/T indeed unveils the occurrence of a significant peak-type structure in Cp at T < 100 mK; such an anomaly most likely signals an impending transition to a masked ground-state order that, unfortunately, evades our experimental probes due to its ultra-low onset temperature. This emergent low-T ordered state appears immediately once the 1/3-plateau phase III terminates and may act as the ground state for the intermediate phases between Hc2 and Hc5 (Fig. 1e); note that these intermediate phases themselves are likely to be ordered with specific magnetic superstructures, as Hc5 closely traces the AFM phase boundary (Fig. 4a). More strikingly, this low-T state persists beyond Hc5 up to a critical field between 10 and 10.5 T, above which its formation is cut off by the full polarization of spins that gaps out all low-lying magnons (Fig. 4). As such, the emergent order must be essentially distinct from the AFM ordering at higher T which ends at Hc5 (Supplementary Section IV). A most tempting phenomenology elucidating such a field evolution considers the magnons originating from the in-plane component of the Ni spins: They can be analogously treated as interacting dilute hard-core bosons in the concerned field region and, upon cooling, these bosons potentially condense into a superfluid state at the lowest T. Besides the superfluidity picture, we recall that the Kitaev interaction has been proposed to play an essential role introducing the frustration in Na3Ni2BiO625; intriguingly, a pronounced peak in Cm has also been predicted to emerges in the Kitaev model at very low T41,48, suggesting a crossover to a fractionalized spin-liquid ground state with aligned fluxes (still, these pure Kitaev models predict gap formation under H-field and are inapplicable in Na3Ni2BiO6). Verification of the link between the Cp anomaly and Kitaev physics awaits future investigations.

The 1/3-plateau phases in Na3Ni2BiO6 are also unconventional, in the sense that the plateau comprises at least three phases (maybe more at the lower T, Fig. 2c) with slightly different M yet distinct phase boundaries (Figs. 1 and 2). This marks a clear departure from familiar 1/3 magnetization plateaus in frustrated magnets, including both the classical (e.g., the plateaus occurring in triangular magnets via the order-by-disorder mechanism4,7,9,11,28) and the quantum ones (arising from entangled spins)34,35. We also note that the magnetization of plateau phases in Na3Ni2BiO6 show marginal time and history dependence (Supplementary Section II), thus are distinct from the nonequilibrium magnetization steps observed in spin-chain compound Ca3Co2O649. Nevertheless, the gapped magnon spectrum revealed by Cp(T) (Supplementary Fig. 6) suggests that these plateau phases are still likely to be solid states of hard-core bosons. A previous study postulates a nonclassical zero-up-zero-down-up-up spin configuration for the plateau phase, in which two out of the six spins in a magnetic unit aligned in the ab plane25. Starting from such model, we conjecture that the formation of plateau phases I-III have two possible origins: (i) They correspond to three distinct spin configurations, perhaps with different alignments of in-plane spin components. One likely scenario is that the two spin-flop (‘zero’) sites25 in the 6-site spin chain change their positions at the field-induced spin-reconfiguration transitions H1* and H2*; according to our first-principles calculations (Methods and Supplementary Table 3), such site changes render the energies of spin system slightly different, thus allow the successive development of nearly-degenerate plateau phases under increasing H. (ii) The weak interplane exchange coupling gives rise to the segmentation of 1/3 plateau into states with different ordering vector q along c*, similar to the famous “Devil’s staircase” (a cascade of commensurate magnetization plateaux) in the axial next-nearest neighbor Ising (ANNNI) model50 (see Supplementary Section II for more discussions). We emphasize that the plateau phases I, II and III do not have a distinguishable upper boundary with increasing T (Fig. 1e) and their emergence completely rely on quantum fluctuations; the interplane q in Na3Ni2BiO6, if it exists, is likely to be incommensurate.

Unexpectedly, the H − T phase diagram of Na3Ni2BiO6 phenomenologically emulates those of quasi-2D triangular lattice antiferromagnets which have predominant in-plane nearest-neighbor interactions15,17,23, in spite of the pure exchange (geometric) frustration for the former (latter). In both cases, the spin system undergoes consecutive magnetic transitions under elevated H, from zero-field AFM state to the 1/3 plateau phase(s), the intermediate phases with appreciable low-T magnetic entropy and the gapped fully-polarized state, in the sequential order. Clear discrepancies do exist: In triangular spin lattices, the 1/3 magnetization plateau corresponds to a classical up-up-down state as mentioned above and the exotic intermediate phases are supersolid15 or superfluid17 states with gapless Goldstone excitations (magnons), characterized by the simultaneous breaking of U(1) gauge symmetry and an additional (e.g., translational15 or spin rotational17) symmetry; in contrast, the plateau phases are of essential quantum nature in Na3Ni2BiO6, and the intermediate phases beyond them cannot be standard supersolids of hard-core bosons — for the reason that the U(1) symmetry is implicitly broken by the active Kitaev interaction — thus the “hidden” ground state (presumably a superfluid state, or a more exotic disordered state provoked by Kitaev physics) is not necessarily gapless. Nevertheless, the ostensible resemblance is already impressive, highlighting the astonishingly rich outcomes of Kitaev interaction in honeycomb lattices. Recent studies reveal that the Kitaev K and off-diagonal Γ interactions in an extended Kitaev model are crucial in stabilizing the 1/3-plateau phase in Na3Ni2BiO6 (details will be presented in a forthcoming parallel paper from some of us); apart from giving rise to strong quantum fluctuations, they may as well create natural pairing terms when mapped into a bosonic representation, thus provoking the series of anomalous phenomena observed herein. Our results therefore pinpoint the great promise of bringing forth emergent spin states in honeycomb spin lattices via introducing the Kitaev coupling. Further experimental explorations down to millikelvin temperatures are required to determine the nature of 1/3-plateau and intermediate phases in Na3Ni2BiO6 and we anticipate that such efforts may reveal brand-new many-body physics in frustrated spin systems.

Methods

Sample preparation and characterizations

High-quality single crystals of Na3Ni2BiO6 were synthesized using a Na2CO3-Bi2O3 self-flux method25,26. The typical size of the plate-shaped crystals was 4 × 4 × 0.3 mm3. In this work, totally five samples were studied (#1–#5, see Supplementary Section I for details). Low-temperature magnetic properties were investigated down to 0.4 K using a Quantum Design MPMS3 system with He-3 probe. The isothermal magnetization M(H) (T ≥ 2 K) was measured up to 14 T in a Quantum Design Physical Property Measurement System (PPMS-14 T) equipped with the vibrating sample magnetometer module. Magnetic torque was measured in a DynaCool-14 T PPMS using a piezoresistive cantilever (SCL Sensortech PRSA-L300), which converts the magnetic torque of the sample to an electrical resistance signal. The measurement configuration is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2a. A small Na3Ni2BiO6 sample (#4) with dimensions of ~0.2 × 0.2 × 0.05 mm3 was cut from the as-grown crystals and attached to the tip of the piezoresistive cantilever using Apiezon N grease (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Specific heat measurements were performed in a Quantum Design PPMS (DynaCool-14 T) utilizing a dilution refrigerator (DR) insert.

AC magnetostriction measurement

The ac magnetostriction measurement is achieved based on a composite magnetoelectric (ME) technique that is developed based on the interfacial coupling between the sample and a piezoelectric transducer29,51,52: the magnetostrictive response of the sample to the external ac H-field is sensed by the transducer via the interfacial strain, subsequently giving rise to an ac ME voltage signal across the latter due to the piezoelectric effect. Compared with conventional methods of magnetostriction measurement, this technique has the advantage that it directly measures the linear magnetostriction coefficient λ instead of ΔL(H)/L0, thus avoids the excess noise introduced by taking the H derivative of ΔL(H)/L0 and provides a particularly sensitive probe for the determination of field-induced phase transitions (we emphasize that it is so far the only experimental method which is able to capture the in-plateau transitions H1* and H2* at T > 2 K). In this work, the experiment was performed using a commercial composite ME probe (MultiField Tech.) that can be loaded into a Quantum Design PPMS. In our study, the ME composite was a Na3Ni2BiO6/PMN-PT magnetoelectric laminate device (Fig. 1a) prepared by bonding a Na3Ni2BiO6 single crystal (with as-grown size) to a piezoelectric PMN-PT (0.7Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3-0.3PbTiO3) substrate with a thickness t = 200 μm using silver epoxy (Epoxy Technology, EpoTek H20E). The contacting surface of the sample was mechanically polished with fine sandpaper before mounting to improve the interfacial strain coupling of the laminate. Before experiment, the PMN-PT was pre-polarized by a 150 V voltage supplied with a Keithley 2400 source meter. During the measurements, the superconducting magnet in the PPMS provided the dc field Hdc that controls the properties of the sample (thus noted as H in this work), whereas a small ac field Hac ≃ 2–4 Oe superposed on Hdc was generated by a Helmholtz coil in the probe; the ac sine-wave current (with a frequency f = 200–1000 Hz) passing through the coil was supplied by a Keithley 6221 current source. The ac voltage signal VME = Vx + iVy between the top and bottom surface of PMN-PT substrate (Fig. 1a) was collected by a Stanford Research SR830 lock-in amplifier; note that the silver epoxy between the sample and PMN-PT also served as the electrical contact. The ME voltage coefficient αME = αx + iαy = (Vx + iVy)/(Hact) was then derived from the voltage signal. Since VME arises from an electric field Ez perpendicular to the surface of laminate (we defined this direction as the z-axis of the setup, which is also the direction of both Hdc and Hac), the measured αME was indeed αMEzz. According to previous studies29,30,51,

where dxz (dyz) is the piezoelectric coefficient of PMN-PT characterizing the electrical field along z generated by deformation along x(y), λxz(λyz) is the transverse linear magnetostriction coefficient along x(y) under H∥z [λxz(yz) = (1/L0)∂ΔLx(y)/∂μ0Hz], AME is a constant relying on sample volume and interfacial coupling. Here, we took the real part of αME, i.e., αx(H), as an indicator of the H-dependent in-plane linear magnetostriction λab for Na3Ni2BiO631. Note that λab represents a sum of the components of λ along two perpendicular symmetry axes in the honeycomb plane of Na3Ni2BiO6; in this study we did not distinguish the in-plane directions of the Na3Ni2BiO6 crystal, because magnetic domains along three equivalent axes are demonstrated to exist in this compound25.

The calibration of ac magnetic field Hac generated by the Helmholtz coil was performed before each measurement (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). We replaced the sample with a small three-turn induction coil and estimated the magnitude of Hac by sensing the induced ac voltage signal picked up by the induction coil. Hac increases almost linearly when the amplitude of excitation current Iac raises from 20 mA to 75 mA (Supplementary Fig. 8b); with the amplitude of Iac kept constant, we found that an increase of its frequency f initially leads to a reduction of Hac, whereas a saturation is reached in the high-f region (Supplementary Fig. 8a). The data presented in Fig. 1 were collected with f = 997.3 Hz and the αx(H) curves were obtained using the calibrated values of Hac. We also performed testing measurements using f = 211.3 Hz and, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 8c, no frequency dependence of the data was observed. Another factor to be considered during the ME experiment is the Joule heating from the excitation. To testify its influence, we set the system temperature at T = 3 K and applied an excitation of Iac = 50 mA; the temperature drift was proved to be less than 0.1 K, implying good thermal isolation between the sample and driven coil. Since the data collection was performed with an excitation smaller than this, we expect that our measurements were free from noticeable heating effect.

As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8d, the identified phase transitions, most prominently Hc2 − Hc5, also manifest as peak features in the imaginary part of the ME coefficient, αy(H), though the features are weaker than those in αx(H). It has been pointed out that the αy signal reflects the dissipation of the sample during the piezomagnetic process29,30. The finite dissipation (with such a low excitation frequency) at the critical fields thus reflect the first-order nature of the corresponding phase transitions; moreover, the opposite signs for the signatures in αy (negative) and αx (positive) mimic the character of ME signals at the first-order transitions in Na3Co2SbO653. Further analysis of αy is beyond the scope of this work. We also perform the ME measurement with H applied within the ab plane of Na3Ni2BiO6. Under in-plane H, the magnetization of Na3Ni2BiO6 exhibits no fractional plateau; instead, M(H) displays a step-like feature that indicates a spin-flop transition (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Such a transition manifests itself as a predominant peak in αx(H), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 5d. No other signature can be resolved from the ME data with H∥ab.

Theoretical calculations

Our first-principles calculations were performed based on the density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)54. The projected augmented wave (PAW) method is employed to solve the valence electron-ion interaction55. The exchange correlation functional is described by the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional56. Based on the existing experimental results25, we constructed 1 × 3 × 1 supercell to investigate the energy difference between different magnetic configurations. Among the four considered configurations, the 6-site spin chains in the honeycomb plane of Na3Ni2BiO6 (magnetic unit of the 1/3-plateau phase proposed in ref. 25) have two spin-flop sites (spins aligned in-plane) with varying positions, and the spins on the 4 remaining sites are aligned along the c* axis with three up spins and one down spins25. Within the DFT+U scheme, the value of Hubbard Ueff was chosen as 4.0 eV for Ni 3d electrons. The plane-wave-basis cutoff energy was set to 550 eV and the Brillouin zone are sampled by the 9 × 2 × 9 Γ-centered k-point mesh. The energy convergence criterion is 10−5 eV, and the residual force is 0.02 eV/Å. Results of calculations were presented in Supplementary Table 3.
