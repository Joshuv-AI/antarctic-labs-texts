Rota-type operators on 2-dimensional dendriform algebras
Imed Basdouri 1 , Bouzid Mosbahi 2

Abstract
. We describe Rota–Baxter operators, Reynolds operators, Nijenhuis operators, and Averaging operators on 2-dimensional dendriform algebras over ℂ \mathbb{C} .

17A30,17B38, 16W20, 16S50

1.
Introduction

In the early 1990s, Loday introduced a new class of algebras, known as
dendriform algebras

[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. Since their introduction, dendriform algebras have found applications in various areas of mathematics and mathematical physics
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. A dendriform algebra
E
over a field
\mathbb{K}
is a
\mathbb{K}
-vector space equipped with two binary operations

\displaystyle\succ:E\otimes E\rightarrow E,\quad\prec:E\otimes E\rightarrow E,

satisfying the following identities for all
u,v,w\in E
:

\displaystyle(u\prec v)\prec w

\displaystyle=u\prec(v\prec w)+u\prec(v\succ w),

(1)

\displaystyle(u\succ v)\prec w

\displaystyle=u\succ(v\prec w),

(2)

\displaystyle u\succ(v\succ w)

\displaystyle=(u\prec v)\succ w+(u\succ v)\succ w.

(3)

These identities decompose the associativity condition into two operations, creating a structure essential to fields such as non-commutative geometry, operad theory, and combinatorics.

Another important concept in this study is the notion of Rota–Baxter operators. These operators have been applied in many fields
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
. A
Rota–Baxter operator
on a dendriform algebra
E
over a field
\mathbb{C}
is a linear map
P:E\to E
satisfying

\displaystyle P(u)\prec P(v)

\displaystyle=P\left(P(u)\prec v+u\prec P(v)+\lambda\,u\prec v\right),

(4)

\displaystyle P(u)\succ P(v)

\displaystyle=P\left(P(u)\succ v+u\succ P(v)+\lambda\,u\prec v\right),

(5)

for all
u,v\in E
and some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. If
P
is a Rota–Baxter operator of weight
0
, it is also a Rota–Baxter operator of weight
1
, so it suffices to consider these two cases.

This paper focuses on specific types of Rota-type operators, such as Reynolds, Nijenhuis, and averaging operators, which are defined as follows.

Reynolds operator:

\displaystyle P(u)\prec P(v)

\displaystyle=P\left(u\prec P(v)+P(u)\prec v-P(u)\prec P(v)\right),

(6)

\displaystyle P(u)\succ P(v)

\displaystyle=P\left(u\succ P(v)+P(u)\succ v-P(u)\succ P(v)\right).

(7)

Nijenhuis operator:

\displaystyle P(u)\prec P(v)

\displaystyle=P\left(P(u)\prec v+u\prec P(v)-P(u\prec v)\right),

(8)

\displaystyle P(u)\succ P(v)

\displaystyle=P\left(P(u)\succ v+u\succ P(v)-P(u\succ v)\right).

(9)

Averaging operator:

\displaystyle P(u)\prec P(v)

\displaystyle=P\left(u\prec P(v)\right)=P\left(P(u)\prec v\right),

(10)

\displaystyle P(u)\succ P(v)

\displaystyle=P\left(u\succ P(v)\right)=P\left(P(u)\succ v\right).

(11)

The classification of low-dimensional dendriform algebras provides a foundation for exploring these operators. Previous work includes Rikhsiboev, I. M., Rakhimov, I. S., and Basri, W. I. T. R. I. A. N. Y. (2010).The description of dendriform algebra structures on two-dimensional complex space
[
3
]
.

In this paper, we study Rota-type operators on 2-dimensional dendriform algebras. The focus is on identifying, constructing, and analyzing these operators on low-dimensional structures, providing insights into the broader theory.

2.1.
Rota-Baxter operator

Theorem 2.1
.

There is a type of Rota-Baxter operator of weight 0 for the
2
-dimensional dendriform algebra

Dend_{2}^{1}
, which is as follows:

P_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&a_{12}\\
0&0\end{array}\right)

Proof.

The Rota-Baxter operator
P
of weight
0
for a two-dimensional dendriform algebra, satisfies the relation:

\displaystyle P(u)\prec P(v)

\displaystyle=P(P(u)\prec v+u\prec P(v)),

\displaystyle P(u)\succ P(v)

\displaystyle=P(P(u)\succ v+u\succ P(v)),

where
\prec
and
\succ
are the dendriform operations defined by the algebra

Dend_{2}^{1}:\begin{array}[]{ll}e_{1}\prec e_{1}=e_{1},\end{array}\quad\begin{array}[]{ll}e_{1}\succ e_{2}=e_{2}.\end{array}

Given
P
as a linear operator on
E
represented by a matrix
R
such that:

P(e_{1})=a_{11}e_{1}+a_{12}e_{2}\;and\;P(e_{2})=a_{21}e_{1}+a_{22}e_{2}

Case 1.
For
u=e_{1}
and
v=e_{1}

\displaystyle P(e_{1})\prec P(e_{1})=(a_{11}e_{1}+a_{12}e_{2})\prec(a_{11}e_{1}+a_{12}e_{2})=a_{11}^{2}e_{1},

\displaystyle P(P(e_{1})\prec e_{1}+e_{1}\prec P(e_{1}))=P(a_{11}e_{1}+e_{1})=P((a_{11}+1)e_{1})=a_{11}(a_{11}+1)e_{1}.

Setting these equal to each other, we get:
a_{11}^{2}e_{1}=a_{11}(a_{11}+1)e_{1}
. So,
a_{11}^{2}=a_{11}(a_{11}+1)

This simplifies to:
a_{11}^{2}=a_{11}^{2}+a_{11}
. Hence,
a_{11}=0
,

Case 2.
For
u=e_{1}
and
v=e_{2}

\displaystyle P(e_{1})\succ P(e_{1})=(a_{11}e_{1}+a_{12}e_{2})\succ(a_{21}e_{1}+a_{22}e_{2})=a_{11}a_{21}e_{2},

\displaystyle P(P(e_{1})\succ e_{2}+e_{1}\succ P(e_{2}))=P(a_{11}e_{2}+a_{21}e_{2})=P((a_{11}+a_{21})e_{2})=a_{12}(a_{11}+a_{21})e_{2}.

Setting these equal to each other, we get:
a_{11}a_{21}e_{2}=a_{12}(a_{11}+a_{21})e_{2}
. So,
a_{11}a_{21}=a_{12}(a_{11}+a_{21})
.
Since
a_{11}=0
, we have:
0=a_{12}a_{21}

This simplifies to:
a_{11}^{2}=a_{11}^{2}+a_{11}
. Hence,
a_{11}=0
, which implies either
a_{12}=0
or
a_{21}=0

Case 3.
For
u=e_{2}
and
v=e_{1}

\displaystyle P(e_{2})\prec P(e_{1})=(a_{21}e_{1}+a_{22}e_{2})\prec(a_{11}e_{1}+a_{12}e_{2})=0,

\displaystyle P(P(e_{2})\prec e_{1}+e_{2}\prec P(e_{1}))=P(0+0)=0.

This condition is automatically satisfied as both sides are zero.

Case 4.
For
u=e_{2}
and
v=e_{2}

\displaystyle P(e_{2})\succ P(e_{2})=(a_{21}e_{1}+a_{22}e_{2})\succ(a_{21}e_{1}+a_{22}e_{2})=0,

\displaystyle P(P(e_{2})\succ e_{2}+e_{2}\succ P(e_{2}))=P(0+0)=0.

This condition is also automatically satisfied as both sides are zero.
Finally, combining all these results, we get:

a_{11}=0,\;a_{21}=0,\;a_{12}=\text{arbitrary},\;a_{22}=0
.
∎

Theorem 2.2
.

The Rota-Baxter operators of weight
1
on the
2
-dimensional dendriform algebra
Dend_{2}^{1}
are the following:

P=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)

Proof.

A linear operator
P
on the 2-dimensional space spanned by

\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle P(e_{1})

\displaystyle=a_{11}e_{1}+a_{12}e_{2},\quad P(e_{2})=a_{21}e_{1}+a_{22}e_{2},

which corresponds to the matrix:

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\begin{array}[]{cc}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{array}\right).\end{array}

We now determine the values of
a_{11},a_{12},a_{21},
and
a_{22}
that satisfy the Rota–Baxter equations.

Case 1.

u=v=e_{1}

From the multiplication rules:

\displaystyle e_{1}\prec e_{1}

\displaystyle=e_{1},\quad e_{1}\succ e_{1}=0.

Applying the Rota–Baxter operator equation:

\displaystyle P(e_{1})\prec P(e_{1})

\displaystyle=P\left(P(e_{1})\prec e_{1}+e_{1}\prec P(e_{1})+e_{1}\prec e_{1}\right).

Substituting
P(e_{1})=a_{11}e_{1}+a_{12}e_{2}
, we get:

\displaystyle(a_{11}e_{1}+a_{12}e_{2})\prec(a_{11}e_{1}+a_{12}e_{2})

\displaystyle=a_{11}e_{1}.

The right side becomes:

\displaystyle P\left((a_{11}+1)e_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=(a_{11}+1)P(e_{1})=(a_{11}+1)(a_{11}e_{1}+a_{12}e_{2}).

Thus, we must have
a_{11}=0
and
a_{12}=0
.

Case 2.

u=e_{1},v=e_{2}

From the multiplication rules:

\displaystyle e_{1}\prec e_{2}

\displaystyle=0,\quad e_{1}\succ e_{2}=e_{2}.

Applying the Rota–Baxter operator equation:

\displaystyle P(e_{1})\succ P(e_{2})

\displaystyle=P\left(P(e_{1})\succ e_{2}+e_{1}\succ P(e_{2})+e_{1}\prec e_{2}\right).

Substituting
P(e_{1})=0
and
P(e_{2})=ce_{1}+de_{2}
, we get:

\displaystyle 0\succ(a_{21}e_{1}+a_{22}e_{2})

\displaystyle=0.

The right side becomes:

\displaystyle P\left(a_{22}e_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=a_{22}P(e_{2})=d(a_{21}e_{1}+a_{22}e_{2}).

Thus, we must have
a_{21}=0
and
a_{22}=0
.

From the above cases, we have:

a_{11}=a_{12}=a_{21}=a_{22}=0.

Therefore, the only Rota–Baxter operator of weight
1
on
Dend_{2}^{1}
is the zero operator:

P=\begin{array}[]{cc}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right).\end{array}

∎

.

All Reynolds operators on the

Since
P

\displaystyle P(e_{1})\prec P(e_{1})

\displaystyle=(a_{11}e_{1}+a_{21}e_{2})\prec(a_{11}e_{1}+a_{21}e_{2})=a_{11}^{2}e_{1}.

\displaystyle P(e_{1}\prec P(e_{1})+P(e_{1})\prec e_{1}-P(e_{1})\prec P(e_{1})).

Simplifying gives:

\displaystyle e_{1}\prec P(e_{1})

\displaystyle=a_{11}e_{1},

\displaystyle P(e_{1})\prec e_{1}

\displaystyle=a_{11}e_{1},

\displaystyle P(e_{1})\prec P(e_{1})

\displaystyle=a_{11}^{2}e_{1}.

Thus, the expression inside
P
becomes:

\displaystyle a_{11}e_{1}+a_{11}e_{1}-a_{11}^{2}e_{1}=(2a_{11}-a_{11}^{2})e_{1}.

Applying
P
:

\displaystyle P((2a_{11}-a_{11}^{2})e_{1})

\displaystyle=(2a_{11}-a_{11}^{2})P(e_{1})=(2a_{11}-a_{11}^{2})(a_{11}e_{1}+a_{21}e_{2}).

For equality to hold with the left side
a_{11}^{2}e_{1}
, we require:

\displaystyle a_{21}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{and}\quad a_{11}^{2}=2a_{11}-a_{11}^{2}.

This gives
a_{11}=0
or
a_{11}=1
.

Case 2.

u=e_{1},v=e_{2}

\displaystyle P(e_{1})

\displaystyle=a_{11}e_{1},\quad P(e_{2})=a_{12}e_{1}+a_{22}e_{2}.

We find:

\displaystyle P(e_{1})\prec P(e_{2})

\displaystyle=a_{11}a_{12}e_{1}.

Using the Reynolds condition:

\displaystyle P(e_{1}\prec P(e_{2})+P(e_{1})\prec e_{2}-P(e_{1})\prec P(e_{2})).

After simplification, we find:

\displaystyle a_{12}

\displaystyle=0.

From the previous conditions, we have:

a_{21}=0,a_{12}=0,a_{11}anda_{22}

can take arbitrary values.
Thus, the matrices representing Reynolds operators are:

P_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&a_{12}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad P_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad P_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{22}&0\\
0&a_{22}\end{pmatrix}
.
∎

Theorem 2.5
.

Proof.

We will verify each of the proposed averaging operators
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
, and
P_{3}
against the averaging operator conditions.

Condition for
\prec
:

P_{1}(u)\prec P_{1}(v)=P_{1}(u\prec P_{1}(v))=P_{1}(P_{1}(u)\prec v)

For
u=e_{1}
,
v=e_{2}
:

P_{1}(e_{1})=0,\quad P_{1}(e_{2})=a_{12}e_{1}

Thus,

P_{1}(e_{1})\prec P_{1}(e_{2})=0\prec(a_{12}e_{1})=0

And:

P_{1}(e_{1}\prec P_{1}(e_{2}))=P_{1}(0)=0

And:

P_{1}(P_{1}(e_{1})\prec e_{2})=P_{1}(0)=0

Both sides are equal, satisfying the condition.

Condition for
\succ
:

P_{1}(e_{1})\succ P_{1}(e_{2})=P_{1}(e_{1}\succ P_{1}(e_{2}))=P_{1}(P_{1}(e_{1})\succ e_{2})

Calculating:

P_{1}(e_{1})\succ P_{1}(e_{2})=0\succ(a_{12}e_{1})=a_{12}e_{2}

And:

P_{1}(e_{1}\succ P_{1}(e_{2}))=P_{1}(a_{12}e_{2})=a_{12}P_{1}(e_{2})=a_{12}\cdot 0=0

Thus, this condition also holds.

2.

Condition for
\prec
:

Following similar steps:

P_{2}(e_{1})=a_{11}e_{1},\quad P_{2}(e_{2})=0

Thus,

P_{2}(e_{1})\prec P_{2}(e_{2})=(a_{11}e_{1})\prec 0=0

And:

P_{2}(e_{1}\prec P_{2}(e_{2}))=P_{2}(0)=0

And:

P_{2}(P_{2}(e_{1})\prec e_{2})=P_{2}(a_{11}e_{1}\prec e_{2})=P_{2}(0)=0

Thus, the first condition holds.

Condition for
\succ
:

Calculating:

P_{2}(e_{1})\succ P_{2}(e_{2})=a_{11}e_{1}\succ 0=0

And:

P_{2}(e_{1}\succ P_{2}(e_{2}))=P_{2}(0)=0

Thus, both conditions are satisfied.

3.

Condition for
\prec
:

Calculating:

P_{3}(e_{1})=a_{22}e_{1},\quad P_{3}(e_{2})=a_{22}e_{2}

P_{3}(e_{1})\prec P_{3}(e_{2})=(a_{22}e_{1})\prec(a_{22}e_{2})=a_{22}e_{1}\prec a_{22}e_{2}=a_{22}e_{1}
