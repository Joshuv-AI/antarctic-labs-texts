Automated Algorithm Design via Nevanlinna-Pick Interpolation
Ibrahim K. Ozaslan 1, Tryphon T. Georgiou 2, Mihailo R. Jovanovic 1

Abstract
The synthesis of optimization algorithms typically follows a design-first–analyze-later approach, which often obscures fundamental performance limitations and hinders the systematic design of algorithms operating at the achievable theoretical boundaries. Recently, a framework based on frequency-domain techniques from robust control theory has emerged as a powerful tool for automating algorithm synthesis. By integrating the design and analysis stages, this framework enables the identification of fundamental performance limits. In this paper, we build on this framework and extend it to address algorithms for solving strongly convex problems with equality constraints. As a result, we obtain a new class of algorithms that offers sharp trade-off between number of matrix multiplication per iteration and convergence rate.

Introduction

The design and analysis of optimization algorithms typically begins with selecting an appropriate algorithmic structure based on the problem’s optimality conditions. This is followed by a convergence analysis, which often relies on creative and problem-specific reasoning. Such a
design-first-analyze-later
approach typically requires deriving tight analytical inequalities
-
an inherently challenging and non-systematic task. This difficulty contributes, in part, to the limited understanding of fundamental performance limits within specific algorithm classes.

Viewing optimization algorithms through the lens of dynamical systems and applying Lyapunov stability theory enables a system-theoretic understanding of their behavior. The challenging aspect comes from the fact that designing Lyapunov functions that lead to the optimal convergence rates requires deep problem-specific insight
Su et al. 2016
;
Wibisono et al. 2016
;
Hu and Lessard 2017
;
Shi et al. 2021
;
Muehlebach and Jordan 2019
;
Ozaslan and Jovanović 2025
;
Zheng et al. 2023
. A complementary approach involves fixing the structure of the Lyapunov function and the algorithm, and then using computational tools to search for valid certificates and parameters
Lessard et al. 2016
;
Van Scoy et al. 2017
;
Michalowsky et al. 2021
;
Scherer et al. 2023
;
Boyd et al. 2024
. While this method has the potential to yield sharp results
Van Scoy et al. 2017
;
Scherer and Ebenbauer 2021
, its reliance on fixed functional forms restricts its capacity to uncover fundamental performance limits.

Recently a frequency-domain approach leveraging tools from robust control theory emerged as a powerful tool for automating algorithm design and analysis
Ugrinovskii et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024
. This framework models optimization algorithms as transfer functions within a feedback system, derives analytic conditions to guarantee desirable algorithmic properties, and uses Nevanlinna-Pick interpolation to construct transfer functions that meet these conditions. When the algorithm specifications are expressed through necessary and sufficient conditions, this framework provides a means for identifying fundamental performance limits. For instance, a constructive proof that establishes Nesterov’s lower bound on the complexity of gradient-based methods in unconstrained minimization, which was originally obtained for infinite dimensional problems using adversarial examples, has been recently provided in
Ugrinovskii et al. 2023
using this framework, and then independently in
Wu et al. 2024
building on
Zhang et al. 2024
.

In this paper, we extend this framework to address algorithms for convex optimization problems with equality constraints,

\displaystyle\minimize\limits_{x}~f(x)~\subjectto~Ex\,=\,q,~~E\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}

(1)

where
f
is an
m
-strongly convex function with an
L
-Lipschitz continuous gradient. Such problems arise in a host of applications spanning federated learning
Li et al. 2020
, imaging
Chambolle and Pock 2016
, optimal transport
Peyre and Cuturi 2019
, and fluid dynamics
Taha et al. 2023
. A particularly important instance is the decentralized optimization where
E
is a gossip (or Laplacian) matrix of the network,
q=0
, and the objective function is the sum of local costs
f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(x_{i})

Scaman et al. 2017
.

Existing algorithms:

A lower bound on the iteration complexity of the gradient-based algorithms for problem (
1
), along with an algorithm achieving the bound, was established in
Salim et al. 2022a
. This algorithm attains
\epsilon
-accuracy in
O(\sqrt{\kappa_{f}}\log\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}))
iterations, but requires
O(\sqrt{\kappa_{E}})
matrix-vector multiplications per iteration. Here,
\kappa_{f}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=L/m
and
\kappa_{E}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sigma_{1}/\sigma_{r}
, where
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{r}
denote the largest and smallest (nonzero) singular values of
E^{\top}E
, respectively. When matrix-vector multiplications dominate the cost, e.g., in distributed optimization with limited communication budget
Lan et al. 2020
;
Qu and Li 2019
, inner-loop iterations are undesirable. Among single-loop methods, Gradient Descent-Ascent (GDA) achieves
O(\kappa_{f}\kappa_{E}\log\tfrac{1}{\epsilon})
iteration complexity, while a variant of Condat-Vu algorithm
Condat 2013
;
Vu 2013
, known as the PAPC
Salim et al. 2022b
, achieves
O(\max(\kappa_{f},\kappa_{E})\log\tfrac{1}{\epsilon})
. In the special case, where
E
is the gossip matrix and
q=0
, the accelerated PAPC algorithms attains
O(\max(\sqrt{\kappa_{f}\kappa_{E}},\kappa_{E})\log\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}))
complexity
Kovalev et al. 2020
, while accelerated EXTRA achieves
O(\sqrt{\kappa_{f}\kappa_{E}}\log(\kappa_{f}\kappa_{E})\log\tfrac{1}{\epsilon})

Li and Lin 2020
.

Our contribution
lies in the general framework we develop, which potentially enables automated algorithm design across diverse settings. Using this framework, we synthesize the Interpolated - Gradient Method (I-GM) for solving problem (
1
) with an elegant structure that provides a sharp trade-off between the number of matrix-vector multiplications per iteration,
\ell
, and the worst-case convergence rate. The resulting iteration complexity is
O(\max(\kappa_{f},\kappa_{E}/\ell)\log\tfrac{1}{\epsilon})
. For example, in the decentralized setting with limited computation/communication budget, one can effectively balance the local information propagation and convergence rate by adjusting the parameter
\ell
.

2
Automated algorithm synthesis

Our framework consists of the following steps. We first outline the algorithm specifications (Sec.
2.1
) and utilize
\mathcal{Z}
-transform to represent the class of algorithms under consideration (Sec.
2.2
). Then, we formulate the algorithm design as a controller synthesis problem (Sec.
2.3
) and obtain analytical characterization of the design specifications (Sec.
2.4
) in terms of the underlying transfer functions. By recasting control synthesis as an interpolation problem (Sec.
2.5
), we provide a solution that leads to an implementable optimization algorithm with a guaranteed convergence rate (Sec.
2.6
).

For clarity of exposition, we simplify our synthesis procedure by replacing the constraint with
E^{\top}Ex=E^{\top}q
and assuming
q=0
. The first modification incurs no loss of generality, as multiplying the equality constraint by
E^{\top}
leaves the feasible set unchanged. Crucially, the synthesized algorithm never explicitly forms
W\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=E^{\top}E
, it requires only matrix-vector products with
E^{\top}
and
E
. The second assumption,
q=0
, is purely expository; extending our framework to handle arbitrary
q
is achieved by a straightforward modification in Section
2.6
while preserving all theoretical guarantees of the synthesized algorithm.

2.1
Design specifications

Linearity:

We investigate optimization algorithms with linear updates both in optimization variables and gradients.

Explicitness:

We design single-loop algorithms that avoid circular dependence in the evaluation of
x^{k+1}
. In other words, the computation of
x^{k+1}
should not exploit
Ex^{k+1}
or
\nabla f(x^{k+1})
. This requirement can be relaxed if one can afford to utilize linear system solvers
Ozaslan et al. 2023
or evaluation of proximal operators
Wu et al. 2024
in the iterative scheme.

Optimality:

The algorithm should asymptotically converge to the unique solution
x^{\star}
of problem (
1
). A necessary and sufficient condition for
x^{\star}
(when
q=0
) is given by the existence of unique vectors
\delta^{\star}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
and
w^{\star}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-r}
such that

\nabla f(x^{\star})=V_{1}\delta^{\star}_{1}
and
x^{\star}=V_{2}w^{\star}_{2}
. Here,
r<n
determines the rank of matrix
W=E^{\top}E
with the singular value decomposition,

W\,=\,\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}V_{1}&V_{2}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Sigma&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}V_{1}^{\top}\\
V_{2}^{\top}\end{array}\right],~~\Sigma\,\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\,\diag(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{r}),~~V\,\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\,\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}V_{1}&V_{2}\end{array}\right].

Linear convergence:

We require algorithms to linearly converge at a rate of at least
\rho
, i.e., there exist
\rho\in(0,1)
and
M>0
such that
\|x^{k}-x^{\star}\|\leq M\|x^{0}-x^{\star}\|\rho^{k}
for any
x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.2
Algorithm representation

The algorithms with linear updates both in optimization variables and gradients take the form of

z\mathcal{K}_{0}(z,W)\widehat{x}(z)\,=\,\mathcal{K}_{1}(z,W)\widehat{x}(z)\,+\,\mathcal{K}_{2}(z,W)\widehat{\nabla f}(z)

(2)

where
\widehat{x}(z)
and
\widehat{\nabla f}(z)
are the
\mathcal{Z}
-transforms of sequences
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
and
\{\nabla f(x^{k})\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
. Here, transfer function
\mathcal{K}_{0}(z,W)
determines the mapping between the optimization variable and the input of the gradient, while
\mathcal{K}_{1}(z,W)
and
\mathcal{K}_{2}(z,W)
determine how the past iterates and past gradients, respectively, are used to update the optimization variable. These three transfer functions are (potentially) parametrized by constraint matrix
E
, or in this particular setting, by
W\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=E^{\top}E
.

The class of algorithms that can be brought into the form of (
2
) includes, but not limited to, accelerated methods such as Nesterov’s Accelerated Gradient
Nesterov 2003
, distributed algorithms such as DIGing
Nedic et al. 2017
, EXTRA
Shi et al. 2015
, and
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{N}

Xin et al. 2019
as well as descent-ascent type methods such as GDA
Alghunaim and Sayed 2020
and PAPC method
Salim et al. 2022a
; see appendix for a detailed example.

2.3
From algorithm to controller design

The optimization algorithms given by (
2
) can be viewed as the Lur’e system in Fig.
1

\widehat{x}(z)=\mathcal{H}(z,W)\widehat{\nabla f}(z)
and a static nonlinear block
\Delta

\mathcal{H}(z,W)\,\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\,(z\mathcal{K}_{0}(z,W)\,-\,\mathcal{K}_{1}(z,W))^{-1}\mathcal{K}_{2}(z,W),~~\Delta(e)\,\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\,\nabla f(e+x^{\star})\,-\,\nabla f(x^{\star}).

(3)

The nonlinear map
\Delta
is sector bounded in
[m,L]
, i.e.,

m\|e\|^{2}\leq\langle\Delta(e),e\rangle\leq L\|e\|^{2}
for all
e\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Our task is to design a linear controller

2
)). To this end, we obtain an analytical characterization of design specifications, but first, we employ the following transformations to bring the Lur’e system into a form that is more convenient for analysis/synthesis.

2.4
Analytical characterization of design specifications

Explicitness as causality:

A necessary condition for explicitness is the strict causality of the linear controller in the Lur’e system. The controller is strictly causal when transfer function
\mathcal{H}(z,W)
is strictly proper, meaning
\mathcal{H}(\infty,W)=0
. This is equivalent to
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\mathcal{H}}(\infty,W)=I
, i.e.,

\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{h}(\infty,\sigma)\,=\,1,~~\forall\,\sigma\,\in\,[\underline{\sigma},\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\sigma}]\,\cup\,\{0\}.

(5a)

Optimality as input tracking and disturbance rejection:

The asymptotic convergence of the optimization algorithm to the optimal solution is equivalent, in the Lur’e system, to the asymptotic tracking of the step input
\tfrac{z}{z-1}x^{\star}
with unknown magnitude
x^{\star}
and rejection of the step disturbance
\tfrac{z}{z-1}\nabla f(x^{\star})
with unknown magnitude
\nabla f(x^{\star})
. The internal model principle
Francis and Wonham 1976
implies that asymptotic tracking and rejection can be achieved if the subsystems
\mathcal{H}(z,\Sigma)
and
\mathcal{H}(z,0)
have a blocking zero and a pole at
z=1
, respectively. For the transformed system, this is equivalent to

\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{h}(1,\sigma)=1,~~\forall\sigma\,\in\,[\underline{\sigma},\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\sigma}],~~~~~\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{h}(1,0)\,=\,L/m.

(5b)

Linear convergence as
\rho
-stability:

A necessary and sufficient condition for linear convergence of the algorithm is the
\rho
-stability of the Lur’e system. The circle criterion
(
Khalil 2002
, Sec. 7.1)
implies
\rho
-stability of the transformed Lur’e system
(\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\mathcal{H}},\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\Delta})
, if the transfer function
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{h}(\gamma z,\sigma)
is
strictly positive real
for all
\gamma\in(\rho,1]
. This can be equivalently expressed as, for all
\gamma\in(\rho,1]
and
\sigma\in[\underline{\sigma},\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\sigma}]\cup\{0\}
,

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{i})~~\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{h}(\gamma z,\sigma)\in\mathcal{RH}_{\infty},~~~~~\mathrm{ii})~~\re\,(\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{h}(\gamma z,\sigma))>0,~\forall z\in\partial\mathbb{D}\end{array}

(5c)

where

denotes set of all real, rational, stable transfer functions,

is the real part of a complex number,

, and

is the complex space.

2.5
From controller design to interpolation

With the algorithm representation (
2

5

Find a
\rho\in(0,1)
and design a
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{h}(z,\sigma)
such that conditions (

\gamma\in(\rho,1]
.

(IP)

Using suitable conformal maps, we can cast (

\begin{array}[]{c}\text{Given data pairs $\{(z_{i},w_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}$ with each $z_{i}\in\mathbb{D}$, find a holomorphic function $\psi(z)$}\\
\text{that satisfies $\{|\psi(z)|\leq 1,$ for all $z\in\mathbb{D}\}$ and $\psi(z_{i})=w_{i}$, where $\mathbb{D}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C},\,|z|<1\}$.}\end{array}

The interpolation problem is then solved using the Nevanlinna-Pick (NP) method
(
Doyle et al. 1992
, Ch. 9)
, which determines: (i) when a solution exists, i.e., a lower bound on

For any given constants

,
\alpha_{1}\in(0,2/L)
,
\alpha_{2}\in(0,2/\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{\sigma})

\rho\,=\,\max\big(|1-\alpha_{2}\underline{\sigma}|^{\ell},\,|1-\alpha_{1}m|\big),~~~~\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{h}(\gamma z,\sigma)\,=\,\dfrac{\gamma z(\gamma z-g^{2}(\sigma))\,+\,\rho g(\sigma)(\gamma z-1)}{\gamma z(\gamma z-g^{2}(\sigma))\,-\,\rho g(\sigma)(\gamma z-1)}.

(6)

The convergence rate
\rho
is optimized by setting
\alpha_{1}=2/(m+L)
and
\alpha_{2}=2/(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{r})
, resulting in
\rho^{\star}=\max\big((\kappa_{f}-1)/(\kappa_{f}+1),\boldsymbol{(}(\kappa_{E}-1)/(\kappa_{E}+1)\boldsymbol{)}^{\ell}\big)

Appendix B
Algorithm Representation

In this section, we demonstrate how formulation (
2
) encompasses a wide range of algorithms including those with primal-dual updates. As a concrete instance, consider Proximal Alternating Predictor-Corrector (PAPC) algorithm
Salim et al. 2022a
:

\displaystyle x^{k+1/2}

\displaystyle\,=\,x^{k}\,-\,\alpha_{1}\nabla f(x^{k})\,-\,\alpha_{1}E^{\top}y^{k}

(9a)

\displaystyle y^{k+1}

\displaystyle\,=\,y^{k}\,+\,\alpha_{2}(Ex^{k+1/2}\,-\,q)

(9b)

\displaystyle x^{k+1}

\displaystyle\,=\,x^{k}\,-\,\alpha_{1}\nabla f(x^{k})\,-\,\alpha_{1}E^{\top}y^{k+1}.

(9c)

Replacing the equality constraint with
E^{\top}Ex=E^{\top}q
, i.e., multiplying the dual update (
9b
) by
E^{\top}
, we introduce a new “dual” variable
v^{k}=E^{\top}y^{k}
, which reduces the PAPC algorithm to

\displaystyle x^{k+1/2}

\displaystyle\,=\,x^{k}\,-\,\alpha_{1}\nabla f(x^{k})\,-\,\alpha_{1}v^{k}

(10a)

\displaystyle v^{k+1}

\displaystyle\,=\,v^{k}\,+\,\alpha_{2}E^{\top}(Ex^{k+1/2}\,-\,q)

(10b)

\displaystyle x^{k+1}

\displaystyle\,=\,x^{k}\,-\,\alpha_{1}\nabla f(x^{k})\,-\,\alpha_{1}v^{k+1}.

(10c)

Notably, all variables in (
10
) has the same dimension as primal variable
x
. While matching dimensions is not required for our representation (
2
), it substantially simplifies the transfer function structure by avoiding block matrix forms. For clarity of exposition, we also assume
q=0
(this assumption can be avoided by defining an affine operator
W(x)=E^{\top}(Ex-q)
as discussed in Section (
2.6
)). Substituting the intermediate variable
x^{k+1/2}
into (
10b
), we obtain the following primal-dual updates:

\displaystyle v^{k+1}

\displaystyle\,=\,(I-\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}E^{\top}E)v^{k}\,+\,\alpha_{2}E^{\top}E(x^{k}\,-\,\alpha_{1}\nabla f(x^{k}))

(11a)

\displaystyle x^{k+1}

\displaystyle\,=\,x^{k}\,-\,\alpha_{1}\nabla f(x^{k})\,-\,\alpha_{1}v^{k+1}.

(11b)

In the most general form, any primal-dual update (for solving problem (
1
)) that is linear both in optimization variable and gradients, can be represented as

\displaystyle z\widehat{x}

\displaystyle\,=\,\mathcal{K}_{1}(z,E)\widehat{x}\,+\,\mathcal{K}_{2}(z,E)\widehat{\nabla f}\,+\,\mathcal{K}_{0}(z,E)\widehat{v}

(12a)

\displaystyle z\widehat{v}

\displaystyle\,=\,\mathcal{L}_{0}(z,E)\widehat{v}\,+\,\mathcal{L}_{1}(z,E)\widehat{x}\,+\,\mathcal{L}_{2}(z,E)\widehat{\nabla f}.

(12b)

For instance, the primal version of PAPC algorithm (
11
), a.k.a., PriLiCo
Salim et al. 2022b
, corresponds to the following transfer function definitions,

\begin{split}\mathcal{K}_{0}(z,E)&\,=\,-\alpha_{1}zI,\\
\mathcal{K}_{1}(z,E)&\,=\,I,\\
\mathcal{K}_{2}(z,E)&\,=\,\alpha_{1}I,\end{split}~~~~~~\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{0}(z,E)&\,=\,I\,-\,\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}E^{\top}E\\
\mathcal{L}_{1}(z,E)&\,=\,\alpha_{2}E^{\top}E\\
\mathcal{L}_{2}(z,E)&\,=\,-\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}E^{\top}E.\end{split}

To establish a connection with our representation (
2
), we express
\widehat{v}
in terms of
\widehat{x}
and
\widehat{\nabla f}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{v}

\displaystyle\,=\,(zI\,-\,\mathcal{L}_{0})^{-1}(\mathcal{L}_{1}\widehat{x}\,+\,\mathcal{L}_{2}\widehat{\nabla f})

(13a)

\displaystyle\,=\,\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\widehat{x}\,+\,\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{2}\widehat{\nabla f}

(13b)

where, omitting the arguments,
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(zI-\mathcal{L}_{0})^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(zI-\mathcal{L}_{0})^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{2}
. Substituting (
13b
) into (
12a
) yields

\displaystyle z\widehat{x}

\displaystyle\,=\,(\mathcal{K}_{1}\,+\,\mathcal{K}_{0}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{1})\widehat{x}\,+\,(\mathcal{K}_{2}\,+\,\mathcal{K}_{0}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{2})\widehat{\nabla f}

(14a)

\displaystyle\,=\,\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{1}\widehat{x}\,+\,\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{2}\widehat{\nabla f}

(14b)

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{K}_{1}\,+\,\mathcal{K}_{0}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{K}_{2}+\mathcal{K}_{0}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{2}
.

The reduction from (
12
) to (
14
) confirms that algorithms following the primal-dual structure (
12
) can be equivalently cast in the primal-only form (
14
), thereby establishing the generality of representation (
2
).
