Long-lived giant circular Rydberg atoms at room temperature
Authors: Einius Pultinevicius, Aaron Götzelmann, Fabian Thielemann, Christian Hölzl, Florian Meinert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stability achieved by large angular momentum is ubiquitous in nature, with examples ranging from classical mechanics, over optics and chemistry, to nuclear physics. In atoms, angular momentum can protect excited electronic orbitals from decay due to selection rules. This manifests spectacularly in highly excited Rydberg states. Low angular momentum Rydberg states are at the heart of recent breakthroughs in quantum computing, simulation and sensing with neutral atoms. For these applications the lifetime of the Rydberg levels sets fundamental limits for gate fidelities, coherence times, or spectroscopic precision. The quest for longer Rydberg state lifetimes has motivated the generation, coherent control and trapping of circular Rydberg atoms, which are characterized by the maximally allowed electron orbital momentum and were key to seminal experiments with single atoms and photons. Here, we report the observation of individually trapped circular Rydberg atoms with lifetimes of more than 10 milliseconds, two orders of magnitude longer-lived than the established low angular momentum orbitals. This is achieved via Purcell suppression of blackbody modes at room temperature. We coherently control individual circular Rydberg levels at so far elusive principal quantum numbers of up to n = 103, and observe tweezer trapping of the Rydberg atoms on the hundred millisecond scale. Our results pave the way for quantum information processing and sensing utilizing the combination of extreme lifetimes and giant Rydberg blockade.

Introduction

Systems of neutral atoms trapped in optical tweezer arrays evolved over the past few years into one of the most promising platforms for quantum computing and quantum simulation1,2. The unprecedented control over interactions in these arrays, mediated by laser excitation to highly excited Rydberg states, made it possible to encode and simulate quantum many-body Hamiltonians of paradigmatic lattice models3–7. In the context of digital quantum computing, Rydberg states are a key ingredient to perform high-fidelity gates between two or more atoms8,9 to operate large-scale quantum processors10,11. A fundamental limitation for coherence times or gate fidelities, which inevitably comes with the commonly used Rydberg states (S or D orbitals), is their finite lifetime on the order of 100 μs due to optical and blackbody radiation (BBR) induced decay12. Circular Rydberg states (CRS)13, characterized by a maximal angular momentum (i.e., ∣m∣ = l = n − 1, where l and m are the orbital and magnetic quantum numbers, and n is the principal quantum number), provide means to overcome this limitation14–16. These states exhibit exceptionally long lifetimes due to the strong inhibition of spontaneous optical decay by selection rules17. This inhibition originates from its large angular momentum18, a stabilization mechanism that is omnipresent in nature19–22. While optical decay for these highest possible angular momentum states is inhibited by selection rules17, BBR also needs to be suppressed, e.g., via a cryogenic environment as in the Nobel prize-winning works on single-atom single-photon interaction23.

Here, we observe lifetimes of strontium circular Rydberg atoms which exceed 10 ms in a room temperature neutral atom tweezer experiment. Instead of cryogenic cooling24–26, we modify the mode density at microwave frequencies at the position of the atom14–16,27,28. This causes a more than 20-fold suppression of BBR-induced decay, and effectively provides lifetimes that in free space would require a 14 K environment. Further, we demonstrate coherent excitation of large CRS up to n = 103 (1.1 μm orbit diameter) using microwave control over a ladder of Rydberg levels, which allows us to characterize the blackbody suppression and Rydberg state lifetime enhancement across a wide range of frequencies.

Coherent interaction control between circular Rydberg atoms has recently been demonstrated at n ≈ 50 29. For comparison, our high n brings about a thousand-fold stronger van der Waals blockade, and more than ten times larger dipole-exchange coupling. Under the condition that the technical factors limiting the currently achieved dephasing time30 are addressed, these results push the fundamental barrier (i.e., the Rydberg state’s T1 time) for establishing quantum simulation on the Rydberg platform on timescales two orders of magnitude longer than what is currently possible. Reduced blackbody decay to other Rydberg states is also beneficial for erasure conversion schemes31,32, and mitigates anomalous dephasing in atom arrays33,34.

For a given mode density suppression, which in detail depends on the capacitor design (see “Methods C”), we exploit a second possibility to enhance the CRS lifetime, namely by increasing the principal quantum number n. This is because the rate for BBR-induced transitions to neighboring circular Rydberg levels decreases with ∝ n−2 35. Realization of CRS with n significantly larger than ≈50, however, remained elusive because of increasing state degeneracy (∝ n2), and increasing time required for state preparation. Only recently, our group showed trapped CRS with n = 79 30. Now we gain access to much higher CRS by combining adiabatic state preparation with up to 12 subsequent microwave pulses. The pulses coherently link CRS from n = 79 to 103 (Fig. 1c).

Results

Large circular states with n > 100

To this end, our experiments start with the preparation of a single trapped 88Sr atom inside our capacitor structure (Fig. 1a)36. We promote the atom to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\vert 79{{{\rm{C}}}}\right\rangle$$\end{document}79C CRS by a combination of optical excitation and adiabatic state transfer with a circularly polarized radio-frequency drive applied to the remaining four ring electrodes of our electrode structure (details are described in ref. 30). From there on, the principal quantum number of the circular orbit is successively increased in steps of two by a series of two-photon microwave transitions. To achieve robust coherent state transfer up to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\vert 103{{{\rm{C}}}}\right\rangle$$\end{document}103C, we apply adiabatic Landau-Zener (LZ) sweeps through the two-photon resonance for each of the transitions (Fig. 1d). The Rydberg state is finally read out using state-selective field-ionization (SSFI) (Fig. 1b), and detection of the produced ions on a microchannel plate detector (MCP). The arrival time at the MCP is then mapped to the principal quantum number of the ionized CRS (Fig. 1d).

The established control over many high-n circular Rydberg levels requires extensive microwave spectroscopy to identify and optimize all necessary transitions (Fig. 2a). This is because the population dynamics is affected by secondary transitions back into the initial n-manifold24,30,37. We take this into account by describing the decay dynamics between the n-manifolds with a rate equation model (see “Methods E”). With increasing n we observe first a strong enhancement effect by the capacitor on the lifetimes around n ≈ 80. When these strongest transitions become suppressed, the about 160 (n = 80) to 200 (n = 100) times weaker coupling to elliptical states of the next higher n contributes significantly to the CRS lifetime (see “Methods E”). Those are π transitions (Δm = 0), which are not suppressed by the boundary conditions of the plate capacitor. On the contrary, these modes are even enhanced by our ring electrode configuration for certain frequencies, leading to a dip in the measured lifetime around n ≈ 90 (see “Methods C”). For example, we measure a lifetime of 11.5(8) ms for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\vert 101{{{\rm{C}}}}\right\rangle$$\end{document}101C, which is around 21 times larger than the free-space lifetime of 545 μs. Additionally, the above-mentioned π modes between the ring electrodes are suppressed for the same range of n. Our experimental findings are supported by detailed numerical analysis of the capacitor structure (see “Methods C”). The trend of the data, including the lifetime enhancement steps around n ≈ 80 and n ≳ 95, is relatively well explained by a simplified model assuming an infinitely extended plate capacitor with finite reflectivity (gray shaded region in Fig. 3e). The decreased lifetime around n ≈ 90, however, is not captured. For a more sophisticated simulation, we model the BBR suppression induced by the full electrode structure (cf. Fig. 1a) using finite-element methods. Effectively, together with the ring-shaped electrodes, the capacitor is now extended to a cylindrical cavity. Such a structure supports additional discrete π-polarized modes, where the one with the lowest energy and longest wavelength causes the lifetime reduction around n ≈ 90 (blue shaded region in Fig. 3e). Optical Tweezer trapping lifetimes

The measurement of these extreme lifetimes requires that we keep the Rydberg atoms trapped and that atom loss from the tweezer is negligible. Trapping is also essential when exploiting the long lifetimes for quantum simulation or computing. Typically, Rydberg atoms are expelled from standard Gaussian optical tweezers because of the repulsive ponderomotive force on the Rydberg electron38,39. Here, we use alkaline-earth atoms, where the polarizable Sr+ ionic core provides a sufficiently strong attractive potential to confine our CRS in standard Gaussian tweezer30,40. Moreover, trapping does not require a circular Rydberg orbit but remains effective also when the Rydberg electron slowly decays into other Rydberg levels with different angular momentum or principal quantum number. To characterize the trapping lifetime, we initialize the atom in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\vert 97{{{\rm{C}}}}\right\rangle$$\end{document}97C as before, but now measure the total number of detected ions as a function of th without differentiating between product Rydberg states in different n manifolds (Fig. 4). An exponential fit of the data yields a 1/e trap lifetime of 133(6) ms, about an order of magnitude longer than the initial state lifetime. A control measurement with the tweezer trap turned off (inset of Fig. 4) shows that without trapping, the ion signal fades after a millisecond. Notably, this measurement gives a lower bound for the trapping time, because of the limited detection range of Rydberg levels arriving at the MCP for the chosen SSFI ramp in combination with ion-optical constraints. Discussion

To conclude, we have observed Purcell-enhanced lifetimes at record high-n trapped CRS of more than 10 ms in a room-temperature optical-tweezer apparatus. These are the longest-lived Rydberg states produced in a laboratory so far, even exceeding recent achievements in a cryogenic environment24. We characterized the Purcell effect on BBR far beyond the cut-off condition of our suppression structure via the enhancement of circular state lifetimes over a wide range of coherently connected n. This revealed the effects of additional resonant modes inside our electric field control in agreement with our theoretical models, demonstrating important aspects in the design of blackbody-radiation suppressing structures.

Combined with the recently demonstrated control of interactions for CRS29, our results pave the way for realizing a novel Rydberg-based quantum simulator capable of boosting simulation times by orders of magnitude, augmented by local optical addressing via the optical active ionic core41,42. The next steps to fully exploit the long lifetimes for quantum simulations require to also push the T2 coherence time for qubits encoded in circular states into the millisecond range. In our setup technical sources, specifically magnetic field noise and electric field homogeneity, currently limit this T2 time to the 100 μs scale30, which can be improved by active feedback and careful cancellation of gradients. Thermal trap-induced dephasing can also be addressed exploiting laser cooling on the ionic core43. Furthermore, dynamical decoupling provides additional intriguing perspectives44,45, particularly regarding dephasing from static gradients and the comparatively slow thermal motion in the tweezers. Overall, we expect that with these measures coherence times on the scale of the circular state lifetime are within reach.

The demonstrated control over a wide range of coherently connected CRS opens perspectives for implementing circular electron qudits or synthetic dimensions46. We have not yet explored the even larger state space available when including also elliptical states, which has recently been employed for sensing applications47, and proposed for encoding large-spin systems48, or effective dual-species models49. The hundreds of millisecond trapping times in combination with the alkaline-earth atom explored here, opens up exciting possibilities for coherent motional control of individual or interacting Rydberg atoms43,50, or high-precision ion clock spectroscopy inside a Rydberg orbit51.

B. State-selective field ionization

For state-selective field ionization (SSFI), we apply a linear high-voltage ramp to two of the ring-shaped electrodes (to the right in Fig. 1a) which ionizes the Rydberg atom and accelerates the ion toward the MCP detector. The MCP is located on one side of the electrode setup (outside the visible region in Fig. 1a toward positive z). Ion trajectories are manipulated by additional fields between the ITO plates and further deflector electrodes in the detection region. The SSFI scheme has been optimized, in order to enable the detection of a large range of n required in this work. This involved adiabatic rotation of the quantization from z to x axis prior to ionization, as indicated in Fig. 1b, and adjustments in deflector settings. Two separate sets of SSFI parameters were determined for the measurement of the CRS lifetimes (Fig. 3). One set allowed for simultaneous detection of n between 79 and 93 and was used for measurements up to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\vert 89{{{\rm{C}}}}\right\rangle$$\end{document}89C. For all higher-lying CRS the second SSFI parameter set for detection between n = 89 and n = 104 has been used. The populations of the respective states are determined from the detected ion counts in all accessible bins with the used ionization ramp. This includes residual elliptical states in the n = 79 bin, which are not addressed by our MW pulses (see also Fig. 1d). Consequently, the initial target circular state populations of n > 79 for the lower CRS branch (Fig. 3c) do include the 70% fidelity of CRS preparation30, while the higher CRS branch (Fig. 3d) does not.

While our SSFI scheme mainly distinguishes principal quantum numbers n, we note that the intra-manifold states ionize slightly differently. Generally, states with lower angular momentum tend to ionize at lower fields because of a reduced centrifugal barrier. In addition, the linear Stark effect can further affect the ionization threshold35. Indications of this are, for example, visible in Fig. 6b–d in the n = 91 window. Here, the ion signal stemming from elliptical Rydberg states (Fig. 6b, c) is shifted to lower arrival times (on the order of 100 ns) when compared to the signal from the CRS (Fig. 6d). A second example is apparent in the integrated ion signal on top of Fig. 3a. Enhanced π polarized decay for this n favors population of elliptical states, which can lead to a substructure within the n-manifold windows. In order to unambiguously assign arrival time windows for all n, we take histograms of all measurements with the same SSFI settings into account.

E. Validation of the rate model

As stated in the main text, the CRS lifetimes are obtained by fitting a rate model to the experimental data (see Fig. 3). This model assumes a ladder of states, each corresponding to a principal quantum number n, which are connected by a transition rate to neighboring states \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${n}^{{\prime} }=n\pm 1$$\end{document}n′=n±1. This analysis raises the question, to what extent the simplified rate model introduced above allows for accurately determining the CRS lifetimes. One may be tempted to extend the model to more states, but we find that this leads to overfitting of the experimental data. Instead, we show in the following that our approach allows for reliable extraction of lifetimes. To this end, we start with numerically simulating the CRS decay dynamics in its full complexity. This is done by solving an extended rate equation model, which includes additional 229 elliptical states besides the CRS ladder. Transition rates are computed from the analytic hydrogen model as introduced in ﻿“Methods D”. Note that rates between states with l < n − 1 can be calculated via additional recursion formulas55. From the numerical results, we sum up all state populations belonging to the same n-manifold, which effectively mimics the experimental data. Such simulated decay dynamics for n-manifolds are then fitted with our simplified rate model.
