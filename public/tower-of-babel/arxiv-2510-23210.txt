Higher order numerical schemes for SPDEs with additive Noise
Abhishek Chaudhary, Andreas Prohl

Abstract
We present high-order numerical schemes for linear stochastic heat and wave equations with Dirichlet boundary conditions, driven by additive noise. Standard Euler schemes for SPDEs are limited to an order convergence between 1 / 2 1/2 and 1 1 due to the low temporal regularity of noise. For the stochastic heat equation, a modified Crank-Nicolson scheme with proper numerical quadrature rule for the noise term in its reformulation as random PDE achieves a strong convergence rate of 3 / 2 3/2 . For the stochastic wave equation with additive noise a corresponding approach leads to a scheme which is of order 2.

1
Introduction

Stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) provide robust mathematical frameworks for modeling complex systems influenced by
randomness
across fields such as physics, engineering, finance, and biology. These equations describe phenomena like heat diffusion under
random
forcing, wave propagation with stochastic perturbations, financial models with spatial correlations, and biological processes affected by environmental noise. In this paper, we focus on two fundamental SPDEs: the linear parabolic stochastic heat equation and the stochastic wave equation, both driven by
additive
noise and defined on a smooth bounded domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with Dirichlet boundary conditions.

A fundamental challenge in the numerical analysis of SPDEs stems from the limited temporal regularity of the driving Wiener process which is only Hölder continuous of order at most
1/2
in time. This inherent roughness directly constrains the strong convergence rates attainable by a standard numerical analysis of temporal discretizations — as is shortly evidenced in Remark
1.1
. For instance, typical error analyses for the classical Euler–Maruyama scheme for parabolic SPDEs typically lead to a strong order of convergence of at most
1/2
. An improvement is obtained in
[
1
,
2
]
, where an order of convergence
1
is established for an implicit Euler discretization of SPDEs with colored
additive
noise. This result is achieved by employing a transformation technique that reformulates the original SPDE into a
random
PDE. In this work, we use this idea as an
algorithmic
tool in first line to develop high-order numerical schemes for both, the stochastic heat and wave equations driven by additive noise.

1.1
Stochastic Heat Equation

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be smooth, bounded domain, and
T>0
. We consider the stochastic heat equation driven by
additive
noise:

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}X(t)+AX(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\Phi\,\mathrm{d}W(t),&\text{in}\,(0,T]\times D,\\[4.0pt]
X=0,&\text{on}\,(0,T]\times\partial D,\\[4.0pt]
X(0)=X_{0},&\text{in}\,D,\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
W
is an
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued Wiener process, the initial datum
X_{0}:D\to\mathbb{R}
,
\Phi:D\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the
additive
noise coefficient, and
A:\mathbb{H}^{2}(D)\cap\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)\to\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)
is a second-order linear uniformly elliptic operator of the form

\displaystyle A=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{i,j}(x)\partial_{x_{i},x_{j}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}(x)\partial_{x_{i}}+c(x),

(1.2)

with smooth enough coefficients
a_{i,j},b_{i},c
for all
i,j=1,\dots,d
; see,
e.g.

[
9
, Sec. 3.1]
. A standard approach to time-discretization of SPDE (
1.1
) is the implicit Euler-Maruyama scheme:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle X_{j+1}-X_{j}+\tau AX_{j+1}=\Phi\Delta_{j+1}W,&j=0,\dots,N-1,\\[4.0pt]
X(0)=X_{0},\\[4.0pt]
X_{j}\big|_{\partial D}=0,\qquad j=1,\dots,N,\end{cases}

(1.3)

with a uniform time grid
t_{j}=j\tau
,
j=0,1,\dots,N
,
\tau=\frac{T}{N}
and the Wiener increment
\Delta_{j+1}W=W(t_{j+1})-W(t_{j})
. Due to the low temporal regularity of the noise, typical proofs of strong convergence for (
1.3
) show a rate of
1/2
, see
e.g.

[
11
]
, a limited order result that is shortly evidenced in the following remark.

Remark 1.1
.

Let
e_{j}=X(t_{j})-X_{j}
, where
\{X_{j};1\leq j\leq N\}
solves the scheme (
1.3
). In integrated form on
[t_{j},t_{j+1}]
, the first equation in (
1.1
) reads as

X(t_{j+1})-X(t_{j})+\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}AX(s)\,{\rm d}s=\Phi\Delta_{j+1}W.

Taking differences of this equation and (
1.3
) amounts to

\displaystyle e_{j+1}-e_{j}+\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}A\bigl[X(s)-X_{j+1}\bigr]\,{\rm d}s=0.

(1.4)

The order limiting term is now the integrand, since the solution
\{X(t);t\in[0,T]\}
to (
1.1
) is known to be Hölder continuous in time up to
1/2
.

Different ansatzes to ours given below were considered in the last decades to construct a scheme of (improved) strong order
1
, which employs the
explicit
knowledge of the related semigroup for (
1.2
) to approximately solve (
1.1
). A particular example where eigenvalues and -functions are explicitly known is
A=-\Delta
on the torus to use the related semigroup for an algorithm; see
e.g.

[
5
]
and the cited references there, as well as how to herewith set up the exponential Euler method in this context to settle strong order
1
. Despite its elegant analysis, such algorithms are of limited use in practice since their related characterizations are not available for
general

A
in (
1.2
), acting on
general
domains
D
. We therefore here aim to construct implementable higher-order methods that may be
used to approximate the solution of (
1.1
) for general data settings —
i.e.
, general
A
from (
1.2
), as well as general smoothly bounded domains
D
.

To accomplish this goal, we take motivation from the works
[
1
,
2
]
, and consider instead of the solution
X
from (
1.1
) the transformation

\displaystyle u=X-\Phi W,

(1.5)

and therefore convert the SPDE (
1.1
) into a
random
PDE:

\begin{cases}u_{t}(t)+Au(t)=-A\bigl[\Phi W(t)\bigr],&\text{in}\,(0,T]\times D,\\[4.0pt]
u=0,&\text{on}\,(0,T]\times\partial D,\\[4.0pt]
u(0)=X_{0},&\text{in}\,D,\end{cases}

(1.6)

where
u_{t}:=\partial_{t}u
. It is known that the solution
u
of (
1.6
) now
does
possess temporal derivatives; in fact, it can be shown that
u_{t}\in\mathbb{L}_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{L}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2}(D))
, provided
X_{0}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)
and
\Phi\in(\mathbb{H}^{2}(D)\cap\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D))^{m}
; see (
3.3
). This is the guiding motivation to verify order
1
for (
1.3
) in the context of more regular data
X_{0}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)\cap\mathbb{H}^{2}(D)
and
\Phi\in(\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)\cap\mathbb{H}^{2}(D))^{m}
with compatibility condition
A\Phi\in(\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D))^{m}
. To verify this result in
[
1
,
2
]
— and instead of starting with (
1.1
) directly — we consider first implicit Euler scheme for (
1.6
) which reads as

\begin{cases}\displaystyle U_{j+1}-U_{j}+\tau AU_{j+1}=-\tau A\bigl[\Phi W(t_{j+1})\bigr],&j=0,\dots,N-1,\\[8.0pt]
U^{0}=X_{0},\\[4.0pt]
U_{j}\big|_{\partial D}=0,\qquad j=1,\dots,N,\end{cases}

(1.7)

and exploit improved the temporal regularity for the solution
u
of (
1.6
) to verify

\displaystyle\sup_{1\leq j\leq N}\mathbb{E}\left[\|u(t_{j})-U_{j}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\right]\leq C\tau^{2}.

(1.8)

Afterwards, we revert the transformation procedure on a discrete level, by defining
X^{j}:=U_{j}+\Phi W(t_{j})
as approximate for
X(t_{j})
from (
1.1
). In fact, it can be immediately seen that
X^{j}=X_{j}
, such that we have

\displaystyle\sup_{1\leq j\leq N}\mathbb{E}\left[\|X(t_{j})-X_{j}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\right]=\sup_{1\leq j\leq N}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|u(t_{j})-U_{j}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}\bigr]\leq C\tau^{2}.

(1.9)

This convergence proof of rate
1
for implicit scheme
(
1.3
)
via (
1.6
) –and (
1.7
) – in the presence of regular data
X_{0}
and
\Phi
nourishes the hope to construct even higher order methods since the time regularity of the solution
u
to
random
PDE (
1.6
) is even higher,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle u\in C^{1,{1}/{2}}\big([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D))\big),

(1.10)

if data
X_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{4}(D)\cap\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)
and
\Phi\in\mathbb{H}^{3}(D)\cap\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)
with compatibility conditions
AX_{0}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)
and
A\Phi\in(\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D))^{m}
; see (
3.4
). This result for the solution
u
of (
1.6
) again rests on regularity theory for deterministic PDEs with temporal Hölder regularity of the right-hand side; see
[
9
, Thm. 4.3.1]
. For this purpose, we propose the new
modified Crank–Nicolson Scheme

3.1
for SPDE (
1.1
) which is a strong convergence rate of
3/2
; see Theorem
3.1
.

1.2
Stochastic wave equation

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be smooth, bounded domain, and
T>0
. We consider the stochastic wave equation driven by
additive
noise:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\rm d}X_{t}(t)+AX(t)\,{\rm d}t=\Phi\,{\rm d}W(t),&\text{in}\,(0,T]\times D,\\[6.0pt]
X(0)=X_{0},\quad X_{t}(0)=X_{1},&\text{in}\,D,\\[6.0pt]
X(t)=0,&\text{on}\,[0,T]\times\partial D,\end{cases}

(1.11)

where
X
and
X_{t}:=\partial_{t}X
denote displacement and velocity, and
X_{0}
and
X_{1}
are given initial data; again,
A
is a second-order linear uniformly elliptic operator from (
1.2
). Next to the (explicit) Euler method, first numerical schemes proposed for (
1.11
) also use the explicit knowledge of the related semigroup for
A=-\Delta
in
D=(0,1)
to construct methods of order
1
; see
[
4
,
8
]
for surveys of the relevant literature. A different viewpoint takes
[
8
]
where a numerical scheme of strong order
1.5
is given for the stochastic wave equation driven by a multiplicative noise, which is applicable for general
A
from (
1.2
) and smooth, bounded domains
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. To even construct a higher order approximations for SPDE
1.11
, we use another transformation than (
1.5
) here, which is

\displaystyle u(t)=X(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\Phi W(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,

(1.12)

to reformulate (
1.11
) as a
random
PDE:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle u_{tt}(t)+Au(t)=-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}A\bigl[\Phi W(s)\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}s,&\text{in}\,(0,T]\times D,\\[8.0pt]
u(t,x)=0,&\text{on}\,[0,T]\times\partial D,\\[6.0pt]
u(0)=X_{0},\quad u_{t}(0)=X_{1},&\text{in}\,D.\end{cases}

(1.13)

Similar to (
1.10
), the solution
u
to
random
PDE (
1.13
) ensures (see (
4.1
))

\displaystyle u\in C^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{H}^{2}(D)),\qquad\text{and}\qquad u_{t}\in C^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{H}^{1}(D))).

(1.14)

If compared to (
1.10
), we observe improved temporal regularity of the solution of
(
1.13
)
, which is why we may hope for an improved rate of convergence for the new
modified Crank–Nicolson
Scheme
4.1
whose construction is detailed in Section
4
. In fact we prove convergence with rate
2
for iterates which eventually approximate the solution
X
of
(
1.11
)
; see Theorem
4.1
.

Mathematical setup and preliminarily results

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a smooth bounded domain. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
with
\mathbb{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{t};\,t\in[0,T]\}
be a complete filtered probability space.
Let
\mathrm{B}
be a Banach space. Let

\mathbb{L}^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}\bigl(\Omega\times[0,T];\mathrm{B}\bigl)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{L}^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}\bigl([0,T];C([0,T];\mathrm{B})\bigr)

be the spaces of
\mathrm{B}
-valued
\mathbb{F}
-adapted stochastic processes and
\mathrm{B}
-valued continuous
\mathbb{F}
-adapted stochastic processes, respectively. For some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
W=(W_{1},...,W_{m})
be an
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued
\mathbb{F}
-adapted Wiener process on the filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
. Let
\Phi=(\Phi_{1},...,\Phi_{m})
be a
(\mathbb{L}^{2}(D))^{m}
-valued
additive
noise coefficient. We then define

\Phi W(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Phi_{i}W_{i}(t).

Let
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathrm{K}}
denote the inner product on Hilbert space
\mathrm{K}
. Let
<\cdot,\cdot>_{\mathcal{D}}
denote the dual pairing between
\mathbb{H}^{-1}(D)
and
\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)
. For convenience, we define

\langle u,v\rangle:=\int_{D}u(x)\,v(x)\,{\rm d}x.

We also use the standard fact that if
f\in C([0,T];\mathrm{B})
and
f^{\prime}\in L^{1}([0,T];\mathrm{B})
, then for every subinterval
[a,a+\kappa]\subset[0,T]
, the fundamental theorem of calculus for Bochner integrals holds with

f(a+s)=f(a)+\int_{0}^{s}f^{\prime}(a+\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,\qquad 0\leq s\leq\kappa.

The following lemma addresses the quadrature error in the context of the Crank–Nicolson scheme where the integrand is of limited regularity (see,
e.g.,
[
6
, Theorem 2]
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(\mathrm{B},\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{B}})
be a Banach space. Assume
f\in C^{1,\gamma}([0,T];\mathrm{B})
for some
\gamma\in(0,1]
and there exists a constant
\widetilde{C}>0
such that

\displaystyle\|f^{\prime}(t)-f^{\prime}(s)\|_{\mathrm{B}}\leq\widetilde{C}|t-s|^{\gamma}\quad\forall s,t\in[0,T]\,.

(2.1)

Then for any interval
[a,a+\kappa]\subset[0,T]
we have that

\left\|\frac{f(a)+f(a+\kappa)}{2}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{a}^{a+\kappa}f(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\right\|_{\mathrm{B}}\leq\frac{\widetilde{C}}{(\gamma+2)(\gamma+3)}\kappa^{1+\gamma}.

3
Stochastic Heat Equation

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a smooth bounded domain. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},{\mathbb{P}})
be a given probability space, let
W
as specified in Section
2
and
A
from (
1.2
). The existence of a unique variational solution
X\in\mathbb{L}^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}\big(\Omega;{C}([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2}(D))\big)\cap\mathbb{L}^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}\big(\Omega;\mathbb{L}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D))\big)
to SPDE (
1.1
) may
e.g.
be found in
[
3
, Ch. 3]
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|X\|^{2}_{{C}([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2}(D))}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|X\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D))}\bigr]\leq C\big(\|X_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}+\|\Phi\|_{(\mathbb{L}^{2}(D))^{m}}^{2}\big).

(3.1)

Moreover, by standard regularity theory for more regular data
X_{0}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)
and
\Phi\in(\mathbb{L}^{2}(D))^{m}
, it satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|X\|^{2}_{{C}([0,T];\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D))}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|X\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{H}^{2}(D))}\bigr]\leq C\big(\|X_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)}+\|\Phi\|_{(\mathbb{L}^{2}(D))^{m}}^{2}\big).

(3.2)

For the
random
PDE (
1.6
), if
X_{0}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)
and
\Phi\in(\mathbb{H}^{2}(D)\cap\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D))^{m}
, we have the existence and uniqueness of a variational solution

u\in\mathbb{L}^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}\big(\Omega;{C}([0,T];\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D))\big)\cap\mathbb{L}^{2}_{\mathbb{F}}\big(\Omega;\mathbb{L}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{H}^{2}(D))\big),

such that
u_{t}\in\mathbb{L}_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}\big(\Omega;\mathbb{L}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2}(D))\big),
and the following variational formulation holds:
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., for all
\varphi\in\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)
, a.e.
s\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\left<u_{t}(s),\varphi\right>=-\left<\nabla u(s),\nabla\varphi\right>+\left<\Delta\bigl[\Phi W(s)\bigr],\varphi\right>,

with
u(0)=X_{0}
. In addition, the following estimate holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|u\|_{{C}([0,T];\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D))\cap\mathbb{L}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{H}^{2}(D))}^{2}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|u_{t}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2}(D))}^{2}\bigr]\leq C\big(\|X_{0}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)}^{2}+\|\Phi\|_{(\mathbb{H}^{2}(D))^{m}}^{2}\big)\,;

(3.3)

see,
e.g.,

[
7
, Ch. 7, Sec. 1]
. Moreover, if
X_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{4}(D)\cap\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)
and
\Phi\in(\mathbb{H}^{3}(D)\cap\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D))^{m}
with compatibility condition
AX_{0},A\Phi\in\bigl(\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)\bigr)^{m}
, then with the help of Hölder continuity of
A[\Phi W]
in time
(
i.e.,

A[\Phi W]\in C^{1/2}\bigl([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D))\bigr)
)
, the solution
u
to
random
PDE (
1.6
) satisfies
u\in C^{1,1/2}\big([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D))\big),
with the following higher regularity bound

\displaystyle\|u\|_{{C}^{1,1/2}([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)))}^{2}\leq C\big(\|X_{0}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{4}(D)}^{2}+\|\Phi\|_{(\mathbb{H}^{3}(D))^{m}}^{2}\big).

(3.4)

This result may
e.g.
be deduced from
[
9
, Prop. 4.1.2, Thm 4.3.1]
by exploiting temporal Hölder regularity of the Wiener process on the right-hand side of (
1.6
).

3.1
Modified Crank–Nicolson Scheme

For simplicity, in this section, we take
A
to be the negative Laplace operator. Our main result (Theorem
3.1
below), however, remains valid for any
A
from (
1.2
). The strong solution
u
to the
random
PDE (
1.6
) then satisfies the integral identity

\displaystyle u(t_{j+1})-u(t_{j})-\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}\Delta u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}\Delta[\Phi W(s)]\,\mathrm{d}s.

(3.5)

To exploit the improved time-regularity (
3.4
) of
u_{t}
, we use the Crank-Nicolson quadrature rule for the integral
\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}\Delta u(s)\,{\rm d}s
in (
1.6
) since its integrand is
Lipschitz in time, and a Riemann sum for the non-Lipschitz integrand in

\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}\Delta[\Phi W(s)]\,{\rm d}s
on a finer mesh of size
\tau^{2}
to partition intervals
[t_{j},t_{j+1}]
. We proceed as follows:

1.

Approximation of the deterministic integral:

by using the trapezoidal rule, the term

\mathcal{Q}_{j}^{\Delta}:=\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}\Delta u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

is approximated by
\frac{\tau}{2}\Delta\bigl[u(t_{j+1})+u(t_{j})\bigr]=:\mathcal{I}_{j}^{\Delta}.

2.

Approximation of the integral with stochastic integrand:

on each interval
[t_{j},t_{j+1}]
, we introduce a finer uniform mesh of step size
\tau^{2}
, with nodes

t_{j,\ell}=t_{j}+\ell\tau^{2},\quad\ell=0,\dots,\mathrm{M},

where
\mathrm{M}=\tau^{-1}
. By using a Riemann sum on a finer mesh, the integral

\mathcal{Q}_{j}^{W}:=\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}W(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

is then approximated by

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathrm{M}}\tau^{2}W(t_{j,\ell})=:\mathcal{I}_{j}^{W}.
This idea is inspired from
[
8
]
.

We remark that to apply the trapezoidal rule to
\mathcal{Q}_{j}^{W}

Y_{j+1}-Y_{j}&=\dfrac{\tau}{2}\,\Delta\,\big[X_{j+1}+X_{j}\big]\;+\;\Phi\Delta_{j+1}W\;+\;\mathfrak{E}_{2,j}^{\Delta}(W).\end{cases}

9:

end

for

10:

Output:

\{(X_{j},Y_{j});\,0\leq j\leq N\}
.

Note that the correction terms
\mathfrak{E}_{1,j}(W)
and
\mathfrak{E}_{2,j}^{\Delta}(W)
in the
modified Crank–Nicolson
Scheme
4.1
play a key role in improving the overall convergence rate. The following theorem settles
{\mathcal{O}}(\tau^{2})
convergence for the
modified Crank–Nicolson
Scheme (
4.1
).

Theorem 4.1

(second main result)
.

Let
X_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{4}(D)\cap\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)
,
X_{1}\in\mathbb{H}^{3}(D)\cap\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D),\Phi\in\bigl(\mathbb{H}^{4}(D)\cap\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)\bigr)^{m}
, with compatibility conditions
AX_{0},AX_{1}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)
, and
A\Phi\in\bigl(\mathbb{H}_{0}^{1}(D)\bigr)^{m}
hold. Let
\{(X_{j},Y_{j})\}_{j=0}^{N}
be generated by the modified Crank-Nicolson Scheme
4.1
and
X
solves SPDE (
1.11
) with
A=-\Delta
. Then there exists a constant
C=C(T,X_{0},X_{1},\Phi,m)>0
, independent of
\tau
, such that

\max_{0\leq j\leq N}\Big\{\mathbb{E}\big[\|X(t_{j})-X_{j}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{1}_{0}(D)}^{2}\big]^{1/2}\;+\;\mathbb{E}\big[\|X_{t}(t_{j})-Y_{j}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\big]^{1/2}\Big\}\leq C\,\tau^{2}.

(4.6)

Proof.

We prove this result with the help of the modified Crank–Nicolson scheme (
4.4
) applied to the
random
PDE (
1.13
). The proof proceeds through the following steps

Step 1. Error inequality:

We define the errors

e_{1,j}=u(t_{j})-U_{j},\qquad e_{2,j}=u_{t}(t_{j})-V_{j}.

Therefore from (
4.2
) and (
4.4
) we get the following discrete error system

\displaystyle e_{1,j+1}-e_{1,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau}{2}\bigl[e_{2,j+1}+e_{2,j}\bigr]+\tau\mathcal{Q}^{u_{t}}_{j+1/2},

(4.7a)

\displaystyle{e_{2,j+1}-e_{2,j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau}{2}\Delta\bigl[e_{1,j+1}+e_{1,j}\bigr]+\tau\Delta\mathcal{Q}^{u}_{j+1/2}+\Delta\bigl[\Phi(\mathcal{Q}_{j+1}^{W}-\mathcal{I}_{j+1}^{W})\bigr],

(4.7b)

where

\mathcal{Q}^{u}_{j+1/2}=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}u(s)\,{\rm d}s-\frac{1}{2}\bigl[u(t_{j})+u(t_{j+1})\bigr],

\mathcal{Q}^{u_{t}}_{j+1/2}=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}u_{t}(s)\,{\rm d}s-\frac{1}{2}\bigl[u_{t}(t_{j})+u_{t}(t_{j+1})\bigr].

By taking the
\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)
-inner product of (
4.7a
) with
-\Delta e_{1,j+1/2},
where
e_{1,j+1/2}:=\frac{1}{2}{\bigl[e_{1,j+1}+e_{1,j}\bigr]}
and (
4.7b
) with
e_{2,j+1/2}:=\frac{1}{2}\bigl[e_{2,j+1}+e_{2,j}\bigr]
, then we add the both identities to get the error identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\|\nabla e_{1,j}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}-\|\nabla e_{1,j}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\|e_{2,j}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}-\|e_{2,j}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle={\tau}\left<\nabla\mathcal{Q}_{j+1/2}^{u_{t}},\nabla e_{1,j+1/2}\right>+\tau\left<\Delta\mathcal{Q}_{j+1/2}^{u},e_{2,j+1/2}\right>-\tau\left<\frac{1}{\tau}\nabla\bigl[\Phi\bigl(\mathcal{Q}_{j+1/2}^{W}-\mathcal{I}_{j+1}^{W}\bigr)\bigr],\nabla e_{2,j+1/2}\right>.

For any
1\leq J\leq N
, by applying Young’s inequality, taking expectation, and summing over all steps from
0
to
J-1
, we get the error inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\nabla e_{1,J}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|e_{2,J}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\nabla e_{1,0}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|e_{2,0}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle\qquad+C\,\tau\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\nabla e_{1,j}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|e_{2,j}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr]\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad+\tau\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\Delta\mathcal{Q}_{j+1/2}^{u}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr]+\tau\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\nabla\mathcal{Q}_{j+1/2}^{u_{t}}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl\|\nabla\bigl[\Phi\bigl(\mathcal{Q}_{j+1}^{W}-\mathcal{I}_{j+1}^{W}\bigr)\bigr]\bigr\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr].

(4.8)

Step 2. Trapezoidal rule truncation error:
We apply Lemma
2.1
for
f=u,a=t_{j},\kappa=\tau,\mathrm{B}=\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{H}^{2}(D))
and
\gamma=1
to get

\lVert\mathcal{Q}^{u}_{j+1/2}\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{H}^{2}(D))}\leq\widetilde{C}\tau^{2},

where
\widetilde{C}=\frac{4}{35}\|u\|_{C^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{H}^{2}(D)))}
which is finite due the estimate (
4.1
). Finally, we get

\displaystyle\tau\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lVert\Delta\mathcal{Q}^{u}_{j+1/2}\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr]\leq C\tau^{4},

(4.9)

where the constant depends on the initial data
X_{0}
and the noise coefficients
\Phi
; see (
4.1
). Again we apply Lemma
2.1
for
f=u_{t},a=t_{j},\kappa=\tau,\mathrm{B}=\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{H}^{1}(D))
and
\gamma=1
to get

\lVert\mathcal{Q}^{u_{t}}_{j+1/2}\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{H}^{1}(D))}\leq\widetilde{C}\tau^{2},

where
\widetilde{C}=\frac{4}{35}\|u_{t}\|_{C^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{H}^{1}(D))})
which is finite due the estimate (
4.1
). Finally, we get

\displaystyle\tau\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lVert\nabla\mathcal{Q}^{u}_{j+1/2}\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr]\leq C\tau^{4},

(4.10)

where the constant depends on the initial data
X_{0}
and the noise coefficients
\Phi
; see (
4.1
).

Step 3. Micro-step error:

In this step, we prove the following estimate

\sum_{j=0}^{J}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\mathcal{Q}_{j+1}^{W}-\mathcal{I}_{j+1}^{W}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}^{2}\bigr]\leq C\tau^{5}.

(4.11)

For convenience we define

\mathcal{J}_{j}^{W}:=\mathcal{Q}_{j+1}^{W}-\mathcal{I}_{j+1}^{W}=\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{old}}+\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{local}},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{old}}

\displaystyle:=\tau\int_{0}^{t_{j}}W(s)\,{\rm d}s\;-\;\tau\sum_{m=1}^{j-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathrm{M}}\tau^{2}\,W(t_{m,\ell})=\tau\sum_{m=1}^{j-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathrm{M}}\int_{t_{m-1,\ell}}^{t_{m,\ell}}\bigl(W(s)-W(t_{m,\ell})\bigr)\,{\rm d}s,

(4.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{local}}

\displaystyle:=\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}(t_{j+1}-s)\,W(s)\,{\rm d}s-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathrm{M}}(t_{j+1}-t_{j,\ell})\,\tau^{2}\,W(t_{j,\ell})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathrm{M}}\Bigg(\int_{t_{j,\ell-1}}^{t_{j,\ell}}(t_{j+1}-s)\,W(s)\,{\rm d}s-(t_{j+1}-t_{j,\ell})\,W(t_{j,\ell})\Bigg).

(4.13)

For further simplification of the term
\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{local}}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathrm{M}}\mathcal{J}_{j,\ell}^{\mathrm{local}}
, we split
W(s)=W(t_{j,\ell})+(W(s)-W(t_{j,\ell}))
inside the integral to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{j,\ell}^{\mathrm{local}}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{j,\ell-1}}^{t_{j,\ell}}(t_{j+1}-s)\bigl(W(s)-W(t_{j,\ell})\bigr)\,{\rm d}s+W(t_{j,\ell})\int_{t_{j,\ell-1}}^{t_{j,\ell}}\bigl((t_{j+1}-s)-(t_{j+1}-t_{j,\ell})\bigr)\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{j,\ell-1}}^{t_{j,\ell}}(t_{j+1}-s)\bigl(W(s)-W(t_{j,\ell})\bigr)\,{\rm d}s+W(t_{j,\ell})\int_{t_{j,\ell-1}}^{t_{j,\ell}}(t_{j,\ell}-s)\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{j,\ell-1}}^{t_{j,\ell}}(t_{j+1}-s)\bigl(W(s)-W(t_{j,\ell})\bigr)\,{\rm d}s+\frac{\tau^{4}}{2}\,W(t_{j,\ell}).

Then after taking sum over
\ell
from
0
to
\mathrm{M}
, it gives

\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{local}}:=\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{curr}}+\mathcal{S}^{W}_{j},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{curr}}:

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathrm{M}}\int_{t_{j,\ell-1}}^{t_{j,\ell}}(t_{j+1}-s)\bigl(W(s)-W(t_{j,\ell})\bigr)\,{\rm d}s,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{W}_{j}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathrm{M}}\frac{\tau^{4}}{2}\,W(t_{j,\ell}).

Hence the exact decomposition is

\mathcal{J}_{j}^{W}=\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{old}}+\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{curr}}+\mathcal{S}_{j}^{W}.

(4.14)

Step 3.1. Estimate of
\mathcal{S}_{j}^{W}
:

We now estimate the discrete term
\mathcal{S}_{j}^{W}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\mathcal{S}^{W}_{j}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau^{8}}{4}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Bigl\|\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathrm{M}}W(t_{j,\ell})\Bigr\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}^{2}\bigg]=\frac{\tau^{8}}{4}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}=1}^{\mathrm{M}}\mathbb{E}\bigl\langle W(t_{j,\ell}),W(t_{j,\ell^{\prime}})\bigr\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{m\tau^{8}}{4}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}=1}^{\mathrm{M}}\min\{t_{j,\ell},t_{j,\ell^{\prime}}\}.

Since
t_{j,p},t_{j,\ell^{\prime}}\in[t_{j},t_{j+1}]
and
\min\{t_{j,\ell},t_{j,\ell^{\prime}}\}\leq t_{j+1}\leq T
, we obtain

\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}=1}^{\mathrm{M}}\min\{t_{j,\ell},t_{j,\ell^{\prime}}\}\leq\mathrm{M}^{2}\,T.

By using
\mathrm{M}=\tau^{-1}
, this gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\mathcal{S}^{W}_{j}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}^{2}\bigr]\leq m\tau^{8}\tau^{-2}T=mT\tau^{6}.

(4.15)

Step 3.2. Estimate of
\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{old}}
:

We fix one micro interval
[t_{m-1,\ell},t_{m,\ell}]=[a,b]
of length
\tau^{2}
and set

I_{m}:=\int_{a}^{b}\bigl(W(s)-W({b})\bigr)\,{\rm d}s.

For
s,r\in[a,b]
, the covariance identity gives

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl\langle W(s)-W(b),\,W(r)-W(b)\bigr\rangle\bigr]=\bigl(b-\max\{s,r\}\bigr)\,{I}.

Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|I_{m}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=m\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{b}\bigl(b-\max\{s,r\}\bigr)\,{\rm d}s\,{\rm d}r=m\int_{0}^{\tau^{2}}\int_{0}^{\tau^{2}}\min\{x,y\}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y\leq\frac{\tau^{6}m}{3},

where in the second line we used the change of variables
x=b-s,\;y=b-r
. By summing over the disjoint micro intervals before
t_{j}
and by independence of Wiener increments on micro intervals, we yield

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{old}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}^{2}\bigr]=\tau^{2}\sum_{m=1}^{j-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathrm{M}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|I_{m,\ell}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}^{2}\bigr]\leq\frac{(t_{j}/\tau)m\,\mathrm{M},\tau^{8}}{3}=\frac{t_{j}m\tau^{5}}{3}\leq\frac{Tm\tau^{5}}{3},

where the facts
j-1\leq{t_{j}}/\tau
,
t_{j}\leq T
and
\mathrm{M}=\tau^{-1}
are used.
Finally we obtain

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{old}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}^{2}\bigr]\leq C\tau^{6}.

(4.16)

Step 3.3. Estimate of
\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{curr}}
:
For a micro interval
[t_{j,\ell-1},t_{j,\ell}]\subset[t_{j},t_{j+1}]
, we define

I_{j,\ell}:=\int_{t_{j,\ell-1}}^{t_{j,\ell}}(t_{j+1}-s)\bigl(W(s)-W(t_{j,\ell})\bigr)\,{\rm d}s.

Then, similarly as previous in the sub-step, we compute

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|I_{j,\ell}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}^{2}\bigr]=m\int_{t_{j,\ell-1}}^{t_{j,\ell}}\int_{t_{j,\ell-1}}^{t_{j,\ell}}(t_{j+1}-s)(t_{j+1}-r)\bigl(t_{j,\ell}-\max\{s,r\}\bigr)\,{\rm d}s\,{\rm d}r.

For
s,r\in[t_{j,\ell-1},t_{j,\ell}]
we have
0\leq t_{j+1}-s\leq\tau
and
0\leq t_{j,\ell}-\max\{s,r\}\leq\tau^{2}
. Therefore we get

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|I_{j,\ell}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}^{2}\bigr]\leq m\tau^{4}\int_{t_{j,\ell-1}}^{t_{j,\ell}}\int_{t_{j,\ell-1}}^{t_{j,\ell}}\,{\rm d}s\,{\rm d}r=m\tau^{8}.

Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{curr}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathrm{M}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|I_{j,\ell}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}^{2}\bigr]\leq m\mathrm{M}\;\tau^{7}.

By using
\mathrm{M}=\tau^{-1}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{curr}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}^{2}\bigr]\leq m\tau^{6}.

(4.17)

Finally, we combine (
4.15
)– (
4.17
) to get (
4.11
).

Step 4. Rate of convergence for
random
PDE (
1.13
):
By making use of the estimates (
4.9
)-(
4.11
) and
e_{1,0}=e_{2,0}=0
, from the energy inequality (
4.1
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\nabla e_{1,J}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|e_{2,J}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr]\leq\tau\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\nabla e_{1,j}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|e_{2,j}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr]\bigr)+C\tau^{4}.

(4.18)

We now apply discrete Gronwall’s inequality to get

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq j\leq N}\Bigg\{\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla\big(u(t_{j})-U_{j}\big)\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\big]^{1/2}\;+\;\mathbb{E}\big[\|u_{t}(t_{j})-V_{j}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\big]^{1/2}\Bigg\}\leq C\,\tau^{2}.

(4.19)

Step 5. Rate of convergence for SPDE (
1.11
):

To get the estimate (
4.6
), we use the transformations (
1.12
), (
4.5
), and identity (
4.12
) to conclude that

\displaystyle X(t_{j})-X_{j}

\displaystyle=u(t_{j})-U_{j}+\int_{0}^{t_{j}}\Phi W(s)\,{\rm d}s-\sum_{m=1}^{j-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathrm{M}}\tau^{2}\Phi W(t_{m,\ell})=u(t_{j})-U_{j}+\frac{1}{\tau}\Phi\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{old}},

(4.20)

\displaystyle X_{t}(t_{j})-Y_{j}

\displaystyle=u_{t}(t_{j})-V_{j}.

(4.21)

It implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\nabla\bigl(X(t_{j})-X_{j}\bigr)\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr]\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla\big(u(t_{j})-U_{j}\big)\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\big]+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\mathcal{J}_{j}^{\mathrm{old}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}^{2}\bigr]\|\nabla\Phi\|_{(\mathbb{L}^{2}(D))^{m}}^{2}.

By using the estimates (
4.16
) and (
4.19
), we conclude that there exists
C>0
, independent of
j
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\nabla\bigl(X(t_{j})-X_{j}\bigr)\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(D)}^{2}\bigr]\leq C\tau^{4}.

(4.22)

Finally we combine (
4.19
)–(
4.22
) to get the convergence rate estimate (
4.6
) of this theorem.
