Predicting Clinical Improvement in Chronic Low Back Pain Using Exercise‐Induced IVIM Perfusion: Threshold Evaluation and Disability‐Dependent Effects
Authors: Frederique Dupuis, David B Berry, Lara Havandjian, Armin Zavareh, Zachary Brumm, Anirudh Anand, Samuel R Ward, Kamshad Raiszadeh, Bahar Shahidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChronic low back pain (LBP) is the leading cause of disability worldwide, yet responses to exercise‐based rehabilitation vary substantially and remain not well characterized. Impaired activation of the lumbar paraspinal muscles has been proposed as a mechanism limiting rehabilitation treatment response. Intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) magnetic resonance imaging assesses muscle diffusion and perfusion after exercise, offering physiological markers of dynamic function. In a previous pilot study, we reported that acute exercise‐induced IVIM responses were reduced in individuals with LBP and that thresholds in diffusion (D) and pseudodiffusion (D*) parameters could predict those who would or would not respond to an exercise‐based rehabilitation program. The present study aimed to prospectively validate these findings in a larger, independent cohort. Thirty‐seven individuals with chronic LBP underwent IVIM before and after an acute standardized lumbar extension exercise, followed by a 12‐week rehabilitation program. Participants were classified as responders or nonresponders based on changes in disability after 12 weeks (Δ Oswestry Disability Index Score ≥ or < 0). In line with the pilot study, responders exhibited higher increase in D following acute exercise than nonresponders (p = 0.05). Previously identified thresholds for D and D* did not discriminate between responders and nonresponders within this new cohort (Sn < 0.40 and Sp = 0.65). Exploratory analyses revealed that baseline disability moderated IVIM responses, as a marked increase in D was primarily observed in responders with low baseline disability, whereas individuals with higher disability showed attenuated responses. New significant thresholds were explored within the validation cohort while controlling for disability levels. An increase ≥ 0.014 × 10−3 mm2/s for D predicted who would respond to the exercise‐based program, with a Sn of 0.59 and a Sp of 0.90 (area under the curve [AUC] 0.72, 95%CI 0.55 to 0.90, p = 0.02). These findings confirm that acute IVIM responses are associated with rehabilitation outcomes but may be better understood as part of a broader, integrative framework characterizing the physiological mechanisms associated with treatment response. Integrating physiological and clinical factors to better understand variability in treatment response will help guide more personalized approaches to LBP rehabilitation.Keywords: intravoxel incoherent motion, low back pain, magnetic resonance imaging, paraspinal muscles, perfusion, resistance exercise

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is the most common musculoskeletal disorder and the leading cause of disability worldwide [1]. LBP is a complex condition, impacted by a range of physiological and psychological factors [2]. From a physiological standpoint, chronic LBP is associated with structural and functional alterations of the lumbar paraspinal muscles, including compositional changes, atrophy, inflammation, and reduced vascularity [2, 3]. Growing evidence suggests that paraspinal muscle‐specific physiologic mechanisms may contribute to symptom persistence [4, 5, 6], by limiting the muscles' capacity to adapt to exercise‐based interventions [7, 8]. Consistent with the importance of muscular adaptation, clinical improvement has been associated with increases in paraspinal muscle volume and reduction in intramuscular fat infiltration among individuals with chronic LBP undergoing exercise‐based rehabilitation [4, 9, 10, 11], although such morphological changes are not consistently observed following treatment. The mechanisms underlying impaired muscular adaptation to exercise in chronic LBP remain poorly understood but may involve altered muscle activation during exercise [12, 13].

Intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) magnetic resonance imaging provides a noninvasive approach to quantify exercise‐induced changes in muscle perfusion [14, 15]. IVIM yields three tissue‐specific biomarkers: the diffusion coefficient (D), reflecting extravascular molecular diffusion; the pseudodiffusion coefficient (D*), related to capillary blood flow; and the relative perfusion fraction (f). These parameters are sensitive to exercise intensity in the lumbar muscles [16] and may serve as spatially sensitive proxies of muscle activation [16, 17, 18, 19]. Importantly, perfusion assessment is particularly relevant in chronic LBP, as vascular permeability, fluid content, and capillary densities are altered in chronically inflamed muscles [3, 20]. Because IVIM is sensitive to both intra‐ and extravascular blood motion, it provides insight into microvascular and fluid‐related alterations that may constrain the muscles' adaptive response to exercise training [3].

Previously, we demonstrated that individuals with chronic LBP exhibited attenuated increases in IVIM response to acute exercise compared with healthy controls within the lumbar paraspinal muscles, specifically the multifidus and erector spinae, following an acute bout of lumbar extension exercise [21]. Furthermore, acute exercise‐induced IVIM metrics were associated with reductions in disability after a 12‐week exercise‐based rehabilitation program in the chronic LBP group. Threshold values for the D and D* parameters were identified as candidate values based on ROC analyses within the pilot cohort, with a sensitivity of 1.00 and a specificity of 0.87 for distinguishing responders from nonresponders to the rehabilitation program. These perfusion‐related deficits in lumbar muscles suggest activation deficit, which may limit their capacity for training‐induced adaptation, thereby constraining clinical improvement. However, given the small sample size of the first cohort (Cohort 1), prospective validation in a larger cohort is required.

The objective of this study was to prospectively validate the IVIM‐derived thresholds for predicting response to an exercise‐based rehabilitation program in a new, larger cohort of individuals with chronic LBP (Cohort 2). We hypothesized that the diffusion (D) and pseudodiffusion (D*) parameters would discriminate between people who will exhibit functional improvement following the 12‐week exercise program (i.e., responders) and those who would not (i.e., nonresponders): the nonresponders will exhibit attenuated or absent increases in D and D* following an acute bout of lumbar extension exercise.

Methods

Cohort 2 participants were recruited from a local spine rehabilitation clinic as they were prescribed a rehabilitation program for their LBP by their healthcare provider. Participants were consecutively invited to participate in the study between April 2021 and February 2025. They were eligible if they (1) were between 18 and 85 years of age, (2) reported LBP for longer than 3 months, (3) had no contraindications to exercise, and (4) no contraindications to magnetic resonance (MR) imaging. Individuals were excluded if they did not complete their initial rehabilitation evaluation, had prior back surgery, or if they were receiving treatment for multiple body regions. The protocol of this study was approved by the University of California San Diego Review Board (IRB #201425). All participants provided written consent prior to their participation in the study, in accordance with the standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants completed a single assessment session prior to the start of their 12‐week rehabilitation program, where they completed (i) self‐administered questionnaires, (ii) MR imaging at rest, (iii) lumbar resistance extension exercise outside the scanner, and (iv) a second MR imaging immediately following exercise. After this evaluation session, they completed a 12‐week exercise‐based rehabilitation program, and self‐administered questionnaires were completed again at 12‐week follow‐up (Figure 1). Data from Cohort 1 were extracted from previously published data [21].

Study design.

Imaging Assessment

MR Imaging was performed at 3.0 T with a CTL phased‐array spine coil (GE Discovery MR750, GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI, USA). Subjects were lying supine, positioned head‐first, and acquisition was centered at the level of L3/L4.

IVIM Acquisition and Processing

The imaging acquisition parameters for IVIM were replicated from Cohort 1 to ensure comparability [21]. All imaging was performed at 3.0 T (GE MR 750, GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI, USA) with a CTL phased‐array spine coil for signal reception. Subjects were positioned head‐first, supine, and centered at the level of L3/L4. IVIM data were acquired with an axial, multislice, 2D diffusion‐weighted spin‐echo EPI sequence using the following parameters: FOV = 256 × 256 mm2; slice thickness = 8 mm; 22 slices covering the entire lumbar spine from L1–S1; acquisition matrix = 128 × 128; TR/TE = 2295/52.5 ms; flip angle = 90°; spatial‐spectral fat saturation; averages = 4; and three directions of diffusion encoding with b‐values of 0, 10, 20, 40, 70, 110, 160, 220, 300, 400, 500, 600, and 700 s/mm2. The total acquisition time was 347 s. Diffusion‐weighted images were preprocessed with phase and distortion correction [22, 23]. They were then denoised using a principal component analysis filter [24], spatially smoothed with a 2D median filter with a three‐voxel radius and then averaged over the three diffusion‐encoding directions and the four temporal repeats for each b‐value. IVIM coefficients D, f, and D* were solved using a three‐step constrained fitting approach from the slice‐wise IVIM data: S(b)/S0 = (1‐f) e‐bD + fe‐bD*, where S(b)/S0 is the measured signal at each b‐value, normalized by the nondiffusion‐weighted acquisition, D the molecular diffusion coefficient, D* the pseudodiffusion coefficient, and f the perfusion fraction representing the fractional volume of blood in each voxel. The approach to solve IVIM parameters on a voxel‐by‐voxel basis has been previously reported [21, 25]. The three‐step fitting procedure was selected a priori to reproduce the analysis pipeline used in the original threshold‐derivation study, thereby permitting direct prospective evaluation of those thresholds in an independent cohort. T1‐weighted images were acquired with the same field of view, slice thickness, and orientation as IVIM data. Regions of interest (ROI) were defined for the bilateral erector spinae and multifidus muscles combined [26] using Horos software [27] (Purview, Annapolis, MD) and then applied to the IVIM parameter maps. Data were averaged across the combined muscles from L1 to S1. Signal to noise ratio (SNR) was estimated from magnitude images by placing a ROI within the target muscle and a second ROI in background air outside the subject. SNR was calculated as

SNR exceeded 30 in every participant. Across the cohort, the median SNR was 195, the mean was 284, and the range was 32–902. Given the known dependence of IVIM parameter bias and dispersion on SNR, these values were interpreted in the context of current IVIM consensus recommendations [28].

Acute Bout of Exercise

IVIM data were acquired before (at rest) and after exercising. The lumbar exercise protocol performed between the scans consisted of repeated resisted lumbar extension movement, performed on an exercise machine outside the scanner (Nautilus Evo, Nautilus Inc.). A load corresponding to 60% of their 1‐repetition maximum was used, which was determined during participants' initial physical therapy evaluation using a MedX isokinetic dynamometer (MedX Holdings Inc.). Participants performed the exercise self‐paced, within their comfortable range of motion. They were instructed to continue until they reached a perceived level of exertion of 6‐7/10 on the Borg Rating Scale (corresponding to high intensity exercise), for approximately 3 min. Exercise duration, resistance used (% of body weight), perceived level of exertion, and time to scanner were recorded. Time to scanner corresponds to the delay between the end of the acute bout of exercise and start of postexercise IVIM data acquisition.

Muscle Volume and Fat Fraction

To assess muscle size and quality, an Iterative decomposition of water and fat with Echo Asymmetry and Least squares estimation (IDEAL) MRI pulse sequence was acquired as previously reported with a voxel dimension of 1 × 1 × 1mm321. Regions of interest of the erector spinae and multifidus muscles were defined separately from L1 to S1 using a previously published approach [29], using Horos [27]. All ROIs were imported into MATLAB software program (Mathworks, Natick, MA), and cross‐sectional area (CSA) and fat fraction (FF) were measured using an in‐house algorithm. To measure fat fraction, two sets of images were acquired including a water only image (Sw) and a fat only image (Sf) [29]. Independent signal contributions from fat and water signals for each voxel were isolated using the following formula: fat fraction signal = Sf/Sf + Sw. CSA of the left and right muscles was summed, and FF was averaged between sides.

Self‐Reported Questionnaires

At baseline, all participants completed self‐administered questionnaires assessing sociodemographic characteristics (i.e., age, gender, height, and weight), symptom duration, and LBP‐related disability. LBP‐related disability was measured using the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), which was completed again after the 12‐week rehabilitation program.

Rehabilitation Program

Consistent with Cohort 1, all participants completed a 12‐week rehabilitation program, consisting of 1 to 3 session(s)/week supervised by a physical therapist. The program included machine‐based resistance exercises targeting the lumbar extension muscles in addition to education and activity modification. A detailed description of this program has been previously reported [4].

Statistical Analysis

After the completion of the 12‐week program, participants were classified either as responders based on positive or negative changes in disability (ΔODI = PostODI—BaselineODI), consistent with the previous study [21]; responders were classified as ΔODI < 0 and nonresponders as ΔODI ≥ 0. Sociodemographic and acute exercise‐related characteristics were compared between the two groups using independent t‐tests. A two‐way repeated measures ANOVA was used to determine the effect of Time (pre‐ vs. postexercise), Group (responders vs. nonresponders) and the interaction between Time × Group on IVIM parameters (D, D*, and f). In Cohort 1, significant thresholds for D and D* were previously identified: an increase of D ≥ 0.055 × 10−3 mm2/s and an increase of D* ≥ 6.94 × 10−3 mm2/s were identified as candidate thresholds for a positive response to the exercise‐based program [21]. Sensitivity (Sn) and Specificity (Sp) of these thresholds were assessed in Cohort 2 using a 2 × 2 contingency table, and the AUC was calculated.

Exploratory Analysis

After the evaluation of the predictive capacity of the thresholds, sociodemographic, and exercise‐related characteristics were compared between Cohort 1 and Cohort 2 using independent t‐tests. Based on characteristics that differ between cohorts, simple correlation analyses were performed to examine associations between IVIM responses (Δ) and these characteristics to assess whether they may explain variation in IVIM responses in Cohort 2. Variables that were identified as potential moderators were further explored using sensitivity analysis and where a significant moderation was observed, a receiver operating characteristic curve was performed to define new optimal thresholds.

Results

Thirty‐seven participants were included in this study (Cohort 2): n = 20 were classified as responders (mean ΔODI: −9.9 ± 8 points), and n = 17 were classified as nonresponders (mean ΔODI: 4.7 ± 4.3 points). Individuals were on average in their fifth decade of life, overweight, and experienced minimal to moderate disability (Table 1). There were no between‐group differences between any of the exercise‐related parameters or the mean number of physical therapy sessions (Table 1). A significant difference in baseline disability level was identified between the groups, with responders presenting a higher ODI score at baseline. ODI score was thus used as a covariate in the subsequent between‐groups analysis.

Predictive Capacity of the Previously Identified Thresholds

The previously identified thresholds [21] from Cohort 1 did not retain discriminative capacity when applied to Cohort 2, with a Sn of 0.35 (95%CI 0.18 to 0.57) and Sp of 0.65 (95%CI 0.41 to 0.83) for D, and Sn 0.40 (95%CI 0.22 to 0.61) and Sp 0.65 (95%CI 0.41 to 0.83) for D*. The AUC was 0.50 (95%CI 0.34–0.65) and 0.52 (95%CI 0.37–0.68) for D and D*, respectively.

Exploring New Thresholds

Finally, new optimal thresholds were explored within this Cohort while controlling disability levels. Binary logistic regression models were performed with Group (responder vs. nonresponder) as the dependent variable, IVIM parameters (ΔD, ΔD*, or Δf) as predictors, and ODI score as a covariate. Predicted probabilities from these models were then used to construct receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The optimal probability cutoff was determined using the highest Youden's index, and this probability threshold was subsequently converted into the corresponding IVIM parameter value.

Significant thresholds were identified for all three parameters within Cohort 2. An increase ≥ 0.014 × 10−3 mm2/s for D could adequately predict who would respond to an exercise‐based program, with a poor Sn of 0.59 and an excellent Sp of 0.90 (AUC 0.72, 95%CI 0.55 to 0.90, p = 0.02) (Figure 4a). An increase ≥ 0.6 for f showed a good Sn and a poor Sp of 0.82 and 0.60, respectively (AUC 0.71, 95%CI 0.54 to 0.88, p = 0.02) (Figure 4b). Finally, an increase of 0.853 × 10−3 mm2/s for D* showed a good Sn of 0.88 and a poor Sp of 0.50 (AUC 0.68, 95%CI 0.50 to 0.86, p = 0.05) (Figure 4c).

Discussion

This study aimed to prospectively validate previously defined IVIM‐derived thresholds for predicting response to an exercise‐based rehabilitation program in individuals with chronic LBP. Our hypothesis was not verified, as the previously identified thresholds for D and D* yielded no predictive capacity in this new and larger cohort of participants. However, consistent with our previous findings [21], we observed a significant between‐groups difference for D, with only the responders exhibiting a significant increase following the acute bout of lumbar exercise.

Our exploratory analyses identified several exercise‐related differences between cohorts that may account for variability in the absolute IVIM values. Compared with Cohort 1 [21], participants in Cohort 2 reported lower mean perceived exertion and exercised against lower resistance levels. These differences may reflect a reduced overall muscle workload. IVIM parameters are sensitive to exercise intensity [16], which might have impacted the measured parameters. In addition, the interval between exercise cessation and the postexercise MRI acquisition was longer in Cohort 2, by a little less than 1.5 min. Although the temporal evolution of IVIM signals in skeletal muscle following exercise is not fully characterized, some evidence suggests a rapid decline during the initial postexercise period. For example, Mastropietro et al. reported that all IVIM parameters returned to resting values within 3 min following plantar‐flexion exercise in the calf muscles [30]. However, Adelnia et al. demonstrated that coefficient D in the quadriceps remained significantly elevated for at least 20 min after active knee‐extension exercise [31]. It should however be noted that Mastropietro et al. used isometric intermittent exercise, which is associated with a lower blood flow response compared with the isotonic concentric/eccentric exercise used in the present study. Our preliminary pilot testing showed that the signal in the lumbar muscles persisted for up to 15 min following the lumbar extension exercise. Postexercise temporal response of IVIM parameters may depend on both the muscle group examined and the nature of the exercise performed [14]. Despite these between‐cohort differences in exercise‐related characteristics, no significant associations were observed between these factors and IVIM measurements, suggesting that their impact on IVIM variability was limited.

A novel finding of the present study is the moderation effect of baseline disability on molecular diffusion response (D). Specifically, ΔD values were reduced in individuals with higher levels of disability. This finding is particularly noteworthy, as the significant difference observed between responders and nonresponders appears to be driven by a subgroup of responders with low baseline disability. This pattern should not be interpreted as ΔD being correlated to disability level only. Baseline ODI was included as a covariate in the between‐groups analysis, and the Time × Group interaction for D remained significant, indicating that responder status carries explanatory value beyond disability alone. Furthermore, nonresponders with low disability did not exhibit a significant increase in D. We did not observe differences in sociodemographic characteristics, exercise‐related variables, or muscle volume between subgroups that could otherwise explain the differential behavior of IVIM parameters. There was a tendency for low disability responders to have lower fat fraction in the erector spinae muscles compared to the other three subgroups. Muscles with higher levels of fatty infiltration have been shown to exhibit lower D values compared to normal healthy muscles, potentially because of the loss of water content and restricted diffusion [32]. Adjustment for fat fraction did not alter the Time × Group × Disability interaction. However, this covariate analysis does not exclude residual fat‐related IVIM estimation bias. Because the effects of residual fat depend on SNR and the underlying diffusion/perfusion, they may differ between pre‐exercise and postexercise measurements and therefore could contribute to the observed subgroup differences in ΔD [31].

Other metrics that were not measured may also explain that only responders with low disability showed significant increase in D, such as a difference in the total mechanical workload. Even if exercise‐related characteristics such as duration, RPE, and relative resistance did not differ, the range of motion and cadence were self‐determined but not measured. These metrics may impact the total workload on paraspinal muscles, which is likely to impact ΔD [16] as acute increase in molecular diffusion following exercise reflects myofiber swelling and tissue heating during muscle activation. Responders with lower disability may have been less limited by pain, symptoms, or even fear of movement during exercise, allowing them to sustain a greater workload during the 3‐min bout. However, this explanation does not account for why the subgroup of nonresponders with low disability failed to show a significant IVIM response, as these individuals presumably faced comparably limited constraints related to LBP‐related disability. Structural or physiological muscle alterations, such as fibrosis or reduced vascularity, may also contribute to the attenuated acute physiological response to exercise observed in nonresponders, independent of disability level [3, 7, 33, 34]. Muscles' IVIM response to exercise is multifactorial, and further work is needed to identify the underlying causes of altered IVIM response in individuals who do not respond to rehabilitation.

As to why IVIM response is associated with clinical improvement, individuals in the low‐disability subgroup who subsequently respond to rehabilitation may represent individuals in whom paraspinal muscle health or activation capacity, reflected by higher IVIM response and lower fat fraction, is sufficient to drive meaningful muscular adaptation through structured exercise‐based rehabilitation. Among high‐disability responders, clinical improvement despite an attenuated IVIM response suggests that treatment response may be mediated through mechanisms other than exercise‐driven muscle activation, such as patient education on health behaviors [35, 36] and pain‐related psychological factors [37, 38]. It is also possible that this subgroup was able to improve their IVIM muscle response to exercise through the 12‐week rehabilitation program, but this cannot be verified without a postintervention IVIM assessment. In contrast, both nonresponder subgroups, regardless of disability level, presented with an attenuated IVIM response, suggesting that impaired muscle health or activation capacity may limit recovery potential. However, it is more likely that this alone does not fully explain lack of treatment response, as additional factors such as central sensitization, fear‐avoidance behaviors, or reduced motivation to engage with exercise may further limit rehabilitation outcomes [39, 40, 41].

Taken together, our present findings suggest that IVIM parameters retain association with clinical improvement, but baseline disability is a key factor to consider. Accordingly, rather than functioning as standalone predictive biomarker, IVIM parameters may be better understood as part of a broader, integrative framework aimed at characterizing the physiological mechanisms associated with treatment response. Low back pain is a complex condition [2], and treatment response is likely driven by multiple mechanisms. In particular, among responders presenting with moderate to high baseline disability, clinical improvement is likely to be mediated by mechanisms other than muscle adaptation, such as the effects of active rehabilitation and patient education on health behaviors and pain‐related psychological factors [42].

Limits and Future Directions

First, these findings add meaningful value by contributing to a growing body of evidence suggesting that baseline disability level influences both the IVIM response to acute exercise and the thresholds that best discriminate responders from nonresponders. However, the thresholds identified in Cohort 2 are exploratory in nature, derived retrospectively from the same cohort in which they were evaluated, and therefore reflect in‐sample model fit rather than true predictive performance. This approach is prone to optimistic estimation of sensitivity and specificity.

Second, it should be noted that both cohorts were acquired on the same scanner using identical acquisition parameters, minimizing the likelihood that between‐cohort differences in IVIM thresholds are attributable to scanner or postprocessing variability. Rather, the failure of Cohort 1 thresholds to generalize to Cohort 2 more likely reflects differences in baseline disability levels and exercise‐related characteristics between cohorts. Nonetheless, IVIM values remain sensitive to acquisition and postprocessing variations across sites, and the use of normalized or relative metrics may be important to consider when generalizing these findings to other acquisition contexts.

Third, the exercise stimulus was self‐paced and RPE‐targeted, and the amount of mechanical work performed during the exercise bout was not objectively quantified. Although no significant differences in exercise duration, resistance, or perceived exertion were found between responders and nonresponders, RPE is known to be influenced by factors such as pain, fatigue, and disability, and unmeasured differences in mechanical workload may have contributed to the observed differences in ΔD. Our central aim was to establish a link between the acute physiological response to a prescribed exercise and subsequent clinical improvement, in order to maintain direct clinical relevance for this approach. Nonetheless, the precise biomechanical, structural, or physiological reasons underlying differences in this acute response now warrant further investigation to guide targeted interventions capable of improving this response and, in turn, clinical outcomes. Future studies incorporating objective, quantitative workload metrics are needed to disentangle this contribution. Fourth, although spatial‐spectral fat saturation was applied, residual fat signal in the diffusion‐weighted images was not quantified. Simulations have demonstrated that IVIM estimation bias depends on the residual fat signal, SNR, and underlying diffusion/perfusion and thus may differ between pre‐exercise and postexercise [31]. Consequently, the contribution of residual‐fat‐related estimation bias cannot be distinguished from physiological differences in the present study. The contribution of the fat signal to diffusion MRI measurements is a common issue, and although techniques such as Dixon olefinic fat suppression have been employed for diffusion tensor imaging, these techniques have largely not been adapted to IVIM [43]. Fifth, although the median SNR was 195, SNR varied substantially across participants, with a range of 32–902. IVIM parameter estimates obtained at the lower end of this distribution may be subject to greater bias and dispersion, particularly for f and D*. The three‐step fitting pipeline was retained a priori to allow direct validation of thresholds derived using the same pipeline in the original cohort. Although this approach demonstrated low spatial and temporal variability in experimental lumbar‐muscle data, fitting accuracy remains dependent on SNR and the underlying IVIM parameters [28]. Consequently, limited SNR in a subset of measurements and fitting‐related uncertainty may have reduced sensitivity to between‐group differences.

Finally, responders and nonresponders were classified based on positive/neutral versus negative changes in participants' ODI scores, and the clinical interpretability of these changes is limited. Specifically, the threshold used (ΔODI < 0 for responders) was chosen for consistency with the previous pilot study and represents any positive directional change; however, it does not account for the minimal clinical important difference (MDC) in ODI. Although minimal clinically important difference thresholds are often used to enhance interpretability, their validity is increasingly questioned [44], as cutoffs vary substantially depending on calculation methods [45], and the magnitude of clinically meaningful improvement may differ according to sample characteristics [46, 47]. In this context, complementing functional outcomes with measures of participants perceived change may better capture the clinical relevance of improvement.

Conclusion

A significant difference in the change of molecular diffusion in the lumbar muscles in response to acute exercise was observed between responders and nonresponders to a rehabilitation program, confirming the notion that the immediate physiological response to exercise as measured by IVIM may reflect clinically relevant muscle adaptive potential. However, the previously identified thresholds for molecular diffusion (D) and capillary blood flow (D*) parameters yielded no predictive capacity in this new and larger cohort of participants. A novel finding was the moderating role of baseline disability on IVIM‐derived parameters, which suggests that IVIM parameters retain association with clinical improvement, but level of disability should be considered. Based on these results, IVIM parameters may hold clinical relevance in predicting response to exercise treatment when interpreted in combination with other clinical measures and may be best understood within a broader integrative framework aimed at characterizing the physiological mechanisms associated with treatment response rather than functioning as standalone predictive biomarkers.
