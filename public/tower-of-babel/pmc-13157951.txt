Actinomyces urogenitalis and Peptoniphilus asaccharolyticus brain abscess in an immunocompetent patient: A case report
Authors: Sergio Esteban Chacón, Maria Isabel Ocampo-Navia, Daniela Castaño, Manuel Vergara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Brain abscesses are serious, life-threatening infections that require prompt diagnosis and treatment. Although Actinomyces species are recognized causes of brain abscesses, infections caused by Actinomyces urogenitalis and Peptoniphilus asaccharolyticus are exceptionally rare and, to our knowledge, have not been previously reported.Case Description: We describe a 62-year-old man with a long history of chronic bilateral otitis media who presented with progressive vertigo, severe hemicranial headache, nausea, and vomiting. Within hours of admission, his neurological status worsened, leading to urgent neuroimaging that revealed a right temporal brain abscess associated with subfalcine herniation. The patient underwent emergent craniotomy with abscess drainage. Microbiological cultures identified A. urogenitalis and P. asaccharolyticus. He was treated with prolonged intravenous antibiotics followed by oral therapy, achieving complete clinical recovery and full radiological resolution of the lesion.Conclusion: This report documents the first known case of a brain abscess caused by A. urogenitalis and P. asaccharolyticus. The case emphasizes the importance of considering unusual pathogens in brain abscesses and highlights the value of early surgical management combined with targeted, long-term antimicrobial therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Brain abscesses are severe, life-threatening infections characterized by localized collections of pus within the brain parenchyma. They can arise from direct extension of nearby infections, hematogenous spread, or trauma. Chronic otitis media is a well-recognized risk factor, as middle ear infections can extend to the brain through bony erosions, leading to suppurative complications such as meningitis, subdural empyema, and brain abscesses. Despite advances in imaging and antimicrobial therapy, brain abscesses remain a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge due to their nonspecific clinical presentation and potentially devastating neurological consequences.

Actinomyces species, a group of facultative anaerobic Gram-positive bacteria, are rare but known causative agents of brain abscesses, typically linked to cervicofacial, pulmonary, or abdominal infections. Actinomyces urogenitalis, however, has not been previously implicated in intracranial infections. Similarly, Peptoniphilus asaccharolyticus, an anaerobic Gram-positive coccus commonly found in the human genitourinary and gastrointestinal tracts, has been associated with chronic wounds and soft tissue infections but has not been reported as a causative agent of brain abscesses.

Here, we present the first documented case of a brain abscess caused by A. urogenitalis and P. asaccharolyticus in a patient with chronic bilateral otitis media. This report highlights the importance of thorough microbiological identification in brain abscess cases, particularly in patients with a history of chronic ear infections, and underscores the need for timely surgical intervention and prolonged antimicrobial therapy.

CASE DESCRIPTION

Clinical presentation

A 62-year-old left-handed male, an ornament worker from Bogotá, presented with a progressive 1-week history of vertigo, rotational dizziness, nausea, vomiting, and severe hemicranial headache described as pinching in nature. He also reported asthenia, adynamia, and decreased appetite. Notably, he experienced a 6 kg weight loss and nocturnal diaphoresis during this period. There was no associated fever, chest pain, dyspnea, or history of seizures. Similar symptoms were reported 1 year prior, which improved with treatment. His past medical history included chronic bilateral otitis media, for which he had been treated with amoxicillin/clavulanate. There was no history of chronic illnesses, substance use, or immunosuppression. In the computed tomography (CT) scan of the neck, thorax, and abdomen, no abscesses or other pathological findings were observed.

On the initial neurological examination, the patient had a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 15, was alert, oriented, and without neurological deficits. However, in the following hours, he developed a decline in consciousness (GCS 13), somnolence, disorientation, weakness in the left hand, and signs of meningeal irritation. Empiric antibiotic treatment was initiated, and due to neurological deterioration, the patient underwent urgent surgical management.

Imaging findings

A noncontrast CT scan of the brain revealed a subcortical lesion in the right temporal lobe (45 × 36 × 38 mm) with surrounding vasogenic edema, subfalcine, and uncal herniation. Findings were suggestive of a brain abscess, likely secondary to otomastoiditis, evidenced by bony erosions in the tegmen tympani and mastoid [Figure 1].

In the postoperative contrast-enhanced brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), recent postsurgical changes were observed, indicating apparently complete drainage of the abscess in the right temporal lobe, with persistent vasogenic edema. There was smooth thickening and increased dural enhancement at the basal aspect of the right temporal region, adjacent to the petrous apex, consistent with the extension of the inflammatory process from the ipsilateral otomastoid region.

Treatment

The patient underwent cerebral abscess drainage through craniotomy with intraoperative findings of a cerebral abscess, and 25 cc of pus was drained. Cultures showed abundant growth of A. urogenitalis and P. asaccharolyticus. No specific antimicrobial susceptibility profile was available for these isolates, and antimicrobial therapy was guided by established susceptibility patterns reported in the literature and clinical response. Blood cultures obtained at admission and before the initiation of antimicrobial therapy were negative for bacterial growth. Despite the absence of bloodstream pathogens, broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics were started empirically due to the severity of the clinical presentation and radiological evidence of a large brain abscess with significant mass effect. Our patient was treated for 4 weeks with vancomycin+ceftriaxone+metronidazole, and a follow-up MRI showed almost complete obliteration of the residual cavity in the surgical bed compared to the previous study, with enhancement at its periphery, so an additional 4 weeks of ceftriaxone and metronidazole were prescribed. Subsequently, the patient completed 6 months of antibiotics from the day of drainage with doxycycline.

Follow-up and outcome

The patient completed the antibiotic treatment with good tolerance. There was satisfactory clinical progression, remaining asymptomatic with no neurological deficits. Paraclinical tests were within normal limits. Brain MRI with gadolinium showed postsurgical changes of drainage and capsule resection of a pyogenic abscess in the right temporal lobe, with obliteration of the postsurgical cavity and marked reduction of the residual vasogenic edema [Figure 2].

DISCUSSION

A brain abscess is defined as an encapsulated area of pus within the brain parenchyma. It can be a life-threatening infection with a high risk of neurological deficits.[3] The incidence of intracranial abscesses ranges from 0.33 to 1.3/100,000 inhabitants per year. The most commonly identified bacteria in brain abscesses originate from the oral cavity, including Streptococcus anginosus, Fusobacterium spp., and Aggregatibacter spp., which are associated with dental infections and chronic otitis. Other frequently detected pathogens include Staphylococcus aureus and Gram-negative bacilli, as well as Mycobacterium tuberculosis in endemic regions. In severely immunocompromised patients, Nocardia spp., fungi, and parasites may also be present.[3,13,23]

The predominant risk factors were head trauma, cyanotic congenital heart disease, and chronic ear infections; however, dental infections and immunosuppression have become more important predisposing conditions.[3,4,5,8]

The classic triad of headache, fever, and focal neurological deficit is present in only ~20% of patients at the time of admission. Headache is reported in 69% of cases, fever in 53%, and focal neurological deficit in 48%.[6,20] Other common neurological symptoms include seizures and altered consciousness. Rupture of the abscess into the ventricular system may lead to ventriculitis, a complication associated with a high risk of obstructive hydrocephalus and increased mortality.[1,6]

Laboratory tests are frequently not helpful in supporting the clinical diagnosis of brain abscess. Neuroimaging is essential for patients with suspected brain abscess. CT allows for the rapid detection of the size, number, and location of abscesses. MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging helps differentiate brain abscesses from primary, cystic, or necrotic tumors.[11,17]

Blood and cerebrospinal fluid cultures identify the pathogen in approximately 25% of cases. However, lumbar puncture should only be performed if there is suspicion of meningitis or rupture of the abscess into the ventricular system. Underlying infections in teeth, paranasal sinuses, ears, lungs, abdomen, skin, or other potential dissemination sites should be investigated.[3,6] The differential diagnosis includes a variety of neurological and infectious diseases, such as brain tumors, stroke, bacterial meningitis, epidural abscess, and subdural empyema.[6,17]

Surgical management is essential for the diagnosis and treatment of brain abscesses. Various techniques are available for management. Stereotactic techniques enable precise abscess aspiration while minimizing damage to critical brain areas. Resection is considered for superficial abscesses, especially when specific fungal or bacterial infections, such as Actinomyces, are suspected. The placement of catheters for continuous drainage and the direct administration of antimicrobials into the cavity are options, though not routinely recommended due to limited evidence. In patients with multiple abscesses, aspiration of the largest one is prioritized. Surgical intervention is critical for abscesses causing brain herniation or at risk of ventricular rupture.[3,6,17]

Antimicrobial treatment should be initiated immediately in suspected cases of brain abscess to prevent complications. The choice of empirical treatment depends on the probable cause of the abscess, the mechanism of infection, and the patient’s condition, including specific antibiotics based on the suspected infection. Intravenous treatment typically lasts 6–8 weeks. Regular neuroimaging is essential to evaluate progress. Once the infecting pathogen has been isolated, antimicrobial agents can be modified to achieve the most effective therapy.[6,11,17]

The genus Actinomyces comprises a phenotypically heterogeneous group of anaerobic and facultatively anaerobic, asporogenous, Gram-positive, nonacid-fast organisms with filamentous or diphtheroid rod shapes. Histologically, they appear as colonies of filamentous bacteria surrounded by a zone of granulation tissue known as sulfur granules. This slow-growing microorganism is a commensal of the mucosal membranes of the oropharyngeal cavity, gastrointestinal tract, and genitourinary tract.[9,16] The microbiological differential diagnosis includes Actinomyces turicensis, the most prevalent species found in urine or genital tract samples. A. urogenitalis is sensitive to penicillin, clindamycin, and erythromycin, resistant to ciprofloxacin, and shows variable susceptibility to tetracycline.[9]

Although penicillin remains the cornerstone of therapy for actinomycotic infections, severe intracranial presentations frequently require initial broad-spectrum antimicrobial coverage.[9,11] Otogenic brain abscesses are commonly polymicrobial, and early targeted therapy is often limited by delayed microbiological characterization. In this setting, broad-spectrum intravenous treatment during the acute phase followed by stepwise de-escalation and prolonged therapy represents a pragmatic approach to ensure infection control and prevent relapse.

Actinomyces species are indistinguishable from Nocardia in a Gram stain, but they can be differentiated through special cultures and a modified acid-fast stain. Nocardia is partially acid-fast, while Actinomyces is not. Differentiating between actinomycosis and nocardiosis is essential for selecting the appropriate antimicrobial treatment.[19]

Actinomyces species are primarily associated with brain abscesses, but cases of meningitis, meningoencephalitis, epidural abscess, and subdural empyema have also been reported. Central nervous system (CNS) involvement typically occurs through hematogenous spread from the lungs or contiguously from cervicofacial actinomycosis or after a penetrating head injury. Actinomycosis in the CNS is often polymicrobial.[21] Most infections caused by Actinomyces are polymicrobial and occur within their natural habitat (oral cavity, pharynx, intestine, genitourinary tract, and skin). However, they are also involved in other systemic and monomicrobial infections, both in immunocompromised and immunocompetent patients.[2] Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of Actinomyces species is limited in clinical microbiology laboratories, primarily because it is assumed that strains are generally susceptible to beta-lactams.

The reviewed studies identify several microorganisms involved in the formation of brain abscesses, particularly A. turicensis, Actinomyces meyeri, and Actinomyces israelii. These bacteria, which are typically commensals of the oral, gastrointestinal, and pelvic mucosa, can become pathogenic when the mucosal barrier is disrupted, facilitating their spread and subsequent invasion of necrotic or damaged tissue.[7,12,15,16] Infections caused by actinomycosis are commonly observed after trauma in the cervicofacial region, abdomen, and thorax. Involvement of the middle ear and mastoid is less frequent and usually results from direct spread through the Eustachian tube from the nasopharynx.[18] A. turicensis is rarely associated with human infections, though it has been documented in cases affecting the anogenital area, lungs, gynecological system, and CNS.[7] A. israelii, known for causing chronic infections, is classically linked to intrauterine devices and bisphosphonate-induced osteonecrosis of the jaw. In cases of Actinomyces, manifestations often include painless masses in the cervicofacial region, though hematogenous spread can lead to distant infections, including the CNS.[12,21] Diagnosing these microorganisms is complex and may be delayed due to their similarity to malignancies, tuberculosis, or nocardiosis. In the reviewed cases, advanced techniques, such as 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing, were used for accurate identification of Actinomyces. In addition, histopathology may reveal the presence of the characteristic “sulfur granules” associated with these bacteria. In the cases analyzed, surgical management was crucial for the patient’s outcome, along with microbiological isolation and appropriate adjustment of antibiotic therapy.

P. asaccharolyticus are Gram-positive anaerobic cocci that are primarily human commensals found on the skin, genitourinary system, and intestinal tract. This bacterium is especially prevalent in chronic wounds and diabetic ulcers, where it is usually part of polymicrobial spectra. However, clinical reports and studies addressing the role of P. asaccharolyticus in more severe infections, such as skin infections, soft tissue infections, vertebral osteomyelitis, and spinal abscesses, are limited.[10] P. asaccharolyticus is commonly recognized as an obligate anaerobe coexisting in chronic wounds or diabetic ulcers, ranking among the most prevalent anaerobic pathogens. Despite this, there is little literature on its prevalence in more invasive infections, and it is generally considered a rare pathogen in severe infections such as bone, joint, or bloodstream infections. Peptoniphilus species are often recovered as part of a mixed bacterial flora in complex abscesses, particularly in the abdomen or pelvis.[22] A study that included 15 cases of bloodstream infections caused by Peptoniphilus species between 2007 and 2011 showed that half of these infections were polymicrobial, with common underlying conditions such as septic abortion, exacerbated chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, skin and soft tissue infections, and bowel or bladder diseases.[5] In the review, a case of a brain abscess caused by Trueperella bernardiae and Peptoniphilus harei was reported in a patient with chronic suppurative otitis media complicated by a cholesteatoma, which constitutes the first documented case in the literature caused by these microorganisms.[14] P. asaccharolyticus can also cause secondary infections in paraspinal abscesses and can coexist with microorganisms such as M. tuberculosis.[24]

A. urogenitalis and P. asaccharolyticus are microorganisms that are part of the normal flora of the urinary and intestinal tracts, although they have also been associated with infections in the ear, such as chronic otitis. Despite their presence in these areas, their role in more severe infections, such as brain abscesses, is not well documented. In the reviewed clinical case, significant growth of both microorganisms was observed in the sample obtained during surgery, which, in the context of the patient’s medical history, including chronic otitis media, suggests that both organisms acted as causative agents of the brain abscess. The coincidence of these microorganisms in such a rare case highlights their possible involvement in CNS infections. According to current literature, no cases of brain abscesses caused by A. urogenitalis or P. asaccharolyticus, either separately or in combination, have been previously reported. This finding makes this case the first documented brain abscess caused by these organisms, opening new perspectives on their pathogenic role in severe infections and emphasizing the importance of considering these microorganisms in the diagnosis of rare and complex infections.

CONCLUSION

This case represents the first documented report of a brain abscess caused by A. urogenitalis and P. asaccharolyticus, suggesting their potential role in invasive infections of the CNS. Their identification highlights the importance of detailed microbiological studies for accurate diagnosis and appropriate antibiotic treatment. In addition, it underscores the need to consider these microorganisms in patients with predisposing factors such as chronic otitis media. A comprehensive approach combining microbiological diagnosis, surgical management, and targeted therapy is essential to improving the prognosis of these rare but potentially severe infections.
