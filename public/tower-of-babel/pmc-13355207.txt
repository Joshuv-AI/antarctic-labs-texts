Perceived communication barriers with spouses among young adult patients with cancer and their association with psychological distress
Authors: Yingjie Yao, Jinghan Chen, Yinglong Duan, Jianfei Xie, Xi Chen, Qinqin Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePsychological distress is prevalent among young adult (YA) patients with cancer, yet the role of couples' communication in this specific population remains underexplored. This study aimed to investigate the status of couples' communication and examine its independent association with depression and anxiety in YA patients with cancer.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among YA patients with cancer. Participants completed the Cancer-Related Communication Problems Scale, PHQ-9 (depression), and GAD-7 (anxiety). Hierarchical linear regression analyses were performed to determine the association between communication barriers and psychological outcomes after controlling for demographic and clinical variables.ResultsA total of 202 married YA patients with cancer participated in this study. The mean age was 34.69 ± 4.11 years (Range: 22–39 years). Participants reported a mild level of overall communication barriers (Total Score Mean ± SD: 13.41 ± 3.93; Range: 1–22). The mean anxiety and depression scores were 6.43 ± 5.16 (Range: 0–21) and 6.97 ± 5.47 (Range: 0–27). The top five specific barriers were difficulty with partner understanding of body image changes (Item 11, Mean ± SD = 1.46 ± 0.69), difficulty discussing worsening conditions (Item 13, Mean ± SD = 1.30 ± 0.68), difficulty sharing worries about treatment effectiveness (Item 9, Mean ± SD = 1.28 ± 0.71), avoidance of discussing sexual experience (Item 15, Mean ± SD = 1.11 ± 0.72) and selective disclosure to protect the spouse (Item 14, Mean ± SD = 1.07 ± 0.70). Univariate analysis indicated that unemployment and female sex were significantly associated with higher depression scores (P < 0.05), while no demographic variables were significantly associated with communication barriers. After adjusting for sex and unemployment status, communication barriers remained independently associated with depression (B = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.08, 0.44, P = 0.008), but not with anxiety (P > 0.05).ConclusionsCommunication barriers were independently associated with depressive symptoms, but not anxiety, after adjustment for sex and employment status. These item-level findings suggest that body image and intimacy may be key topics for future dyadic communication interventions. Future psychosocial interventions may consider addressing open spousal dialogue about body image and sexual concerns in this population.Keywords: Young adults, Communication barriers with spouses, Depression, Anxiety, Body image, Cancer

Introduction

Young adult (YA) patients with cancer are defined as those diagnosed between the ages of 20 and 39 years.1 They represent a unique and urgent group in modern oncology. Their disease burden is a growing global public health challenge. The Global Burden of Disease 2019 analysis, published in 2022, reported 1.19 million new cancer cases in this young population worldwide.2 Furthermore, the cancer incidence rate in this group has risen rapidly over the past three decades. This trend is also concerning in China. In 2024, the National Cancer Center reported a significant overall increase in cancer cases in China, adding substantial challenges to the social workforce and family structures.3

Unlike children or older patients, the YA population is at a highly demanding life stage. They are navigating the establishment and consolidation of their own nuclear families, bearing significant socioeconomic responsibilities, and deepening stable marital relationships. Cancer not only endangers their lives but also severely disrupts their core developmental tasks of maintaining family stability and intimacy.4 During this period, communication between spouses is vital. It is not only a source of emotional support but also a key pathway to help patients rebuild self-worth, manage financial crises (such as unemployment), and cope with death anxiety.5 However, the communication needs of YA patients with cancer are often overlooked. Blocked communication is often associated with severe psychological maladjustment. Research shows that the high prevalence of depression and anxiety in this group (approximately 20% to 40%)6, 7, 8 is closely related to the quality of interaction in intimate relationships. Therefore, focusing on the communication barriers of YA patients with cancer is critical for promoting their psychological health.

Among social support systems, the spousal relationship is paramount for YA patients with cancer.9,10 Within the context of marriage, spouses are the primary source of emotional and instrumental support, as well as the main partners in facing severe disease-related burdens.11 However, the protective effect of this support is contingent upon the quality of communication. Communication extends beyond mere information exchange; it serves as the central mechanism for spouses to engage in “dyadic coping” Extensive empirical evidence confirms that spouses employing effective dyadic coping strategies report better psychological adjustment and higher relationship functioning.12 Open, constructive communication facilitates the joint processing of mortality fears, the coordination of complex treatment decisions, and the maintenance of genuine emotional connection.13,14 Conversely, negative communication patterns can negate these benefits. For instance, Lyons revealed that “protective buffering”,15 a strategy where spouses hide their distress to avoid burdening each other, is prevalent among YA spouses. Although well-intentioned, this avoidance paradoxically correlates with higher psychological distress for both parties, promoting relational distancing and diminishing intimacy, thereby exacerbating the patient's maladjustment.16,17

Despite the established importance of spousal communication in psycho-oncology, research on YA patients with cancer remains limited by methodological shortcomings. This limitation results in fragmented evidence. First, current insights into the communication experiences of YA patients with cancer rely heavily on qualitative studies. Interviews by Basinger et al.18 illustrate the communication dilemmas spouses face. However, qualitative data cannot quantify the prevalence or severity of specific barriers. Clinically, vague observations of “communication issues” fail to identify specific needs, such as the distinction between discussing disease progression and emotional expression. Consequently, this lack of precision impedes effective risk stratification and targeted intervention.19 Second, the few existing quantitative studies often restrict their scope to single cancer types or isolated functional dimensions. Regarding disease type, most samples predominantly feature females, such as patients with breast cancer.20 This sex bias limits generalizability to males or patients with hematological malignancies who face distinct prognostic challenges.9 Regarding dimensions, research has focused disproportionately on specific topics like fertility preservation or sexual dysfunction.21,22 This narrow focus overlooks the fact that communication barriers often stem from a complex interplay of factors, including altered body image, future uncertainty, and disrupted social roles. Therefore, the field urgently needs a multi-dimensional, multi-disease quantitative study. Such a study would provide a comprehensive assessment of communication barriers and their independent association with mental health in this population.

To bridge these gaps, this study aims to systematically investigate perceived communication barriers with spouses among Chinese YA patients with cancer, with a specific focus on its association with psychological outcomes, particularly depressive symptoms. The specific objectives are: (1) to identify the most prominent types and distributional characteristics of communication barriers; and (2) to examine the independent association between communication barriers and psychological distress (anxiety and depression) after controlling for sociodemographic and clinical variables. We hypothesize that greater communication barriers are associated with higher levels of depressive symptoms in YA patients with cancer.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted at a provincial tertiary cancer hospital from November 2025 to January 2026. The reporting of this study follows the STROBE guidelines. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were: (1) pathologically diagnosed with malignant tumors; (2) diagnosed with cancer between the ages of 20 and 39 years; (3) currently married; (4) aware of their cancer diagnosis; and (5) able to read and understand Chinese. Patients were excluded if they had severe cognitive impairments or pre-existing psychiatric disorders that prevented effective communication.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined using G∗Power 3.1.9.7 software based on the requirements for hierarchical linear regression analysis.23 We adopted a robust statistical approach by setting the power (1-β) at 0.90, the significance level (α) at 0.05, and assuming a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15).24 To comprehensively control for potential confounders, we considered 9 sociodemographic and clinical variables (specifically: age, sex, education level, employment status, residence, medical insurance, time since diagnosis, cancer stage, and treatment modality) as associated factors in the regression model. Considering the inclusion of dummy variables for categorical variables, the model comprised approximately 20 factors. The calculation indicated a minimum required sample size of 191 participants. Our final sample of 202 participants exceeded this requirement, ensuring adequate statistical power for the analysis.

Measures

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

Participants reported their sociodemographic information, including age, sex, education level, marital status, employment status, residence and medical insurance. We collected clinical data from medical records or patient self-reports. These data included cancer diagnosis, time since diagnosis, cancer stage, and treatment type.

Communication barriers

We assessed perceived communication barriers with spouses using the Cancer-Related Communication Problems Scale (CRCP), originally developed by Kornblith25 and translated and applied in the Chinese context by Li et al.26 Although the original instrument comprises both patient and spouse versions across multiple sub-dimensions, this study administered only the patient version to evaluate the overall severity of communication barriers as a single construct. This section consists of 15 items rated on a 3-point Likert scale (0 = not true, 1 = sometimes true, 2 = always true). Total scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating more severe communication barriers. Clinical cutoffs classify scores of ≤ 15 as mild, 16–22 as moderate, and ≥ 23 as severe. In the present study, the scale demonstrated high internal consistency, yielding a Cronbach's α coefficient of 0.920.

Anxiety

Anxiety symptoms over the past two weeks were assessed using the Chinese version of the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), originally developed by Spitzer et al.27 and validated by Tong et al.28 The scale consists of 7 items scored on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), with a total score range of 0–21. Higher scores indicate more severe anxiety. Severity is categorized as follows: 0–4 (no anxiety), 5–9 (mild), 10–14 (moderate), and ≥ 15 (severe). In the current study, the Cronbach's α coefficient for the GAD-7 was 0.928.

Depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a widely used clinical screening tool developed by Kroenke et al.29 and adapted into Chinese by Wang et al.30 The scale comprises 9 items evaluating the frequency of depressive symptoms experienced in the past two weeks. Each item is scored from 0 to 3, with higher cumulative scores (range 0–27) indicating greater depression severity. Scores are generally categorized as none (0–4), mild to moderate (5–14), and severe (≥15). Reliability analysis in this study showed a Cronbach's α of 0.888, indicating good psychometric properties.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and means and standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables, were used to summarize participant characteristics.31 The internal consistency of the measurement tools was assessed using Cronbach's α coefficients. For specific communication barrier items, the frequencies and percentages for all three response categories (‘not true’, ‘sometimes true’, and ‘always true’) were calculated to comprehensively characterize the distribution of communication barriers. Univariate analyses, including independent samples t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), were conducted to compare differences in psychological outcomes across demographic and clinical subgroups. Pearson's correlation analysis was performed to examine the bivariate relationships among communication barriers, anxiety, and depression. To explore the association of communication barriers with psychological distress, hierarchical linear regression models were constructed. Demographic and clinical variables that demonstrated a statistically significant association (P < 0.05) with psychological outcomes in the univariate analyses were selected as covariates and entered in Step 1 as control variables. The communication barrier score was entered in Step 2 to assess its association with psychological distress. Additionally, to test the robustness of our primary findings against potential clinical confounders, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using a fully adjusted hierarchical regression model. In this supplementary model, selected major variables (specifically age, sex, employment status, time since diagnosis, cancer stage, and treatment modality) were forced into Step 1 as covariates, regardless of their univariate significance, followed by the communication barrier score in Step 2. Model fit and the incremental explanatory power were assessed using statistical indices, including R2, ΔR2, and F values. Multicollinearity was checked using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with values < 5 considered acceptable.32 All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics and univariate analysis

A total of 202 eligible YA patients with cancer were included in the study, with a mean age of 34.69 years (SD = 4.11). The sample was predominantly female (77.2%), and nearly one-third of participants held an undergraduate degree or higher (29.2%). Notably, the unemployment rate was high, with 40.1% of patients reporting being unemployed. Regarding clinical characteristics, cancer stages were relatively evenly distributed, although stage information was unknown for 34.7% of the sample. In terms of psychological and relational status, participants reported mild levels of communication barriers and psychological symptoms (Table 1).

The overall communication barriers were mild (Mean = 13.41, SD = 3.93) as reported in Table 1. The most prominent barrier was “Difficulty with partner understanding of body image changes” (Item 11, Mean = 1.46, SD = 0.69) (Table 2). This was followed by “Difficulty discussing worsening conditions” (Item 13, Mean = 1.30, SD = 0.68) and “Difficulty sharing worries about treatment effectiveness” (Item 9, Mean = 1.28, SD = 0.71). Other top barriers included avoidance of discussing sexual experience (Item 15, Mean = 1.11, SD = 0.72) and selective disclosure to protect the spouse (Item 14, Mean = 1.07, SD = 0.70). In contrast, the lowest barrier score was observed for “Not knowing when the partner wants to talk about cancer” (Item 5, Mean = 0.57, SD = 0.67), indicating a relatively high level of dyadic attunement regarding the timing of communication.

Factors associated with perceived communication barriers with spouses

Univariate analysis indicated that none of the demographic or clinical variables included in this study were significantly associated with communication barrier scores (Table 3). Specifically, no statistically significant differences in communication levels were observed across sex (P = 0.120), age groups (P = 0.516), education levels (P = 0.180), or employment status (P = 0.749). Similarly, residence (P = 0.141), medical insurance type (P = 0.909), and clinical characteristics such as cancer stage and treatment modality did not significantly influence communication scores. Furthermore, a supplementary Pearson correlation using age as a continuous variable confirmed this non-significant association (r = 0.034, P = 0.629).

Association of communication barriers with psychological distress

First, Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the bivariate relationships among the study variables. The results indicated a significant positive correlation between communication barriers and depressive symptoms (r = 0.145, P = 0.040). In contrast, no significant correlation was observed between communication barriers and anxiety symptoms (r = 0.069, P = 0.332).

To identify potential confounders for the regression models, univariate analyses were conducted with depression and anxiety as dependent variables. The results indicated that unemployment (P < 0.001) and sex (P < 0.05) were significantly associated with higher depression scores. Therefore, sex and unemployment status were thus entered in Step 1 as control variables. The total communication barrier score was subsequently entered in Step 2. Association with depressive symptoms

The VIF values for all predictors were below 2.0, indicating no severe multicollinearity. For depressive symptoms (PHQ-9), the control variables in Step 1 accounted for 8.3% of the variance (R2 = 0.083, P < 0.001). The inclusion of communication barriers in Step 2 significantly improved the model's explanatory power (Fchange = 7.42, P = 0.008), explaining an additional 3.3% of the variance in depression (ΔR2 = 0.033). Specifically, after controlling for the robust effect of unemployment (B = 2.85, 95% CI: 1.30, 4.40, P < 0.001) and sex, communication barriers remained a significantly associated factor with higher depressive symptoms (B = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.08, 0.44, P = 0.008). Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis using an extended model (controlling for age, sex, employment status, time since diagnosis, cancer stage, and treatment modality) confirmed the robustness of this finding. Even with all these variables included, communication barriers remained a significantly associated factor with higher depressive symptoms (B = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.07, 0.43, P = 0.006) (Supplementary Table S1).

Association with anxiety symptoms

In contrast, for anxiety symptoms (GAD-7), the addition of communication barriers in Step 2 did not significantly increase the explanatory power of the model (ΔR2 = 0.008, Fchange = 1.69, P = 0.205). While unemployment remained a significant associated factor (B = 2.33, 95% CI: 0.84, 3.82, P = 0.004), no significant statistical association was found between communication barriers and anxiety levels (B = 0.12, 95% CI: −0.06, 0.30, P = 0.205).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate perceived communication barriers with spouses specifically among the Chinese YA patients with cancer and examine their independent association with psychological symptoms. While previous research often focused on single cancer types or older adults, YA patients with cancer occupy a critical life stage. They are building families, advancing careers, and deepening marital bonds.18 Consequently, our findings provide essential empirical evidence to guide dyadic coping and psychological adaptation for this vulnerable group.9,13

Our data reveal a unique communication pattern among Chinese YA patients with cancer. They show high relational attunement but significantly avoid body-related topics. The overall communication barrier score was mild (Mean = 13.41). This score is slightly higher than the 9.2 reported by Li et al.26 in older patients with breast cancer. This difference suggests that YA patients with cancer face more severe communication challenges.33,34 Married YA patients with cancer are navigating intense disruptions to their marital and sexual lives. Notably, item-specific scores showed sharp polarization. Barriers were highest for “Difficulty with partner understanding of body image changes” (Item 11, Mean = 1.46) and “Difficulty discussing worsening conditions” (Item 13, Mean = 1.30). In contrast, “Not knowing when the spouse is willing to talk” (Item 5) scored lowest (0.57). This indicates that YA spouses are not emotionally disengaged. They maintain good “dyadic adjustment” regarding the timing of communication. However, this foundational rapport fails to facilitate open discussion on sensitive topics. When issues involve core self-identity and marital sexuality, spouses appear to enter an unspoken agreement to maintain mutual silence.35,36 Crucially, this specific pattern links closely to mental health. We controlled for high unemployment rates and sex. Even then, communication barriers remained a significant independent correlate of depressive symptoms.15,37 This finding suggests that the content of communication matters more than the mere ability to converse. Specifically, for YA patients with cancer, the overall communication barriers are associated with depressive symptoms, and these item-level findings suggest that body image and intimacy may be key topics for future dyadic communication interventions.

The item scores reveal two main barriers: body image issues and fears about disease progression. First, high scores on Item 11 (Body Image) and Item 15 (Sexual Experience) reflect a specific challenge for YA patients with cancer. Physical attractiveness and sexual intimacy are central to marital satisfaction at this stage. Therefore, these patients are highly sensitive to physical changes caused by treatment. Shame or fear of losing their spouse's physical attraction often stops them from talking. This aligns with Wettergren et al.,38 who identified sexuality and body issues as top unmet needs for young survivors. Second, high scores on Item 13 (Difficulty discussing worsening conditions) and Item 9 (Difficulty sharing treatment worries) show deep concerns about the disease outcome. For married YA patients with cancer, this involves profound fears of leaving a young family behind and destroying shared life plans.19 Interestingly, patients are generally willing to talk, yet they avoid specific “high-threat” topics. Item 14 (“I only tell my spouse what he/she wants to hear”) explains this mechanism. This avoidance does not stem from emotional coldness. Instead, it reflects “protective buffering.” Patients hide information to protect their spouses.15,17 This pattern may limit opportunities for couples to face future uncertainty together and may contribute to perceived emotional isolation.

We found no link between education level and communication barriers. This challenges the assumption that higher education means better communication skills.39 Cancer distress involves deep fears of death and unfulfilled lives. Our results confirm a universal vulnerability. Fear of disfigurement and death affects everyone, regardless of education. High health literacy does not protect against this emotional distress.40 Therefore, clinical support must be universal. We recommend including body image and intimacy assessments in routine care.21 Clinicians should use brief screening tools to identify needs hidden behind “protective silence.” A patient may appear socially functional, but this often masks their internal struggle.

Finally, multivariate analysis (Table 4) established the independent and specific association of perceived communication barriers with spouses and psychological symptoms. Hierarchical regression showed that adding communication barriers increased the model's explanatory power for depressive symptoms to 11.6%, with barriers contributing an additional 3.3% of independent variance (ΔR2 = 0.033). The unstandardized regression coefficient (B = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.08, 0.44, P = 0.008) confirms communication barriers as a significantly associated factor with depressive symptoms. This suggests that open communication may be a potential target for future psychosocial intervention by reducing emotional isolation and fostering mutual support.5 Even amidst unchangeable economic adversity, improving spousal communication remains a viable avenue for psychological intervention. In contrast, we found no independent statistical association between communication barriers and anxiety symptoms (P > 0.05). This differs from Liu et al.,41 who linked negative dyadic coping to both anxiety and depression. In the context of cancer, depression and anxiety may operate through distinct mechanisms. Depression is often tied to emotional alienation and isolation;42 spousal communication serves as a primary channel for emotional expression, and its blockage may exacerbate negative rumination. Conversely, cancer-related anxiety relates more to cognitive fears of recurrence and uncertainty, as noted by Simard et al.43 Although patients avoided discussing “worsening condition” (Item 13), this avoidance may be a consequence rather than a cause of anxiety. Furthermore, spousal emotional support may have limited efficacy in alleviating anxiety tied to medical outcomes. Thus, our results provide a rationale for differentiated clinical interventions. Strategies for depression should focus on enhancing spousal communication to reduce isolation. Meanwhile, strategies for anxiety should prioritize cognitive reframing and information support.

Clinical implications

These findings offer actionable guidance for optimizing communication and psychosocial support for YA patients with cancer. First, clinicians should incorporate spousal communication quality into routine psychosocial assessments. They must particularly focus on distress related to body image. Body image issues constitute the primary communication barrier and often trigger “protective silence.” Health care providers must proactively initiate these discussions rather than passively waiting for patients to raise them. For example, guided questions (e.g., “How have treatment-related physical changes affected your intimacy?”) help normalize sensitive concerns and break the cycle of protective silence.

Second, psychosocial interventions require precise stratification based on the patient's dominant psychological symptoms. For patients presenting primarily with depressive symptoms, we recommend prioritizing spouse-based communication interventions. These should focus on enhancing emotional expression and reciprocal responsiveness to alleviate isolation. Conversely, for patients dominated by anxiety symptoms, strategies should focus on providing medical information and cognitive-behavioral techniques to manage uncertainty. Pure relational communication training may have limited efficacy for anxiety. Finally, support services must be universally accessible. Educational level does not mitigate the emotional difficulty of communication. Therefore, psychosocial services should aim to provide equitable support resources to ensure interventions broadly benefit all YA patients with cancer.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between variables. We observed a significant association between communication barriers and depressive symptoms. However, we cannot rule out reverse causality. Patients with severe depression might perceive greater communication difficulties due to cognitive bias. Future longitudinal research is necessary to clarify the temporal sequence of these associations. Second, the recruitment of participants from a single tertiary cancer center may limit the generalizability of our findings. Third, reliance solely on patient self-reports excludes the spousal perspective. Future studies should collect dyadic data to comprehensively capture perceptual differences and interaction mechanisms. Furthermore, a notable proportion of cancer stage data was classified as “unknown” due to the heterogeneity of the cancer types included in our study, which precluded the use of a unified staging system. This limitation prevents us from fully adjusting for disease severity as a potential confounder in our regression analysis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study indicates that perceived communication barriers with spouses among YA patients with cancer are generally mild. However, specific challenges remain prominent. The five most reported barriers involve difficulty with partner understanding of body image changes, difficulty discussing worsening conditions, difficulty sharing worries about treatment effectiveness, avoidance of discussing sexual experience, and selective disclosure to protect the spouse. Crucially, our findings indicate that perceived communication barriers were independently associated with depressive symptoms. In contrast, no independent association was found between communication barriers and anxiety. This pattern may suggest a closer relationship with depressive symptoms than with generalized anxiety, although the underlying mechanisms require further study. Therefore, future psychosocial services must move beyond survival-centric models. Clinicians should prioritize interventions that facilitate open spousal dialogue.
