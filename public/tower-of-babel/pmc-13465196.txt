Evaluating the learnability of single-cell large language models on multiple tasks
Authors: Yu Yan, Xutao Wang, Dongyuan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rise of single-cell foundation models (scFMs) has sparked interest in their potential to unify diverse biological tasks. However, their practical utility and the validity of scaling laws—the assumption that performance improves with model and data size—remain under-examined. Here, we systematically evaluate two representative scFMs, Geneformer and scGPT, across perturbation prediction and cell type annotation tasks. Our findings suggest that the benefits of large-scale pretraining are strongly task-dependent, conferring substantial advantages in cell type annotation but limited gains in perturbation prediction. Furthermore, our results indicate that increasing model size does not guarantee improved performance and can even be detrimental, challenging the “bigger is better” paradigm for the models and tasks examined here. By comparing model performance on real versus synthetic data with different levels of complexity, our analysis suggests that for perturbation prediction, the tested scFMs may capture little more than simple summary statistics, suggesting limited capacity to learn complex biological interactions within our experimental design. Based on our evaluation of Geneformer and scGPT, these results highlight the need to move beyond scaling and toward developing models that integrate deeper biological knowledge. We suggest that a renewed focus on task-specific architectures and biologically-informed priors may be critical for unlocking the true potential of foundation models in single-cell biology.

Introduction

The confluence of high-throughput single-cell sequencing and advanced artificial intelligence (AI) has ushered in a new era of biological discovery. Single-cell technologies now generate data at an unprecedented scale, offering a high-resolution lens into cellular heterogeneity and molecular programs [1–4]. In parallel, AI has produced large-scale models capable of learning powerful representations from vast, unannotated datasets [5–8]. These dual advancements have catalyzed the development of single-cell foundation models (scFMs)—large neural networks pretrained on massive single-cell atlases. The goal of scFMs is to produce universal, reusable representations that can be adapted to a wide array of downstream biological tasks, from cell type annotation to perturbation effect prediction [9, 10]. This paradigm, inspired by the success of foundation models in natural language processing and computer vision, holds the promise of unlocking scaling laws, where model performance systematically improves with increasing data and parameter counts [11–13].

Recent years have witnessed an explosion of scFMs, including scGPT [9], Geneformer [10], scFoundation [14], scBERT [15], UCE [16], CellPLM [17], GenePT [18], and GeneCompass [19], among others. Despite their promise, a growing body of comprehensive benchmarking studies has begun to raise critical questions about their practical utility. For instance, systematic evaluations of scGPT and Geneformer for cell type classification revealed that their performance can be inconsistent, sometimes failing to outperform simpler baseline models [20]. The PertEval-scFM framework further demonstrated that embeddings from scFMs do not consistently improve perturbation effect prediction, especially under distribution shifts [21]. Similarly, the scEval framework, which benchmarked ten scFMs across eight downstream tasks, reported that these models often fail to outperform task-specific architectures such as CellTypist [22] and SingleR [23], challenging the assumption that foundation models inherently offer superior generalization [20]. Moreover, a deeper evaluation of one scFM showed that pre-training does not meaningfully affect downstream performance on cell-type annotation, and that the model could perform well on its pre-training task without learning meaningful gene representations [24]. Benchmarking efforts have also extended to other tasks, including drug response prediction, where scFMs similarly showed scenario-dependent performance with no single model consistently outperforming others [25]. Further benchmarking efforts have corroborated these concerns across a range of tasks: systematic evaluations of cell type classification have found scFMs to be inconsistent across datasets, with some models underperforming simpler baselines in certain settings [26, 27], and benchmarks of perturbation prediction have similarly reported that foundation models can be outperformed by straightforward baseline approaches [28]. Standardized evaluation frameworks [29] and field-level perspectives [30] have further emphasized the need for rigorous, systematic assessment before broader deployment of scFMs. We focus on Geneformer and scGPT as they are among the most extensively studied scFMs and represent two of the main gene expression encoding strategies in the field: Geneformer employs rank value encoding, in which genes are ordered by their within-cell expression rank, while scGPT employs expression value tokenization, in which gene expression levels are directly used as model inputs. While other encoding strategies exist, these two approaches are among the most widely adopted, making Geneformer and scGPT a reasonable basis for our evaluation.

Despite these critical findings, the reasons why large-scale models often fail to outperform smaller, specialized counterparts remain poorly understood [31–34]. In this work, we move beyond benchmarking to investigate the central question of when and partially why scFMs succeed or fail. We revisit fundamental assumptions—such as the validity of scaling laws—that have been largely taken for granted. By systematically evaluating the interplay between pretraining data, model size, and data complexity, we aim to uncover the conditions under which these models provide tangible benefits.

Our work suggests three key insights. First, we find that pretraining offers task-dependent benefits, substantially improving performance on cell type annotation but providing minimal advantage for perturbation prediction. Second, for the two models evaluated, we question the “bigger is better” paradigm by showing that smaller, less complex models can sometimes match or even exceed the performance of their larger counterparts within our tested configurations. Third, to investigate what these models learn—a question that task-level benchmarking alone cannot fully address—we introduce a synthetic data probing strategy in which fine-tuning data is replaced with synthetic counterparts generated by scDesign3 [35] that retain only controlled levels of statistical structure. By testing whether performance is maintained when higher-order biological complexity is removed, this approach offers a mechanistic complement to performance-based evaluation. Our analysis suggests that for perturbation prediction, performance was largely maintained on such simplified data, indicating that the tested scFMs may not learn beyond simple statistical moments (mean, variance).

These findings have important implications for the future of single-cell foundation models. We suggest that rather than focusing on ever-increasing model and dataset size, the community could benefit from a deeper, more mechanistic understanding of what current models learn and under what conditions pretraining is genuinely beneficial. This work contributes to the ongoing discourse on how to design next-generation models that can more effectively capture the rich, multi-faceted complexity of cellular systems.

Results

Our evaluation of single-cell foundation models (scFMs) focuses on the two most widely used downstream tasks: perturbation effect prediction and cell type annotation. Across both tasks, we systematically compare two representative scFMs—Geneformer [10] and scGPT [9]—under a range of controlled experimental settings (Fig. 1). Specifically, we assess whether scFM pretraining provides meaningful benefits by examining three key factors: (i) pretraining data size through varying levels of subsampling, including full-scale pretraining, minimal subsampling, and no pretraining; (ii) model capacity, as characterized by differences in the number of transformer layers; and (iii) data complexity, evaluated by simplifying task-specific datasets using synthetic data generated from estimated distributional parameters accounting for mean and variance only, or for mean, variance, and correlations (Fig. 1). Together, these analyses enable a principled assessment of the relative contributions of pretraining scale, architectural capacity, and data complexity to downstream performance in single-cell modeling.

Overview of the single-cell foundation model training and adaptation framework. Large-scale single-cell datasets are used for self-supervised pretraining of transformer-based models. The resulting pretrained foundation model is subsequently fine-tuned with task-specific data to produce multiple specialist models for downstream applications. Perturbation prediction: pretraining and model size show limited impact

Large-scale pretraining offers minimal advantage

To assess the dependency of perturbation prediction on large-scale pretraining, we compared models pretrained on the full data corpus with those pretrained on minimal subsets (0.1% and 1%) or trained from scratch (no pretraining). To control for the intrinsic randomness in model training and data generation, for every setting we repeated the full pipeline with five random seeds. We hypothesized that if pretraining captures robust biological priors, its benefits would degrade gracefully with decreasing data, while its absence would lead to a significant performance drop.

The results, however, revealed a more complex picture than anticipated (Fig. 2A). For Geneformer, there was a clear dose-response relationship between pretraining data and downstream performance. The model pretrained on the full dataset achieved the highest Delta Pearson Correlation, and this performance noticeably declined when pretrained on the 1% and 0.1% subsamples. This suggests that for Geneformer, a certain level of pretraining is indeed beneficial. In stark contrast, scGPT’s performance was not only consistently lower than Geneformer’s across all conditions, but it also remained almost entirely flat, regardless of the amount of pretraining data. Surprisingly, the model trained from scratch performed just as well as (and sometimes even better than) the one that was fully pretrained on 33 million cells. This observation of a performance plateau, particularly for scGPT, is consistent with recent findings that the benefits of scaling pre-training data diminish beyond a certain point [36]. While that study systematically evaluated a broad spectrum of subsampling sizes, our focus on minimal pre-training regimes (0.1% and 1%) further underscores that for some models, performance plateaus almost immediately, with little to no benefit over training from scratch. This raises the question of whether other factors, such as model capacity, might be a more dominant driver of performance.

Increasing model size yields diminishing returns

Next, we examine how model capacity, simply parameterized by the number of transformer layers, affects perturbation prediction, using models trained from scratch (Fig. 2B). This allows us to test the hypothesis that larger models are better equipped to capture the complex biological dependencies required for this task. However, our results do not support this hypothesis. For both Geneformer and scGPT, performance did not monotonically increase with model size. For Geneformer, performance peaked with 6 layers and then declined with 12 layers. A similar trend was observed for scGPT, which also peaked at 6 layers. Intriguingly, the smallest models (1 or 3 layers) performed on par with, or even slightly better than, the largest models. This suggests that for perturbation prediction, there is a “sweet spot” of model complexity, and that simply increasing model size is not an effective strategy for improving performance. This led us to question what these models are actually learning from the data, regardless of their size or pretraining.

Models may primarily learn simple statistics

To probe what these models learn, we finetuned them on simplified synthetic data, which only contains partial information of real data. Using the statistical simulator we proposed before, scDesign3 [35], to learn the distributional properties in each condition of real data, we generated two types of synthetic data: one that preserves only the mean and variance of the real data but without gene-gene correlation structures (W/o correlation) and another that also preserves gene-gene linear correlations but no higher-order interactions (W/ correlation) (see Supplementary Fig. 5A in Appendix 2). Note that scDesign3 does not preserve higher-order interactions across genes (see Fig. 6 in Appendix 2). This feature was intentionally used here to evaluate model performance under a controlled level of data complexity. If the models were learning complex biological structure, we would expect their performance to drop significantly when finetuned on this simplified data.

The results were striking (Fig. 2C). For both Geneformer and scGPT, the performance difference between models finetuned on real data and those finetuned on either type of synthetic data was minimal. For Geneformer, the Delta Pearson Correlation for models finetuned on real data did not show stronger performance compared to the simulated data and was even worse in some cases (Fig. 2C). Similarly, for scGPT, there was little improvement when finetuned on real data compared to the synthetic datasets. The fact that performance on the independence data was almost as high as on the real data suggests that for perturbation prediction, both models may be learning little beyond simple, single-gene summary statistics (i.e., mean and variance). The lack of a significant improvement with the W/ correlation data further suggests that they may not be effectively leveraging even pairwise gene-gene linear correlation information. Taken together, these results suggest that for the task of perturbation prediction, the tested scFMs may not be effectively learning the complex biological relationships that are thought to be essential for accurate predictions. As a complementary biological evaluation, we also computed the precision-recall area under the curve (PR AUC) for differentially expressed gene recovery (Supplementary Fig. 7 in Appendix 2). Across pretraining subsampling, model size, and synthetic data configurations, PR AUC showed limited variation across most settings, echoing our main observation that larger pretraining data and model size do not yield substantial improvements for perturbation prediction. Notably, for Geneformer on the synthetic data condition (Supplementary Fig. 7C in Appendix 2), performance on real data was even slightly lower than on synthetic data preserving only summary statistics.

Cell type annotation: pretraining is effective, but model size is not

Large-scale pretraining confers a clear advantage

We next evaluated the models on cell type annotation, a fundamental task used to benchmark the quality of learned representations. For this, we used three benchmark datasets from the original scGPT study—Multiple Sclerosis (MS), Myeloid (Mye), and Pancreas—which span diverse tissues and cell populations. We first assessed annotation performance across different levels of pretraining data (Fig. 3A). In contrast to the perturbation task, the benefits of large-scale pretraining were clear and substantial. For both scGPT and Geneformer, the models pretrained on the full dataset significantly outperformed those that were pretrained on minimal subsets or not at all. For Geneformer, the Macro F1 score jumped from around 0.6 for the unpretrained model to over 0.8 for the fully pretrained model. A similar, though less pronounced, trend was observed for scGPT.

The models pretrained on minimal data (0.1% and 1%) performed only marginally better than the unpretrained models, with performance levels that were very similar across these three conditions. This suggests that sufficient pretraining data might be required to learn the generalizable features necessary for high-performance cell type annotation. Geneformer consistently achieved slightly higher accuracy than scGPT across all conditions, but the overall trend was similar for both models. These results strongly suggest that for cell type annotation, large-scale pretraining is a highly effective strategy. This led us to investigate whether model size also plays a significant role in performance.

Increasing model size without pretraining is detrimental

We then asked whether architectural scale alone could improve performance by training models of varying sizes from scratch (Fig. 3B). Surprisingly, not only did performance fail to improve with size, but we observed a consistent trend of decreasing accuracy as the models became larger. For both Geneformer and scGPT, the smallest models (1 or 3 layers) achieved the best performance among the unpretrained models. As the number of layers increased, the F1 score steadily dropped.

It should also be noted that even the best-performing unpretrained models did not match the performance of fully pretrained models (compare Fig. 3B to A). For example, the best unpretrained Geneformer model achieved an F1 score of around 0.7, while the fully pretrained model reached over 0.8. This reinforces the conclusion that large-scale pretraining is critical for this task. The fact that larger models performed worse suggests that without the regularizing effect of pretraining, these high-capacity models are prone to overfitting the relatively small downstream datasets. To better understand what these models are learning, with or without pretraining, we next turned to our synthetic data experiments.

Evidence for higher-order feature learning varies between models

Finally, we again used our synthetic data strategy to probe what the models are learning (Fig. 3). Similar to the design in the perturbation prediction task, we generated two types of synthetic data preserving different levels of information in real data (Supplementary Fig. 5B in Appendix 2). The W/o correlation design preserves the mean and variance of each gene in each cell type, but no gene-gene correlations, while the W/ correlation design additionally preserves the gene-gene linear correlation in each cell type, but no higher-order or nonlinear interactions (Supplementary Fig. 5B in Appendix 2 middle and right). Here, the two models exhibited strikingly different behaviors, revealing a key distinction in their learning capabilities. For Geneformer, there was a clear performance gap between finetuning on real versus synthetic data. The model finetuned on real data achieved a much higher Macro F1 score than the models finetuned on either the W/ correlation or W/o correlation synthetic data.

A visual inspection of the UMAP embeddings for the Pancreas dataset (Fig. 3D) provides further insight. For both Geneformer and scGPT, the synthetic data (with and without correlation) qualitatively reproduces the broad clustering of cell types seen in the real data. However, the boundaries between clusters are less distinct, and there is a noticeable increase in misclassifications (highlighted cells with cross marks), particularly for scGPT when using synthetic data. This visual evidence reinforces the quantitative results, suggesting that while synthetic data captures the main axes of variation, it lacks the finer-grained information required for precise cell type annotation, which Geneformer appears better able to exploit from real data. More detailed UMAP visualizations for additional datasets and experimental conditions are presented in Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9 in Appendix 2.

In stark contrast, scGPT’s performance was nearly identical across all three conditions. Whether it was finetuned on real data, data preserving per gene mean/variance but without gene-gene correlation, or data with gene-gene correlation, the macro F1 score remained essentially unchanged. This suggests that even for a task where pretraining provides a benefit, scGPT’s representations appear to be largely dominated by low-order statistics. This inability to capture more complex biological structures may explain why scGPT consistently underperforms Geneformer on this task. Overall, our results for cell type annotation show that while large-scale pretraining is beneficial, the ability to learn and exploit complex biological features is not a given and can vary significantly between different foundation models.

Synthesis of findings

To create a more comprehensive landscape of the factors affecting model performance, we conducted additional experiments where we pretrained models of varying capacities on a small-scale subset (1% of the full data). This allows for a fairer comparison of different model sizes, all of which have undergone a degree of pretraining. For perturbation prediction, we found that there is no simple relationship between performance and scale. The most effective models for this task were often the smaller ones (1-6 layers) with little or no pretraining. This suggests that for this task, large, high-capacity models may not be the best choice, and that a more modest approach may be more effective.

For cell type annotation, the results highlight the importance of finding the right balance between model size and pretraining data. For Geneformer, performance scaled with both data and model size, with the fully pretrained, large-scale model achieving the best results. For scGPT, however, the optimal configuration was a smaller model (6 layers) with minimal pretraining. This intriguing divergence suggests that the two models have different learning dynamics, and that the optimal scaling strategy is not universal. For scGPT, the benefits of pretraining appear to saturate early, while Geneformer may continue to benefit from even larger datasets.

Our findings, within the context of Geneformer and scGPT evaluated on cell type annotation and perturbation prediction, contribute a new dimension to the conversation on scaling laws in single-cell biology. While recent studies have shown that performance gains from increasing pre-training data volume tend to plateau [36], our work reveals a critical interplay between data size and model size. Specifically, for tasks like cell type annotation with scGPT, we found that with limited pre-training data, a smaller model (6 layers) outperforms a larger one. This suggests that when pre-training data is not at a massive scale, simply using a larger model is not only suboptimal but can be detrimental. Therefore, a more nuanced approach to scaling is needed, where model capacity is chosen in concert with the available pre-training data volume.

In summary, the performance landscape presented here demonstrates that there is no one-size-fits-all strategy for scFM development. The simple idea of “scaling up” is not sufficient. Instead, our results point to a more complex and nuanced reality, where the optimal model size and pretraining strategy depend on a delicate balance between the model architecture, the nature of the downstream task, and the amount of available data. The key challenge for the field is not just to build bigger models, but to understand and identify the optimal balance for each specific application.

Discussion

Our systematic evaluation across two representative downstream tasks reveals that the two scFMs evaluated here—Geneformer and scGPT— do not uniformly benefit from large-scale pretraining or increased model size. Rather, their effectiveness is strongly task-dependent, and the assumptions underlying the current trajectory of scaling in computational biology require re-examination.

For perturbation prediction, neither Geneformer nor scGPT demonstrated strong or consistent benefits from large-scale pretraining. Furthermore, their performance was nearly indistinguishable when finetuned on real data versus simulated data that preserved only basic summary statistics. This suggests that these models may not effectively capture complex biological dependencies such as concerted gene regulatory responses, network-level perturbation propagation, and coherent changes across transcriptional manifolds. Instead, they appear to rely primarily on simple statistical properties of the data. These findings align with recent reports showing that even sophisticated neural architectures do not consistently outperform linear models for perturbation effect prediction. We also note that Geneformer does not natively output gene-level perturbation predictions. Its built-in in silico perturbation procedure generates perturbed embeddings, and thus, following prior benchmarking work, we used an embedding-perturbation-plus-ridge-decoder pipeline to obtain gene-level predictions [31]. Because this additional decoding step may not fully recover nonlinear information contained in the perturbed embeddings, Geneformer results should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

In contrast, cell type annotation revealed a different pattern: large-scale pretraining provided substantial benefits, and Geneformer in particular demonstrated evidence of capturing biologically meaningful structure. However, increased model size alone did not yield better performance, and large unpretrained models frequently exhibited reduced accuracy, likely due to overfitting. These observations highlight the importance of pretraining data quality and task alignment over sheer architectural scale.

These findings suggest important directions for the next generation of scFMs. Rather than focusing solely on scaling model parameters or pretraining datasets, future work should prioritize (i) incorporating biological priors such as regulatory networks or causal perturbation relationships, (ii) designing architectures explicitly aligned with biological structure, and (iii) developing task-specific objectives that encourage models to learn beyond simple statistical moments. By shifting emphasis from scale to substance, the field may develop models that truly capture the rich biological complexity of single-cell systems.

Single cell foundation models

Geneformer

Model architecture and pretraining hyperparameters were adopted from the official Geneformer [10, 37] Hugging Face repository. The model was pretrained on approximately 95 million cells using a 20-layer transformer architecture with a maximum input sequence length of 4,096 tokens. Each transformer layer comprises 14 attention heads, an embedding dimension of 896, and an intermediate feedforward dimension of 1,792.

Single-cell generative pre-trained transformer (scGPT)

A pretrained version of scGPT is obtained from its GitHub [38], where the “whole-human” model, which was pretrained on 33 million normal human cells, was used. Perturbation task and cell type annotation task are performed following the original paper description [9].

Experiment Setup

Subsampled data training

We pretrain the models with subsampled data following the description and code provided in the original paper with default settings for both Geneformer and scGPT. In particular, the full data was downsampled at the level of 0.1% and 1% of its total.

Model size adjustment

Model capacity was varied by reducing the number of transformer layers from the original architectures. For Geneformer, which consists of 20 transformer layers, models with 1, 3, 6, and 12 layers were evaluated. For scGPT, which has 12 transformer layers, additional models with 1, 3, and 6 layers were considered.

Classification tasks

Cell type annotation

Cell type annotation datasets were obtained from the original scGPT study [9] and include three benchmark datasets: Multiple Sclerosis [39], Myeloid [40], and Pancreas [41]. These datasets span diverse tissues and cell populations and are commonly used to evaluate cell type classification performance in single-cell transcriptomics. For each dataset, cells were randomly split into training and testing sets using a 70%/30% ratio, with resampling applied to ensure that all cell type labels were represented in both splits. See the Code availability section for details on how the analysis was performed.

Perturbation prediction

The perturbation prediction task was evaluated using the Norman [42] and Adamson [43] Perturb-seq datasets from the GEARS study [44], which measure single-cell transcriptional responses to genetic perturbations. These datasets include both single-gene and combinatorial perturbations and are widely used to benchmark models’ ability to predict gene expression changes under unseen perturbation conditions. Earlier non-foundation-model approaches for this task include scGen [45] and CPA [46], which employ generative modeling frameworks. For each dataset, perturbation conditions were randomly split into training and testing sets using a 75%/25% ratio. Note that Geneformer does not provide a native perturbation prediction module; instead, we followed the protocol described by Ahlmann-Eltze et al. [31] to perform in silico perturbation. Specifically, genetic perturbations were simulated by deleting the tokens corresponding to the perturbed genes from the rank-value–encoded input sequences, recomputing cell embeddings from the perturbed sequences, and averaging these embeddings to obtain a condition-level representation. A ridge regression model was then trained to map condition embeddings to predicted gene expression profiles. See the Code availability section for details on how the analysis was performed.

Evaluation metrics

Macro F1 was used as the evaluation metric for the cell type annotation task. The F1 score was first computed for each cell type and then averaged across all classes to yield the Macro F1 score. Delta Pearson Correlation was used as the evaluation metric for the perturbation prediction task. For each perturbation condition, it quantifies the Pearson correlation between the mean value of the predicted post-perturbation gene expression and true post-perturbation expression relative to control state, defined as

Differential expression (DE) was computed separately for the real and model-predicted perturbation profiles by comparing each perturbation against the shared control cell population, producing per-gene statistics including fold change and false discovery rate (FDR). For evaluation, genes were treated as truly DE if they were significant in the real data at FDR < 0.05, and the model assigned each gene a confidence score based on the predicted significance, defined as -log10(predicted FDR). Precision-recall area under the curve (PR AUC) was then computed for each perturbation by ranking genes with this score and measuring how well highly ranked predicted genes recovered the truly significant genes from the real DE analysis. PR AUC is particularly informative in this setting because DE genes typically represent a minority of all tested genes; accordingly, a higher PR AUC indicates that the model places true DE genes near the top of its ranked list with fewer false positives, whereas a lower PR AUC indicates poorer enrichment of true DE genes among the model’s highest-confidence predictions. Final model-level performance was summarized as the mean PR AUC across all perturbation conditions.

Evaluation datasets

Cell type annotation

Processed benchmark datasets were obtained from the official scGPT [47] GitHub repository.

Multiple Sclerosis (MS) [39]: The dataset was originally published by Schirmer et al. and accessed from EMBL-EBI. It includes nine healthy control samples and twelve MS samples. Control cells were used as the training set and MS cells as the testing set. The final training and testing sets contained 14,909 and 6403 cells, respectively.

Myeloid (Mye) [40]: The dataset originally sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus under accession GSE154763 and comprises single-cell RNA-seq profiles from nine cancer types. Following the original setup, six cancer types (UCEC, PAAD, THCA, LYM, cDC2, and KIDNEY) were used as the reference set for training, while three cancer types (MYE, OV-FTC, and ESCA) were reserved as the query set for evaluation. The dataset was randomly subsampled, resulting in 9,218 cells in the training set and 3,960 cells in the testing set.

Pancreas [41]: The training set includes the Baron and Muraro datasets, while the testing set includes the Xin, Segerstolpe, and Lawlor datasets. The training set contains 10,365 cells, while the testing set contains 4,453 cells.

For all datasets, original cell-type annotations were used as ground truth, and preprocessing retained 3,000 highly variable genes. To assess robustness, additional train–test splits were generated using random seeds 1–5.

Perturbation prediction

Norman [42]: The processed Perturb-seq dataset was obtained from the official GEARS [48] GitHub repository and includes 148 single-gene and 128 two-gene perturbations.

Adamson [43]: The processed dataset was retrieved from the official GEARS [48] GitHub repository and includes 81 single-gene perturbations.

To assess robustness, additional train–test splits were generated using random seeds 1–5.
