Fractional Cointegration of Geometric Functionals
Alessia Caponera, Domenico Marinucci, Anna Vidotto

Abstract
In this paper, we show that geometric functionals (e.g., excursion area, boundary length) evaluated on excursion sets of sphere-cross-time long memory random fields can exhibit fractional cointegration, meaning that some of their linear combinations have shorter memory than the original vector. These results prove the existence of long-run equilibrium relationships between functionals evaluated at different threshold values; as a statistical application, we discuss a frequency-domain estimator for the Adler-Taylor metric factor, i.e., the variance of the field’s gradient. Our results are illustrated also by Monte Carlo simulations. • Keywords and Phrases: Sphere-cross-time Random Fields, Geometric Functionals, Long Memory, Fractional Cointegration • AMS Classification: 60G60; 62M10, 62M15, 62M40

1.1
Sphere-cross-time random fields and geometric functionals

We consider a centered, unit-variance, Gaussian isotropic and stationary
sphere-cross-time random field, that is a collection
\{Z(x,t),\ (x,t)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}\}
of jointly Gaussian random variables with
unit variance and such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z(x,t)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z(x_{1},t_{1})Z(x_{2},t_{2})]

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\Gamma(\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle,t_{2}-t_{1}),

with
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
being the standard inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. It is well-known (see, e.g.,
[
19
]
) that the space
of square-integrable functions on the sphere admits as an orthonormal basis
the fully-normalized spherical harmonics

\left\{Y_{\ell m},\ m=-\ell,\dots,\ell,\ \ell=0,1,2,\dots\right\}\text{ },

from which it is possible to obtain the following spectral representation

Z(x,t)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell m}(t)Y_{\ell m}(x)\,,

(1)

where
\left\{a_{\ell m}(t):=\langle Z,Y_{\ell m}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}\right\}_{\ell=0,1,...,m=-\ell,...,\ell}
are the so called
(random) spherical harmonics coefficients; under isotropy, for any given
\ell
the processes
a_{\ell m}(.)
are independent and have the same law,
for all
m=-\ell,...,\ell
. The representation
1

called the multipole expansion of the field

\ell

\ell

is also called a multipole when no confusion is possible.

We assume that there exists at least a
\ell

a_{\ell m}(.)
are long memory, meaning that their covariance
function
E[a_{\ell m}(t_{2})a_{\ell m}(t_{1})]=C_{\ell}(t_{2}-t_{1})
is

\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\frac{C_{\ell}(\tau)}{C_{\ell}(0)(1+\tau)^{2d_{\ell}-1}}=const>0\,,\quad 0<d_{\ell}<\frac{1}{2}\,.

In particular, note that
d_{\ell}>d_{\ell^{\prime}}
implies that the
multipole
\ell
has “longer memory” than the multipole
\ell^{\prime}
- i.e., covariances that decay at a slower rate. As a consequence, we will
assume that there exists

d_{\ast}:=\max\{d_{\ell}:\ell\in\widetilde{\mathbb{N}},\ell\geq 1\},\quad\text{ with }\quad\widetilde{\mathbb{N}}:=\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}:C_{\ell}(0)\neq 0\}\,,

and we are going to denote by

\widetilde{d}_{\ast}\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
). Essentially, the covariance

\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{I}^{\ast}}^{\infty}Z_{\ell}(x,t)=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{I}^{\ast}}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell m}(t)Y_{\ell m}(x).

We are interested in the following local geometric functionals, that are
just the area and the boundary length of the

We call

1.2
Memory of the field
vs
memory of the functionals

Before going into the main results of our work, let us discuss briefly the
relationship between the dependence properties of the primitive random field

Z=\{Z(x,t),\ (x,t)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}\}
and the memory of
the geometric functionals considered here. Recall that the covariance decay
of
Z
is completely determined by the multipoles
Z_{\ell}
such that
d_{\ell}=\widetilde{d}_{\ast}
, that is

Z_{\ast}(x,t)=\sum_{\ell:d_{\ell}=\widetilde{d}_{\ast}}Z_{\ell}(x,t)

and hence its order of integrations is simply given by
\widetilde{d}_{\ast}
, that is

Z(x,t)\sim I\left(\widetilde{d}_{\ast}\right)\sim Z_{\ast}(x,t)\,.

On the other hand, we have that our geometric functionals can be written as
a sum of their chaotic components, as we have seen in (
2
) and (
3
). Hence, in order to
establish the memory of
A(u,t)
and
\mathcal{L}(u,t)
we investigate the
covariance decay of their chaotic components. Both the first chaoses of
A(u,t)
and
\mathcal{L}(u,t)
are given by some constants (involving the
level
u
) times the zeroth Fourier components of the original field
Z(x,t)
, that is
a_{00}(t)
; as a consequence, it is immediate to get that

A(u,t)[1]\,,\,\mathcal{L}(u,t)[1]\sim I(d_{0})\,.

For the
q
-th order chaotic component,
q\geq 2
, which involves integrals
of
q
-th order Hermite polynomials evaluated at
Z(x,t)
, it holds

A(u,t)[q]\,,\,\mathcal{L}(u,t)[q]\sim I\left(q\,\widetilde{d}_{\ast}-\frac{q-1}{2}\right)\,.

(4)

This is a consequence of the so-called
diagram formula
(see
[
19
, Proposition 4.15]
), which relates the moments of Hermite
polynomials of Gaussian random variables with powers of their covariances;
in our case we obtain:

\mathbb{E}\left[H_{q}(Z(x,t))H_{q}(Z(y,s))\right]=q!\,\mathbb{E}\left[Z(x,t)Z(y,s)\right]^{q}\,.

Since, for any fixed
d\in(0,1/2)
,
q\,d-(q-1)/{2}
is a decreasing
function in
q
, the order of integration of
A(u,t)
and
\mathcal{L}(u,t)

is determined either by the relationship between
d_{0}
and
2\,d_{\ast}-1/2
(in the cases in which the second order chaotic component do not
vanish) or by the one between
d_{0}
and
3d_{\ast}-1
. We are neglecting
for brevity a further case, namely the one of asymptotically monochromatic
random fields (see
[
22
]
and below) where a cancellation at some
specific threshold level
u^{\ast}
introduces two further alternatives for
the boundary length.

We are now in the position to establish our main results.

Main Results

2.1
Fractional cointegration of geometric functionals

Let us define the vector processes

\displaystyle A(u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}};t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(A(u_{1};t),...,A(u_{p_{1}};t))^{\prime}\,,\quad u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}}\in\mathbb{R}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{L(}v_{1},...,v_{p_{2}};t\mathcal{)}

\displaystyle\mathcal{=}

\displaystyle\mathcal{(L(}v_{1};t\mathcal{)},...,\mathcal{L(}v_{p_{2}};t\mathcal{))}^{\prime}\,,\quad v_{1},...,v_{p_{2}}\in\mathbb{R}\,.

Let us now introduce some more notation, namely the (
p_{1}-1)\times p_{1}

matrices

\Gamma_{1}(u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}}):=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&-\frac{\phi(u_{1})}{\phi(u_{2})}&0&...&0\\
1&0&-\frac{\phi(u_{1})}{\phi(u_{3})}&...&0\\
...&....&...&...&...\\
1&0&...&...&-\frac{\phi(u_{1})}{\phi(u_{p_{1}})}\end{array}\right)\,\text{\ },

and

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}(u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}})=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&-\frac{u_{1}\phi(u_{1})}{u_{2}\phi(u_{2})}&0&...&0\\
1&0&-\frac{u_{1}\phi(u_{1})}{u_{3}\phi(u_{3})}&...&0\\
...&....&...&...&...\\
1&0&...&...&-\frac{u_{1}\phi(u_{1})}{u_{p_{1}}\phi(u_{p_{1}})}\end{array}\right)\,.

Our first results are the following

Proposition 1

For
u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}}\in\mathbb{R}\backslash 0,
and
d_{0}>2d_{\ast}-\frac{1}{2},
the vector process
A(u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}};t)

is fractionally cointegrated of rank
p_{1}-1
and order:

CI(d_{0},d_{0}-(2d_{\ast}-{1}/{2})).

For
u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}}\in\mathbb{R}\backslash 0,
and
d_{0}<2d_{\ast}-\frac{1}{2},
the vector process
A(u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}};t)
is fractionally
cointegrated of rank
p_{1}-1
and order:

CI(2d_{\ast}-{1}/{2},(2d_{\ast}-{1}/{2})-[d_{0}\vee(3d_{\ast}-1)]).

The cointegrating space is spanned by the vectors given by the rows of
\ \Gamma_{1}(u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}})
and
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}(u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}}),
respectively in the first and second case.

Proof.
First assume that
d_{0}>2d_{\ell^{\ast}}-\frac{1}{2}
. Take the first row
of
\Gamma_{1}
, that is the
1\times p_{1}
vector

\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{1}(u_{1},u_{2}):=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&-\frac{\phi(u_{1})}{\phi(u_{2})}&0&\cdots&0\end{array}\right)\,,

then

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}A(u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}};t)

\displaystyle=A(u_{1},t)-\frac{\phi(u_{1})}{\phi(u_{2})}A(u_{2},t)\,.

Exploiting the chaos expansion of the geometric functional
A(u;t)
, that is (
2
), we have

\displaystyle A(u_{1},t)-\frac{\phi(u_{1})}{\phi(u_{2})}A(u_{2},t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[A(u_{1},t)]+\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\phi(u_{1})a_{00}(t)+\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}A(u_{1},t;q)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\phi(u_{1})}{\phi(u_{2})}\left[\mathbb{E}[A(u_{2},t)]+\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\phi(u_{2})a_{00}(t)+\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}A(u_{2},t;q)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[A(u_{1},t)]-\frac{\phi(u_{1})}{\phi(u_{2})}\mathbb{E}[A(u_{2},t)]+\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}A(u_{1},t;q)-\frac{\phi(u_{1})}{\phi(u_{2})}\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}A(u_{2},t;q)\,.

Since the first chaotic components
1/\sqrt{4\pi}\phi(u_{i})a_{00}(t)\sim I(d_{0})
of the excursion areas at different levels
u_{i},\,i=1,\dots,p_{1}
have been cancelled by the premultiplication by
\gamma_{1}
, it is clear that the order of integration of
\gamma_{1}A(u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}};t)
is determined by the second chaotic components of
the remaining terms, that is

\gamma_{1}A(u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}};t)\sim I\left(2d_{\ast}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\,.

Now assume that
d_{0}<2d_{\ell^{\ast}}-\frac{1}{2}
. Take the first row of

\widetilde{\Gamma}
, that is the
1\times p_{1}
vector

\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}=\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}(u_{1},u_{2}):=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&-\frac{u_{1}\phi(u_{1})}{u_{2}\phi(u_{2})}&0&\cdots&0\end{array}\right)\,,

then

\displaystyle\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}A(u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}};t)=A(u_{1};t)-\frac{u_{1}\phi(u_{1})}{u_{2}\phi(u_{2})}A(u_{2};t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[A(u_{1};t)]-\frac{u_{1}\phi(u_{1})}{u_{2}\phi(u_{2})}\mathbb{E}[A(u_{2};t)]+\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\phi(u_{1})\left(1-\frac{u_{1}}{u_{2}}\right)a_{00}(t)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\phi(u_{1})}{6}\left[H_{2}(u_{1})-\frac{u_{1}}{u_{2}}H_{2}(u_{2})\right]\int_{S^{2}}H_{3}(Z(x,t))dx+R_{4}(u_{1},u_{2})\,,

where
R_{4}(u_{1},u_{2})
is just the sum of the chaotic components of order
q\geq 4
. Since the second chaotic components of the excursion areas at
different levels
u_{i},\,i=1,\dots,p_{1}
have been cancelled by the
premultiplication by
\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}
, it is clear that the order of
integration of
\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}A(u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}};t)
is determined
by either the first or the third chaotic components of the remaining terms,
that is (see also Section
1.2
)

\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}A(u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}};t)\sim I(d_{0}\vee 3d_{\ast}-1)\,,

and this concludes the proof.

It is important to stress that fractional cointegrating relationships are
not unique - not only in the sense that they actually live in a
p_{1}-1

dimensional space spanned by the vectors we listed in Proposition
1
, but also in a more subtle sense. Indeed, there can exist stronger
cointegrating relationships, meaning linear combinations that have shorter
and shorter memory. More precisely, in the next proposition we show that for
a suitably large vector evaluated at different thresholds, there always
exist a cointegrating relationship yielding short memory residuals. Indeed,
define the
(q_{\ast}+1)\times p_{1}
matrix, with
p_{1}\geq q_{\ast}+1
,

X_{A(u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}};t)}^{q_{\ast}}=X_{A}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\phi(u_{1})&\cdots&\phi(u_{p_{1}})\\
\phi(u_{1})H_{1}(u_{1})&\cdots&\phi(u_{p_{1}})H_{1}(u_{p_{1}})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\phi(u_{1})H_{q_{\ast}}(u_{1})&\cdots&\phi(u_{p_{1}})H_{q_{\ast}}(u_{p_{1}})\end{array}\right)\,;

we have the following statement.

Proposition 2

Let

q_{\ast}=\left[\frac{1}{1-2d_{\ast}}\right]+1\,,

where
[\cdot]
denotes the integer part; then any
p_{1}\times 1
vector
\gamma_{2}
such that
\gamma_{2}^{\prime}X_{\mathcal{A}}=0
is
cointegrating: in particular,

\gamma_{2}^{\prime}A(u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}};t)\sim I(0).

Proof.
Recall the chaotic expansion of the area functionals, which is given by

A(u)=\mathbb{E}[A(u)]+\phi(u)J_{0}+H_{1}(u)\phi(u)J_{1}+H_{2}(u)\phi(u)J_{2}+H_{3}(u)\phi(u)J_{3}+\cdots\,,

where
J_{k}=\frac{1}{(k+1)!}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}H_{k+1}(Z(x,t))\,dx
. Let
\gamma_{2}
be a
p_{1}\times 1
vector such that
\gamma_{2}^{\prime}X_{\mathcal{A}}=0
, then

\gamma_{2}^{\prime}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\phi(u_{1})H_{k-1}(u_{1})\\
\phi(u_{2})H_{k-1}(u_{2})\\
\vdots\\
\phi(u_{p_{1}})H_{k-1}(u_{p_{1}})\end{array}\right)=0\,,\quad\mbox{for all}\quad k=1,...,q+1\,.

As a consequence, denoting
\gamma_{2}:=(\gamma_{2,1}\quad\cdots\quad\gamma_{2,p_{1}})^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}^{\prime}A(u_{1},...,u_{p_{1}};t)=\gamma_{2}^{\prime}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}A(u_{1},t)\\
A(u_{2},t)\\
\vdots\\
A(u_{p_{1}},t)\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{2,1}A(u_{1},t)+\cdots+\gamma_{2,p_{1}}A(u_{p_{1}},t)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{2}^{\prime}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbb{E}[A(u_{1})]\\
\mathbb{E}[A(u_{2})]\\
\vdots\\
\mathbb{E}[A(u_{p_{1}})]\end{array}\right)+J_{0}\,\gamma_{2}^{\prime}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\phi(u_{1})\\
\phi(u_{2})\\
\vdots\\
\phi(u_{p_{1}})\end{array}\right)+\cdots+J_{q^{\ast}}\,\gamma_{2}^{\prime}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\phi(u_{1})H_{q^{\ast}}(u_{1})\\
\phi(u_{2})H_{q^{\ast}}(u_{2})\\
\vdots\\
\phi(u_{p_{1}})H_{q^{\ast}}(u_{p_{1}})\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma_{2}^{\prime}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\sum_{q\geq q_{\ast}+1}A(u_{1},t;q)\\
\sum_{q\geq q_{\ast}+1}A(u_{2},t;q)\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{q\geq q_{\ast}+1}A(u_{p_{1}},t;q)\end{array}\right)

=\gamma_{2}^{\prime}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbb{E}[A(u_{1})]\\
\mathbb{E}[A(u_{2})]\\
\vdots\\
\mathbb{E}[A(u_{p_{1}})]\end{array}\right)+\gamma_{2}^{\prime}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\sum_{q\geq q_{\ast}+1}A(u_{1},t;q)\\
\displaystyle\sum_{q\geq q_{\ast}+1}A(u_{2},t;q)\\
\vdots\\
\displaystyle\sum_{q\geq q_{\ast}+1}A(u_{p_{1}},t;q)\end{array}\right)\sim I(0)\quad\text{by construction}.

Indeed, one has that

\displaystyle\text{Cov}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{q\geq q_{\ast}+1}A(u,0;q),\displaystyle\sum_{q\geq q_{\ast}+1}A(u,\tau;q)\right)

\displaystyle\approx C_{\ell}(\tau)^{q_{\ast}+1}\simeq\left(C_{\ell}(0)(1+\tau)^{2d_{\ast}-1}\right)^{q_{\ast}+1}\,,

which is integrable since
q_{\ast}=\left[(1-2d_{\ast})^{-1}\right]+1
.

2.2
The averaged periodogram and a conjecture

In this section, we present the averaged periodogram estimator introduced by
Robinson in 1994, see
[
26
]
, and then we present a conjecture
about the extension of those results to Hermite polynomial transforms of
stationary long memory Gaussian processes.

Consider a centered process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
whose covariance
function is given by
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}X_{s}]=\rho(t-s)
and spectral
density
f_{X}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{\tau=-\infty}^{\infty}\rho(\tau)\cos(\tau\lambda).
Let us recall the spectral distribution
function

F_{X}(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\lambda}f(\theta)d\theta

and the averaged periodogram

\widehat{F}_{X}(\lambda)=\frac{2\pi}{T}\sum_{j=1}^{[T\lambda/2\pi]}I_{X}(\lambda_{j})

where
[\cdot]
denotes the integer part,
\lambda_{j}:=2\pi j/T
, and we
have introduced also the sample periodogram, given by

I_{X}(\lambda)=|w_{X}(\lambda)|^{2}\qquad w_{X}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}\exp(-i\lambda t)\text{ .}

Now we need to introduce a few regularity conditions, which follow
[
26
]
.

•

Condition A.
(
Long Memory Behaviour
) For some
d\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
,

f_{X}(\lambda)\simeq L\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\lambda^{-2d},

where
L(\lambda)
is a slowly varying function at infinity, see
[
4
]
.

•

Condition B.
(
Bandwidth Condition
)
\frac{1}{m}+\frac{m}{T}\rightarrow 0
as
T\rightarrow\infty
.

•

Condition C.
(
Linearity
) The process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
has the following Wold representation

X_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}e_{t-j}\,,\qquad\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}^{2}<\infty

where

(i)

\mathbb{E}[e_{t}e_{s}]=0\,,\quad t>s
;

(ii)

\mathbb{E}[e_{u}e_{v}e_{t}e_{s}]=\begin{cases}\sigma^{4}&\quad t=s>u=v\\
0&\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases};

(iii)

There exists a nonnegative random variable
e
such that, for
all
\varepsilon>0
and some
K<1
,

\mathbb{E}[e^{2}]<\infty\qquad\mathbb{P}(|e_{t}|>\varepsilon)\leq K\,\mathbb{P}(|e|>\varepsilon);

(iv)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[e_{t}^{2}|e_{s}^{2}\,,\,\,s<t\right]\rightarrow_{p}\sigma^{2}\quad\text{as}\quad T\rightarrow\infty;

We are in the position to recall the following result, which was given in

[
26
, Theorem 1]
:

Theorem 6

(See
[
26
]
) Let Conditions A, B and C hold. Then

\frac{\widehat{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}{F_{X}(\lambda_{m})}\rightarrow_{p}1\qquad\text{as}\quad T\rightarrow\infty.

Heuristically, the result can be illustrated as follows: it is classical in
time series analysis that a form of locally averaged periodogram converges
under regularity conditions to the spectral density. In the case of long
range dependence, these results can not even be formulated, as the spectral
density diverges at the origin; however, it can be still shown that the sum
of the periodogram ordinates converge to the integral of the spectral
density, in a neigbourhood of the origin.

Now consider our Gaussian isotropic and stationary sphere-cross-time random
field
\{Z(x,t),\ (x,t)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}\}
and assume
that

\widetilde{d}_{\ast}\in\left(\frac{q-1}{2q},\frac{1}{2}\right)\qquad\text{with}\quad\widetilde{d}_{\ast}=d_{\ast}\vee d_{0}\,.

Denote by

\mathcal{X}_{t}^{q}:=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}H_{q}\left(Z(x,t)\right)dx

and consider the corresponding averaged periodogram

\widehat{F}_{q}(\lambda)=\frac{2\pi}{T}\sum_{j=1}^{[T\lambda/2\pi]}I_{q}(\lambda_{j})

where

I_{q}(\lambda):=|w_{q}(\lambda)|^{2}\qquad w_{q}(\lambda):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{X}_{t}^{q}\exp(-i\lambda t)\text{ .}

Using the diagram formula, (see
[
19
, Proposition 4.15]
and also
Section
1.2
), we have immediately

\displaystyle\rho_{q}(\tau)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{X}_{0}^{q}\mathcal{X}_{\tau}^{q}\right]=q!\,\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Gamma(\langle x,y\rangle,\tau)^{q}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\simeq L_{q}(1+\tau)^{q(2d_{\ast}-1)}=L_{q}(1+\tau)^{2d_{q}-1}\text{ }

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad d_{q}

\displaystyle:=q\widetilde{d}_{\ast}-\frac{q-1}{2}

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad L_{q}

\displaystyle=q!\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Bigg(\sum_{\ell:d_{\ell}=\widetilde{d}_{\ast}}(2\ell+1)C_{\ell}(0)P_{\ell}(\langle x,y\rangle)\Bigg)^{q}dx\,dy\text{.}

Let us also recall the following Tauberian-type results (see
[
4
, Theorem
4.1.10]
). As usual, we say that a function is slowly-varying if and
only if

\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\frac{L(c\ell)}{L(\ell)}=1\text{ , for all }c>0\text{ .}

Theorem 7

(See
[
4
]
) Assume
L(\cdot)
is slowly varying and

\rho(\tau)\geq 0
for
\tau
large enough. Then we have that, if

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\frac{f(\lambda)}{\pi^{-1}\Gamma(2d)L(1/\lambda)\lambda^{-2d}\sin\left(\frac{\pi(1-2d)}{2}\right)}=1

then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{2d}{n^{2d}L(n)}\sum_{\tau=0}^{n}\rho(\tau)=1\text{ .}

Moreover, assuming that

\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 1}\liminf_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\min_{\tau\leq\tau^{\prime}\leq\eta\tau}\frac{\rho(\tau^{\prime})-\rho(\tau)}{\tau^{2d-1}L(\tau)}\geq 0

then

\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\rho(\tau)}{L(\tau)\tau^{2d-1}}=1\text{ .}

Remark 8

Note that the condition on the autocovariances is trivially satisfied if
they are monotonically decreasing. Corollary 4.10.2 in the same reference
implies that if
\rho(\tau)
is monotonic then the converse statement is
also true.

Using the previous Theorem
7
, we have that

f_{q}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{\tau=-\infty}^{\infty}\rho_{q}(\tau)e^{-i\tau\lambda}\simeq\pi^{-1}\Gamma(2d_{q})L_{q}\lambda^{-2d_{q}}\sin({\pi(1-2d_{q})}/{2})\lambda^{-2d_{q}}

and hence we define

{F}_{q}(\lambda)=\pi^{-1}\Gamma(2d_{q})L_{q}\lambda^{-2d_{q}}\sin({\pi(1-2d_{q})}/{2})\int_{0}^{\lambda_{m}}\lambda^{-2d_{q}}d\lambda\text{ .}

Note that, given the assumption
q>\left(1-2\widetilde{d}_{\ast}\right)^{-1}
, the process
\{\mathcal{X}_{t}^{q}\}_{t}
is still long memory,
since this implies that
0<d_{q}<\frac{1}{2}\,
. However, it should be noted
that the “Hermite” processes that we are
considering in this paper satisfy Condition C in
[
26
]
only
for
q=1
; otherwise, they are characterized by a nonlinear structure which
does not necessarily fulfill such a condition. Let us define

\widehat{F}_{q}(\lambda_{m})=\frac{2\pi}{T}\sum_{j=1}^{[T\lambda_{m}/2\pi]}I_{q}(\lambda_{j})=\frac{1}{T^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{t,s=1}^{T}\mathcal{X}_{t}^{q}\mathcal{X}_{s}^{q}e^{-i\lambda_{j}(t-s)}

We believe that a result analogous to Theorem 1 in
[
26
]
holds
in this generalized setting as well; in particular, we propose the following

Conjecture 9

Under Condition B and the previous assumptions and notations on
Z(x,t)
, we have that

\frac{\widehat{F}_{q}(\lambda_{m})}{F_{q}(\lambda_{m})}\rightarrow_{p}1\,.

We believe that Conjecture
9
can be proved along lines similar to
those in
[
26
]
. More precisely, the leading term in the second
moment of the previous ratio can be decomposed into two summands; the first
one corresponds to the squared expected value, which we expect to be
dominating, while the variance term can presumably be shown to be
o(1)
by
a careful analysis of cumulants of the Fourier transforms, by means of the
diagram formula, see
[
19
, Proposition 4.15]
. A full investigation
of this argument however seems demanding and it is beyond the purpose of
this paper; as we believe these results would have independent interest, we
leave them for further research.

In the next section we explore a statistical estimator for a crucial
parameter in sphere-cross-time random fields, under the assumption that the
previous conjecture holds.

2.3
Statistical applications: estimation of
\sigma_{1}

In this section, we show how the existence of geometric fractional
cointegrating relationships can be exploited to produce very simple and
practical estimators for the most important parameter when studying the
excursion sets of Gaussian random fields. In particular, it is well-known
that, under isotropy, the expected value of the boundary length for
isotropic random fields, and more generally the expected value for all
Lipschitz-Killing curvatures (thus including also the Euler-Poincaré
characteristic, which we do not study in this note) can be expressed in
terms of a single free parameter: the so-called first spectral moment of the
field. The latter corresponds to the variance of the first derivative of the
field itself; for our purposes it is sufficient to recall that the
Kac-Rice formula gives (see e.g.,
[
1
]
,
[
22
]
)

E[\mathcal{L(}u;t\mathcal{)]=\sigma}_{1}2\pi e^{-u^{2}/2},\qquad\sigma_{1}^{2}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}C_{\ell}\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}\,.

A natural way to estimate
\sigma_{1}
would then be to consider the
statistic

\widehat{\sigma}_{1}:=\frac{1}{2\pi e^{-u^{2}/2}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L(}u;t\mathcal{)}

which is clearly unbiased, with asymptotic variance of order

\text{Var}(\widehat{\sigma}_{1})=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}e^{-u^{2}}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{\tau=1-T}^{T-1}\left(1-\frac{|\tau|}{T}\right)\text{Cov}(\mathcal{L(}u;0\mathcal{)},\mathcal{L(}u;\tau\mathcal{))}\approx T^{2d_{0}-1}+T^{4d_{\ast}-2}.

However, this estimator need not be very efficient, in fact the previous
rates of convergence can become arbitrarily slow when
d_{0}
or
d_{\ast}

are close to
\frac{1}{2}
. Our idea is to introduce an alternative
estimator which, under Conjecture
9
, can lead to faster consistency
rates.

2.3.1
A consistent estimator under Conjecture
9

Now we must make the following distinction:

•

case a
.
d_{0}>2d_{\ast}-\frac{1}{2}
;

•

case b
.
d_{0}<2d_{\ast}-\frac{1}{2}
and there exists a unique
\ell=\ell^{\ast}
such that
d_{\ell}=d_{\ast}
.

Note that in
case a
the residuals are dominated by the second
chaos, whereas in
case b
by the first. Let us indeed recall the
chaotic expansions (
2
) and (
3
). In

case a
we rewrite them as follows:

\displaystyle A(u;t)-\mathbb{E}[A(u;t)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\phi(u)a_{00}(t)+R_{A}(u,t)\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u;t)-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(u;t)]

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}\sqrt{2}\pi u\phi(u)a_{00}(t)+R_{\mathcal{L}}(u;t)\,,

where
R_{A}(u,t):=\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}{A}(u,t;q)
and
R_{\mathcal{L}}(u;t)=\sum_{q=2}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}(u,t)[q]
.

Then, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L(}u;t\mathcal{)-}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L(}u;t\mathcal{)]}

\displaystyle=\beta(u,\sigma_{1})\left\{A(u,t)-\mathbb{E}[A(u,t)]\right\}+R_{\mathcal{L}}(u;t)-\beta(u,\sigma_{1})\,R_{A}(u,t)

where

\displaystyle\beta=\beta(u,\sigma_{1})

\displaystyle:=\sigma_{1}\sqrt{8}\pi^{3/2}u\,,

For brevity sake, now denote
X^{u}_{t}:=A(u;t)-\mathbb{E}[A(u;t)]
,
Y^{u}_{t}:=\mathcal{L(}u;t\mathcal{)-}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L(}u;t\mathcal{)]}
and
\varepsilon_{t}^{u}:=R_{\mathcal{L}}(u;t)-\beta\,R_{A}(u,t)
. We have hence
achieved the following regression form

Y^{u}_{t}=\beta X^{u}_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}^{u}\,,

with two main problems:

•

both the dependent variable and the regressor depend on an unknown
expected value, which is itself function of the unknown parameter
\sigma_{1}
;

•

the regressor and dependent variable are correlated.

Both problems are solved resorting to Narrow-Band Least Squares. Consider
indeed the Discrete Fourier Transforms

\displaystyle w_{Y_{t}^{u}}(\lambda_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}Y_{t}^{u}\exp(-i\lambda_{j}t)\,,

\displaystyle w_{X_{t}^{u}}(\lambda_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}^{u}\exp(-i\lambda_{j}t)\,,

and introduce the narrow-band least square estimator

\widehat{\beta}:=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{X_{t}^{u}}(\lambda_{j})\overline{w}_{Y_{t}^{u}}(\lambda_{j})}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{X_{t}^{u}}(\lambda_{j})\overline{w}_{X_{t}^{u}}(\lambda_{j})}\,.

which implies that a narrow-band least square estimator for
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}
is given by

\widehat{\sigma}_{1}(u):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{8}\pi^{3/2}u}\widehat{\beta}\,.

Assuming that Conjecture
9
holds, we have the following

Proposition 10

In
case a
, that is
d_{0}>2d_{\ast}-\frac{1}{2}
, we have, as
\frac{1}{m}+\frac{m}{T}\rightarrow 0
, that
the estimator
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}
is consistent and

|\widehat{\sigma}_{1}-\sigma_{1}|=O_{p}\left(\left(\frac{m}{T}\right)^{d_{0}-(2d_{\ast}-1/2)}\right)\,.

Proof.
We want to prove that
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}
is consistent, that is
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}\rightarrow\sigma_{1}
in probability, as
m,T\rightarrow\infty
.We have

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{X_{t}^{u}}(\lambda_{j})(\beta\overline{w}_{X_{t}^{u}}(\lambda_{j})+\overline{w}_{\varepsilon_{t}^{u}}(\lambda_{j}))}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}|w_{X_{t}^{u}}(\lambda_{j})|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\beta+\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{X_{t}^{u}}(\lambda_{j})\overline{w}_{\varepsilon_{t}^{u}}(\lambda_{j})}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}|w_{X_{t}^{u}}|^{2}}

and hence, following
[
26
, §5.3]
, we have

\displaystyle|\widehat{\beta}-\beta|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{X_{t}^{u}}(\lambda_{j})\overline{w}_{\varepsilon_{t}^{u}}(\lambda_{j})}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}|w_{X_{t}^{u}}|^{2}}\right|=\left|\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}I_{X\varepsilon}(\lambda_{j})}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}I_{X}(\lambda_{j})}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\widehat{F}_{X\varepsilon}(\lambda_{m})}{\widehat{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}\right|\leq\left\{\frac{\widehat{F}_{\varepsilon}(\lambda_{m})}{\widehat{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}\right\}^{1/2}\,,

where the last inequality follows from an application of Cauchy-Schwarz
inequality, that is

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\widehat{F}_{X\varepsilon}(\lambda_{m})}{\widehat{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{w_{X}(\lambda_{j})}{\sqrt{\widehat{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}}\frac{\overline{w}_{\varepsilon}(\lambda_{j})}{\sqrt{\widehat{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{w_{X}(\lambda_{j})\overline{w}_{X}(\lambda_{j})}{\widehat{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{w_{\varepsilon}(\lambda_{j})\overline{w}_{\varepsilon}(\lambda_{j})}{\widehat{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\widehat{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}{\widehat{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\widehat{F}_{\varepsilon}(\lambda_{m})}{\widehat{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}\right)^{1/2}=\left\{\frac{\widehat{F}_{\varepsilon}(\lambda_{m})}{\widehat{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}\right\}^{1/2}\,.

As a consequence, starting from the following inequality

|\widehat{\beta}-\beta|\leq\left\{\frac{\widehat{F}_{\varepsilon}(\lambda_{m})}{\widehat{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}\right\}^{1/2}=\left\{\frac{\widehat{F}_{\varepsilon}(\lambda_{m})}{{F}_{\varepsilon}(\lambda_{m})}\frac{{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}{\widehat{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}\right\}^{1/2}\left\{\frac{F_{\varepsilon}(\lambda_{m})}{F_{X}(\lambda_{m})}\right\}^{1/2}

and using the fact that (Conjecture
9
)

\frac{\widehat{F}_{\varepsilon}(\lambda_{m})}{{F}_{\varepsilon}(\lambda_{m})}\frac{{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}{\widehat{F}_{X}(\lambda_{m})}\rightarrow_{p}1

and

\left\{\frac{F_{\varepsilon}(\lambda_{m})}{F_{X}(\lambda_{m})}\right\}^{1/2}=\left\{\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\lambda_{m}}\lambda^{-2d_{\varepsilon}}d\lambda}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\lambda_{m}}\lambda^{-2d_{X^{u}}}d\lambda}\right\}^{1/2}=\lambda_{m}^{d_{X^{u}}-d_{\varepsilon}}=\left(\frac{2\pi\,m}{T}\right)^{d_{X^{u}}-d_{\varepsilon}}

where, by construction,

\varepsilon_{t}\sim I\left(2d_{\ast}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\qquad X_{t}^{u}\sim I(d_{0})

and hence
d_{\varepsilon}=2d_{\ast}-\frac{1}{2}
while
d_{X^{u}}=d_{0}
,
we have

|\widehat{\beta}-\beta|=O_{p}\left(\left(\frac{2\pi\,m}{T}\right)^{d_{0}-(2d_{\ast}-1/2)}\right)

which concludes the proof.

In
case b
, we fix the level

u=u^{\ast}:=\sqrt{2-\frac{\lambda_{\ell^{\ast}}/2}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}}\implies(u^{2}-1)+\frac{\lambda_{\ell}/2}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}=1\,,

and we rewrite the two chaotic expansions (
2
) and (
3
) as follows:

\displaystyle A(u^{\ast};t)-E[A(u^{\ast};t)]=u^{\ast}\phi(u^{\ast})(2\ell^{\ast}+1)\left\{\widehat{C}_{\ell^{\ast}}(t)-C_{\ell^{\ast}}\right\}+R_{A}(u^{\ast},t)^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u^{\ast};t)-E[\mathcal{L}(u^{\ast};t)]=\frac{\sigma_{1}}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\phi(u^{\ast})(2\ell^{\ast}+1)\left\{\widehat{C}_{\ell^{\ast}}(t)-C_{\ell^{\ast}}\right\}+R_{\mathcal{L}}(u^{\ast},t)^{\prime}

which implies

\mathcal{L}(u^{\ast};t)-E[\mathcal{L}(u^{\ast};t)]=\frac{\sigma_{1}}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{1}{u^{\ast}}\{A(u^{\ast};t)-E[A(u^{\ast};t)]\}+R_{\mathcal{L}}(u^{\ast},t)^{\prime}-\frac{1}{u^{\ast}}R_{A}(u^{\ast},t)^{\prime}

We have hence achieved the following regression form

Y^{u^{\ast}}_{t}=\alpha X^{u^{\ast}}_{t}+\eta_{t}^{u^{\ast}}\,,

where
\alpha=\alpha(\sigma_{1})=\frac{\sigma_{1}}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{1}{u^{\ast}}
and
\eta_{t}^{u^{\ast}}=R_{\mathcal{L}}(u^{\ast},t)^{\prime}-\alpha R_{A}(u^{\ast},t)^{\prime}
. Following what has been done for
case a
, we introduce the narrow-band least square estimator

\widehat{\alpha}:=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{X_{t}^{u^{\ast}}}(\lambda_{j})\overline{w}_{Y_{t}^{u^{\ast}}}(\lambda_{j})}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{X_{t}^{u^{\ast}}}(\lambda_{j})\overline{w}_{X_{t}^{u^{\ast}}}(\lambda_{j})}\,.

which implies that a narrow-band least square estimator for
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}
is given by

\widehat{\sigma}_{1}({u^{\ast}}):=2{u^{\ast}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\widehat{\alpha}\,.

Proposition 11

In
case b
, that is
d_{0}<2d_{\ast}-\frac{1}{2}
and there
exists a unique
\ell=\ell^{\ast}
such that
d_{\ell}=d_{\ast}
, we have, as
T,m\rightarrow\infty
, that the estimator
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}
is
consistent and

\widehat{|\sigma}_{1}-\sigma_{1}|=O_{p}\left(\left(\frac{m}{T}\right)^{(2d_{\ast}-1/2)-[d_{0}\vee(3d_{\ast}-1)\vee(2d_{\ast\ast}-1/2)]}\right)\,.

Proof.
Here, by construction, the residuals of the regression
\varepsilon_{t}^{u}

contains the first and third chaoses of
A(u^{\ast};t)
and
\mathcal{L}(u^{\ast};t)
, and the following term

u^{\ast}\phi(u^{\ast})\sum_{\ell\neq\ell^{\ast}}(2\ell+1)\left(\alpha\left[(u^{\ast})^{2}-1+\frac{\lambda_{\ell}/2}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\right]-1\right)\left\{\widehat{C}_{\ell}(t)-C_{\ell}\right\}

which are the multipoles of the second chaoses of the area and length
functionals that have shorter memories, and hence its order of integration
is
d_{0}\vee(3d_{\ast}-1)\vee(2d_{\ast\ast}-1/2)
which means

\varepsilon_{t}\sim I\left(d_{0}\vee(3d_{\ast}-1)\vee(2d_{\ast\ast}-1/2)\right)

As a consequence

\displaystyle|\widehat{\alpha}-\alpha|

\displaystyle=O_{p}\left(\left(\frac{m}{T}\right)^{(2d_{\ast}-1/2)-[d_{0}\vee(3d_{\ast}-1)\vee(2d_{\ast\ast}-1/2)]}\right)\,,

which concludes the proof.

Remark 12
(Estimation of the leading multipoles)

One example of application of the previous result is for random field which
are known to be monochromatic, for an unknown eigenvalue; in these
circumstances our proposal for the derivation of
\sigma_{1}
is equivalent
to the estimation of the dominating eigenvalue.

3
Numerical results

In this section, we validate our theoretical results with simulations.
Specifically, we consider a finite-rank random field

Z(x,t)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}a_{\ell m}(t)Y_{\ell m}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{S}^{2},\ t\in\mathbb{Z},

for some finite
L\geq 0
. Each
\{a_{\ell m}(t),\ t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is
modelled as the increments of a fractional Brownian motion (fBm) with Hurst
parameter
H_{\ell}=d_{\ell}+1/2,\ 0<d_{\ell}<1/2
. More precisely, for each
fixed multipole
\ell
, we consider
(2\ell+1)
independent zero-mean
Gaussian processes with covariance function

C_{\ell}(\tau)=\frac{\gamma(0)}{2}\left(|\tau+1|^{2H_{\ell}}+|\tau-1|^{2H_{\ell}}-2|\tau|^{2H_{\ell}}\right),\qquad\tau\in\mathbb{Z},

where
\gamma(0)=4\pi\big/\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}(2\ell+1)
—so that
the field has unit variance. It is immediate to see that this is a
stationary covariance function with memory parameter
d_{\ell}
, and that the
assumptions used throughout the paper are satisfied.

We choose
L=10
; moreover, we consider the case
d_{0}>2d_{*}-1/2
, by
choosing
d_{\ell}=0.3
for every
\ell\in\{0,\dots,L\}
. In other
words, we are assuming to have the same degree of memory at every multipole,
and we expect
\Gamma(1,|\tau|)\approx|\tau|^{-0.4}
as
\tau\to+\infty
. The same behaviour is also expected for the autocovariance of the
geometric functionals computed at a fixed level
u
.

On the other hand, if we consider, for instance, the cointegrated residuals

A(u_{1},u_{2};t)=A(u_{1},t)-\frac{\phi(u_{1})}{\phi(u_{2})}A(u_{2},t),\qquad t\in\mathbb{Z},

for given levels
u_{1},u_{2}
, we expect the corresponding autocovariance
function to decay as
|\tau|^{-0.8}
: loosely speaking, these residuals
have ”less memory” —this is indeed the definition of cointegration.

shows the excursions sets at given level
u=0.1

t=1,2,3,10.

(left) shows the evolution over time of the centered area at
levels
u_{1}=-0.1
and
u_{2}=0.1
, along with the corresponding
cointegrated residuals; the right panel shows the same for
u_{1}=-0.5
and

u_{2}=0.5
.

We validate these theoretical predictions by examining the empirical
autocovariance function

\hat{\rho}(\tau)=\frac{1}{T-\tau}\sum_{t=1}^{T-\tau}X(t)X(t+\tau),\qquad\tau=1,\dots,T-1,

where

X(t)=\begin{cases}Z(N,t),\\
A(u_{1},t)-\mathbb{E}[A(u_{1};t)],\\
A(u_{2},t)-\mathbb{E}[A(u_{2};t)],\\
A(u_{1},u_{2};t)-\mathbb{E}[A(u_{1},u_{2};t)].\end{cases}

Note that
\mathbb{E}[A(u_{j};t)]=4\pi\int_{u_{j}}^{\infty}\phi(u)\,du
, for
j=1,2
, and
N
denotes the
North Pole
(although any other
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
can be chosen). There is a wide array of available estimators
for long memory parameters; for simplicity, we choose a log-regression on
the autocovariance function, i.e., we estimate the decay rate by solving the
following ordinary least squares problem

\min_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{\tau=1}^{q_{T}}\left(\log|\hat{\rho}(\tau)|-\alpha-\beta\log\tau\right)^{2},

with
q_{T}=\min\{\lfloor\log_{10}T\rfloor,T-1\}
.

In practice, for each
\ell
, we simulate
(2\ell+1)
i.i.d. fractional
Brownian motions with Hurst parameter
H_{\ell}=d_{\ell}+1=0.8
on the
discrete grid
\{0,1,\dots,T\}
, taking into account the normalisation
given by
\gamma(0)
. Then, we compute the increments of the differences
between the fBm realisations at time
t
and those at time
t-1
, for
t=1,\dots,T
. We set
T=1000
.

From these simulated coefficients, we use the Python packages
healpy

and
pynkowski
to obtain the field and the associated geometric
functionals for given threshold levels
u_{1}=-0.1
and
u_{2}=0.1
; finally,
we compute the realized empirical autocovariance function for each of these.
We repeat this routine
B=1000
times and average the results to obtain
more stable estimates: as a final step, we perform an ordinary least squares
log-regression, as described above.

; we can observe that the
estimates obtained from the simulations match the theoretical decay rates

|\tau|^{-0.4}
