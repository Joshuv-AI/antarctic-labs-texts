Implementing genetics clinic for hereditary cancer in resource-constrained settings: a narrative review
Authors: Archi Rungta, Akhil Kapoor, Gaurav Redkar, Ankita Rungta Kapoor, Bal Krishna Mishra, Anuj Gupta, Bipinesh Sansar, Amit Kumar, Zachariah Chowdhury, Shashikant Patne, Satyajit Pradhan, Rajiv Sarin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHereditary cancer syndromes, driven by pathogenic germline mutations such as those in BRCA1 and BRCA2 and mismatch repair genes, represent a critical but under-addressed frontier in cancer care in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including India. Genetic clinics—multidisciplinary platforms offering counselling, testing, and cascade screening—have emerged globally as foundational to precision oncology. However, their implementation in resource-constrained settings remains highly uneven, hindered by infrastructural gaps, socioeconomic barriers, and sociocultural complexities.ObjectiveThis narrative review critically synthesises the implementation landscape of genetics clinic for hereditary cancer care, with India as a contextual case. It offers actionable strategies for scaling equitable genetic services in low-resource settings.MethodsA targeted literature search was conducted, supplemented by policy documents and global guidelines (ASCO, IARC, WHO). Thirty-one empirical studies were reviewed. Studies were selected based on relevance to cancer genetics service delivery in clinical or public health settings, particularly in low-resource environments. A thematic synthesis approach was used to distil evidence across six domains: clinical value, operational frameworks, barriers to access, ethical and cultural challenges, public engagement, and delivery innovations.ResultsGenetics clinic demonstrate significant clinical and preventive impact through risk stratification, targeted therapy, and cascade testing. However, integration into routine care in LMICs is constrained by limited workforce capacity, poor awareness, financial inaccessibility, and weak policy frameworks. Ethical concerns—such as inadequate informed consent and fear of genetic discrimination—compound these barriers. Promising delivery models include tele-genetic counselling, mobile clinics, and decentralised integration into existing oncology services. Effective strategies combine institutional partnerships, legal safeguards, and culturally contextualised communication.ConclusionGenetics clinic hold transformative potential for hereditary cancer care in resource-limited settings. Achieving equitable implementation requires a locally adapted, ethically grounded, and policy-integrated approach. Investments in training, infrastructure, public education, and governance must align with community needs to enable sustainable genomic integration in LMIC health systems.Keywords: Hereditary cancer, Genetic counselling, Low-resource settings, BRCA, Cascade testing, Health equity, India

Introduction

Hereditary cancers, caused by germline mutations in cancer susceptibility genes, constitute a clinically significant but under-addressed fraction of India’s cancer burden. Globally, 5–10% of breast cancers are attributable to inherited mutations in BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes (Valencia et al. 2017), while Lynch syndrome accounts for approximately 3% of colorectal cancers and a similar proportion of endometrial cancers (Dong et al. 2020; Jiang et al. 2019; Kahn et al. 2019; Moreira et al. 2012). According to GLOBOCAN 2020, India reported over 1.3 million new cancer cases, more than 850,000 cancer-related deaths, and a five-year prevalence exceeding 2.7 million cases, with breast, oral cavity, and cervical cancers accounting for the highest incidence rates (Ferlay et al. 2024). Early identification of individuals at elevated genetic risk enables tailored treatment, prophylactic strategies, and cascade screening of at-risk family members—representing a shift from reactive treatment to precision prevention. In this context, genetics clinic have emerged globally as central pillars of hereditary cancer care—a model increasingly relevant for India and other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). LMICs are defined as countries with a gross national income (GNI) per capita between USD 1,136 and USD 4,465, with India classified within this group (World Bank 2025). Their inclusion is central to this review, as LMICs collectively face unique systemic and sociocultural challenges that hinder equitable delivery of hereditary cancer services.

In India, however, the clinical integration of hereditary cancer genomics remains limited. Public and professional awareness of inherited cancer syndromes is low, contributing to delays in diagnosis, fragmented treatment pathways, and ultimately missed opportunities for proactive intervention. Access to genetic counselling and testing is largely restricted to urban tertiary centres, impeded by high costs, limited infrastructure, and a shortage of trained professionals. For example, studies highlight poor awareness of genetic discrimination protections among Indian patients, persisting financial barriers to BRCA testing, and the absence of standardised training pathways for genetic counsellors. Furthermore, the absence of national guidelines, reimbursement mechanisms, and structured training programmes for the genetics workforce has limited equitable service delivery (Gopalakrishnan et al. 2024; Mohanty and Das 2011). These gaps are particularly acute in resource-limited regions, where broader health inequities already constrain access to specialised cancer care.

While prior reviews have examined specific aspects of hereditary cancer genetics—such as cascade testing, cost-effectiveness modelling, or ethical issues—most originate from high-income countries and provide limited contextual insight for LMICs. Few have attempted to integrate clinical, ethical, cultural, and infrastructural perspectives within a single framework, or to contextualise findings for India, where the health system faces distinctive challenges. Therefore, the objective of this narrative review is to synthesise relevant insights from published literature, policy frameworks, and clinical guidance to inform practical and ethical considerations in establishing genetics clinic for hereditary cancer care in resource-constrained settings, with India as a contextual case.

This article presents a narrative review of the current landscape, challenges, and strategies for implementing genetics clinic dedicated to hereditary cancer care in resource-constrained settings, with India as a contextual case study. By consolidating current knowledge and offering pragmatic recommendations, this work aims to inform clinical practice, shape health policy, and promote the equitable integration of genetic services into mainstream oncology care across India and other LMICs.

Materials and methods

While not a systematic review, sources were selected for their relevance to multidisciplinary cancer care in low-resource settings, particularly in India. This narrative approach was chosen due to the breadth of literature types and the need for thematic integration across clinical, infrastructural, and policy domains.

Literature search strategy

Databases searched included PubMed, Scopus, and Medline. The search was conducted between January 2025 to May 2025. Titles and abstracts were screened manually against inclusion criteria. Keywords and Boolean operators used in the search included: “genetics clinic” OR “hereditary cancer” OR “genetic counselling” AND “India” OR “low-resource settings” OR “implementation” OR “cascade screening” OR “oncogenetics”. In addition, relevant grey literature — such as policy briefs, clinical guidelines (e.g., ASCO, ACMG), and reports from global agencies (e.g., WHO, IARC) were included to supplement peer-reviewed academic sources.

This approach ensured that relevant implementation insights were not excluded solely on the basis of geography. Editorials, opinion pieces without supporting data, and duplicate studies were excluded.

Screening procedure

Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two authors (Archi Rungta, Akhil Kapoor) using a manual Excel-based tracking sheet. Conflicts regarding inclusion/exclusion were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted in cases of disagreement. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were then reviewed in detail by at least two authors. No automated screening platforms (e.g., Rayyan, Covidence) were used; the process was fully manual.

Data extraction

All studies that met inclusion criteria following full-text review were included in the extraction stage. “Selected sources” therefore refers to these included studies rather than a subset. Data were extracted manually into a structured template capturing author(s), year, country/setting, study design, focus area, and relevance to LMIC implementation.

Identification of key domains

Key domains for data extraction and synthesis—clinical applications, operational models, barriers, ethical issues, public engagement, and innovative delivery approaches—were informed by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) and prior studies in cancer genetics implementation. Domains were refined iteratively to ensure alignment with the review objective of informing practical and ethical considerations in establishing genetic clinics.

CFIR encompasses five major domains: intervention characteristics, outer setting, inner setting, characteristics of individuals, and process, which collectively inform the contextual determinants of implementation success (Damschroder et al. 2022). These domains guided the categorisation of findings within the present synthesis.

Thematic synthesis

Recurring concepts were identified inductively across included studies. For a code to be considered a recurring theme, it needed to appear in at least three empirical studies or in one authoritative guideline/policy document. This threshold ensured that findings reflected consistent trends rather than isolated reports. Data extraction was performed by two authors (Ankita Rungta Kapoor, Bal Krishna Mishra) and independently cross-checked by a third (Gaurav Redkar). Any discrepancies in interpretation were resolved through consensus discussion. Thematic synthesis was jointly conducted by the author team to ensure reliability of interpretation and to integrate multidisciplinary perspectives from oncology, genetics, and public health.

Quality considerations

As this was a narrative rather than systematic synthesis, no formal quality appraisal tool (e.g., CASP or PRISMA) was applied. Instead, the credibility and relevance of the sources were prioritised, including high-impact journals, recognised guideline publications, and peer-reviewed implementation studies. As this review included only English-language sources, there is a potential for publication and language bias, which may limit the inclusion of region-specific innovations or grey literature.

Results

This narrative review synthesised findings from 31 empirical studies on genetics clinic implementation for hereditary cancer care in diverse settings.

Study selection

The initial search identified 423 records across PubMed, Scopus, and Medline. After removal of duplicates (n = 62), titles and abstracts were screened (n = 361). Of these, 247 were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria (e.g., not addressing hereditary cancer genetics, not relevant to LMICs). The remaining 114 full-text articles were assessed, with 83 excluded due to being commentary/editorials, not available in English, or lacking implementation focus. Most studies were descriptive or qualitative in design, with a few interventional studies on tele-genetic counselling, patient satisfaction, or targeted therapies.

Clinical applications and population impact of genetics clinic

The reviewed literature strongly affirms the multidimensional clinical value of genetics clinic in hereditary cancer management. Genetic testing enables precision oncology by identifying pathogenic variants—most notably in BRCA1 and BRCA2 and mismatch repair (MMR) genes—that guide risk stratification, surveillance protocols, and eligibility for targeted therapies such as PARP inhibitors (Chiang et al. 2024; Domchek et al. 2016; Kuchenbaecker et al. 2017; Ledermann et al. 2012; Manchanda et al. 2015).

Beyond individual-level treatment, genetics clinic support early detection by identifying asymptomatic mutation carriers who can then undergo risk-reducing screening and prophylactic interventions. This has been associated with reduced morbidity, earlier stage at diagnosis, and improved survival rates (Domchek et al. 2016; Kuchenbaecker et al. 2017; Manchanda et al. 2015). Moreover, cascade testing—systematic testing of biological relatives of index cases—amplifies clinical and preventive benefits across families and communities (Chiang et al. 2024; Frey et al. 2022; Manchanda et al. 2015). Some modelling studies further indicate that cascade testing may be cost-effective in population-based screening programs (Frey et al. 2022; Manchanda et al. 2015).

This foundation of evidence highlights the clinical imperative for service expansion and leads into the next theme on implementation logistics.

Implementation frameworks and operational models

The literature outlines five foundational components necessary for establishing functional genetic clinics in cancer care settings. First, the clinic’s scope must be clearly defined. This includes its target populations, range of services (e.g., counselling, testing, follow-up), and degree of integration with oncology care pathways. Second, the establishment of multidisciplinary teams is crucial. These teams typically include oncologists, genetic counsellors, pathologists, molecular scientists, and data managers. Genomic multidisciplinary teams (MDTs), in particular, have been shown to improve case interpretation and enhance clinical decision-making (Auvin et al. 2019; Ma et al. 2024).

Third, standard operating procedures (SOPs) must be developed to guide testing protocols, variant interpretation, patient communication, and long-term follow-up. Studies show that clinics with defined SOPs exhibit greater safety, consistency, and reproducibility in care pathways (National Academies of Sciences, 2018; Taylor et al. 2019). Fourth, successful implementation depends on the availability of essential infrastructure—including next-generation sequencing platforms, electronic record systems, and sustainable funding sources (Scheuner et al. 2019). Finally, institutional partnerships with academic centres and clinical research networks help support ongoing training, data exchange, and protocol refinement. Models that foster transparency, governance clarity, and shared workflows are considered more scalable and resilient (National Academies of Sciences, 2018; Rehm et al. 2015).

While these frameworks offer structural clarity, real-world implementation faces persistent barriers, detailed next.

Barriers to genetic service integration

Despite clear benefits and structured models, significant barriers hinder the implementation and utilisation of genetic services in low-resource contexts. Limited public and provider awareness, poor genetic literacy, and cultural stigma around cancer often suppress demand. Health literacy barriers, particularly in rural populations, compromise the quality of informed consent and delay appropriate referrals. For instance, one study noted, “patient-provider communication, lack of awareness, and literacy gaps… contribute to disparities in genetic service delivery (Amendola et al. 2022)”.

Financial impediments are stark: one study found most low-income families could not afford even USD 250 for BRCA testing, with low-income individuals nearly half as likely to be tested (Hughes and Dillon 2024; Korte and Terry 2023). Ethical concerns, including fear of genetic discrimination in employment or insurance, persist amid limited public awareness of protective legislation (Gopalakrishnan et al. 2024; Hudson et al. 1995).

These factors intertwine and disproportionately impact marginalised populations. Understanding these obstacles sets the stage for a deeper exploration of ethical and cultural dimensions of access.

Ethical and cultural dimensions of genetic testing

Ethical and sociocultural issues are central—not peripheral—to the delivery of genetic services. Informed consent procedures, while widely practiced, often fail to address the complexity of multiplex testing, intergenerational implications, and cultural norms around risk disclosure. As one study observed, “it may be difficult to apply the current model of informed consent” in genomic contexts where multiple uncertain findings may arise (Institute of Medicine (US) Committee 1994; Zhong et al. 2021).

Privacy concerns and fear of data misuse are frequently cited deterrents to testing. Survey data reveal that fewer than 20% of participants are aware of genetic non-discrimination protections, even as over two-thirds express concern about insurers accessing their genetic data (Parkman et al. 2015). Reports of genetic discrimination have emerged from healthcare, workplace, and family contexts—especially when patients lack understanding of their legal rights (Geelen et al. 2012; Hudson et al. 1995).

Cultural interpretations of genetic risk also shape engagement. In some communities, hereditary risk is interpreted through fatalistic or religious lenses; in others, testing may conflict with beliefs around fate, gender roles, or communal responsibility (Zhong et al. 2021). These ethical challenges call for parallel action in education and outreach, discussed in the next section.

Strategies to enhance public engagement and acceptance

Enhancing public acceptance of genetic services requires multidimensional, community-rooted strategies. Educational interventions aimed at the general public have demonstrated measurable success. One initiative reached over 39,000 households, with nearly 10,000 individuals reporting increased understanding of hereditary cancer risks and screening options (Piniewski-Bond et al. 2003). A notable example is the GRACE Project, which used personalized remote risk communication combined with navigation support to significantly increase motivation and reduce barriers for cancer genetic services, especially in underserved populations (Kinney et al. 2023).

In parallel, provider-focused strategies—particularly training primary care physicians to act as referral agents—have yielded tangible improvements in cascade testing uptake, with some interventions increasing uptake from under 30% to nearly 70% when structured protocols were used (Chiang et al. 2024).

Financial assistance—via philanthropic coverage, insurance reforms, or government subsidies—is consistently identified as an enabler of testing uptake. For example, a Singaporean study found that free cascade testing increased uptake among first-degree relatives from 6.1% to 21.6%, highlighting cost as a significant barrier (Courtney et al. 2019). Community mobilisation, mass media, and patient advocacy networks help reduce stigma and promote family communication about genetic risk. Qualitative studies report that facilitated family communication increases willingness and knowledge transfer among relatives, especially in diverse and medically underserved communities (Bednar et al. 2024).

These efforts gain further momentum when integrated with decentralised service models, as seen below.

Innovative delivery models and decentralised access

Despite growing interest in genetic services, access remains unevenly distributed. Two primary models—tele-genetic counselling and mobile outreach clinics—have emerged as promising decentralised strategies. Tele-genetic counselling—delivered via video or phone—has demonstrated non-inferior outcomes compared to in-person counselling across satisfaction, emotional preparedness, and decision-making domains (Danylchuk et al. 2021; Zierhut et al. 2018). A randomised trial further confirmed that telephone-based services were equally effective in key psychosocial and clinical metrics (Hilgart et al. 2012; Peshkin et al. 2015).

Mobile genetics clinic, though less well-documented, have shown promise in increasing access among remote populations. Outreach programs that brought testing services directly into rural communities reported improvements in both engagement and follow-up (Hilgart et al. 2012; Senier et al. 2015). Collaborations with community health centres and integration of genetic services into existing national screening programs were also found to facilitate earlier identification and referral of at-risk individuals (Raspa et al. 2021).

However, the success of these models hinges on key enablers, including technological infrastructure, workforce training, cultural competency, and privacy safeguards. Without these, decentralised models risk replicating the same inequities they aim to dismantle.

Synthesis of main findings

Across the reviewed literature, there is a clear consensus that genetics clinic play a transformative role in hereditary cancer care. Their benefits span early diagnosis, targeted treatment and population-level risk reduction. However, successful implementation in low-resource settings depends on navigating significant barriers, including infrastructural constraints, financial limitations, and sociocultural dynamics. A patient-centred, ethically robust and context-sensitive implementation model is essential for ensuring the sustainability and equity of genetic services. Discussion

This narrative review offers an integrative evaluation of the implementation landscape for genetics clinic in hereditary cancer care across low-resource settings. Although few included empirical studies originated from India, we used India as the interpretive lens for transferability—triangulating global implementation evidence with India-specific policy and qualitative sources to derive context-appropriate implications (Gopalakrishnan et al. 2024; Government of India Ministry of Health & Family Welfare and Department of Health Research, 2024; Mohanty and Das 2011; National Cancer Grid 2021). While the reviewed literature affirms the clinical potential of genetics clinic—from facilitating precision oncology to enabling cascade testing and early risk stratification (Chiang et al. 2024; Kuchenbaecker et al. 2017; Manchanda et al. 2015) — it simultaneously exposes key gaps in system readiness, equity, and sustainability. These gaps are not merely logistical but reflective of deeper structural and epistemic challenges, including the dominance of Western implementation models, limited patient engagement, and insufficient attention to the sociopolitical context in which genomic services are delivered (Rehm et al. 2015; Zhong et al. 2021). This aligns with critiques from implementation science literature, including the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), which underscores the underrepresentation of the ‘Outer Setting’ domain—encompassing sociopolitical, economic, and regulatory influences—in genomic intervention research (Damschroder et al. 2022).

Our findings align with prior reviews demonstrating the clinical value of genetic clinics and the reach of decentralised models such as tele-genetics and outreach (Chiang et al. 2024; Danylchuk et al. 2021; Domchek et al. 2016; Frey et al. 2022; Hilgart et al. 2012; Kuchenbaecker et al. 2017; Ledermann et al. 2012; Manchanda et al. 2015; Peshkin et al. 2015; Raspa et al. 2021; Senier et al. 2015; Zierhut et al. 2018). The India-relevant difference is the persistence of cost barriers and urban concentration of services, workforce and governance gaps, and lower public/legal awareness regarding genetic discrimination—constraints that are less pronounced in high-income settings (Amendola et al. 2022; Geelen et al. 2012; Gopalakrishnan et al. 2024; Hughes and Dillon 2024; Korte and Terry 2023; National Cancer Grid 2021; Parkman et al. 2015; Zhong et al. 2021). The present review adds value by integrating clinical, organisational, ethical, and delivery considerations and translating them into pragmatic levers for the Indian context.

Genetics clinic and the risk of replicating inequities

Despite their promise, genetics clinic risk becoming instruments of exclusion when introduced into health systems marked by infrastructural stratification. Studies from India and other LMICs show that access remains concentrated in urban tertiary hospitals, while rural populations are underserved and often unaware of hereditary risk (Gopalakrishnan et al. 2024; Hughes and Dillon 2024; Senier et al. 2015). Recent data from India’s National Cancer Grid suggest that hereditary cancer services remain unevenly distributed across public oncology centres, with limited emphasis in strategic planning documents (National Cancer Grid 2021). Chiang et al. and Frey et al. argue that the effectiveness of cascade testing is significantly compromised when socioeconomic or geographic barriers limit initial patient access (Chiang et al. 2024; Frey et al. 2022). This challenges the assumption that expanding testing availability will translate to equitable uptake.

Equity-focused implementation must therefore go beyond expanding access to embedding structural reforms—such as subsidised testing, decentralised delivery, and legal safeguards against genetic discrimination. As highlighted in the literature, tele-genetics and community-based navigation have shown potential to overcome geographic barriers but require robust digital infrastructure and culturally competent design to be effective (Danylchuk et al. 2021; Hilgart et al. 2012; Peshkin et al. 2015; Raspa et al. 2021; Senier et al. 2015). Without these safeguards, decentralised models may unintentionally replicate the same inequities they seek to address. In India, these dynamics manifest as concentration of services in urban tertiary hubs, uneven availability across the National Cancer Grid network, and limited structured planning for hereditary services (National Cancer Grid 2021).

Implementation frameworks: from theory to practice

The implementation models identified in the Results section often draw from policy guidelines or conceptual frameworks developed in high-income settings (Auvin et al. 2019; Lehmann 2012; Ma et al. 2024; National Academies of Sciences, E. and M.H. and et al. 2018; Taylor et al. 2019). However, their translation into real-world contexts is limited. Several sources—including Auvin et al. and Taylor et al.—propose structured SOPs, MDT coordination, and integrated data platforms, but few provide empirical validation of these models in LMIC settings (Auvin et al. 2019; Taylor et al. 2019). This points to a common critique of implementation science in global health: the over-reliance on prescriptive toolkits without contextual grounding (Rehm et al. 2015; Zhong et al. 2021).

Moreover, much of the literature focuses on clinical and procedural infrastructure, while omitting essential enabling conditions such as public engagement, workforce development, and legal regulation. This fragmented focus underscores the need for implementation models that are iterative, co-designed with stakeholders, and continuously evaluated in situ (World Health Organization 2022). For India, embedding SOPs and data standards within National Cancer Grid pathways and operating hub-and-spoke genomic MDTs can bridge this theory–practice gap (Auvin et al. 2019; Ma et al. 2024; National Academies of Sciences, E. and M.H. and et al. 2018; National Cancer Grid 2021; Taylor et al. 2019).

Patient-centred genomic care: Moving beyond rhetoric

While patient-centred care is universally espoused in genomic medicine, its operationalisation remains inconsistent. Evidence from Zhong et al. and Bednar et al. demonstrates that emotional responses—such as anxiety, stigma, or fatalism—are frequently shaped by religious, communal, or familial worldviews, particularly in non-Western contexts (Bednar et al. 2024; Zhong et al. 2021). When genetic testing is offered without adequate emotional or cultural scaffolding, patients may misinterpret results, withdraw from follow-up, or avoid disclosing risk to relatives (Bednar et al. 2024; Hudson et al. 1995).

Shared decision-making frameworks and culturally attuned counselling have been shown to improve both understanding and engagement, yet these are not consistently incorporated into practice. The GRACE project exemplifies how tailored risk communication and navigation support can improve uptake, especially in underserved groups (Kinney et al. 2023). However, scale-up remains limited due to workforce shortages and insufficient training in culturally competent care. Indian studies on BRCA testing in tribal and rural populations similarly highlight the lack of culturally contextualised counselling (Mohanty and Das 2011). In India, consent and results materials should be local-language, low-literacy friendly, and explicitly address family implications, privacy, and available anti-discrimination protections; culturally contextualised counselling has been highlighted as especially important for tribal and rural communities (Geelen et al. 2012; Gopalakrishnan et al. 2024; Mohanty and Das 2011; Parkman et al. 2015; Zhong et al. 2021).

Evaluation gaps: the need for impact-driven metrics

One of the most critical weaknesses identified in the literature is the lack of longitudinal and outcome-focused evaluations of genetic clinics. While some studies highlight improved satisfaction and risk-reducing behaviours (Kinney et al. 2023; Peshkin et al. 2015), few measure long-term impact on morbidity, mortality, or cost-effectiveness—especially in the Indian context. Scheuner et al. and Domchek et al. emphasise that without real-world metrics on treatment pathways, system efficiency, or familial outcomes, clinics risk becoming pilot projects without sustained utility (Domchek et al. 2016; Scheuner et al. 2019).

This evidentiary gap is particularly concerning given resource constraints in LMICs. As Manchanda et al. show, cost-effectiveness modelling can justify population-based genetic screening, but such models require locally validated data on prevalence, uptake, and behavioural follow-through (Manchanda et al. 2015). However, these models often rely on assumptions from high-income settings, limiting their applicability in India where genomic infrastructure, cultural beliefs, and healthcare-seeking behaviours differ substantially.

Future studies should incorporate metrics such as time from risk identification to testing, proportion of relatives screened via cascade testing, and impact on cancer stage at diagnosis to assess real-world effectiveness. Priority Indian metrics include time from index diagnosis to testing, first-/second-degree relative cascade uptake, and stage at diagnosis among carriers identified through clinics, alongside service costs to inform payer decisions (Damschroder et al. 2022; Domchek et al. 2016; Manchanda et al. 2015; Scheuner et al. 2019).

Workforce capacity: the silent limitation

The literature consistently identifies workforce shortages as a major bottleneck (Hughes and Dillon 2024; Ma et al. 2024; Taylor et al. 2019). India currently lacks a nationally standardised certification for genetic counselling, and existing training programs remain geographically clustered and underfunded. Rehm et al. and Ma et al. highlight that without interprofessional collaboration and task-sharing with trained non-specialist providers, the burden will remain concentrated on already overstretched oncologists (Ma et al. 2024; Rehm et al. 2015).

Moreover, evidence from Lehmann and Parkman et al. suggests that lack of legal awareness and professional support structures discourages clinicians from taking on additional responsibilities in genetic care (Parkman et al. 2015; Rehm et al. 2015). These structural gaps must be addressed through formal accreditation systems, financial incentives, and inclusion of genomic content in medical and nursing curricula. India’s Department of Health Research has acknowledged this need in recent white papers on health workforce development(Government of India Ministry of Health & Family Welfare and Department of Health Research, 2024). Short-term relief can come from modular training and accreditation for genetic counsellors and task-sharing with oncology nurses/allied staff, aligned with national HRD schemes (Government of India Ministry of Health & Family Welfare and Department of Health Research, 2024).

Role of patient organisations in LMIC implementation

Patient organisations have been instrumental in shaping research agendas, health policy, and service design for genetic conditions in high-income economies—driving public awareness, peer support, and reimbursement change (Poortman et al. 2024). Interpreted for India and comparable LMICs, these groups can: (i) co-design culturally and linguistically appropriate consent and results materials; (ii) provide peer navigation that supports disclosure and cascade testing among relatives; (iii) run awareness campaigns to counter stigma and low legal-protection literacy; (iv) participate in governance forums (e.g., National Cancer Grid working groups) to inform SOPs and outcomes priorities; and (v) advocate for targeted subsidies/fee waivers for first-degree relatives. Practical next steps include establishing formal liaison pathways between oncology centres and patient groups, training peer navigators with standard scripts for family communication, and offering micro-grants for travel/testing in pilot cascade programmes. These partnerships can increase trust, improve uptake, and accelerate equitable scale-up without major new infrastructure.

Aligning genetics clinic with broader health system reform

Finally, the future of genetic clinics lies in integration—not just with oncology services, but with the broader digital and public health ecosystem. Data interoperability, EHR integration, and privacy-preserving data sharing are essential to enable pharmacogenomics, surveillance, and policy planning (Hudson et al. 1995; Rehm et al. 2015; Senier et al. 2015). As Hilgart et al. and Raspa et al. note, the success of these advancements will depend not only on technological readiness but also on clear governance, ethical frameworks, and public trust (Hilgart et al. 2012; Raspa et al. 2021).

In this context, initiatives such as India’s Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission could serve as platforms for genomic integration—if accompanied by legal safeguards and community participation. Cross-sectoral collaboration with pharmaceutical regulators, bioethics bodies, and HTA institutions will be crucial to ensure that advances in genetic science translate into tangible population health gains. Integration with India’s Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission, with clear privacy governance and interoperability, could enable longitudinal tracking and equitable access if paired with legal and community safeguards.

Strengths and limitations

This synthesis integrates clinical, infrastructural, and socio-ethical dimensions of hereditary cancer care and consolidates a diverse body of evidence across income settings. Its strength lies in the comprehensive thematic breadth and context-sensitive analysis. However, being narrative in nature, the review may not capture all eligible studies, especially unpublished or region-specific grey literature. Approximately two-thirds of the included studies originated from high-income countries, which may limit generalisability despite efforts at contextual adaptation. Furthermore, exclusion of non-English and unpublished grey literature may have omitted valuable local or informal initiatives, particularly those led by non-governmental or community-based programmes. Future research should include outcome-focused metrics such as the time from risk identification to genetic testing, uptake rates of cascade screening among relatives, and cancer stage at diagnosis post-intervention to assess real-world impact. Given the modest number of India-based empirical studies, Indian applicability was inferred by triangulating international evidence with Indian policy and qualitative literature, underscoring the need for local outcome evaluations (Gopalakrishnan et al. 2024; Government of India Ministry of Health & Family Welfare and Department of Health Research, 2024; Mohanty and Das 2011; National Cancer Grid 2021). Finally, the evidence base on patient organisation–led models in LMICs is sparse; our interpretations draw on high-income literature and warrant local evaluation (Poortman et al. 2024).

Conclusion

Genetics clinic are vital to modern cancer care, especially for hereditary cancer syndromes, offering early detection, personalised treatment, and family-based screening. However, their reach in resource-limited settings like India is hindered by structural, financial, workforce, and sociocultural challenges. This review highlights both the benefits and the practical and ethical complexities of establishing genetics clinic. Locally adapted, multidisciplinary models with culturally sensitive counselling, community engagement, and alignment with national policies are crucial. Future efforts should prioritise workforce training, telehealth, mobile clinics, genomic data integration, and national frameworks. Enhancing public awareness, addressing cultural barriers, and ensuring ethical safeguards are key to acceptance and sustainability. Realising the potential of genomic medicine in LMICs requires collaborative, accessible, and ethically grounded strategies for scaling genetic services. Interpreted through an Indian lens, scalable implementation hinges on SOP-driven workflows, hub-and-spoke genomic MDTs, navigator-supported cascade testing, tele-enabled counselling and outreach, targeted financing to lower price barriers, culturally attuned consent/privacy safeguards, and workforce expansion via national programmes.
