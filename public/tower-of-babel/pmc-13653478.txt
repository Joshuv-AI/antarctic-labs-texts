Genomics of multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates from community hospitals in Pretoria, South Africa
Authors: Mokawane Victor Mohlamoyane, Johann David Daniel Pitout, Tshepiso Innocent Lentswe, Sarah Liknaitzky, Kafilat Taiwo Salvador-Oke, Andrew Moultrie, Mishalan Moodley, Jonathan Featherston, Anthony Marius Smith, Arshad Ismail, Mohamed Said, Marthie Magdaleen Ehlers, Marleen Martha Kock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The sequence type 307 clone is highly prevalent in South African hospitals.•Pandrug ST307 high-risk clone with three carbapenemases is a public health threat.•Emerging double-carbapenemase producers reduce available treatment options.•Diverse plasmids drive antibiotic resistance in Klebsiella pneumoniae, enabling spread.•No convergence of multidrug resistance and hypervirulence.

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) is a major clinical bacterial pathogen responsible for a variety of nosocomial infections, often associated with high morbidity and mortality [1]. It has emerged as a significant public health concern due to increasing levels of antibiotic resistance (ABR), with limited treatment options [2]. The production of carbapenemases and extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) has largely contributed to the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and pandrug-resistant (PDR) bacterial clones, conferring resistance to β-lactams and other antibiotic groups [3]. The most clinically significant carbapenemases include K. pneumoniae carbapenemase (KPC), New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM), Verona integron-encoded metallo-β-lactamase, imipenemase, and oxacillinase-48-like, whereas ESBLs include cefotaximase-Munich (CTX-M) [4,5].

The global expansion of K. pneumoniae high-risk clones fuels the rapid spread of carbapenemase and ESBL genes. Major MDR K. pneumoniae high-risk clones include sequence types (STs) 14, 15, 101, 147, 307 and those belonging to clonal group 258 (ST11, ST258, and ST512) [6,7]. In addition, an emerging convergence of multidrug resistance and hypervirulence has been reported in various K. pneumoniae clones, resulting in MDR hypervirulent strains [7]. The K. pneumoniae ST307 clone is a well-characterized clone that is endemic in South African hospitals and is associated with the spread of blaOXA-181 on incompatibility (Inc)X3 plasmids [8,9]. Plasmids play an important role in the horizontal transfer of ABR genes (antimicrobial resistance genes [ARGs]), including carbapenemases and ESBLs, between different clones [10].

The prevalence of carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae (CPKP) and ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae (ESBL-Kp) remains high in South Africa, with a 35.2% increase in carbapenemase genes observed in clinical K. pneumoniae isolates from Africa between 2021 and 2023 [11,12]. There is limited information on the clonal diversity and molecular characteristics of CPKP and ESBL-Kp isolates from community hospitals in Pretoria, South Africa. Theis study aimed to determine the prevalence of ST307 (relative to non-ST307), clonal diversity, and molecular characteristics of ESBL-Kp and CPKP isolates from community hospitals in Pretoria, South Africa, linked to multidrug resistance phenotypes (MDR, XDR, and PDR) using polymerase chain reaction (PCR)–based assays and whole genome sequencing (WGS).

Methods

Study setting and isolate collection

This retrospective, descriptive, laboratory-based study characterized antibiotic-resistant K. pneumoniae isolates collected from private diagnostic laboratories serving community hospitals in Pretoria, South Africa. The study was conducted at the Department of Medical Microbiology, University of Pretoria. A total of 152 non-duplicate, non-consecutive antibiotic-resistant K. pneumoniae isolates stored at the University of Pretoria culture bank (obtained from previous studies) were included. The isolates were collected over a 2-year period (between January 26, 2021 and November 3, 2022) from nine hospitals in Pretoria and originated from non-invasive sources and invasive infections. A subset of 34 ESBL-Kp and 118 CPKP from patients in these hospitals were selected based on the presence of at least one ESBL or carbapenemase gene. Initial species identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) were performed using the VITEK 2 system (bioMérieux, France). Microbroth dilution was used to confirm colistin minimum inhibitory concentrations. Purity was confirmed by culturing on MacConkey agar (Oxoid, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and Gram staining. Associated data, including patient demographics, AST, and PCR screening for ESBL and carbapenemase genes, were collected. The AST data were interpreted using the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute 2020 guidelines. Standard definitions were used to classify MDR, XDR, and PDR [13].

Genomic deoxyribonucleic acid extraction

Genomic deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) was extracted using the ZR Fungal/Bacterial DNA MiniPrep Kit (Zymo Research, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA purity and concentration were assessed using the Nano spectrophotometer (Labtron Equipment Ltd, UK), with 260/280 ratios near 1.8 being considered pure.

ST307 clone screening

Isolates were screened for the ST307 clone using previously described PCR primers and conditions [14]. Positive (a previously ST307-sequenced isolate) and negative (nuclease-free water) controls were included. Amplicons were separated on a 1.5% agarose gel in 0.5 M Tris-Borate-EDTA buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) stained with ethidium bromide (0.5 µg/mL) and electrophoresed at 90 V for 90 minutes. Amplicon sizes were determined using a 100-base pair DNA ladder (Zymo Research, USA). The PCR amplicons were visualized under ultraviolet light using the Gel Doc EZ Imager (Bio-Rad, USA). The gel images were captured, analyzed, and stored using the Image Lab software (Bio-Rad, USA).

Whole genome sequencing

A total of 16 representative isolates were selected for WGS based on ST (proportional to prevalence), resistance profiles (MDR, XDR, and PDR), and the presence of multiple carbapenemase genes (at least two). Multiplexed, paired-end libraries (2 × 150 bp) were prepared using the Illumina DNA Prep kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), followed by sequencing on the Illumina NextSeq 1000/2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with 100 × coverage at the National Institute for Communicable Diseases Sequencing Core Facility, South Africa. Bioinformatic analysis was performed as previously described by Salvador-Oke et al. [8,15]. Briefly, generated raw reads were processed using the Jekesa pipeline, with ARGs, mutations, STs, plasmid replicons, and serotypes detected using public databases (refer to the Appendix for full genome quality analytics). A phylogenetic tree with 335 publicly available reference K. pneumoniae genomes was generated through PathogenWatch and annotated using the interactive tree of life [15]. Virulence genes were detected using the BacWGSTdb 2.10 K. pneumoniae database [16]. Integrons were identified using IntegronFinder v2.0 [17]. Chromosome/plasmid contig origin was inferred using mlplasmids v2.1.0 [18].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using STATA 18.0 (StataCorp, USA). Chi-square (χ²) tests were applied to assess the association between categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results and Discussion

The emergence and spread of MDR K. pneumoniae high-risk clones are a major public health concern, particularly carbapenemase- and ESBL-producing strains, because they present significant clinical challenges due to limited therapeutic options [19]. This study aimed to investigate the genomic characteristics of MDR K. pneumoniae high-risk clones in community hospitals from Pretoria, South Africa. A summary of the patients’ demographic and clinical information for the isolates included in the study is provided in Table 1. The most common carbapenemase gene in CPKP was the β-lactamase gene (bla)OXA-48-like (114 of 118; 96.6%), followed by blaNDM (30 of 118; 25.4%), blaKPC (six of 118; 5.1%), and blaVIM (three of 118; 2.5%) (for carbapenemase gene combinations, see Table 1). This result is consistent with local studies but differs from the global epidemiology, where blaKPC, predominates [20,21]. The high blaOXA-48-like gene prevalence can be explained by the successful expansion of MDR high-risk clones such as ST307 with blaOXA-181, located on promiscuous plasmids in South African hospitals [22]. The virulome of the sequenced isolates included 84 genes; however, the hypervirulent biomarkers rmpA, rmpA2, iucA, iroB, and peg-344 were not detected (see Appendix). Prevalence and molecular characteristics of the ST307 high-risk clone

The ST307 high-risk clone emerged in the mid-1990s and is now well distributed across the world, with endemicity in the United States, Italy, Colombia, and South Africa [6,23]. The CPKP ST307 clone with blaOXA-181 has been linked to numerous outbreaks in hospitals across South Africa due to high vertical transmission and promiscuous plasmids [14]. In this study, 34.7% (41 of 118) of the CPKP isolates were ST307, whereas 65.3% (77 of 118) were non-ST307. The high prevalence of CPKP ST307 highlights its endemicity in South African hospitals. The CPKP ST307 clone harbored blaOXA-48-like in 65.9% (27 of 41) of the isolates, blaNDM+blaOXA-48-like in 31.7% (13 of 41), and blaKPC + blaNDM + blaOXA-48-like in one isolate (one of 41; 2.4%). The high number of double-carbapenemase-producing ST307 (blaNDM + blaOXA-48-like) differs from previous reports in South Africa, which described ST307 with only blaOXA-181 or blaNDM-1 [8,15,21,24]. The carbapenemase genes were identified as blaNDM-1 and blaOXA-181 in all sequenced ST307 isolates (n = 5) (Table 2). All sequenced ST307 isolates were phylogenetically related to other South African ST307 isolates with blaOXA-181, indicating local spread (Figure 1). The blaNDM-1 gene was co-localized with sul1 and rmtC genes, as well as an IncFIB(pB171) replicon, in all plasmid-derived contigs of ST307 and ST391 isolates. The IncFII(K), IncFII(Yp), and IncFIB(K) replicon plasmids were also detected in both of these clones. Many IncFIB(pB171)-IncFII(Yp) multireplicon plasmids harboring the blaNDM-1, rmtC, and sul1 genes are common in different K. pneumoniae clones in South Africa [24,25]. The ST307 isolates in this study may have acquired the blaNDM-1 gene from circulating clones in South Africa over time, highlighting the dangers of plasmid-mediated horizontal gene transfer of ARGs The blaOXA-181 and qnrS1 genes were associated with the ColKP3 (eight of eight cases) and IncX3 replicons (six of eight cases). The coexistence of IncX3, ColKP3, blaOXA-181, and qnrS1 genes indicates IncX3-ColKP3 hybrid plasmids (referred to as IncX3 plasmids) harboring blaOXA-181 and qnrS1 genes in ST307 and non-ST307 clones [14,26]. Plasmids such as p72_X3_OXA181 (an IncX3-ColKP3 plasmid) played a significant role in the spread and outbreaks of ST307, ST17, and ST29 in South African hospitals due to their high stability, conjugation ability, and low fitness cost [9,14]. The two cases of invasive ST307 with blaOXA-181 but no IncX3 replicon (KP141 and KP146) suggest a ColKP3 plasmid (>20 kb in size, single or multireplicon unclear). This indicates that the blaOXA-181 gene in the ST307 clone is harbored on different plasmid backbones. The KP146 isolate had a chromosomally encoded blaCTX-M-15 gene, indicating it can disseminate stably in clinical settings regardless of environmental pressure [27].

Antibiotic susceptibility data retrieved for 18 blaNDM + blaOXA-48-like–positive CPKP isolates showed that 88.9% (16 of 18) were resistant to ceftazidime/avibactam (CAZ-AVI), with viable susceptibility only to colistin (100%). CAZ-AVI is a registered β-lactamase inhibitor combination in South Africa as a last-resort treatment for CPKP [28]. High resistance to CAZ-AVI undermines its future use as a therapeutic option against CPKP. Although colistin is a viable therapeutic option against the dual-carbapenemase-producing clones, nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity make it less desirable for treatment [29]. Aztreonam with CAZ-AVI combination is another alternative treatment option [28].

The one PDR ST307 isolate (KP152) from a blood specimen with blaKPC, blaNDM, and blaOXA-48-like genes is extremely rare in the literature. Genomic data confirmed the blaKPC gene to be blaKPC-2. The acquisition of blaKPC-2 and blaNDM-1 genes alongside blaOXA-181 by this high-risk clone highlights its ability to rapidly accumulate ARGs on mobile genetic elements. This isolate was resistant to all tested antibiotics, including colistin, tigecycline, and CAZ-AVI, which are last-resort antibiotics against CPKP. Major chromosomal mutations (mgrB, phoPQ, pmrAB, and crrAB mutations) and mobile colistin resistance (mcr) genes were not detected in this isolate, indicating other mechanisms of resistance to colistin [30]. The unavailability of viable therapeutic options could lead to patient mortality and highlights the urgent need for the development of new antibiotics against CPKP. Clones producing multiple carbapenemases are a major threat because they are harder to control and act as a reservoir of carbapenemase genes for other clones and bacterial species [31]. The emergence of triple-carbapenemase-producing ST307 clones in South Africa is alarming and highlights the need to control the further spread of this clone. The rapid spread of this clone in South Africa was fueled by the inter-regional travel of patients [14]. This triple-carbapenemase-producing ST307 high-risk clone has the potential to spread to other countries due to patient travel and could cause large-scale outbreaks with extremely high health care costs.

For ESBL-Kp, the ST307 prevalence was 29.4% (10 of 34). The blaCTX-M-15 gene was found in 90% (nine of 10) of the ST307 isolates (Table 1 and Appendix). The ST307 clone is rarely reported in the literature among clinical ESBL-Kp from South Africa. The high prevalence highlights the global dissemination of CTX-M-15–associated ST307 ESBL-Kp [23].

Emerging non-ST307 clones carrying multiple carbapenemases

The non-ST307 clones in this study included ST17, ST34, ST391, ST530, ST2497, and ST2680. The ST530 and ST2680 isolates co-harbored blaKPC-2 and blaOXA-181. Both clones are rarely reported in the literature, which represent newly emerging double-carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae clones. The blaKPC-2 gene on a non-Tn4401 element (NTEKPC) (see Figure 2) was co-localized with blaCTX-M-15 and blaTEM genes on a plasmid-derived contig associated with IncFIB(pNDM-Mar) and IncHI1B(pNDM-MAR) replicons. This indicates an IncFIB(pNDM-Mar)/IncHI1B(pNDM-MAR) plasmid harboring blaKPC-2 and blaCTX-M-15 genes in both clones. This study also identified ST2497 isolates (n = 5) co-harboring the blaNDM-1 and blaOXA-232 genes. Four isolates were XDR, with one being PDR. Phylogenetic analysis indicated that the isolates were related to local isolates. The ST2497 clone harboring blaNDM-1 + blaOXA-232 has been reported in South Africa, with XDR and PDR phenotypes [15]. The blaOXA-232 gene was associated with a ColKP3 replicon and a 6 kb plasmid in all isolates. The 6.1 kb ColKP3 plasmids are carriers of the blaOXA-232 gene in South Africa [33]. Other detected plasmid replicons, including IncFIB(pQil), IncFIB(pNDM-Mar), and IncHI1B(pNDM-MAR), are associated with blaNDM-1 [34]. One ST2497 isolate (KP136) also had a chromosomally encoded blaCTX-M-15 gene. The PDR, colistin-resistant ST2497 isolate had an IncFIB(pQil)-associated ISKpn25 element with three substitutions and a disrupted mgrB gene. Insertion sequence–mediated mgrB gene disruption and inactivation are common causes of colistin resistance in K. pneumoniae [35]. More double-carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae clones have been described in South African hospitals, including ST17, ST152, and ST6787, adding to the ABR burden facing the health care system [15,21].

This study also identified an ST17 isolate co-harboring blaKPC, blaNDM, and blaOXA-48-like genes from a blood specimen. The blaNDM and blaOXA-48-like genes were confirmed to be blaNDM-1 and blaOXA-181, respectively, through genomic data. However, genomic data did not confirm the PCR-detected blaKPC gene in this isolate, which could indicate the loss of a blaKPC-encoding plasmid during subculture [36]. A study from a Chinese hospital showed high mortality among bloodstream infections caused by non-ST307 clones co-harboring blaKPC + blaNDM-1 + blaOXA-181 genes, with one recovery after antibiotic therapy with amikacin, levofloxacin, cefoperazone/sulbactam, and imipenem [37]. The cefoperazone/sulbactam β-lactamase inhibitor combination is currently unavailable in South Africa, but future studies can investigate the efficacy of the antibiotic therapy against blaKPC+NDM-1+OXA-181 producers.

Within the sequenced isolates, the study identified 21 plasmid replicons. Different incompatibility group plasmids, associated with different carbapenemases, can coexist in one bacterial cell, resulting in a clone harboring multiple carbapenemase genes [38]. The high plasmid diversity is likely a contributing factor to the emergence of multiple-carbapenemase-producing MDR K. pneumoniae clones. The emergence of double- and triple-carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae clones in hospitals calls for enhanced infection prevention and control measures. The spread of international K. pneumoniae high-risk clones harboring multiple carbapenemase genes in community hospitals in Pretoria, South Africa is a serious public health concern. The findings warrant urgent epidemiologic surveillance to monitor and control the spread of MDR K. pneumoniae clones.

Limitations

This study has a number of limitations. The sample size of sequenced isolates was not large enough to generalize results. Due to a lack of resources, not all study isolates were sequenced. Long-read sequencing was also not performed, which would have helped to assemble full plasmid sequences and override short-read sequencing limitations. Conjugation experiments to screen for plasmid transferability were also not performed due to financial constraints, which limits information on plasmid transmission. Data on hospital, ward, and antibiotic susceptibility profiles of some isolates were not obtained. The study did not screen hospital environmental surface samples. The sizes of large plasmids (>20 kb) could not be determined. The distinct variants of PCR-detected carbapenemase and ESBL genes were not determined.

Conclusion

The study highlighted the endemicity of the ST307 high-risk clone in community hospitals from Pretoria, South Africa, constituting a third of CPKP and ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae isolates. No hypervirulent CPKP lineages were detected. Emerging double-carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae clones include ST307, ST530, ST2497, and ST2680. The detection of multiple-carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae clones reflects the pathogen’s ability to accumulate mobile genetic elements bearing ARGs. The presence of PDR CPKP clones highlights the urgent need to develop new antibiotic therapies against this rapidly evolving pathogen. Continuous genomic surveillance using cost-effective methodologies is imperative to monitor and control possible outbreaks of circulating MDR K. pneumoniae clones in South Africa.
