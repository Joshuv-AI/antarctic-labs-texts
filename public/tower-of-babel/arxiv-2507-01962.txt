Sub-keV dark matter can strongly ionize molecular clouds
Pedro De la Torre Luque, Pierluca Carenza, Thong T. Q. Nguyen

Abstract
We show that the ionization of dense molecular clouds can be used to set strong constraints on dark matter models producing UV/X-ray photons in their annihilation or decay. We place robust and competitive constraints on various dark matter models, such as axion-like particles, scalars and sterile neutrinos, for masses between ∼ 30 \sim 30 eV and 10 10 keV, and project forecasts to illustrate the potential of this target. We discuss how these constraints can be significantly improved by considering a more refined sample of molecular clouds near the Galactic Center and above the Galactic plane, a detailed modeling of the cosmic-ray ionization contribution and, potentially, a more refined analysis of the gas density in clouds through dust extinction maps. Thus, ionization of molecular clouds emerges as one of the most powerful tools for probing sub-keV dark matter.



I
Introduction

Despite compelling evidence for the existence of dark matter (DM) through its gravitational effects, the fundamental nature of DM remains largely unknown. If DM is indeed a new fundamental particle, it is crucial to explore its possible interactions beyond gravity; see Ref.
Cirelli et al. 2024
for a comprehensive review. Numerous studies have placed stringent constraints on heavy fermionic (WIMP-like) DM with masses in the GeV range and above
Aalbers et al. 2024
;
Bo et al. 2025
;
Aprile et al. 2025
;
Agnese et al. 2018
;
Agnes et al. 2023
;
Ajaj et al. 2019
;
Akerib et al. 2019
.
Significant efforts are currently underway to probe lighter DM candidates through both direct and indirect detection strategies
Antel et al. 2023
. These approaches aim to uncover signals from particles light enough that escape the traditional WIMP-focused searches. A wide array of theoretical models extending the Standard Model (SM) suggest the existence of light, weakly interacting particles that could account for some or all of the present-day DM density. These extensions often emerge in attempts to address unresolved issues in particle physics, such as the smallness of neutrino masses or the strong-CP problem. Among the most extensively studied candidates in this mass range there is the QCD axion
Peccei 2008
, a pseudoscalar particle originally proposed to solve the strong-CP problem, and more general axion-like particles (ALPs)
Choi et al. 2021
, which appear in many string-theoretic frameworks and span a broad range of masses and couplings
Gendler et al. 2024
. Significant effort is devoted to sterile neutrinos
Dasgupta and Kopp 2021
, which are singlets under the SM gauge group but may mix with active neutrinos and contribute to the DM and neutrino phenomenology, and dark photons
Fabbrichesi et al. 2020
, hypothetical gauge bosons associated with a hidden
U(1)
symmetry that can kinetically mix with the SM photon.
The search for these light DM candidates employs a diverse set of experimental techniques. Direct detection experiments attempt to measure the scattering or absorption of such particles in terrestrial detectors
Lanfranchi et al. 2021
, while indirect detection methods aim to observe their decay or annihilation products in astrophysical environments
Raffelt 1996
;
Caputo and Raffelt 2024
;
Jacobsen et al. 2023
;
Foster et al. 2022
;
Carenza et al. 2025
.

In this work, we follow this latter path and propose a new method to probe the coupling of DM with masses at the keV scale and below. In particular, we show that the annihilation/decay of such low-mass DM particles into photons from ultraviolet (UV) to X-ray energies is able to produce significant ionization inside molecular clouds (MCs), reaching levels of ionization even above the ionization that cosmic rays (CRs) are expected to cause.
Interactions between DM, or its annihilation/decay products, and the visible sector could ionize molecular hydrogen,
{\rm H}_{2}
, which is abundant within MCs. For example, DM decay into photons can lead to ionization when the DM mass satisfies
m_{X}\gtrsim 30
eV, since the ionization energy of molecular hydrogen is approximately
15.4
eV, producing high levels of ionization when these photons are absorbed inside the MC. Similarly, a coupling between DM and electrons can cause ionization through scattering processes.
In dense molecular media, and, in particular, in dense MCs, UV photons are quickly absorbed, delivering all of their energy into the medium and ionizing the gas. Since DM would be nearly homogeneously distributed inside these clouds (extending over scale order of parsecs), its annihilation/decay would lead to an intense, homogeneous, ionization inside such systems. This potential DM-induced ionization contributes alongside standard ionization sources, such as stellar UV radiation, low-energy CRs, and X-rays emitted by stars within the MC
Krolik and Kallman 1983
;
Norman and Silk 1980
. It is possible to set strong constraints on the DM lifetime, annihilation rate, or on the coupling to photons when DM generates a ionization that exceeds the standard astrophysical sources.

Previous literature already investigated the heating of gas
Bhoonah et al. 2018
;
Wadekar and Farrar 2021
;
Alvey et al. 2023
or the ionization
Blanco et al. 2024
;
Balan et al. 2024
induced by the direct interaction of DM particles with the nuclei in gas systems, such as rich-gas dwarf galaxies, MCs or the early universe, through the
21
cm line
Evoli et al. 2014
for example. However, the effect of particle emissions from DM in the ionization of MCs has not been investigated before, making this the first effort to illustrate the potential of such clouds to indirectly probe light DM. As we show, on astrophysical scales, MCs act as large-volume detectors with ultra-low thresholds for ionization effects.

This paper is organized as follows: In section
II
we overview the expected main drivers of ionization in MCs and briefly outline the measurements of the ionization rate. Then, we discuss the targets that we are going to use in our study and identify optimal MCs to constraint DM. In Sec.
III
we detail our calculation of the ionization caused by photons produced from DM decay/annihilation. Then, we describe our results is Sec.
IV
and summarize our main findings in Sec.
V
, where we discuss possible ways of optimizing our current constraints.

Modeling the contribution of CRs to the ionization of MCs is an intricate problem. Early studies estimated that the ionization rate per atom in clouds composed mainly of atomic hydrogen is within the range of a few

In order to determine with more accuracy the expected ionization rates, a better understanding of the low-energy spectra of CR electrons, protons, and heavier nuclei is required. This is an extremely challenging task. The simplest approach consists of measuring the low-energy CR spectrum reaching Earth and extrapolating it to energies below the CR observations. Unfortunately, local measurements of the CR spectra at low energies are affected by solar modulation and the structure of the interplanetary magnetic field. The Voyager I and II spacecrafts have provided valuable data, measuring the electron spectrum down to a few MeV
Cummings et al. 2016
;
Stone et al. 2019
beyond the heliopause, although these observations are still far from the energy range most relevant for ionization.
An alternative to probing these low energies involves observations of the Galactic synchrotron background. This method is limited by significant uncertainties in modeling the interstellar magnetic field
Padovani and Galli 2018
;
Orlando 2018
;
Ginzburg and Syrovatskii 1965
and it would hardly probe energies of tens of eV without involving a low-energy extrapolation of the CR spectrum. Thus, modeling the ionization of dense MCs is subject to significant uncertainties.

Experimentally, measuring the ionization of MCs is of interest. A particularly effective method to infer the CR ionization rate involves measuring the intensities of emission lines from molecular tracers, whose abundances are closely tied to the free electron population. The choice of tracer strongly depends on the gas density and chemical composition. In diffuse regions of MCs,
{\rm H}^{+}_{3}
is a favorable tracer due to its simple and well-understood chemistry
Indriolo and McCall 2013
. In denser environments, such as MC cores and proto-stellar clusters, commonly used tracers include HCO
+
, DCO
+
, and CO
Caselli et al. 1998
. At present, measurements of the ionization rate in MCs seem challenging to explain by CR ionization.
First, if CRs are the dominant ionization agent of MCs across the Galaxy, one expects that the ionization rate decreases for MCs with larger column densities. Although there are observations that support this trend, no clear correlation is observed
Gabici et al. 2019
;
Gabici 2022
. Second, the levels of ionization from CRs are expected to be lower than that measured in several clouds
Indriolo et al. 2009
;
Phan et al. 2018
. In this context, the high ionization rate observed in the Central Molecular Zone have been found to be incompatible with what the ionization that CRs can provide
Ravikularaman et al. 2025
, and MeV DM has been proposed as a potential explanation of this anomalous ionization
De la Torre Luque et al. 2025
. In addition, there are hints from gamma-ray observations suggesting that CRs do not actually penetrate dense MCs
Yang et al. 2023
.

As opposed to standard ionization mechanisms, UV photon produced by DM interactions would ionize directly within the cloud, providing a constant additional ionization to that provided by CRs. In this work, we explore the potential of dark, or dense, clouds, which are observed with temperatures near
10

\sim 10^{5}
M
⊙

, to probe the emission of UV/X-ray photons from sub-keV DM.
In order to show how promising are such clouds as targets for DM searches, we will derive constraints from a local dark cloud as a pessimistic (and robust) case, because the modeling of the DM density does not need assuming any DM density distribution and will therefore be very robust. Then, we will evaluate constraints from a cloud closer to the Galactic Center (GC), where DM is expected to be concentrated, and we will assume a prototypical Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW)
Navarro et al. 1996
distribution. This is the standard assumption made in most of the indirect DM searches, and allows for a fair estimation with previous constraints. As we will explain below, we follow a very conservative approach in both cases, and better results can be obtained through a more dedicated search of optimal clouds that is left for future work.

One of the local clouds that has received more attention is
L1551
, which is located
150

\sim 1

\zeta^{H_{2}}\approx(5\pm 1.5)\times 10^{-18}\,\,\textbf{s}^{-1}

. We will derive constraints at
95\%

2\sigma

We stress that
L1551
is not the local cloud with the lowest ionization rate. For example, the Taurus molecular cloud (TMC-1) is a dark cloud in the constellations of Taurus and Auriga. For this cloud, Ref.
Flower et al. 2007
found that
\zeta^{H_{2}}\approx 2\times 10^{-18}\,\,\textbf{s}^{-1}
provided the best overall agreement with the observations. Using this cloud would improve our limits by a factor
2-3
on the DM annihilation/decay rate.
However, the lack of a robust estimation of the uncertainties of this measurement makes us choose
L1551
as reference case.
Another interesting example is
Barnard 68
, which was found to be compatible with an ionization rate of
(1-6)\times 10^{-18}\,\,\textrm{s}^{-1}

Maret and Bergin 2007
.
Unfortunately, there is no precise estimation of the uncertainty of these measurements that allow us to use them reliably, but it must be noted that using these clouds could lead to up to an order of magnitude stronger DM constraints than our reference case.
Lower values of ionization can be found in the local environment, but we leave a dedicated survey for a future work.

Interestingly, even lower values of ionization have been found in infrared dark clouds (IRDC), which are very cold (T
<20

While the modeling of DM ionization in local clouds would not require to assume any DM distribution (i.e. leading to really robust bounds), we select a cloud located quite close to the GC to demonstrate the potential of such dark clouds to probe sub-keV DM. The advantage is the higher DM density in this region, with the disadvantage of introducing uncertainties related to the DM distribution profile. For concreteness, we choose

To derive this limit, we choose a value of ionization of

3.8\times 10^{-19}\,\,\textrm{s}^{-1}

As in the case of local MCs, more research is needed to identify more clouds close to the GC. IRDCs located close to the GC but above the Galactic plane are probably the best target to constraint the ionization caused by sub-keV DM, since the impact of CR ionization will be significantly reduced.

V
Discussion and conclusions

In this paper, we investigate the ionization of neutral gas produced in dense MCs by decaying/annihilating DM. In particular, sub-keV DM could generate UV/X-ray photons that are able to produce a high and homogeneous ionization across the cloud since they are absorbed efficiently within such systems. In fact, we show that the ionization produced by the photon emission from sub-keV DM saturates the ionization that is observed in different MCs for decay lifetimes, or annihilation rates, that are not ruled out by any other constraint. Therefore, we test for the first time MCs as a target for indirect DM searches, demonstrating that they allow us to improve current constraints for decaying DM particles such as ALPs, dark photons, scalar particles or sterile neutrinos for masses from a few tens of eV to slightly below
1
keV.

Interestingly, The ionization of dense MCs is expected to be primarily driven by CRs, as UV light cannot effectively penetrate these regions. However, sub-keV unstable DM particles could be producing UV radiation directly inside the MC, leading to a persistent background of ionization, potentially influencing both the star formation rates and the chemical evolution within the clouds.
Notably, a recent study suggests that DM particles with MeV-scale masses might account for the anomalously high ionization rates observed in the Galactic Center
De la Torre Luque et al. 2025
.

We begin by identifying local MCs as a particularly robust probe of this kind of DM. This is because estimating their ionization rate requires only knowledge of the local DM density — without the need to assume a specific DM distribution model. Using the local MC
L1551
, which is not the cloud with the lowest observed ionization rate, we derive constraints that are similar, or even better, than the previous leading constraints.
Many of the existing bounds rely on assumptions that can affect their reliability and expose them to significant uncertainties, as opposed to the more robust MC bounds that we explored.
Then, we examine the case of
G28.37+00.07
, or the
DRAGON
cloud, which has been studied previously in detail and shows regions of it that are exceptionally quiescent in terms of their star formation activity, with ionization rates compatible with
10^{-19}
s
-1
. This constitutes what we call our fiducial constraint, and allows us improve previous constraints from CMB and LeoT gas heating up to several hundreds of eV.
Finally, we use the case of
G1.4-1.8+87
, an MC that is
400
pc away from the Galactic Center, to show the potential of using clouds near the Galactic Center. For this case, we take the values obtained in Ref.
Bhoonah et al. 2019
to illustrate that, assuming a NFW DM density profile, we reach the strongest DM bounds to date in a mass range from a few tens of eV to the keV. However, this must be taken as an optimistic forecast since the characterization of the temperature of that could was put in doubt in Ref.
Farrar et al. 2020
. We highlight that clouds that are closer to the Galactic Center can be found, therefore providing even stronger DM bounds.
Finally, we highlight that the most promising targets for indirectly searching for sub-keV DM decays or annihilations into photons are IRDCs located near the Galactic Center but slightly above the Galactic plane. These regions are expected to be less affected by CR ionization, which is sourced in the Galactic disk. As a result, such clouds are likely to exhibit very low ionization levels, making any DM-induced effects more distinguishable. Although a more comprehensive survey of MCs will further refine the constraints presented here, our results already demonstrate the strong potential of this approach for probing different types of light DM particles. We also note that this strategy can be also applied for MCs in nearby galaxies.

Among the expected future refinements, we list a few below.

i) A more exhaustive survey of the measurements of dark MCs, dedicated to identify clouds with lower ionization fractions. These measurements are often complex, and they are usually focused on measuring the ionization around clouds with regions of star formation. However, we show that dark clouds constitute a very interesting target for DM searches, hoping to increase the interest towards targeting dark MCs for observations. In addition, if dark clouds systems can be found closer to the GC, our limits will be highly improved.
In this regard, there are already extensive surveys of such clouds, like the analyses from Ref.
Simon et al. 2006
. A dedicated exploration and analysis could yield improvements of up to a few orders of magnitude in the derived DM constraints.

ii) A recent paper
Obolentseva et al. 2024
showed that previous measurements of the ionization rate from several dense MCs are biased, due to an inaccurate evaluation of their density. Using dust extinction maps to evaluate their density leads to an ionization rate that is lower by an average factor of
9
. Analyzing interesting targets using this procedure could lead to constraints that are an order of magnitude better than the current ones.

iii) Probably the most obvious improvement would be that of modeling the ionization produced by CRs. Provided that CRs are expected to be the main ionization agent of dense MCs, including their contribution must certainly improve our constraints. Unfortunately, this estimation is not simple and requires an accurate knowledge of the distribution of CRs in different regions of the Galaxy and at very low energies, where our models are not robust enough to produce a reliable constraint. However, reasonable estimations can be made for local MCs, and improvements of a factor of a few in the constraints can be already obtained with conservative assumptions, as discussed above.

As a final point, we also note that X-ray telescopes are very promising for constraining DM above a few hundreds eV. Especially eROSITA could place constraints above
0.2
keV that can beat all previous constraints
Dekker et al. 2021
, once they have enough observational time. However, the the current constraints from eROSITA or Chandra below
1
keV are even weaker than those that we obtain and forecast in this work.

In conclusion, we have shown the potential impact of sub-keV DM decaying/annihilating into photons in dense gas regions, which can be specially relevant for probing ALPs or scalar DM particles. Our results demonstrate that astrochemical observations in MCs can serve as a novel and powerful probe for indirect searches of sub-keV DM. This approach offers particularly robust constraints across a range of particle physics models and has the potential to surpass existing bounds for DM masses between approximately
30
eV and
1
keV.
