Targeted blood-brain barrier penetration for traumatic brain injury therapy via RXR/PPAR-driven microglial M2 polarization
Authors: Songyu Chen, Junyu Lin, Xiaxuan Zhang, Lin Zhou, Zhicheng Xu, Xiao Hou, Dongming Gao, Liang Gao, Lei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a leading global cause of death and disability, traumatic brain injury (TBI) triggers a complex secondary injury process leading to long-term neurological impairment. Current therapeutic efficacy is limited by the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and the absence of integrated approaches to address neuroinflammation and neuroregeneration. We designed a biomimetic nanomedicine (Ali-NPs@Mexo) by encapsulating alitretinoin(Ali)-loaded albumin nanospheres within mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC)-derived exosomal membranes. This platform exploits the natural BBB-crossing and inflammation-targeting capabilities of exosomes to enhance localized drug delivery. Mechanistic studies revealed that Ali-NPs@Mexo triggers RXR/PPAR signaling to promote M1-to-M2 microglial polarization, thereby mitigating acute inflammation. Moreover, it protects neurons by inhibiting reactive astrogliosis and supporting the growth of neural stem cells and oligodendrocytes. In vivo, Ali-NPs@Mexo significantly improved functional and cognitive outcomes in mice. This integrated strategy of targeted immune-modulation and neuro-regeneration provides a translatable framework for effective TBI intervention.Keywords: Traumatic brain injury, Exosomes, Alitretinoin, Nerve repair

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) manifests as a spectrum of neurological damage precipitated by external mechanical insults. The initial trauma, encompassing focal contusions and diffuse axonal injury, invariably triggers a deleterious cascade of secondary injury mechanisms—most notably neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and excitotoxicity [1]. Such pathological shifts underpin the persistent sensory, motor, and cognitive deficits that afflict millions globally, representing a staggering socioeconomic burden [2,3]. Despite its prevalence, the quest for restorative therapies is hampered by a formidable dual-hurdle. On one hand, the blood-brain barrier (BBB) acts as a relentless physiological gatekeeper, precluding the vast majority of therapeutic agents from attaining effective concentrations within the lesioned parenchyma.

On the other, conventional pharmacological approaches often fail to simultaneously orchestrate the resolution of neuroinflammation and the stimulation of neuroregeneration-two intrinsically linked pillars of functional recovery [4]. Consequently, engineering delivery systems capable of circumventing the BBB to synergistically recalibrate the cerebral microenvironment remains a paramount challenge in TBI research.

In this context, cell-based therapies, particularly those employing mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), have shown considerable promise in regenerative medicine for various disorders, including TBI [5]. A compelling alternative that directly addresses the delivery challenge has emerged: MSC-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos). Serving as natural nanocarriers, MSC-Exos facilitate the transfer of bioactive molecules—such as proteins, lipids, and genetic material—from parent MSCs to target cells and tissues [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. MSC-Exos exert immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects, offering a means to harness the therapeutic benefits of MSCs while circumventing the complexities associated with whole-cell delivery [[11], [12], [13]]. Recent reviews have highlighted the therapeutic potential of extracellular vesicle-based strategies in neurological injuries, including TBI, by regulating neuroinflammation, promoting neural regeneration, and facilitating functional recovery [14,15]. Numerous studies have confirmed that MSC-Exos effectively alleviate TBI symptoms and progression in both in vitro and in vivo models [[16], [17], [18]]. Recent advances have further demonstrated that exosome-based biomaterials, including exosome-integrated hydrogels and 3D-printed biological scaffolds, can enhance neural repair after TBI by promoting angiogenesis, neurogenesis, and neural network remodeling [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. To further enhance brain-specific accumulation, innovative delivery vehicles are being integrated. Notably, TBI-induced BBB disruption itself creates a transient window for targeted drug delivery to injury sites [24,25]. Capitalizing on this, bovine serum albumin-based nanoparticle carriers (BSA NPs) with diameters under 100 nm have demonstrated the ability to cross the BBB [26]. Studies have shown that such albumin-based nano-delivery systems can significantly enhance drug entry into and retention within brain tissue [27,28], positioning them as promising vehicles for targeted TBI therapy. The integration of BSA NPs with MSC-Exos is particularly advantageous, as it combines the high-capacity drug loading of albumin nanoparticles-ideal for hydrophobic compounds such as Alitretinoin-with the active inflammation-targeting, immune-evasive, and intrinsic immunomodulatory functions of MSC-derived exosomes. This hybrid platform thus offers a synergistic strategy for both enhanced brain delivery and dual therapeutic effects.

Alitretinoin (9-cis-retinoic acid, Ali) possesses well-documented neuroprotective and regenerative properties [29,30]. As a retinoic acid derivative, Ali mitigates central nervous system injury by fostering neuronal survival while simultaneously dampening inflammatory cascades [31]. Its therapeutic efficacy stems from its role as a pan-agonist, binding effectively to all isoforms of retinoic acid receptors (RARα, RARβ, RARγ) and retinoid X receptors (RXRα, RXRβ, RXRγ). Among these targets, RXRγ1 is prominently expressed in brain tissue, where it governs essential gene regulatory networks within neural crest stem cells [32]. Crucially, Ali-mediated RXR activation engages the RXR/PPAR heterodimer axis—a primary determinant of macrophage polarization. This interaction drives the transition of macrophages from a neurotoxic, pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to an anti-inflammatory M2 state, thereby curtailing deleterious cytokine release and fostering a pro-regenerative microenvironment essential for TBI recovery [33]. Given these pleiotropic mechanisms, Ali represents a compelling therapeutic candidate for TBI, especially when coupled with advanced delivery platforms designed to overcome its inherent bioavailability limits and enhance brain-targeted accumulation. Recent advances in engineered extracellular vesicle-based nanoplatforms have further highlighted their potential to construct pro-regenerative niches by coordinating neurogenesis and angiogenesis [34].

To address these delivery challenges, we developed Ali-NPs@Mexo, a biomimetic nanoplatform designed for targeted intervention and synergistic CNS repair (Scheme 1A). Ali was first encapsulated within BSA nanoparticles (Ali-NPs) via pH-driven self-assembly, achieving a 96.69% encapsulation efficiency. These cores were subsequently cloaked with mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (Mexo) via co-extrusion. This outer membrane endows the nanoplatform with stealth properties against immune clearance and capitalizes on the natural inflammatory homing of Mexo. In vivo tracking revealed that intravenously administered Ali-NPs@Mexo effectively penetrates the blood-brain barrier, maintaining selective accumulation in the injured area for over 12 h (Scheme 1B). Mechanistically, by uniting the specific advantages of its three components, the system thoroughly dampens neuroinflammation via the RXR/PPAR pathway, curtails astrogliosis, and drives neural tissue repair. This integrated biomimetic design represents a robust and innovative strategy for advancing TBI therapeutics.

Materials and methods

Materials

BSA was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Ali was obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit was purchased from Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). RIPA lysis buffer, the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit, and the BCA Protein Assay Kit were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Mouse TNF-α, IL-10, and IL-1β ELISA kits were acquired from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA).

Animals

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Tenth People's Hospital (Approval No. SHDSYY-2022-6426). C57BL/6J (male, 8 weeks) mice were obtained from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co, Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The animals were housed in groups and provided unrestricted access to water and food under a controlled environment with a 12-h light/dark cycle and constant temperature. After the surgical procedures, the animals were individually housed.

Exosome isolation and purification

Exosomes were isolated from the conditioned medium of mouse bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) using differential ultracentrifugation. Upon reaching 60–80% confluence, BMSC cultures were transitioned to a medium supplemented with exosome-depleted fetal bovine serum (FBS). After a 24-h incubation, the conditioned medium was harvested and clarified by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 30 min to remove cellular debris, followed by 10,000 × g for 1 h to deplete larger extracellular vesicles. The supernatant was subsequently ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 2 h to pellet the exosomes. The resulting pellet was resuspended in cold, sterile PBS, passed through a 0.22-μm filter, and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Preparation of BSA, Ali-NPs, and Ali-NPs@Mexo

Bovine serum albumin (BSA) was activated by dissolving 120 mg BSA, 60 mg sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and 4.4 mg dithiothreitol (DTT) in 3 mL of ultrapure water, followed by incubation in a 90 °C oil bath for 2 h. For Ali-NP synthesis, alitretinoin was mixed with Ca solution (25 μL), Te solution (25 μL), and 0.1 M MES buffer (950 μL, pH 4.6). Activated BSA solution (25 μL) was subsequently added to achieve a BSA-to-drug mass ratio of 10:1, and the mixture was continuously agitated for 4 h. To formulate Ali-NPs@Mexo, the resulting Ali-NPs were co-incubated with MSC-derived exosomes (Mexo) at a predetermined ratio. The suspension was extruded 30 times through a polycarbonate membrane using a liposome extruder to homogenize particle size and facilitate biomimetic membrane coating.

Characterization of nanoparticles

The morphology and size of the nanoparticles were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, PHILIPS Model CM200). The surface charge (zeta potential) of the nanoparticles across various pH values was measured using a ZetaPlus analyzer (Brookhaven Instrument Corporation, Holtsville, NY, USA).

To determine drug loading and encapsulation efficiency (EE), Ali-NPs@Mexo suspensions were disrupted in DMSO via sonication, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 10 min. Alitretinoin (Ali) concentration in the supernatant was quantified spectrophotometrically at 290 nm (Model V-530, JASCO, Tokyo, Japan). EE was calculated as the percentage of encapsulated Ali relative to the total initial drug input.

Blast-induced TBI mouse model

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). The blast injury was induced using a custom shock tube (183 × 66 cm) consisting of a pressurized driven section and an expansion section, separated by a Mylar membrane across a 15-cm opening. Anesthetized animals were positioned in the expansion section, 18 cm downstream from the membrane. To isolate the injury to the brain, the torso of each mouse was shielded within a foam-lined metal cylinder, leaving only the left side of the head exposed and unrestrained. The driven section was pressurized with compressed air until the Mylar membrane ruptured at exactly 20 psi, generating a targeted shock wave. The peak incident overpressure was recorded at 149.8 ± 2.09 kPa, with a total positive pressure duration (shock wave and subsequent wind gust) of 10 – 15 ms. Sham-injured control mice underwent identical anesthesia and positioning procedures but were not exposed to the blast.

Barnes maze test

To assess spatial learning and memory, the Barnes maze test was administered 2 weeks post-injury. The apparatus comprised an elevated (60 cm) circular white acrylic platform (1 m diameter, 6 mm thickness) containing 20 evenly distributed peripheral holes (5 cm diameter). A removable escape box was stationed beneath a single designated target hole. To facilitate spatial navigation, distinct extra-maze visual cues were affixed to the testing room walls under uniform illumination from two 120-W upward-facing lamps. Inter-trial olfactory interference was mitigated by thoroughly wiping the platform with 70% ethanol and rotating it clockwise. All behavioral trajectories were recorded and quantified utilizing SMART V2.5.21 software.

The experimental protocol comprised three phases: habituation, acquisition training, and probe trials. On day one, mice underwent habituation, where they were placed in the escape box for 1 min, followed by 5 min of free exploration on the platform. If a mouse failed to enter the tunnel spontaneously, it was gently guided into the box and allowed to stay for 1 min. The first acquisition training session began 1.5 h after habituation. Each mouse was placed in a starting cylinder at the center of the maze for 10 s; subsequently, the cylinder was removed and an acoustic stimulus (buzzer) was activated. The trial ended when the mouse entered the escape tunnel or after 3 min had elapsed. On days 2 and 3, two training sessions were conducted daily with a 1.5-h inter-trial interval. On day 4, three sessions were performed with 15-min intervals. On day 5, a 90-s probe trial was conducted with the escape box removed to assess spatial memory retention. The escape latency and the total number of errors were quantified.

Elevated plus maze

Anxiety-like behavior was assessed 2 weeks post-TBI using an elevated plus maze (EPM) apparatus. The EPM consisted of two open arms (50 × 10 cm) and two closed arms (50 × 10 × 29 cm) extending from a central platform (10 × 10 cm), elevated above the floor. At the start of each trial, the mouse was placed in the central area, facing an open arm. The animal's behavior was recorded for 5 min using a video tracking system.

Recorded sessions were analyzed to extract arm entry frequencies and resident times. Inter-animal olfactory interference was mitigated by sanitizing the maze surfaces with 75% ethanol. Furthermore, to avoid stress-induced behavioral artifacts, the testing environment was strictly maintained under low-light and quiet conditions.

Tissue preparation

Following the designated observation periods, deep anesthesia was induced in both sham and TBI cohorts using intraperitoneal sodium pentobarbital (60 mg/kg). To preserve tissue architecture, animals underwent transcardial perfusion via the left ventricle with 20 mL of ice-cold 0.9% saline, immediately followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.4). Harvested brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight. After dehydration in a standard graded ethanol series, the samples were paraffin-embedded. A Leica microtome was utilized to cut 5-μm coronal sections, which were subsequently mounted on glass slides and baked at 60 °C overnight. Ultimately, six cortical sections per mouse—each separated by a 120-μm interval to adequately represent the target lesion area—were collected for downstream immunofluorescence labeling.

Golgi staining

For the assessment of cytoarchitectural alterations, brain samples were processed utilizing Golgi-Cox impregnation. Briefly, freshly excised brains were rinsed in chilled saline and subsequently submerged in Golgi-Cox solution, where they remained in darkness at room temperature for 1 to 3 weeks. Upon completion of the impregnation phase, the specimens underwent progressive dehydration in ascending ethanol concentrations (70% to 100%, 1–2 h per stage). The dehydrated tissues were then xylene-cleared, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned coronally. Light microscopic examination revealed neuronal somata and neurites (dendrites and axons) stained intensely black, thereby facilitating rigorous structural quantification.

Histological analysis

To evaluate general cytoarchitecture, the previously prepared 5-μm paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) following standard procedures. Histopathological alterations in the cortex were visualized using a Nikon bright-field microscope at 200× magnification. Representative cortical fields were examined to characterize structural damage and cellular integrity across the experimental cohorts.

In vivo biodistribution and targeting capability

To assess the in vivo biodistribution and neurotargeting efficacy of the formulations, mice were administered intravenous injections of either Cy5-labeled BSA or Cy5-BSA-labeled Ali-NPs@Mexo. Whole-body fluorescence tracking was conducted at 6, 8, and 12 h post-administration using an IVIS imaging system (Caliper Life Sciences). At the terminal time point, animals were humanely euthanized to harvest major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and brain). The excised tissues immediately underwent ex vivo imaging for semi-quantitative fluorescence analysis.

Cell culture and drug treatment

Murine BV2 microglial cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 μg/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) under standard incubator conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). Assays were conducted upon cultures reaching 80 – 90% confluence. Following a 4–h serum deprivation period, cells were pre-incubated with Ali for 1 h and subsequently challenged with 1 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 8 h to establish the in vitro inflammatory model.

Cell viability assay

Cell survival following drug intervention was determined via the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) method. Briefly, BV2 cells (3 × 103 cells/well) were plated in 96-well formats. After completing the specific treatment durations (0–48 h), cells were incubated with 100 μL of 10% CCK-8 working solution for 3 h at 37 °C in the dark. Spectrophotometric absorbance was then recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Viability results were expressed as percentages relative to the baseline absorbance of untreated controls.

Cellular uptake analysis

Internalization efficiency of the nano-formulations was evaluated via flow cytometry. Briefly, BV2 microglia at 60–70% confluency (initial seeding density: 1 × 105 cells/mL) were co-incubated with graded concentrations of FITC-labeled Ali-NPs@Mexo (100–200 μg/mL) for 6 h. Post-treatment, cells were enzymatically detached, washed, and collected in PBS for immediate acquisition on a FACS Aria flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson). Cellular uptake capacity was expressed as median fluorescence intensity (MFI), derived from the flow cytometric data processed in FlowJo software.

ROS detection by DCFH-DA staining

Intracellular ROS accumulation was monitored using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. Cells in 48-well plates were loaded with 100 μL of DCFH-DA (1:2000 in serum-free medium) and maintained at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After washing with PBS to remove unbound probes, fluorescence images were immediately acquired using a fluorescence microscope to evaluate ROS production.

Flow cytometry analysis of macrophage polarization

To evaluate macrophage polarization, BV2 cells or brain tissue cell suspensions were stained with antibodies against Cox2 (M1 marker) and YM1/2 (M2 marker). For intracellular staining, cells were fixed, permeabilized using a fixation/permeabilization kit, and then incubated with fluorescently labeled primary antibodies (1:200 dilution; Abcam) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Flow cytometry was performed using a FACS Aria (Becton Dickinson).

ELISA analysis of cytokines

The levels of TGF-β, IL-4, and IL-10 in BV2 cell culture supernatants or brain tissue homogenates were quantified using ELISA kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) following the manufacturer's protocols. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad). Cytokine concentrations were calculated based on the respective standard curves and normalized to the total protein concentration for tissue samples.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from tissues or cells using TRIzol reagent (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). cDNA was synthesized from the extracted RNA using a reverse transcription kit (Qiagen, Germany). Quantitative PCR was performed using the SYBR Green PCR Core reagent kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) on an ABI PRISM 7700 sequence detection system. The 20 μL reaction mixture contained 10 μL 2× SYBR Green mix, 0.4 μL of each primer (10 μM), 2 μL of cDNA, and nuclease-free H2O. The initial reaction conditions were 95 °C for 10 min, then 20 s at 95 °C, 1 min at 55 °C, 30 s at 55 °C, and 40 cycles.

Protein extraction and western blot analysis

To evaluate protein expression, total cellular and tissue proteins were extracted utilizing RIPA lysis buffer. Following quantification with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), equal protein masses were separated by SDS-PAGE and immobilized onto PVDF membranes. To minimize non-specific binding, membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h and then incubated with specific primary antibodies (CD9, TSG101, Calnexin, RXR, PPAR, MCP-1, and β-actin; Cell Signaling Technology) at 4 °C for 12–16 h. Subsequently, the membranes were treated with HRP-linked secondary antibodies (1:1000) for 2 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized via an ECL detection system, and densitometric quantification was performed using ImageJ, with β-actin serving as the internal loading control.

Immunofluorescence analysis

The expressions of GFAP, MAP2, Iba1, and Nestin were determined by immunofluorescence. After fixation and permeabilization, brain tissue slices of each group were taken and cultured overnight at 4 °C with anti-GFAP, MAP2, and Nestin, and Iba1 antibodies (dilution 1:500). GFAP, MAP2, Nestin, and Iba1 were labeled with goat anti-mouse antibodies. After that, sections were counterstained with DAPI (blue). Finally, fluorescence images were captured using confocal laser scanning microscopy (Leica TCS SP5, Leica Co. Ltd). Brain sections underwent analysis using a fluorescence microscopy system with an inverted configuration (Zeiss, Axiovert 200M, Gottingen, Germany) connected to a Zeiss Axio Cam MRc5 digital camera controlled by a computer. Images were acquired using Zeiss Axio Vision v 4.0 software (Gottingen, Germany), and the subsequent assembly and labeling were carried out in Photoshop 7.0 (Adobe Systems, San Jose, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism software 9.0, and all results were reported as means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of Ali-NPs@Mexo

The biomimetic nanoplatform Ali-NPs@Mexo was created by first making Ali-NPs at an optimized pH to get the right core size. These nanoparticles were then wrapped in exosomal membranes using a co-extrusion method. The morphological and physicochemical properties of the final particles were comprehensively evaluated by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and dynamic light scattering (DLS). TEM images showed that both plain Ali-NPs and membrane-coated Ali-NPs@Mexo had a consistent round shape with clear edges (Fig. 1A). This spherical form helps the particles reach their target site more effectively [35]. DLS results showed that the particle size increased throughout the preparation stages (Fig. 1B). The number-weighted size distribution curves demonstrated a distinct peak shift from BSA to Ali-NPs, with a further shift observed after the exosomal membrane was added to form Ali-NPs@Mexo. Notably, the hydrodynamic diameters were precisely maintained between 30 and 40 nm. This compact size and narrow particle size distribution are critical for enhancing cellular uptake and blood-brain barrier penetration [[36], [37], [38]].

Zeta potential analysis was conducted to evaluate the surface charge, an essential metric for both colloidal stability and successful membrane integration. The recorded potentials for BSA, Ali-NPs, and Ali-NPs@Mexo were −11.33, −20.4, and −25.5 mV, respectively (Fig. 1C). Given the inherent anionic nature of cellular membranes, this negatively charged profile of Ali-NPs@Mexo is highly favorable for facilitating biological membrane crossing while mitigating potential cytotoxicity [39]. UV-Vis spectroscopy was utilized to monitor drug loading and release kinetics (Fig. 1D). The engineered Ali-NPs@Mexo exhibited a remarkable encapsulation efficiency of 96.69% and a drug loading capacity of 8.81% (Fig. 1E). Additionally, the hydrodynamic diameter of the nanoparticles remained consistent over a 50-h monitoring period (Fig. 1F), indicating robust structural integrity in an aqueous environment.

To verify the successful biomimetic coating, characteristic exosomal protein markers were assessed via immunoblotting. Mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes conventionally present specific surface markers (e.g., CD9, CD63, TSG101) while lacking intracellular components such as Calnexin [[40], [41], [42]]. As shown in Fig. 1G, both the native exosomes and the biomimetic Ali-NPs@Mexo exhibited pronounced bands for CD9 and TSG101. Conversely, the endoplasmic reticulum-resident marker Calnexin was restricted solely to the stem cell-conditioned medium (CM). This distinct protein expression profile not only confirms the high purity of the extracted exosomal membranes but also corroborates the successful core-shell assembly of the nanoparticles.

Ali-NPs@Mexo reduces inflammation and enhances neuronal repair in vitro

The biocompatibility of the Ali-NPs@Mexo was assessed to evaluate cell viability through the CCK-8 assays. Initial biocompatibility assessments confirmed that Ali, Mexo, and Ali-NPs@Mexo maintained high cell viability in BV2 microglia across all tested time points (Fig. 2A). Subsequent flow cytometric analysis indicated a clear time-dependent internalization of Ali-NPs@Mexo, demonstrating its efficient cellular uptake (Fig. 2B). To investigate the immunomodulatory potential of the nanoparticles, an LPS-stimulated BV2 inflammatory model was employed. While LPS challenge triggered pronounced morphological alterations typical of microglial activation, treatment with Ali-NPs@Mexo effectively restored the baseline cellular morphology (Fig. S1A). Confocal immunofluorescence profiling of macrophage polarization markers revealed that the nanoformulation significantly reversed the LPS-induced downregulation of YM1/2 and concurrent upregulation of Cox2 (Fig. 2C). This anti-inflammatory efficacy was further corroborated by the broad suppression of pro-inflammatory mediators; Ali-NPs@Mexo treatment drastically reduced the expression levels of iNOS, TNF-α, Cox2, CD16, and CD32 (Fig. 2D). Importantly, the biomimetic nanoparticles effectively scavenged intracellular ROS in the activated microglia (Fig. S1B and S1C). By ameliorating oxidative stress and driving the microglial phenotypic shift from a neurotoxic M1 state toward a neuroprotective M2 state, Ali-NPs@Mexo presents a highly viable therapeutic intervention for resolving neuroinflammation and facilitating tissue repair following TBI.

We next extended our investigation to primary neural cells. Following this, primary neural cells were isolated and cultured. Light microscopy images (Fig. S2A) showed the morphology of these cells at different time points, while immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. S2B) confirmed the expression of Nestin (a progenitor marker), GFAP (an astroglial marker), and Iba1 (a microglial marker) [43]. The co-existence of these proteins demonstrates that the isolated population is a heterogeneous mixture of neural-lineage cells, comprising neural stem/progenitor cells and their differentiated glial progeny. Biocompatibility profiling confirmed the negligible neurotoxicity of Ali-NPs@Mexo (Fig. 3A), while FITC-tracking revealed highly efficient cellular internalization by primary neural cells, peaking at 8 h (Fig. 3B). In an LPS-challenged primary neural culture, Ali-NPs@Mexo effectively prevented morphological deterioration (Fig. S2C) and profoundly quenched intracellular ROS accumulation (Fig. 3C), indicating robust anti-oxidative and cytoprotective capabilities.

To comprehensively evaluate tissue-level repair, we mapped the expression of neuro-regenerative (Nestin, MAP2) and inflammatory (GFAP, Iba1) markers via confocal microscopy (Fig. 3D, Fig. S3A and S3B). Compared to the modest effects of unconjugated Ali or Mexo, the biomimetic Ali-NPs@Mexo complex drove a dramatic phenotypic rescue. The treatment markedly upregulated Nestin and MAP2, reflecting enhanced neural stem cell activation and preserved cytoskeletal integrity. Concurrently, the drastic downregulation of GFAP and Iba1 signified a potent suppression of reactive astrogliosis and microglial activation. Collectively, these data establish Ali-NPs@Mexo as a dual-action therapeutic capable of halting neuroinflammation while actively orchestrating neural regeneration.

These findings demonstrate that Ali-NPs@Mexo promotes axonal growth and neural regeneration while mitigating neuroinflammation, consistent with the established anti-inflammatory [29,44,45] and neurorestorative properties [30,31,33] of Ali. Notably, this study is the first to show its effect on ROS levels in microglia. Together, the findings highlight Ali-NPs@Mexo as a strong candidate for treating neuronal injury by lowering inflammation and encouraging nerve recovery.

Ali-NPs@Mexo modulates macrophage polarization in BV2 cells in vitro

We next assessed the modulatory impact of the formulations on microglial polarization following LPS stimulation. Flow cytometric profiling indicated that LPS exposure drove microglia toward an M1-like phenotype, evidenced by the robust upregulation of Cox2 and the concomitant loss of the M2 marker YM1/2. This pro-inflammatory shift was further aggravated upon treatment with the RXR antagonist HX531. By contrast, both free Ali and the engineered Ali-NPs@Mexo effectively blunted Cox2 expression while restoring YM1/2 levels, with the nanoformulation exhibiting a superior restorative capacity (Fig. 4A and B). Cytokine quantification via ELISA corroborated these phenotypic changes; the LPS-induced depletion of anti-inflammatory mediators (TGF-β, IL-4, and IL-10) was worsened by HX531 but significantly reversed by Ali-based treatments. Notably, Ali-NPs@Mexo outperformed free Ali in replenishing these reparative cytokines (Fig. 4C–E). Mechanistically, qRT-PCR and immunoblotting assays revealed that the LPS challenge heavily suppressed RXR and PPAR expression while triggering a surge in the pro-inflammatory chemokine MCP-1—a cascade further intensified by HX531 blockade. However, treatment with Ali-NPs@Mexo profoundly counteracted these molecular alterations, normalizing the mRNA and protein levels of RXR, PPAR, and MCP-1 (Fig. 4F–J). Collectively, these data indicate that Ali-NPs@Mexo successfully reprograms microglia from a neurotoxic M1 state to a neuroprotective M2 phenotype, a transition mechanistically governed by the activation of the RXR/PPAR axis and the ensuing suppression of inflammatory cascades.

Behavioral assessments for functional recovery following TBI

To further evaluate whether the anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects of Ali-NPs@Mexo observed in vitro could translate into functional recovery in vivo, we assessed the cognitive and emotional outcomes in a mouse model of TBI. The Barnes maze test was employed to evaluate the spatial learning and memory capacities of mice following traumatic brain injury (TBI) and subsequent treatments (Fig. 5A and B). As illustrated by the representative motion trajectories in Fig. 5A, TBI and BSA-treated mice exhibited disorganized searching patterns with significantly increased track coverage compared to the Sham group, indicating severe spatial memory impairment. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 5B) revealed that the TBI model group took significantly longer to locate the target opening (escape latency) and committed a higher number of total errors compared to the Sham group. Following the administration of various formulations, these cognitive deficits were alleviated to varying degrees. Notably, the Ali-NPs@Mexo treatment group demonstrated the most robust therapeutic effect: the escape latency was approximately five-fold shorter than that of the TBI model group, and the total number of errors was substantially reduced. These results collectively suggest that Ali-NPs@Mexo treatment significantly restores spatial navigation and memory functions in TBI mice, enabling them to locate the target dark box more efficiently and accurately.

Beyond cognitive deficits, traumatic brain injury (TBI) is frequently accompanied by severe emotional sequelae, including anxiety. To evaluate the anxiolytic potential of Ali-NPs@Mexo, we subjected the mice to the elevated plus maze (EPM) paradigm. Behavioral tracking and heatmap analyses (Fig. 5C) revealed that while untreated TBI and BSA-treated cohorts exhibited stereotypic anxiety-like avoidance—restricting their locomotor activity primarily to the closed arms—treatment with Ali-NPs@Mexo profoundly restored exploratory behavior in the open arms. Quantitative assessments (Fig. 5D) corroborated this phenotypic rescue; the Ali-NPs@Mexo group demonstrated a significant increase in both the duration spent in the open arms and the ratio of open-to-closed arm travel distance compared to the TBI model group. Given that oxidative stress is a primary catalyst for TBI-associated affective disorders [[46], [47], [48]], these robust behavioral improvements underscore the potent neuroprotective and anxiolytic efficacy of Ali-NPs@Mexo, highlighting its translational value for managing the multifaceted neurological complications of brain injury.

Ali-NPs@Mexo promotes M2 macrophage polarization in vivo

Driven by the profound neurobehavioral improvements, we next interrogated the in vivo immunomodulatory mechanisms of Ali-NPs@Mexo. Flow cytometric mapping of the injured brain revealed a classical TBI-induced neuroinflammatory signature: a stark upregulation of M1 microglia (Cox2+) at the expense of the M2 phenotype (YM1/2+). Notably, Ali-NPs@Mexo treatment forcefully reprogrammed this imbalance, driving a robust M2-skewing effect that surpassed that of unencapsulated Ali (Fig. 6A and B). This phenotypic shift translated into a highly anti-inflammatory tissue microenvironment, as evidenced by the maximal restoration of tissue TGF-β, IL-4, and IL-10 levels in the nanoparticle-treated cohort (Fig. 6C–E). Crucially, the mechanistic dependence on the RXR/PPAR-γ axis was validated using the RXR antagonist HX531. Molecular profiling demonstrated that TBI-induced downregulation of RXR, PPAR-γ, and MCP-1 was effectively reversed by Ali-NPs@Mexo at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 6F–J). The observation that HX531 administration systematically abrogated these protective cytokine profiles and receptor expressions confirms that targeted activation of the RXR/PPAR-γ pathway is the primary mechanism by which Ali-NPs@Mexo resolves neuroinflammation and promotes post-TBI tissue repair.

Ali-NPs@Mexo efficiently targets the brain and triggers neural repair in vivo

The brain-targeting capability of Ali-NPs@Mexo was evaluated to determine whether the observed therapeutic effects were attributed to efficient brain delivery. Coating Mexo onto the surface of nanocarriers has been reported to confer them with natural properties from the original cell membrane, which reduces immunogenicity, prolongs their half-life, and enhances their targeting capability [49,50]. Thus, we evaluated the brain-targeting capability of Ali-NPs@Mexo nanoparticles by performing Cy5-based fluorescence imaging in the brains of TBI mice at various time points after injection. Ali-NPs@Mexo exhibited prominent accumulation in the brain compared to free Cy5 and free BSA (Cy5 labeled) at 8 h post-injection, with significant retention observed up to 12 h (Fig. 7A). To confirm the targeting ability of Ali-NPs@Mexo to the brain, a tissue distribution experiment was conducted. As shown in Fig. 7B, the accumulation of Ali-NPs@Mexo nanoparticles in the brain was significantly higher compared to the BSA group.

We next examined the impact of brain-accumulated Ali-NPs@Mexo on synaptic structural integrity. Cortical tissues from different groups of mice were obtained and examined via brain electron microscopy (Fig. 7C). Quantitative analysis of synaptic number, maximal PSD thickness, width of synaptic cleft, and synaptic cleft length demonstrated significant recovery of neural synaptic structures following Ali-NPs@Mexo treatment (Fig. 7C). Additionally, Golgi staining images (Fig. 7D) of cerebral cortical tissues and quantification of dendritic spine density indicated that Ali-NPs@Mexo significantly promoted neural synaptic plasticity. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Ali-NPs@Mexo efficiently targets the injured brain and facilitates structural neurorepair, providing a mechanistic basis for the functional recovery observed in behavioral tests. This evidence supports the potential of Ali-NPs@Mexo to enhance neural repair and recovery induced by TBI. Previous studies have demonstrated that exosome-based biomaterials promote TBI recovery by enhancing angiogenesis, neurogenesis, and neural network reconstruction [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. However, most reported exosome therapies rely on local implantation strategies, whereas our Ali-NPs@Mexo platform enables systemic administration while integrating exosome-mediated immunomodulation with drug delivery.

Neuroprotective effects of Ali-NPs@Mexo in TBI

To comprehensively evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of Ali-NPs@Mexo, we examined its impact on histopathological improvement and neural regeneration in the injured brain. We subsequently evaluated the histopathological changes in the brain cortex area of the mouse model of TBI using HE and Nissl staining approaches. As illustrated in Fig. 8A, treatment with Ali-NPs@Mexo significantly ameliorated the pathological state induced by TBI. Moreover, confocal imaging further revealed that Myelin basic protein (MBP), Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and Nestin expressions in the Ali-NPs@Mexo-treated group were comparable to those in the normal group (Fig. 8B and C). We next evaluated the impact of the formulation on neuroglial dynamics and the broader inflammatory milieu post-TBI. Molecular profiling revealed that Ali-NPs@Mexo significantly curtailed reactive astrogliosis, as evidenced by the robust downregulation of GFAP expression. Concurrently, the treatment upregulated MBP and Nestin, indicating enhanced oligodendrocyte-mediated remyelination and the active recruitment of endogenous neural stem cells. Beyond these proneurogenic and structural benefits, Ali-NPs@Mexo profoundly suppressed the local secretion of classical pro-inflammatory mediators, including IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (Fig. S4A–D). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Ali-NPs@Mexo orchestrates a comprehensive neuroprotective response by simultaneously extinguishing neuroinflammation and establishing a microenvironment highly conducive to neural regeneration.

To substantiate the histological observations, we profiled the expression of key neuroglial markers within the injured parenchyma. Immunofluorescence mapping revealed that Ali-NPs@Mexo treatment significantly enhanced the presence of the axonal marker Tuj1 and the myelination marker MBP, while concurrently suppressing the microglial activation marker Iba1, relative to the untreated TBI cohort (Fig. 9A and B). These spatial observations were quantitatively corroborated by qRT-PCR and immunoblotting assays, which confirmed a marked upregulation of MBP and β3-Tubulin alongside a robust decline in the astrogliosis marker GFAP (Figures S5A-C and S6A-D). Collectively, this biomarker signature indicates that Ali-NPs@Mexo effectively resolves the hostile inflammatory milieu, creating a highly permissive microenvironment that facilitates axonal sprouting and remyelination. Furthermore, comprehensive histological (H&E) and biochemical evaluations of major organs revealed no signs of systemic toxicity following intravenous administration (Figures S7 and S8A-D). These data underscore the dual capacity of Ali-NPs@Mexo to drive profound neurorestoration while maintaining an excellent in vivo safety profile.

Conclusion

Herein, we present Ali-NPs@Mexo, a biomimetic nanoplatform engineered to resolve the intractable neuroinflammation associated with traumatic brain injury (TBI) [[51], [52], [53]]. By leveraging the innate homing capability of mesenchymal stem cell exosomes and the BBB-crossing efficiency of a nanoparticle core, this system achieves prolonged (>12 h), targeted accumulation specifically within the injured brain parenchyma. Once localized, Ali-NPs@Mexo actively remodels the hostile microenvironment; it drives robust microglial repolarization from an M1 to an M2 phenotype via targeted activation of the RXR/PPAR-γ signaling cascade. This precise immunomodulatory intervention successfully halts secondary injury progression and significantly accelerates in vivo cognitive and neurobehavioral recovery. By seamlessly coupling high biosafety with dual-targeted efficacy, the Ali-NPs@Mexo platform emerges as a compelling translational candidate for comprehensive TBI management.
