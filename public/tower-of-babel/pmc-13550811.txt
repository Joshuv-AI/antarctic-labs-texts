Background

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most heritable cancers [1, 2]. Up to 57% of PCa risk may be accounted for by inherited factors [3], which include relatively rare inactivating genetic variants that confer substantially higher risk (e.g., BRCA2). Many of the germline pathogenic variants (gPVs) associated with significantly elevated PCa risk occur in genes important for DNA repair [4]. This study is built on the concept that early cancer detection may facilitate greater chance for curative intervention in individuals who inherit genetic variants associated with increased PCa risk. Early cancer detection will also facilitate better understanding of tumorigenesis in these specific genetic contexts and indicate opportunities for studying earlier use of targeted therapies and/or cancer prevention strategies.

Pathogenic germline variants in BRCA1 and BRCA2 are best recognised in association with autosomal dominant hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (HBOC) predisposition syndrome. They are also estimated to confer up to 3.8‐fold [5, 6] and 8.6‐fold increased risks of PCa [7, 8], respectively. Studies indicate that individuals with PCa who carry BRCA2 gPVs have earlier onset and worse outcomes [9, 10, 11, 12]. gPVs in BRCA1, BRCA2, and 14 other genes have been found to be enriched in the metastatic PCa population [4]. Moreover, the majority of evaluable tumours had evidence of second allele inactivation, a strong argument for the biological significance of the gPV [4]. These data provide rationale for offering those who carry germline cancer predisposition gene variants a customised PCa early detection programme.

Prostate cancer screening using serum PSA has been controversial due to poor specificity and limited survival benefit observed in the European Randomised Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer (ESRPC) study [13, 14]. The Prostate, Lung, Colorectal and Ovarian (PLCO) Cancer Screening Trial, did not find benefit, although was flawed by >80% of the control group undergoing PSA screening [15]. However, in a population with known high genetic risk, the rationale for early detection strategies is established in other genetic cancer predisposition contexts, e.g., breast, ovarian, colorectal, and other HBOC‐ and Lynch syndrome‐related cancers. Modified PCa screening for germline BRCA1/2 mutation carriers is being explored in the context of the UK‐led international study: ‘IMPACT: Identification of Men with a genetic predisposition to ProstAte Cancer: Targeted screening in BRCA1/2 carriers and controls’ [16]. Using a PSA level threshold of >3 ng/mL to guide biopsy, the IMPACT study reported 48% and 32% positive predictive value (PPV) of detecting PCa in BRCA2 gPV carriers and MSH2/MSH6 gPV carriers [16], respectively [16]. A recent update of the IMPACT study using the PSA level threshold of >3 ng/mL for biopsy found that the incidence of clinically significant PCa (csPCa) after five screening rounds was higher in BRCA2 gPV carriers vs controls (3.1% vs 1.3%), and the proportion of csPCa was higher in both BRCA2 and BRCA1 gPV carriers vs controls (65% vs 32% and 56% vs 18%, respectively) [17].

Prostate‐specific antigen has known limitations; in the Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial (PCPT), even at very low PSA levels of <0.5 ng/mL, there was a 6.6% prevalence of PCa of which 12.5% was high grade [18, 19]. This limitation is of greatest concern for those at highest genetic risk of aggressive PCa, including carriers of gPV in rare but important genes such as BRCA2, and those deemed at high risk due to high polygenic risk score (PRS). Several early studies provide support for a tailored programme for carriers of rare gPV. A study of male carriers of BRCA1/2 gPV from Israel used PSA level and prostate multiparametric MRI (mpMRI), where those with elevated age‐adjusted PSA levels and/or suspicious lesions on mpMRI were offered biopsy. PSA levels, MRI findings, PCa incidence, and tumour characteristics were evaluated and the authors reported age‐adjusted PSA and mpMRI may be warranted for individuals aged <55 years. However, the authors noted that 90% of their cohort carried a Jewish founder gPV and warrants further study in other population settings [20]. Amini et al. [21] reported initial findings from the PROGRESS study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05129605), which enrolled male carriers of gPV in 19 PCa risk genes (the majority in BRCA2, BRCA1 or ATM), aged 35–74 years, who were screened with annual PSA and DRE along with triennial mpMRI [21]. Biopsy was offered for abnormal DRE, elevated PSA level for age and/or suspicious mpMRI findings. In the initial screening round of 101 participants, 21 underwent biopsies, of which nine identified PCa and decision curve analysis suggests that, similar to the Segal et al. [20] study, PSA‐based screening for PCa may be enhanced by mpMRI, especially for younger individuals at higher genetic risk of PCa. Finally, although study eligibility was based on high PRS rather than gPV in known or putative PCa risk genes, the BARCODE1 study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03857477) performed MRI and prostate biopsy on 468 of 745 men with a PRS in the top 90th percentile regardless of PSA or MRI results, and 187 (40%) were diagnosed with PCa, of which 55% were (cs)PCa [22]. In addition, evidence suggests that increased cancer screening can have psychosocial impacts, which has been examined in the IMPACT study. The study procedures could potentially affect cancer worry, stress and quality of life, although the IMPACT study did not find an overall adverse impact on psychosocial health [23], we will collect prospective patient‐reported data in our study. Taken together, the emerging data indicate that an optimal tailored PCa screening approach for individuals at highest genetic risk of PCa incorporate starting screening at a younger age, incorporating age‐adjusted PSA thresholds and mpMRI, while recognising that additional refinements using clinical and biological biomarkers, along with patient‐reported measures are needed for further optimisation.

Here, we have initiated an early detection study of PCa using PSA thresholds established around age‐adjusted median PSA level values in conjunction with mpMRI as a trigger for biopsy [21, 24, 25]. Emerging novel biomarkers including blood‐based and novel imaging are being studied. Active surveillance for individuals with low‐risk PCa allows a unique opportunity to follow the natural history of patients with genetic risk factors to understand rates of re‐classification or progression. The primary hypothesis of the ‘Prostate Cancer Screening for People at Genetic Risk of Aggressive Disease’ (PATROL) study is that pre‐defined age‐adjusted PSA level thresholds and imaging, such as mpMRI, to guide prostate biopsy will identify (cs)PCa in a population at genetically high risk of developing PCa. In addition, other important secondary and exploratory endpoints will be facilitated by assembling a large prospective cohort of individuals at increased genetic risk.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint is to determine the PPV of pre‐defined age‐directed PSA level thresholds and prostate mpMRI for biopsy. Other exploratory endpoints include characterising clinicopathological characteristics of diagnosed PCa and understanding the impact of screening on patient‐reported outcomes (e.g., anxiety and decisional regret). Biospecimens being collected include germline DNA, urine, and tumour tissue to evaluate emerging clinical and research biomarkers.

Eligibility Criteria

The PATROL study is recruiting individuals aged ≥40 years who carry a gPV in a known or suspected PCa risk gene. Based upon current research, these genes are defined as including: ATM, BRCA1, BRCA2, BRIP1, CHEK2, EPCAM, HOXB13, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PALB2, PMS2, TP53, and RAD51D. As additional genes may be identified with new genetic variants that may contribute to a predisposition to PCa, patients carrying other genetic risk factors may be included with approval of the Principal Investigator. Eligible participants have a prostate, are aged ≥40 years, and have a documented gPV in one or more known or suspected genes associated with PCa risk. Exclusion criteria include: a prior diagnosis of PCa, a medical contraindication to any study procedures (e.g., prostate biopsy), any ongoing or prior cancer treatment with curative intent within the past 12 months (for all cancer types except non‐melanoma skin cancer), prior or concurrent participation in an interventional clinical trial aimed at preventing cancer for individuals with germline variants associated with increased PCa risk, inability to provide written informed consent, or inability or unwillingness to complete clinical care and study procedures as indicated by the study protocol.

Methods

The study protocol was approved by the individual Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of the Cancer Consortium of the University of Washington and Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center, along with all IRBs of all participating sites. The registration period is 21 May 2020 to 31 August 2030 and we initially sought to enrol at least 450 individuals as a single site, with the current plan to continue enrolment to this observational study so long as funding is secured. Eligible participants are approached through a genetics/high‐risk clinic, and/or urology clinic after previously being identified to be a carrier of a gPV. To be enrolled, participants must provide written informed consent and may withdraw consent at any time. Patients receive clinical care as determined by their treating physician, including PSA screening, prostate MRI, prostate biopsies, and clinical management of any PCa diagnosis (e.g., active surveillance or definitive treatment). Specific to this study, prostate biopsies are recommended based upon age‐adjusted PSA level thresholds, and prostate MRIs are recommended at baseline and prior to any biopsy (Fig. 1). In addition, demographic and medical history information, clinical data and outcomes, patient‐reported information and outcome measures, and biospecimens are collected to assess primary and exploratory outcomes.

Prostate cancer has a long natural history, and long‐term data about disease status is necessary for a complete understanding of the disease, and particularly in the context of germline mutations in cancer predisposition genes. Therefore, long‐term clinical PCa outcomes will continue to be collected until the study closes. Should this information not be available within the electronic health record (EHR), participants may be contacted annually about their disease status. Contact may occur for the lifetime of a participant, unless a participant requests no further contact.

The PCa Screening

Participants receive annual PCa screening inclusive of: physical examination (e.g., vital signs), total PSA evaluation, and imaging assessments (e.g., mpMRI) per treating physician recommendation. At least annually for 10 years, the following study‐specific procedures are performed: collection of clinical data, IPSS, blood and urine biospecimens. Investigational biomarkers may be assessed along with patient‐reported information and outcome measures. If a participant is diagnosed with PCa, their study‐specific procedures will change, as detailed below.

Diagnostic Prostate Biopsy

Participants are recommended to undergo prostate biopsy based on age‐adjusted PSA level thresholds as described in the schema (Fig. 1) or at any time based on clinical decision‐making of the treating physician (e.g., abnormal DRE). Prostate biopsies are taken using ultrasonography or MRI guidance with at least a 12‐core systematic template. Prostate biopsies are interpreted through the site's pathology department. When a diagnostic prostate biopsy is taken, blood, urine, and archival tissue specimens are collected. Prostate MRI should be considered before each prostate biopsy.

Diagnosis of PCa

If a participant is diagnosed with PCa, they will continue to receive clinical care as determined by their treating physician with anticipated care options of active surveillance or definitive treatment.

Active Surveillance of PCa

If a participant elects to pursue active surveillance to manage their cancer, surveillance visits and procedures will continue at the frequency determined by their treating physician and inclusive of the following: physical examination, PSA evaluation, imaging assessments and surveillance biopsies per treating physician recommendation. Starting from the time of PCa diagnosis, and at least annually for 10 years, or until receipt of definitive treatment, the following will be collected: clinical data, IPSS, blood and urine biospecimens, and archival tissue specimens. Investigational biomarkers may be assessed, and patient‐reported information and outcome measures will be collected. When a surveillance prostate biopsy is taken, blood, urine, and archival tissue will be collected for the biorepository.

Definitive Treatment of PCa

If a participant elects to undergo definitive treatment for PCa (e.g., radical prostatectomy or radiation), visits and procedures will continue at the frequency determined by their treating physician, including physical examination, PSA evaluation, and imaging assessments per treating physician recommendation. Prior to definitive treatment, blood and urine biospecimens will be collected (if no blood or urine specimens were collected in the previous 12 months). Archival tissue specimens will be collected for participants who undergo radical prostatectomy. For up to 1 year following definitive treatment initiation, the following will be collected: clinical data, patient‐reported information and outcome measures, and blood biospecimens.

Patient‐Reported Information and Outcome Measures

Validated, participant‐reported health‐related quality of life (HRQOL) instruments are administered to participants at study entry and at least annually via electronic survey or paper survey. Participant‐reported demographics are collected, including race/ethnicity and health information, patient‐reported concomitant medications of interest (e.g., finasteride, aspirin, metformin) and patient‐reported family history of cancer. The study also collects patient‐reported PCa‐specific questions such as perceived risk and severity [23, 26], anxiety [27], and regret [28, 29]. Validated, patient‐reported HRQOL instruments in this protocol are: the Patient‐Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System 29‐question health survey (PROMIS)‐29 version 2.1, Single Item Literacy Screener [30], Expanded Prostate Cancer Index Composite 26‐item version (EPIC‐26), Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test‐Concise (AUDIT‐C), and IPSS. The baseline questionnaire takes ~30 min to complete.

Recruiting sites enter data into a study‐specific Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) database, a secure, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)‐compliant database maintained by the coordinating site. Long‐term clinical PCa outcomes data from the EHR are collected annually for the lifetime of a participant (e.g., vital status, PSA, PCa recurrence or progression, PCa treatments).

Sample Size Considerations and Statistical Analysis Plan

To estimate PPVs of age‐based PSA level thresholds for biopsy referral in this patient population, we will first fit a logistic regression model to estimate the odds that biopsy leads to a PCa diagnosis. The regression will have a binary indicator of PCa diagnosis as the response variable, patient age at biopsy (categorised as <50, 50–59, or ≥60 years to correspond to the age‐based PSA level thresholds) as a fixed effect, and a unique patient identifier as a random effect to account for possibly repeated biopsies. We will then convert the estimated odds that biopsy leads to a PCa diagnosis within each age category into corresponding probabilities of this outcome—i.e., PPV point estimates and associated 95% CIs—using the inverse logit function. A similar approach will be used to estimate the PPV of mpMRI by Prostate Imaging‐Reporting And Data System (PI‐RADS) score in the study population.

Possible bias due to missing biopsy outcome data (i.e., patient non‐adherence) will be investigated using extreme case analysis if the missing rate is <10% and multiple imputation based on patient characteristics (e.g., patient age, total PSA level, number of previous biopsies, and the number of first‐degree relatives with a PCa diagnosis) if the missing rate is ≥10%.

A secondary exploratory analysis will include biopsy scheme (e.g., systematic or MRI‐targeted biopsies) as a fixed effect in the regression model to examine sensitivity of the biopsy technique used. However, results of this analysis will be interpreted with caution owing to possible selection bias in the choice of biopsy scheme. Descriptive statistics will summarise clinical variables, and parametric or non‐parametric tests will characterise univariate associations with PCa diagnosis.

Continued enrolment will facilitate exploratory analyses within subgroups defined by age at enrolment as well as other subgroups of interest, including specific gene variants, race, and family history. Continuing to conduct follow‐up will allow us to more precisely estimate rates of PCa progression. Additional exploratory analyses will examine associations between (a) biomarkers (e.g., Select MDx, or other novel biomarkers) and imaging (e.g., mpMRI) and (b) frequencies of PCa diagnosis, both overall and high‐grade (Grade Group 2–5). Longitudinal trajectories of the effect of PCa screening on patient‐reported outcomes over time will be characterised using standard linear mixed‐effects models or generalised estimating equations.

Discussion

Recent advances have highlighted the role of rare gPVs in BRCA2, BRCA1, HOXB13 and other genes in informing PCa risk as well as, in many cases, serving as a prognostic and predictive biomarker if PCa is subsequently diagnosed. National and international guidelines from groups such as American Cancer Society, American Society for Clinical Oncology, American Urological Association, European Association of Urology, European Society for Clinical Oncology, and National Comprehensive Cancer Network now advise PCa screening for individuals who are carriers of BRCA2 and other PCa risk genes. Most suggest beginning PCa screening at the age of 40 years, and consideration for prostate mpMRI has recently been included in the NCCN Genetic/Familial High‐Risk Breast, Ovarian, Pancreatic and Prostate guidelines for BRCA2 gPV carriers [31]. While the recognition that risk management should be modified for those at high genetic risk is an important first step, much more is needed to further refine current guidelines and to understand the genetic and environmental modifiers of risk, as well as to seek new methods and biomarkers for early detection in a population at high risk of developing PCa.

The PATROL study is a multi‐institutional, collaborative prospective clinical trial open at eight sites around the USA and offers a critical framework for more attentive screening based on age‐adjusted PSA levels and MRI. It is designed to incorporate research opportunities for translational investigations and serves to engage patients and motivate researchers around the country towards an aligned goal of improving methods for tailored early cancer detection in this high‐risk population.
