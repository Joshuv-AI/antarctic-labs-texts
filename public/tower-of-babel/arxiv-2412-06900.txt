Robust Catalysis and Resource Broadcasting: The Possible and the Impossible
Jeongrak Son, Ray Ganardi, Shintaro Minagawa, Francesco Buscemi, Seok Hyung Lie, Nelly H.Y. Ng

Abstract
In resource theories, catalysis refers to the possibility of enabling otherwise inaccessible quantum state transitions by providing the agent with an auxiliary system, under the condition that this auxiliary is returned to its initial state at the end of the protocol. Most studies to date have focused on fine-tuned catalytic processes that are highly sensitive to error: if the initial state of the system deviates even slightly from that for which the catalyst was designed, the catalyst would be irreparably degraded. To address this challenge, we introduce and study robust catalytic transformations and explore the extent of their capabilities. It turns out that robust catalysis is subtly related to the property of resource broadcasting. In particular, we show that the possibility of robust catalysis is equivalent to that of resource broadcasting in completely resource non-generating theories. This allows us to characterize a general class of resource theories that allow neither robust catalysis nor resource broadcasting, and another class where instead resource broadcasting and robust catalysis are possible and provide maximal advantage. Our approach encompasses a wide range of quantum resource theories, including entanglement, coherence, thermodynamics, magic, and imaginarity.



Introduction
—Quantum technologies promise disruptive innovations in computation, communication, and sensing
Acín et al. 2018
, relying on resources like entanglement
Horodecki et al. 2009
, coherence
Streltsov et al. 2017
, and magic
Bravyi and Kitaev 2005
.
However, realizing the full potential of these technologies remains challenging, as such quantum resources are fragile and difficult to generate.
Efficient manipulation and preservation of these resources are therefore crucial in quantum information processing.
The beauty of resource theories
Chitambar and Gour 2019
therein lies: they provide a powerful, unified framework for analyzing quantum resources.

In particular, resource theories set fundamental limits on the efficiency of physical protocols, measured by the resources needed to implement them; the simplest case involves direct transformations between states without any pre-existing resources.
Catalysis in quantum information theory has emerged as a powerful technique for enabling state transformations that would be impossible in this simplest scenario; see Refs.
Lipka-Bartosik et al. 2024
;
Datta et al. 2023
for comprehensive reviews.
The key idea is that having an extra system available—even if it must return to its initial state at the end of the protocol (whence the term “catalysis”)—may help overcome certain limitations.
Catalysis has been shown to enable and enhance entanglement generation
Cirac and Zoller 1994
;
Jonathan and Plenio 1999
, relate one-shot to asymptotic transformations
Duan et al. 2005
, and provide a formulation of thermodynamic second laws
Brandão et al. 2015
.
However, the advantages of catalytic transformations come with a specific requirement: since the catalyst, by definition, is assumed to be reused multiple times, it must return to its initial state at the end of the protocol.
This is a fragile condition that makes catalytic protocols more sensitive than non-catalytic ones.

This fragility becomes apparent when considering potential sources of errors (see Fig.
1
).
Aside from those in the channel that implements the transformation, which cannot be mitigated, errors can occur at two points in the protocol: in preparing the system’s state
\rho
and in preparing the catalyst’s state
\tau
.
The latter case is easily addressed: if, instead of
\tau
, another state
\tau_{\epsilon}
, which is
\epsilon
-far from
\tau
, is prepared, the data-processing inequality ensures that the error remains under control, in the sense that the final catalyst error remains bounded by the initial error (see Supplemental Materialsfor a more detailed discussion).
Instead, when a wrong system state
\rho_{\epsilon}
is prepared, the error can accumulate in the catalyst, which will continue to degrade further as the process is repeated.
In the worst case, the accumulated error on the catalyst grows linearly with the number of repetitions
n
.
Thus, the degradation of the catalyst due to preparation errors in the system is a more serious issue and will be the main focus of this work.

Unfortunately, most previous results on catalytic transformations assume perfect knowledge and control over the preparation of the system state.
This is particularly evident in the design of the catalyst state, which is extremely fine-tuned to the state of the system.
To address this, we formulate the concept of
robust catalysis
, where the catalyst is resilient to small errors in the system preparation. Our first result has the flavor of a no-go theorem: even by assuming only arbitrarily small (albeit non-zero) system preparation errors, robust catalysis becomes equivalent to a much stronger condition—that the transformation be possible using
catalytic channels

Vidal and Cirac 2002
;
Boes et al. 2018
;
Lie and Jeong 2021a
;
Lie and Jeong 2021b
.
In essence, even minimal robustness demands full robustness.
We also uncover a connection between robust catalysis and resource broadcasting
Lu et al. 2013
;
Marvian and Spekkens 2019
;
Lostaglio and Müller 2019
;
Yang et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2024
, the latter being a generalization of quantum state broadcasting
Barnum et al. 1996
;
D’Ariano et al. 2005
;
Buscemi et al. 2006
;
Barnum et al. 2007
;
Piani et al. 2008
;
Parzygnat et al. 2024
.
Finally, we establish general conditions for the possibility or impossibility of robust catalysis in abstract resource theories as well as in specific examples of physical and practical interest.
Notably, when robust catalysis is possible, we identify a resource monotone that strongly characterizes catalytically accessible states.
On one hand, these results have implications for the practical implementation of catalysis by reducing the burden of fine-tuning the system state.
On the other hand, they suggest a deeper relationship between the structure of composite free state sets and catalysis.

Throughout the manuscript we will denote quantum systems with
X,Y
, the set of density matrices for
X
as
\mathcal{D}_{X}
, and the set of quantum channels (i.e., trace-preserving completely positive linear maps) from
X
to
Y
as
\mathrm{CPTP}_{X\to Y}
. Any resource theory is characterized by two elements: a set of free states
\mathcal{S}
and free operations
\mathcal{O}
. We add system subscripts, e.g.
\mathcal{S}_{X}
, when referring to free states on
X
, and similarly for free operations, e.g.
\mathcal{O}_{X\to Y}
. We implicitly assume that the set of free states is a fixed property of each system, e.g. in the athermality theory, qubit systems with different Hamiltonians are treated as distinct systems.

Definition and basic properties
—We start with a rigorous formulation of catalytic processes that are robust against small variations in the initial system.

Definition 1

(Robust catalysis)
.

Given an initial state
\rho_{S}
and a robustness parameter
\epsilon>0
, a channel
\Lambda\in\mathcal{O}_{SC\to S^{\prime}C}
implements
(\rho,\epsilon)
-
robust catalysis
if there exists a catalyst state
\tau_{C}\in\mathcal{D}_{C}
such that, for all system states
\sigma_{S}
with
\|\sigma_{S}-\rho_{S}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon
, the catalyst is recovered exactly, i.e.
\Tr_{S^{\prime}}[\Lambda(\sigma_{S}\otimes\tau_{C})]=\tau_{C}
. We say that
(\rho,\epsilon)
-robust catalysis is
strict
when no correlations are established between the system and the catalyst, i.e.,
\Lambda(\sigma_{S}\otimes\tau_{C})=\tilde{\sigma}_{S^{\prime}}\otimes\tau_{C}
.

Def.
1
seems to be the most natural way of relaxing the fine-tuned dependence of the catalyst on the initial state of the system, which is arguably a major conceptual weakness in the theory of catalytic transformations and the motivation for this work.
Indeed, the above definition makes no assumptions on the system’s state
after
the process.
Moreover, the robustness parameter
\epsilon
, capturing the degree of initial state preparation errors, can be arbitrarily small.
The only truly stringent requirement is that the catalyst be returned to its initial state without error. This is a common requirement in the literature on catalytic transformations, necessary to prevent embezzlement
van Dam and Hayden 2003
;
Leung and Wang 2014
;
Ng et al. 2015
.
We also stress that robust catalysis should not be confused with
universal catalysis
, where universality is achieved by fine-tuning the free operations with respect to input states
Lipka-Bartosik and Skrzypczyk 2021
;
Datta et al. 2024
. A catalytic process that is robust is therefore anticipated to be much more reliably implemented, since it guarantees the reusability of the catalyst in the presence of errors.

And yet, as we prove below, Def.
1
turns out to be equivalent to an apparently much more stringent definition of catalysis, defined as follows.
Consider a process that is catalytic, but in a completely
input-agnostic
way, i.e., a channel
\tilde{\Lambda}\in\mathrm{CPTP}_{S\to S^{\prime}}
with a dilation
\Lambda\in\mathcal{O}_{SC\to S^{\prime}C}
and a catalyst state
\tau_{C}
, such that
\Tr_{S^{\prime}}[\Lambda(\rho_{S}\otimes\tau_{C})]=\tau_{C}
for all input states
\rho_{S}\in\mathcal{D}_{S}
.
Such channels are called
catalytic channels
.
They are typically studied when the dilation
\Lambda
is a unitary operation
Boes et al. 2018
;
Lie and Jeong 2021a
;
Lie and Jeong 2021b
, but here we allow
\Lambda
to be any free operation.
As anticipated, our first result shows that even the seemingly minimalistic requirements set for robust catalysis impose strong demands on the process.

Theorem 1
.

For any
\rho_{S}
and any
\epsilon>0
, a channel
\Lambda\in\mathcal{O}_{SC\to S^{\prime}C}
implements a
(\rho,\epsilon)
-robust catalysis with catalyst’s state
\tau_{C}
, if and only if the channel
\tilde{\Lambda}(\bullet_{S})\coloneq\Tr_{C}[\Lambda(\bullet_{S}\otimes\tau_{C})]
is a catalytic channel.

Thm.
1
clarifies that all forms of catalysis, other than catalytic channels, are inevitably fine-tuned to a very specific initial state of the system and risk degrading the catalyst whenever the system’s state is not prepared with strictly infinite precision.
It also presents a mathematically simplified pathway towards robust catalysis, as one may directly examine the structure of catalytic channels, rather than inspecting different catalyst states and fine-tuned strategies for state transformations.
The idea for the proof of Thm.
1
above is to use the fact that the robust catalytic condition restricts the channel action on a full-dimensional subset of inputs.
We refer readers to the End Matters for the full argument.

With Thm.
1
, an immediate question arises as to whether catalytic channels can provide any meaningful advantage.
Firstly, it is worth noting that whenever the set of achievable processes is not convex, the additional use of randomness is always advantageous; this can be seen as a special instance of robust catalysis
Boes et al. 2018
;
Lie and Jeong 2021b
;
Lie and Jeong 2021a
.
Furthermore, suppose that the catalyst state can be prepared using a free operation.
This is true, e.g. in some cases for the resource theory of thermodynamics, where even Gibbs states can produce a catalytic advantage
Korzekwa and Lostaglio 2022
;
Czartowski et al. 2023
;
Son and Ng 2024a
;
Son and Ng 2024b
.
Robust catalysis also emerges naturally in such theories.
Interestingly, robust catalysis is not limited to these two scenarios; for example, the set of catalytic channels is strictly larger than the set of all convex combinations of unitary channels
Lie and Jeong 2021b
.
The true extent of the robust catalytic advantage therefore emerges as a problem that demands further scrutiny.

Robust catalysis and resource broadcasting
—To focus on more interesting advantages that robust catalysis can offer, we consider free operations that are already convex and do not get enhanced trivially by catalysts.
Henceforth, we make several basic assumptions of the set of free states and free operations:

(A1)

\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}\in\mathcal{S}_{AB}
whenever
\rho_{A}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
and
\rho_{B}\in\mathcal{S}_{B}
,

(A2)

if
\rho_{AB}\in\mathcal{S}_{AB}
, then
\Tr_{B}[\rho_{AB}]\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
and
\Tr_{A}[\rho_{AB}]\in\mathcal{S}_{B}
,

(A3)

\mathcal{S}_{X}
is a convex set for any system
X
, and

(A4)

there always exists a full-rank state
\gamma\in\mathcal{S}
.

These axioms do not uniquely characterize the composite free states
\mathcal{S}_{AB}
given individual
\mathcal{S}_{A},\mathcal{S}_{B}
.
Nevertheless, these criteria cover a wide range of quantum resource theories including those of entanglement, athermality, coherence, asymmetry, and magic.
The first three are often used as basic assumptions, while Assumption (A4) is justified in Supplemental Materials Sec.
IV.4
.
Unless otherwise specified, we consider
completely resource non-generating (CRNG) theories
, where the free operations include all channels
\Lambda\in\mathrm{CPTP}_{A\to B}
, such that

\displaystyle\left(\id_{A^{\prime}}\otimes\Lambda\right)(\gamma_{A^{\prime}A})\in\mathcal{S}_{A^{\prime}B},

(1)

for an arbitrary system
A^{\prime}
and all
\gamma_{A^{\prime}A}\in\mathcal{S}_{A^{\prime}A}
.
Here,
\id_{A^{\prime}}\in\mathrm{CPTP}_{A^{\prime}\to A^{\prime}}
denotes the identity channel.
CRNG operations are the full set of channels that cannot generate any resource from free states, even when acting on subsystems.
Allowing free operations beyond CRNG often trivializes the theory by enabling arbitrary state transformations.
Well-known examples of CRNG operations include separable operations for entanglement
Zanoni and Scandolo 2024
, Gibbs-preserving operations for athermality
Faist et al. 2015
, and covariant operations for asymmetry
Keyl and Werner 1999
;
Gour and Spekkens 2008
.

Since catalysis refers to the activation of processes that would otherwise be impossible, robust catalysis must implement a catalytic channel
\tilde{\Lambda}\in\mathrm{CPTP}_{S\to S^{\prime}}
that is not free.
Therefore, to ascertain the capability of inducing robust catalysis with catalytic channel
\tilde{\Lambda}
, it suffices to find a free state
\gamma_{S}
such that
\tilde{\Lambda}(\gamma_{S})\notin\mathcal{S}_{S^{\prime}}
.
Conversely, if all catalytic channels
\tilde{\Lambda}
maps
\mathcal{S}_{S}
to
\mathcal{S}_{S^{\prime}}
, then no advantage can be gained from robust catalysis
1
1

1

Strictly speaking, this would prove that any catalytic channel is always resource non-generating (RNG).
However, if all catalytic channels
\tilde{\Lambda}
are RNG, then their extensions
\id_{A}\otimes\tilde{\Lambda}
are also catalytic channels, and therefore RNG, which in turns implies that all
\tilde{\Lambda}
are in fact
completely
RNG as well.
.
This property has also been studied in terms of
resource broadcasting

Lu et al. 2013
;
Marvian and Spekkens 2019
;
Lostaglio and Müller 2019
;
Yang et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2024
, where a free operation
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{O}_{A\to AB}
may “propagate” some resource from
A
to another system
B
, i.e.
\Tr_{A}[\mathcal{B}(\rho_{A})]\notin\mathcal{S}_{B}
, while leaving
A
fully intact, i.e., such that
\Tr_{B}[\mathcal{B}(\rho_{A})]=\rho_{A}
.
The outcome of robust catalysis applied to a free system state
\gamma_{S}\in\mathcal{S}_{S}
, is therefore closely related to resource broadcasting, as one wants
\tilde{\Lambda}(\gamma_{S})=\Tr_{C}[\Lambda(\gamma_{S}\otimes\tau_{C})]\notin\mathcal{S}_{S^{\prime}}
.

Theorem 2
.

For any CRNG resource theory that satisfies Assumptions (A1), (A2), and (A3), the existence of a catalytic channel
\tilde{\Lambda}\notin\mathcal{O}_{S\to S^{\prime}}
is equivalent to the existence of resource broadcasting.

Thm.
2
is proven in the End Matters; here we only comment about a subtle difference between robust catalysis and resource broadcasting.
In real experimental setups, implementing the broadcasting channel
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{O}_{C\to S^{\prime}C}
requires an auxiliary state
\gamma_{S^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}_{S^{\prime}}
, which is assumed to be fixed.
Unlike robust catalysis, the broadcasting process may fail to preserve the original state
\tau_{C}
when the auxiliary state
\gamma_{S^{\prime}}
is somehow perturbed.

We now address the central question:
when does robust catalysis offer a net advantage?

It turns out that the answer hinges on an additional degree of freedom that has not been explicitly considered before: how
\mathcal{S}_{AB}
is composed given
\mathcal{S}_{A}
and
\mathcal{S}_{B}
.
In typical resource theories, this composition is usually defined operationally, depending on the particular resource at hand.
However, in abstract resource theories, as those that we consider here, we can freely choose any composition rule, as long as it does not cause inconsistencies with the rest of the theory.
In particular, we can prove that any composition
\mathcal{S}_{AB}
satisfying Assumptions (A1)-(A4) must lie between
minimal
and
maximal
compositions, denoted
\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{min}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}
and
\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{max}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}
, which,
for given
\mathcal{S}_{A}
and
\mathcal{S}_{B}
, are explicitly written as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{min}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathrm{conv}\left\{\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}\,|\,\rho_{A}\in\mathcal{S}_{A},\ \rho_{B}\in\mathcal{S}_{B}\right\},

(2)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{max}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{\rho_{AB}\,|\,\Tr_{B}[\rho_{AB}]\in\mathcal{S}_{A},\Tr_{A}[\rho_{AB}]\in\mathcal{S}_{B}\right\}.

(3)

Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
) evoke the tensor product of convex cones (explained in End Matters) and serve as rules that encapsulate the limits of allowed correlations in free states.
For instance, the resource theories of athermality
Ng and Woods 2018
;
Lostaglio 2019
, where the tensor product of subsystem Gibbs states is a free state, and coherence
Streltsov et al. 2017
, where diagonal states are free, follow the minimal composition rule.
On the other hand, the maximal composition of athermality includes the thermofield double state
Israel 1976
—a pure entangled state with Gibbs state marginals.
These extremal compositions streamline the analysis by making the set of CRNG operations identical to RNG operations, eliminating the need to consider resource-generating effects on larger Hilbert spaces as in Eq. (
1
) (see Lemma
5
, Supplemental Materials for proof).
However, there is no natural inclusion between
\mathrm{CRNG}(\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{min}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B})
and
\mathrm{CRNG}(\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{max}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B})
, which prevents us from defining a minimal/maximal resource theory.
Another notable set is

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{sep}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}\coloneq(\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{max}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B})\cap\mathrm{SEP},

(4)

where
\mathrm{SEP}
represents all separable states across the
A|B
partition, explicitly excluding entanglement between subsystems as a free resource.
While
\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{max}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}
may appear contrived, it can be understood as a theory concerned with
local
, rather than global, resources.
We note that Ref.
Pinske and Mølmer 2024
defines
\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{min}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}
as their composition rule in the context of resource censorship.

When useful robust catalysis and resource broadcasting are impossible
—With this newfound categorization of compositions in quantum resource theories, we establish a no-go theorem for robust catalysis when the composition restricts correlations between the partitions of free states, i.e. when the free state set is minimal.

Theorem 3
.

If a convex CRNG resource theory has minimal composition, it allows neither resource broadcasting nor robust catalysis.

The proof boils down to constructing a strongly super-additive resource monotone, implying that the resource of a composite state exceeds the sum of its marginal resources, which rules out resource broadcasting; see Supplemental Materials Sec.
III
for full proof.
While no-broadcasting has been established specifically for theories of asymmetry under connected Lie groups
Marvian and Spekkens 2019
;
Lostaglio and Müller 2019
and, more recently, stabilizer operations
Zhang et al. 2024
, our result is the first known sufficient condition to guarantee no-broadcasting across generic classes of resource theories.
Furthermore, we demonstrate many significant theories that lie beyond the minimal composition class also lack robust catalytic advantage and do not allow resource broadcasting.
See End Matters for a list of these theories, and Supplemental Materials for proofs.

So far, all our results hold for robust catalysis with catalysts that may retain correlations with the system post-operation.
We can impose a stricter condition that the catalyst must be recovered without correlation (strict robust catalysis in Def.
1
).
By definition, strict robust catalysis cannot yield any advantage whenever general robust catalysis fail to do so.
We demonstrate that in this stricter case, it is impossible to generate non-free operations in any resource theory if the catalyst state is full-rank, even when general robust catalysis would allow it; see Supplemental Materials for the proof.

When useful robust catalysis and resource broadcasting are possible
—Surprisingly, larger composition rules enable useful robust catalysis and resource broadcasting.
We show that by adopting either
\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{max}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}
or
\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{sep}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}
, certain theories permit robust catalysis, with the state transformation condition directly tied to a resource monotone
D_{\max}(\rho_{S}\|\mathcal{S}_{S})\coloneq\inf\{\log r\,|\,\rho_{S}\leq r\omega_{S},\ \omega_{S}\in\mathcal{S}_{S}\}
known as the max-relative entropy of the resource
Datta 2009
.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose the free state set of system
C
is a singleton
\mathcal{S}_{C}=\{\gamma_{C}\}
and the composite free state set
\mathcal{S}_{SC}
is either
\mathcal{S}_{S}\!\otimes_{\text{max}}\!\mathcal{S}_{C}
or
\mathcal{S}_{S}\!\otimes_{\text{sep}}\!\mathcal{S}_{C}
.
Then there exists a state
\tau_{C}\in\mathcal{D}_{C}
and a broadcasting channel
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{O}_{C\to SC}
, such that the state
\sigma_{S}=\Tr_{C}[\mathcal{B}(\tau_{C})]
can be prepared in
S
, if and only if

\displaystyle\sup_{\tau_{C}\in\mathcal{D}_{C}}D_{\max}(\tau_{C}\|\mathcal{S}_{C})\geq D_{\max}(\sigma_{S}\|\mathcal{S}_{S}).

(5)

The full proof is in End Matters; here, we offer a few remarks. Firstly, the theorem holds for any
\mathcal{S}_{S}
, even if it is not a singleton. Furthermore, using this channel
\mathcal{B}
, we can construct
\Lambda\coloneq\mathcal{B}\circ\Tr_{S}\in\mathcal{O}_{SC\to SC}
, which induces robust catalysis with the catalyst
\tau_{C}
. The theorem then implies that robust catalysis can transform any
\rho_{S}\in\mathcal{D}_{S}
to
\sigma_{S}
if
\sigma_{S}
meets Eq. (
5
). Additionally, when
\mathcal{S}_{S}=\{\gamma^{\prime}_{S}\}
with
\gamma^{\prime}_{S}
that has the smallest eigenvalue greater than that of
\gamma_{C}
, transformations between any states become feasible, effectively trivializing the theory.
We note that robust catalysis from
\rho_{S}
to
\sigma_{S}
may still occur even when
\sigma_{S}
violates Eq. (
5
), as there are catalytic channels that cannot be written as
\mathcal{B}\circ\Tr_{S}
.
The possibility of resource broadcasting extends beyond this case to analogous theories such as local coherence and local entanglement.
Furthermore, robust catalysis has shown to be feasible in the resource theory of imaginarity
Takagi et al. 2017
;
Zhang and Li 2024
, see Supplemental Materials for discussion.

Discussion
---Our results reveal a deep connection between noise-robust catalysis and the possibility of resource broadcasting.
Furthermore, it highlights the importance of composition rules for free state sets, an area rarely studied explicitly.
The composition structure determines the extensiveness of the resources, which is in turn directly related to broadcasting.
In particular, an extensive resource cannot be freely broadcast to multiple subsystems, while the existence of robust catalysis and resource broadcasting signals non-extensiveness
2
2

2

See Supplemental Materials Sec.
IV.3
for the resource theory of imaginarity
Wu et al. 2021
, a perfect example of non-extensive resource theory, and Ref.
Kuroiwa and Yamasaki 2020
for a special case of resource broadcasting and its implications to asymptotic state transitions.
.
Our work identifies new classes of resource non-extensive theories by finding compositions that permit resource broadcasting and establish the necessary and sufficient condition for obtaining an outcome state, as in Thm.
4
.

We also showcase the power of fine-tuning in catalysis by examining the alternative scenario without fine-tuning.
It turns out that most quantum resources cannot be broadcast, although many benefit from fine-tuned catalysis, highlighting that fine-tuning offers flexibility at the inevitable cost of catalyst fragility.
In particular, our no-go results indicate that achieving asymptotic conversion rates in the single-copy regime robustly is impossible.

Our results leave open the possibility of robust catalytic advantage for non-CRNG free operations.
For instance, it is unclear whether robust catalysis could implement separable operations via LOCC or stabilizer-preserving operations via stabilizer operations.
However, any potential advantage within such operationally defined theories cannot exceed the limits imposed by their corresponding CRNGs.
Moreover, if CRNG operations are no-broadcasting, all its subsets also cannot broadcast, which proves the no-broadcasting for LOCC, stabilizer operations, thermal operations, etc.
Our findings thus establish an upper bound for resource broadcasting in any well-defined resource theory.
Conversely, observing resource broadcasting within non-CRNG free operations would suggest a robust catalytic advantage.
Beyond performing a non-free operation, resource theories might benefit from dimensional advantages
Boes et al. 2018
from catalytic channels, as quantum catalysts could outperform classical randomness.

Robust catalysis also paves an alternative pathway for investigating channel catalysis, where a free channel induces a non-free one through the assistance of a channel that functions as a catalyst.
Although resource theories of channels have been actively studied
Devetak et al. 2008
;
Chiribella et al. 2008
;
Rosset et al. 2018
;
Liu and Winter 2019
;
Gour and Winter 2019
;
Takagi et al. 2020
;
Gour and Scandolo 2020
, their catalytic versions are largely unexplored
Lipka-Bartosik et al. 2024
;
Datta et al. 2023
.
Our work makes initial strides towards addressing this gap, as robust catalysis can be regarded as a particular form of channel catalysis.
Specifically, if the
catalyst channel
is a preparation channel with a fixed outcome, channel catalysis results in a
catalytic channel
, as in Thm.
1
.
Generic channel catalysis, on the other hand, is not immediately precluded by the absence of robust catalysis, as demonstrated in Ref.
Vidal and Cirac 2002
, where a non-free channel can be transformed to another non-free channel only when a catalyst is present.
This does not fit our definition of robust catalysis as the system-catalyst channel is not free; however, it is robust under errors in initial state preparation.
Given the input-agnostic nature of channel catalysis, it is plausible to assume a closer tie between channel catalysis and robust catalysis.
We leave this question for future investigations.

Lastly, we identify a hierarchy of composition rules for free states within resource theories, revealing a spectrum from the impossibility to the possibility of robust catalysis.
This hierarchy effectively delineates the types of correlations permitted under free operations.
We anticipate that this framework will foster new strategies for extending or even hybridizing resource theories, offering novel approaches to exploring complex resource interactions.

1
—If a channel
\tilde{\Lambda}(\bullet_{S})\coloneq\Tr_{C}[\Lambda(\bullet_{S}\otimes\tau_{C})]
is a catalytic channel, it implements, in particular, a robust catalysis, for any state
\rho_{S}
and any robustness parameter
\epsilon>0
. We thus need to prove only the converse, and we do it by contradiction.
Suppose that
\Lambda
implements a
(\rho,\epsilon)
-robust catalysis, but is not a catalytic channel. This means that there exists at least one state, say
\eta_{S}
, such that

\displaystyle\tau_{C}=\Tr_{S^{\prime}}[\Lambda(\rho_{S}\otimes\tau_{C})]\neq\Tr_{S^{\prime}}[\Lambda(\eta_{S}\otimes\tau_{C})]=:\tau_{C}^{\prime}.

(A1)

Let us then consider the state

\Lambda
implements an

-robust catalysis for

.

Appendix D: Minimal/maximal composition and tensor product of convex cones
—We begin by reviewing the minimal/maximal tensor products of convex cones
Aubrun et al. 2021
;
van Dobben de Bruyn 2022
;
Aubrun et al. 2022
, often discussed in the context of general probabilistic theories
Janotta and Hinrichsen 2014
;
Plávala 2023
.
First, we define convex cones and their duals following Refs.
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
;
Aliprantis and Tourky 2007
.
Let
\mathcal{V}
be a vector space.
A non-empty subset
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{V}
is a convex cone if it is convex and closed under positive scalar multiplication.
We also assume that
\mathcal{C}
is closed and
\mathcal{C}\cap(-\mathcal{C})=\{0\}
.
Let
\mathcal{V}^{*}
be the set of linear functionals on
\mathcal{V}
with the duality
\langle,\rangle:\mathcal{V}^{*}\times\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
.
The dual cone of
\mathcal{C}
is then given by
\mathcal{C}^{*}=\Bqty{x^* \in\mV^*\,|\, \langle x^*, z \rangle\geq 0 \textrm{ for all } z \in\mathcal{C}}
.
The minimal and maximal tensor products of two cones
\mathcal{C}_{A}
and
\mathcal{C}_{B}
are defined as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\mathcal{C}_{A}\!\otimes_{\textrm{min}}\!\mathcal{C}_{B}

\displaystyle\coloneq\textrm{conv}\Bqty{
z_A \otimes z_B
\,|\,
z_A \in\mC_{A},
z_B \in\mC_{B}
},

(A2)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\mathcal{C}_{A}\!\otimes_{\textrm{max}}\!\mathcal{C}_{B}

\displaystyle\coloneq\Bqty{
z
\,|\,
\langle x^*\otimes y^*, z \rangle\geq 0,\,
x^* \in\mC^{*}_{A},\,
y^* \in\mC^{*}_{B}
}.

(A3)

Consider the case where cones are defined on the space of linear Hermitian operators acting on Hilbert spaces, with the duality
\langle,\rangle
given by the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product.
We now observe that for each free state set
\mathcal{S}_{A}
, the associated cone can be defined as
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S}_{A})=\bigcup_{\lambda\geq 0}\lambda\mathcal{S}_{A}
.
Then, it follows that
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S}_{A})\!\otimes_{\textrm{min}}\!\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S}_{B})=\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{min}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B})
, i.e. the minimal tensor product is equivalent to the minimal composition in Eq. (
2
).

For the maximal counterparts, we have
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S}_{A})\!\otimes_{\textrm{max}}\!\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S}_{B})\subset\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{max}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B})
.
To see this, note that choosing
x^{*}=\Tr_{A}\in\mathcal{C}^{*}(\mathcal{S}_{A})
in Eq. (
A3
), ensures the
B
marginal of
\gamma_{AB}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S}_{A})\!\otimes_{\textrm{max}}\!\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S}_{B})
is always in
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S}_{B})
.
Choosing

implies the same for the
A
marginal.
The converse of this inclusion does not hold in general, as one can check by constructing counter-examples.

Supplemental Materials for “Robust Catalysis and Resource Broadcasting: the Possible and the Impossible”

I
Sources of catalytic errors

Any catalytic protocol has three ingredients: the system state
\rho
, the channel
\Lambda
, and the catalyst
\tau
(see Fig.
1
).
We briefly discuss possible sources of errors in a catalytic protocol, which may cause a degradation in the catalyst.
In particular, errors on the catalyst that would accumulate and increase over time would be those of the most challenging nature, as these directly impact the reusability of the catalyst.

1.

Errors on the initial catalyst
\tau
:
A simple computation shows that the catalyst does not degrade upon further iterations.
Intuitively, this can be understood as follows: besides the initial error in catalyst preparation, there are no additional sources of errors during multiple rounds of catalysis, which use the same catalyst state.
In the meantime, the catalytic protocol stabilizes this catalyst state over time.
To be more precise, if the catalyst is initially prepared in a state
\tau_{\epsilon}
, which is
\epsilon
-away from the ideal catalyst state
\tau
, then the final state of the catalyst
\tau_{\epsilon}^{\prime}
remains at most
\epsilon
-away from
\tau
via the data processing inequality,

\displaystyle\norm{\tau_{\epsilon}' - \tau}_{1}=\norm{\Tr_S \bqty{\Lambda(\rho\otimes\tau_{\epsilon})} - \Tr_S \bqty{\Lambda(\rho\otimes\tau)}}_{1}\leq\norm{\tau_{\epsilon} - \tau}_{1}\leq\epsilon.

(A7)

2.

Errors on the initial state
\rho
or the channel
\Lambda
:
These errors are introduced afresh in each iteration, raising the possibility of error accumulation on the catalyst, despite the ideal protocol having a net-zero effect on the catalyst. We include a small explicit example below for illustration.
Conceptually, if one possesses a complete characterization of the errors, e.g. knowing the exact perturbed input state
\rho_{\epsilon}
, it may still be possible to fine-tune the channel
\Lambda_{\epsilon}
accordingly such that
\rho_{\epsilon}
and
\Lambda_{\epsilon}
stabilize the catalyst.
However, assuming full knowledge of such errors and the ability to adjust the channel accordingly is highly impracticable.

Example 1

(Accumulating errors on the catalyst)
.

It suffices to demonstrate that catalyst continues to degrade after the first round of catalysis.
Consider a qutrit system and a qubit catalyst undergoing a joint unitary evolution.
Denote the eigenvalues of the system and catalyst to be:

p_{S}=(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}),\qquad q_{C}=(q_{1},q_{2}).

(A8)

The unitary swaps the eigenstates corresponding to
p_{1}q_{1}\leftrightarrow p_{2}q_{2}
and
p_{2}q_{1}\leftrightarrow p_{3}q_{2}
.
This operation is catalytic whenever
(p_{1}+p_{2})q_{1}=(p_{2}+p_{3})q_{2}
.
Such toy examples are useful for illustrations, and have been used, e.g. in Appendix B of
Boes et al. 2020
.

Now, suppose that in the first round, we have a noisy system state
p_{\varepsilon}=(p_{1}-\varepsilon,p_{2},p_{3}+\varepsilon)
for some
\varepsilon>0
.
This leads to a final degraded catalyst
q^{\prime}=(q_{1}^{\prime},q_{2}^{\prime})
, such that

q_{1}^{\prime}=p_{2}q_{2}+(p_{3}+\varepsilon)q_{2}+(p_{3}+\varepsilon)q_{1}=q_{1}+\varepsilon.

(A9)

From normalization, we also have that
q_{2}^{\prime}=q_{2}-\varepsilon
. In other words, the full amount of error
\varepsilon
has propagated into the catalyst.
Next, suppose that in a second round, we have another noisy system state
p_{-\varepsilon}=(p_{1}+\varepsilon,p_{2},p_{3}-\varepsilon)
.
Under the action of the same catalytic unitary, the catalyst further degrades into
\tilde{q}_{C}=(\tilde{q}_{1},\tilde{q}_{2})
, where

\displaystyle\tilde{q}_{1}

\displaystyle=p_{2}q_{2}^{\prime}+(p_{3}-\varepsilon)q_{2}^{\prime}+(p_{3}-\varepsilon)q_{1}^{\prime}=q_{1}-\varepsilon(p_{2}+p_{3}+q_{2}+q_{1}-p_{3})

\displaystyle=q_{1}-\varepsilon(1+p_{2}).

(A10)

In summary, the error accumulated almost linearly during two rounds of catalysis, as we anticipated.

II
For minimal, maximal, and separable compositions, resource non-generating operations (RNG) is completely resource non-generating operations(CRNG)

Lemma 5
.

If
\mathcal{S}_{AB}
is either
\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{min}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}
,
\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{sep}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}
, or
\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{max}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}
, then RNG = CRNG.

Proof.

We first consider the case
\mathcal{S}_{AB}=\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{min}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}
.
For any system
R
, a free state
\gamma_{AR}\in\mathcal{S}_{AR}
can be written as
\gamma_{AR}=\sum_{i}p_{i}(\gamma^{(i)}_{A}\otimes\gamma^{(i)}_{R})
, where
\gamma^{(i)}_{R}
are free states for
R
.
Let
\Lambda\in\mathrm{CPTP}_{A\to A^{\prime}}
be an RNG channel.
Then the extension

\displaystyle\Lambda\otimes\id_{R}(\gamma_{AR})=\sum_{i}p_{i}(\tilde{\gamma}^{(i)}_{A^{\prime}}\otimes\gamma^{(i)}_{R}),

(A11)

where each
\tilde{\gamma}^{(i)}_{A^{\prime}}=\Lambda(\gamma^{(i)}_{A})\in\mathcal{S}_{A^{\prime}}
.
This implies that
\Lambda\otimes\id_{R}
is an RNG channel and thus
\Lambda
is a CRNG channel.

Now we prove the case
\mathcal{S}_{AB}=\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{max}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}
.
Again, let
\gamma_{AR}\in\mathcal{S}_{AR}
be any free state.
The extension
\tilde{\gamma}_{AR}=\Lambda\otimes\id_{R}(\gamma_{AR})
is also free if and only if its reduced states are free.
Since
\Lambda
is an RNG channel, the
A
reduced state
\Tr_{R}[\tilde{\gamma}]=\Lambda(\Tr_{R}[\gamma_{AR}])
is free.
The
R
reduced state
\Tr_{A}[\tilde{\gamma}]=\Tr_{A}[\gamma_{AR}]
is free because
\gamma_{AR}\in\mathcal{S}_{AR}
.
Therefore,
\Lambda
is a CRNG channel.

The proof is very similar for
\mathcal{S}_{AB}=\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{sep}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}
.
The free state
\gamma_{AR}=\sum_{i}p_{i}(\xi_{A}^{(i)}\otimes\zeta_{R}^{(i)})
for
\xi_{A}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{D}_{A}
,
\zeta_{R}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{D}_{R}
,
p_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
and

\sum_{i}p_{i}\xi_{A}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
,
\sum_{i}p_{i}\zeta_{R}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{S}_{R}
.
The final state after the extended channel becomes

\displaystyle\Lambda\otimes\id_{R}(\gamma_{AR})=\sum_{i}p_{i}(\tilde{\xi}^{(i)}_{A^{\prime}}\otimes\zeta^{(i)}_{R}),

(A12)

where
\tilde{\xi}^{(i)}_{A^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{D}_{A^{\prime}}
for all
i
.
Furthermore, since
\Lambda
is an RNG channel, we have that
\sum_{i}p_{i}\tilde{\xi}_{A^{\prime}}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{S}_{A^{\prime}}
.
Hence,
\Lambda\otimes\id_{R}(\gamma_{AR})
is separable and its reduced states are free, making it a free state in
\mathcal{S}_{A^{\prime}}\!\otimes_{\text{sep}}\!\mathcal{S}_{R}
.
∎

III
Piani’s theorem and the proof of Thm.
3

The proof of Thm.
3
relies mainly on constructing a faithful and strongly super-additive monotone for theories with minimal composition.
To proceed, we introduce two specific monotones that are critical to the analysis.
First, for any convex CRNG resource theory, the relative entropy of resource, is defined as

\displaystyle R(\rho_{X})\coloneq\inf_{\sigma_{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{X}}S(\rho_{X}\|\sigma_{X}),\qquad S(\rho\|\sigma)\coloneq\Tr(\rho\log\rho-\rho\log\sigma),

(A13)

is a monotone, i.e.
R(\Lambda(\rho_{X}))\leq R(\rho_{X})
for any
\rho_{X}
and
\Lambda\in\mathcal{O}_{X\to Y}
.
It is a faithful measure yielding
R(\rho_{X})\geq 0
, with equality if and only if
\rho_{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{X}
.

Similarly, the relative entropy of resource under restricted measurements can be defined.
Consider a quantum measurement
M
, described by positive operator-valued measures (POVMs)
\{E_{i}\}_{i}
that are positive and sum to identity.
Let
M(\rho)
denote the probability vector whose components are the probabilities
\Tr[\rho E_{i}]
of obtaining an outcome
i
.
The Kullback–Leibler divergence
D_{\rm KL}(M(\rho)\|M(\sigma))
between two outcome probabilities vanishes if and only if
M(\rho)=M(\sigma)
.
To consider multiple measurements, let
\mathbb{M}
be a set of quantum measurements of interest.
The relative entropy of resource under
\mathbb{M}
is then defined as

\displaystyle R_{\mathbb{M}}(\rho_{X})=\inf_{\sigma_{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{X}}\sup_{M\in\mathbb{M}}D_{\rm KL}(M(\rho_{X})\|M(\sigma_{X})).

(A14)

The following theorem establishes a relationship between the resource monotones of a composite state
\rho_{XY}
and its marginals.

Theorem 6

(Ref.
Piani 2009
, Thm. 1)
.

Suppose that the free state set
\mathcal{S}_{X}
is convex for any system
X
and that
\rho_{XY}\in\mathcal{S}_{XY}
implies
\Tr_{Y}[\rho_{XY}]\in\mathcal{S}_{X}
for any subsystems
X
and
Y
.
Let
\mathbb{M}
be a set of measurements on
X
, and assume that for all
M\in\mathbb{M}
with POVMs
\{E_{i}\}_{i}
and for all
\gamma_{XY}\in\mathcal{S}_{XY}
, the post-measurement
Y
marginal state
\frac{\Tr_{X}[E_{i}\gamma_{XY}]}{\Tr[E_{i}\gamma_{XY}]}\in\mathcal{S}_{Y}
.
Then, for any
\rho_{XY}\in\mathcal{D}_{XY}
,

\displaystyle R(\rho_{XY})\geq R_{\mathbb{M}}(\Tr_{Y}[\rho_{XY}])+R(\Tr_{X}[\rho_{XY}]).

(A15)

This theorem serves as a tool to prove Thm.
3
.

First, note that
R_{\mathbb{M}}
possesses convenient properties:
i)
data processing implies that
R_{\mathbb{M}}(\rho_{X})\leq R(\rho_{X})
for any
\rho_{X}
and any
\mathbb{M}
. Furthermore, it is known that
ii)
if
\mathbb{M}
includes informationally complete POVMs,
R_{\mathbb{M}}
is faithful
Piani 2009
, i.e.
R_{\mathbb{M}}(\rho_{X})\geq 0
with the equality if and only if
\rho_{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{X}
, and
iii)
if
\mathbb{M}
encompasses all possible POVMs, the monotonicity
R_{\mathbb{M}}(\Lambda(\rho_{X}))\leq R_{\mathbb{M}}(\rho_{X})
for any
\rho_{X}
and
\Lambda\in\mathcal{O}_{X\to Y}
holds from data processing inequality.

Now consider a catalyst
\tau_{C}
and a dilation
\Lambda\in\mathcal{O}_{SC\to S^{\prime}C}
inducing a catalytic channel
\tilde{\Lambda}
.
For any free system state
\gamma_{S}\in\mathcal{S}_{S}
, denote
\chi_{S^{\prime}C}=\Lambda(\gamma_{S}\otimes\sigma_{C})
, where
\Tr_{C}[\chi_{S^{\prime}C}]=\tilde{\Lambda}(\gamma_{S})
and
\Tr_{S^{\prime}}[\chi_{S^{\prime}C}]=\tau_{C}
.
By monotonicity of
R
,

\displaystyle R(\tau_{C})=R(\gamma_{S}\otimes\tau_{C})\geq R(\chi_{S^{\prime}C}),

(A16)

where the first equality follows from both appending and discarding a free state
\gamma_{S}
being a free operation.

To apply Thm.
6
, set
\mathcal{S}_{S^{\prime}C}=\mathcal{S}_{S^{\prime}}\!\otimes_{\text{min}}\!\mathcal{S}_{C}
and
\mathbb{M}
to be the set of
all measurements
.

•

The first requirement, that
\mathcal{S}_{X}
is convex for any system
X
and that
\rho_{XY}\in\mathcal{S}_{XY}
implies
\Tr_{Y}[\rho_{XY}]\in\mathcal{S}_{X}
for any subsystem
X
and
Y
are already imposed as axioms for our framework.

•

The second requirement can be shown using the structure of
\mathcal{S}_{S^{\prime}}\!\otimes_{\text{min}}\!\mathcal{S}_{C}
: note that any
\gamma_{S^{\prime}C}\in\mathcal{S}_{S^{\prime}}\!\otimes_{\text{min}}\!\mathcal{S}_{C}
can be written as

\displaystyle\gamma_{S^{\prime}C}=\sum_{i}p_{i}(\gamma_{S^{\prime}}^{(i)}\otimes\tilde{\gamma}_{C}^{(i)}).

(A17)

Then for any POVM element
E_{S^{\prime}}
that acts on system
S^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\Tr_{S^{\prime}}\left[E_{S^{\prime}}\gamma_{S^{\prime}C}\right]}{\Tr\left[E_{S^{\prime}}\gamma_{S^{\prime}C}\right]}=\frac{\sum_{i}p_{i}\Tr[E_{S^{\prime}}\gamma_{S^{\prime}}^{(i)}]\tilde{\gamma}_{C}^{(i)}}{\sum_{i}p_{i}\Tr[E_{S^{\prime}}\gamma_{S^{\prime}}^{(i)}]}\eqcolon\sum_{i}\tilde{p}_{i}\tilde{\gamma}_{C}^{(i)},

(A18)

where
\{\tilde{p}_{i}\}_{i}
are valid convex coefficients.
By convexity, the resulting state
\frac{\Tr_{S^{\prime}}\left[E_{S^{\prime}}\gamma_{S^{\prime}C}\right]}{\Tr\left[E_{S^{\prime}}\gamma_{S^{\prime}C}\right]}
remains in
\mathcal{S}_{C}
.

Using Thm.
6
,

\displaystyle R(\chi_{S^{\prime}C})\geq R_{\mathbb{M}}(\tilde{\Lambda}(\rho_{S}))+R(\tau_{C}).

(A19)

Combined with Eq. (
A16
), it follows that
R(\tau_{C})\geq R_{\mathbb{M}}(\tilde{\Lambda}(\rho_{S}))+R(\tau_{C})
, or equivalently,

\displaystyle 0\geq R_{\mathbb{M}}(\tilde{\Lambda}(\gamma_{S})),

(A20)

whenever
R(\tau_{C})<\infty
.
The latter is guaranteed by the existence of a full rank free state in
\mathcal{S}_{C}
(criterion 4 of our basic assumptions stated in the main text).
By the faithfulness of
R_{\mathbb{M}}
, we conclude that
\tilde{\Lambda}(\gamma_{S})\in\mathcal{S}_{S^{\prime}}
for any
\gamma_{S}\in\mathcal{S}_{S}
.
In other words, catalytic channels for resource theories with minimal composition are always free operations.

Note that in Ref.
Takagi and Shiraishi 2022
it has been shown that the super-additive monotone, if exists, also restricts marginal or correlated catalysis that are not robust.
Our result then implies that theories with the minimal composition cannot be trivialized via (non-robust) marginal or correlated catalysis.

IV
Miscellaneous resource theories

IV.1
Affine compositions

Sometimes, free state sets have a stronger condition than being convex.
There is a subclass of resource theories whose sets of free states are affine, such as the resource theory of athermality, asymmetry, coherence, and imaginarity
Gour 2017
, i.e. the set of free state
\mathcal{S}_{S}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{aff}\mathcal{S}_{S}\cap\mathcal{D}_{S}\coloneq\left\{\sum_{i}p_{i}\rho_{S}^{(i)}\,\bigg|\,\forall i,\ \rho_{S}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{S}_{S},\ p_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\ \sum_{i}p_{i}=1\right\}\cap\mathcal{D}_{S}=\mathcal{S}_{S}.

(A21)

However, if we impose the minimal composition, even when the free state sets are affine for each subsystem, the composite set might not be affine.
To accommodate such theories, we consider affine composition of free states: the composite free state set is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{aff}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}=\mathrm{aff}\left\{\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}\,|\,\rho_{A}\in\mathcal{S}_{A},\ \rho_{B}\in\mathcal{S}_{B}\right\}\cap\mathcal{D}_{AB},

(A22)

for the system
AB
, given free state sets for
A
and
B
.
However, it is important to remark that
\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{aff}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}
might not satisfy the four basic assumptions in the main text, when
\mathcal{S}_{A}
or
\mathcal{S}_{B}
is not affine.
Suppose that
\gamma_{AB}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{aff}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}
, i.e.
\gamma_{AB}=\sum_{i}p_{i}\gamma_{A}^{(i)}\otimes\gamma_{B}^{(i)}
, where
\{p_{i}\}_{i}
is a set of affine coefficients and
\gamma_{X}^{(i)}
are some free states for system
X=A,B
.
In general,
\Tr_{B}[\gamma_{AB}]=\sum_{i}p_{i}\gamma_{A}^{(i)}\notin\mathcal{S}_{A}
if
\mathcal{S}_{A}
is not affine, breaking the second assumption in the main text. Nevertheless, when the resource theory is affine, the set
\mathcal{S}_{A}\!\otimes_{\text{aff}}\!\mathcal{S}_{B}
is a valid free state set satisfying all four assumptions, and we establish the result analogous to Thm.
3
in the main text.

Theorem 7
.

If an affine CRNG resource theory has the affine composition, then it does not allow non-free catalytic channels and resource broadcasting.

Proof.

The proof is identical to that of Thm.
3
, except for replacing convex coefficients
\{p_{i}\}_{i}
and
\{\tilde{p}_{i}\}_{i}
by affine coefficients.
This replacement does not change the conclusion, since the affine combination of free states are assumed to be free.
∎

IV.2
Intersection of multiple completely resource non-generating operations

In some resource theories of interest, the free operation set is not the set of CRNG operations, but given as the intersection of multiple CRNG operation sets for different resources.
In such cases, if each CRNG operation does not allow broadcasting of a resource, the intersection of them also has no-broadcasting property.
A prominent example is the Gibbs-preserving covariant operations
Ćwikliński et al. 2015
;
Gour et al. 2018
, which is an intersection of Gibbs-preserving operations (athermality) and covariant operations (asymmetry).
Therefore, Gibbs-preserving covariant operations cannot benefit from robust catalysis.

IV.3
Resource theory of imaginarity

Resource theory of imaginarity is defined by the set of free state

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{X}=\left\{\rho_{X}\,|\,\rho_{X}\in\mathcal{D}_{X},\ \bra{i}_{X}\rho_{X}\ket{j}_{X}\in\mathbb{R},\ \forall i,j\right\},

(A23)

where
\{\ket{i}_{X}\}_{i}
is a fixed basis for system
X
prescribed by some restrictions.
When
S
and
C
are qubit systems, CRNG operation
\mathcal{O}_{SC\to SC}
includes CNOT gate, which maps
\ket{0i}_{SC}\mapsto\ket{ii}_{SC}
for
i=0,1
.

Suppose that the maximally imaginary state
\ket{\hat{+}}_{C}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}_{C}+i\ket{1}_{C})
is given as a catalyst.
Then the strict robust catalysis

\displaystyle\Lambda(\outerproduct{0}{0}_{S}\otimes\outerproduct{\hat{+}}{\hat{+}}_{C})=\outerproduct{\hat{+}}{\hat{+}}_{S}\otimes\outerproduct{\hat{+}}{\hat{+}}_{C}

(A24)

is implementable using a CNOT gate
\Lambda

Takagi et al. 2017
. The broadcasting version of this channel,
\mathcal{B}(\outerproduct{\hat{+}}{\hat{+}})=\outerproduct{\hat{+}}{\hat{+}}^{\otimes 2}
is a special case of resource broadcasting, which is dubbed catalytic replication in Ref.
Kuroiwa and Yamasaki 2020
.
To develop more intuition, we invoke Proposition 1 of Ref.
Wu et al. 2021
stating that any pure state
\ket{\psi}
can be transformed to an effectively qubit pure state
\ket{\phi}=\alpha\ket{0}+i\beta\ket{1}
with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, via some real unitary operation.
Since the operation is unitary, the inverse of such operation is also a real operation, i.e. any pure state
\ket{\psi}
is equivalent to some qubit pure state
\ket{\phi}
in terms of the imaginarity.
It also means that any pure state cannot have a resource exceeding that of the maximally imaginary pure qubit state
\ket{\hat{+}}
, even if the former state consists of multiple copies of the latter state.

On the other hand, this non-extensiveness does not indicate that the catalytic replication
\rho\to\rho^{\otimes 2}
is always possible via real operations.
In Ref.
Zhang and Li 2024
, it is shown that the maximally imaginary state is the only state that admits catalytic replication among qubit states or pure states.

IV.4
Limited subspace theories

It is easy to construct a theory that admits robust catalysis if we break the fourth assumption in the main text, that is, if all free states are non-full rank.
Suppose that there exists a catalyst state
\tau_{C}
that is not in the support of any free state in
\mathcal{S}_{C}
.
Then a catalytic channel can be constructed by the following steps.
First, the catalyst part is measured to distinguish whether it is in the support of some free state.
If it is in the support, prepare a system-catalyst free state.
If it is not, which is the case for
\tau_{C}
we assumed, prepare
\sigma_{S}\otimes\tau_{C}
, where
\sigma_{S}
is any system state and
\tau_{C}
is the catalyst reduced state of the initial state.

The simplest example is the resource theory of athermality at temperature
T=0
, where the only free state is the ground state
\gamma_{X}=\outerproduct{0}{0}_{X}
for any system
X
.
If a catalyst
\tau_{C}=\outerproduct{1}{1}_{C}
, the channel

\displaystyle\Lambda(\rho_{SC})=\outerproduct{00}{00}_{SC}\Tr[\rho_{SC}(\mathmybb{1}_{S}\otimes\gamma_{C})]+\sigma_{S}\otimes\Tr_{S}[\rho_{SC}]\Tr[\rho_{SC}(\mathmybb{1}_{S}\otimes[\mathmybb{1}_{C}-\gamma_{C}])]

(A25)

transforms
\Lambda(\outerproduct{00}{00}_{SC})=\outerproduct{00}{00}_{SC}
and
\Lambda(\rho_{S}\otimes\tau_{C})=\sigma_{S}\otimes\tau_{C}
, regardless of the choices for
\rho_{S},\sigma_{S}\in\mathcal{D}_{S}
.
Hence, any state transformation becomes possible with a (strict) robust catalysis.
A very similar construction was used in Ref.
Kuroiwa and Yamasaki 2020
.

IV.5
Local coherences

We generalize Thm.
4
to the resource theory of local coherences.
In this case, the local free state sets are given as incoherent states
\mathcal{S}_{S(C)}=\{p\outerproduct{0}{0}+(1-p)\outerproduct{1}{1}|0\leq p\leq 1\}
.
We choose the composite free state set
\mathcal{S}_{SC}
to be
\mathcal{S}_{S}\!\otimes_{\text{max}}\!\mathcal{S}_{C}
.
This setting of local coherence has been studied in
Horová et al. 2022
.

We aim to show that Thm.
4
holds in this setup, i.e.
\sigma_{S}
is attainable via broadcasting if and only if
\sup_{\tau_{C}\in\mathcal{D}_{C}}D_{\max}(\tau_{C}\|\mathcal{S}_{C})\geq D_{\max}(\sigma_{S}\|\mathcal{S}_{S})
.
This means that any
\sigma_{S}\in\mathcal{D}_{S}
is attainable, since
\mathcal{S}_{S}
and
\mathcal{S}_{C}
are the same.
First note that
\sup_{\tau_{C}\in\mathcal{D}_{C}}D_{\max}(\tau_{C}\|\mathcal{S}_{C})=D_{\max}(\outerproduct{\pm}{\pm}_{C}\|\mathcal{S}_{C})=\log 2
, where
\ket{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}\pm\ket{1})
.

Then, consider the channel

\displaystyle\Lambda(\rho_{SC})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=+,-}(\outerproduct{x}{x}_{S}\otimes\outerproduct{x}{x}_{C})\bra{x}_{C}\Tr_{S}[\rho_{SC}]\ket{x}_{C},

(A26)

that broadcasts coherence, i.e.

\displaystyle\Lambda(\outerproduct{0}{0}_{S}\otimes\outerproduct{+}{+}_{C})=\outerproduct{+}{+}_{S}\otimes\outerproduct{+}{+}_{C}.

(A27)

Since
\outerproduct{+}{+}_{S}
can be transformed to any state
\sigma_{S}\in\mathcal{D}_{S}
via some CRNG channel,
\Lambda
followed by some other free channel can prepare any
\sigma_{S}
by broadcasting as desired.

We only need to show that
\Lambda\in\mathcal{O}_{SC\to SC}
.
Any free state in
\mathcal{S}_{SC}
can be written as a convex combination of states of the form
\outerproduct{z_1}{z_1}_{S}\otimes\outerproduct{z_2}{z_2}_{C}
with
z_{1},z_{2}=0,1
.
Each of them will be transformed into
\Lambda(\outerproduct{z_1}{z_1}_{S}\otimes\outerproduct{z_2}{z_2}_{C})=\frac{1}{2}(\outerproduct{+}{+}_{S}\otimes\outerproduct{+}{+}_{C}+\outerproduct{-}{-}_{S}\otimes\outerproduct{-}{-}_{C})
, whose
A
and
B
reduced states are both
\frac{\mathmybb{1}_{A(B)}}{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{A(B)}
.
Hence,
\Lambda
is an RNG channel.
From Lemma
5
, it is also a CRNG channel.

V
Strict robust catalysis

We show that strict robust catalysis cannot be advantageous when using a full rank catalyst state.
We begin by defining the reversed relative entropy of resource, first used in Ref.
Eisert et al. 2003
in the context of entanglement theory,

\displaystyle\scalebox{-1}[1]{R}\pqty{\rho_{S}}\coloneq\inf_{\gamma_{S}\in\mathcal{S}_{S}}D(\gamma_{S}\|\rho_{S}).

(A28)

It is easy to verify that this is a faithful measure, i.e.
\scalebox{-1}[1]{R}\pqty{\rho_{S}}\geq 0
with equality if and only if
\rho_{S}\in\mathcal{S}_{S}
and that
\scalebox{-1}[1]{R}\pqty{\rho_{S}}<\infty
when
\rho_{S}
is full rank.

Lemma 8
.

The reversed relative entropy of resource is additive, i.e.

\displaystyle\scalebox{-1}[1]{R}\pqty{\rho_{S}\otimes\omega_{S'}}=\scalebox{-1}[1]{R}\pqty{\rho_{S}}+\scalebox{-1}[1]{R}\pqty{\omega_{S'}}.

(A29)

Proof.

Recall the following property of the quantum relative entropy:
D(\gamma_{SS^{\prime}}\|\rho_{S}\otimes\omega_{S^{\prime}})\geq D(\Tr_{S^{\prime}}[\gamma_{SS^{\prime}}]\|\rho_{S})+D(\Tr_{S}[\gamma_{SS^{\prime}}]\|\omega_{S^{\prime}})
, with the equality if and only if
\gamma_{SS^{\prime}}
is an uncorrelated state.
Since for any
\gamma_{SS^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}_{SS^{\prime}}
, the uncorrelated state
\Tr_{S}[\gamma_{SS^{\prime}}]\otimes\Tr_{S^{\prime}}[\gamma_{SS^{\prime}}]\in\mathcal{S}_{S^{\prime}}
, the infimum of
D(\gamma_{SS^{\prime}}\|\rho_{S}\otimes\omega_{S^{\prime}})
over
\gamma_{SS^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}_{SS^{\prime}}
is always obtained when
\gamma_{SS^{\prime}}
is uncorrelated, and we obtain the claim.
∎

Now, suppose a free state
\gamma_{S}\in\mathcal{S}_{S}
can be transformed into another state
\sigma_{S^{\prime}}
via strict robust catalysis with a full rank catalyst
\tau_{C}
.
Then there exists a channel
\Lambda\in\mathcal{O}_{SC\to S^{\prime}C}
, such that

\displaystyle\Lambda(\gamma_{S}\otimes\tau_{C})=\sigma_{S^{\prime}}\otimes\tau_{C}.

(A30)

From the additivity and the monotonicity,

\displaystyle\scalebox{-1}[1]{R}\pqty{\tau_{C}}=\scalebox{-1}[1]{R}\pqty{\gamma_{S}\otimes\gamma_{C}}\geq\scalebox{-1}[1]{R}\pqty{ \sigma_{S'}\otimes\tau_{C}}=\scalebox{-1}[1]{R}\pqty{\sigma_{S'}}+\scalebox{-1}[1]{R}\pqty{\tau_{C}}.

(A31)

By assumption,
\tau_{C}
is full-rank, so
\scalebox{-1}[1]{R}\pqty{\tau_{C}}
is finite, which implies that
0\geq\scalebox{-1}[1]{R}\pqty{\sigma_{S'}}
, or equivalently
\sigma_{S^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{S}_{S^{\prime}}
from the faithfulness.

VI
Robust battery-assisted transformation

Let us investigate the robustness of battery-assisted transformations, introduced in
Ganardi et al. 2024
;
Alhambra et al. 2019
.
In this framework, there is a distinguished measure of resource
R
.
A transformation from
\rho_{S}
to
\sigma_{S^{\prime}}
is said to be possible with a battery if there exists a battery state
\tau_{C}
and a channel
\Lambda\in\mathcal{O}_{SC\to S^{\prime}C^{\prime}}
such that
\Lambda(\rho_{S}\otimes\tau_{C})=\sigma_{S^{\prime}}\otimes\tau^{\prime}_{C^{\prime}}
with
R(\tau^{\prime}_{C^{\prime}})\geq R(\tau_{C})
.
We define a robust analogue as follows:
a
(\rho,\epsilon)
-robust transformation is possible if
\Lambda(\rho_{S}\otimes\tau_{C})=\sigma_{S^{\prime}}\otimes\tau^{\prime}_{C^{\prime}}
with
R(\tau^{\prime}_{C^{\prime}})\geq R(\tau_{C})
, and for all states
\rho^{\prime}_{S}
, such that
\|\rho^{\prime}_{S}-\rho_{S}\rVert_{1}\leq\epsilon
, we have

\displaystyle R\left(\Tr_{S}\left[\Lambda(\rho^{\prime}_{S}\otimes\tau_{C})\right]\right)\geq R(\tau_{C})

(A32)

In addition, we can allow arbitrarily small error in the target state as long as the resource in the battery is uniformly bounded.

Ref.
Ganardi et al. 2024
showed that if
R
is a finite and additive monotone, then
\rho_{S}
can be transformed into
\sigma_{S^{\prime}}
with a battery if and only if
R(\rho_{S})\geq R(\sigma_{S^{\prime}})
.
We now demonstrate that such transformations are robust when
R
is convex and continuous.
If
R(\rho_{S})>R(\sigma_{S^{\prime}})
, then by continuity, there exists an
\epsilon>0
such that for all
\rho^{\prime}_{S}
satisfying
\|\rho^{\prime}_{S}-\rho_{S}\rVert_{1}\leq\epsilon
, we have
R(\rho^{\prime}_{S})\geq R(\sigma_{S^{\prime}})
.
Following the proof in Ref.
Ganardi et al. 2024
, we can show that the transformation is robust.
If
R(\rho_{S})=R(\sigma_{S^{\prime}})
, convexity ensures that for any
\delta>0
, there exists a state
\sigma^{\delta}_{S^{\prime}}
, such that
\|\sigma^{\delta}_{S^{\prime}}-\sigma_{S^{\prime}}\rVert_{1}\leq\delta
and
R(\sigma^{\delta}_{S^{\prime}})\leq(1-\delta)R(\sigma_{S^{\prime}})=(1-\delta)R(\rho_{S})<R(\rho_{S})
.
Consequently, for any sequence
\Bqty{\delta_n}\to 0
, there exists a robust battery-assisted transformation from
\rho_{S}
to
\sigma^{\delta_{n}}_{S^{\prime}}
, and
\sigma^{\delta_{n}}_{S^{\prime}}\to\sigma_{S^{\prime}}
.
Furthermore, the resource stored in the battery can be assumed to be uniformly bounded by
R(\sigma_{S^{\prime}})
.
Therefore, we conclude that there exists a robust battery-assisted transformation from
\rho_{S}
to
\sigma_{S^{\prime}}
.
