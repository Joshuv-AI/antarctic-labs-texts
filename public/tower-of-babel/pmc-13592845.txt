Emerging antifungal strategies against Candida albicans using metal–organic frameworks and novel therapeutics: Opportunities and challenges
Authors: Amanpreet Singh, Sandeep Kumar, Sehrabpreet Singh, Jeewanjot Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCandida albicans is an opportunistic fungal pathogen responsible for infections ranging from superficial mucosal disease to life-threatening systemic candidiasis, particularly in immunocompromised hosts. Its pathogenicity is enhanced by morphological plasticity, biofilm formation, and immune evasion, contributing to antifungal resistance. Limitations of existing antifungals have driven interest in emerging therapeutics. This review highlights strategies including metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), host-directed therapies (HDTs), CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing, and phytochemicals. MOFs offer high drug-loading capacity, controlled release, and enhanced penetration into biofilms, showing promise against drug-resistant strains. Selected phytochemicals demonstrate inhibitory effects on virulence traits such as hyphal formation and enzyme secretion. CRISPR-Cas9 enables functional genomic studies to identify and disrupt key pathogenicity genes, while HDTs aim to boost host immune responses. Additionally, the potential role of gut microbiota modulation in controlling C. albicans colonization is discussed. By integrating mechanistic insights with translational perspectives, this review provides a concise overview of emerging antifungal strategies, opportunities, and challenges with the potential to overcome current therapeutic limitations.Keywords: Antifungal resistance, Biofilm, Candida albicans, CRISPR-Cas9, Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)

1. Introduction

1.1. Overview of Candida albicans

The human mucosal surfaces normally host the diploid fungus Candida albicans as a commensal organism when health is optimal [1–3]. The fungus shows opportunistic tendencies yet becomes a substantial pathogenic agent after immune system weakness or changes in environmental conditions [4–6]. The transition from commensalism to pathogenicity results in candidiasis [7–9]. Its pathogenic potential increases due to its ability to switch between yeast and hyphal forms [10,11] and form biofilms [6,12]. The dual roles of C. albicans as both commensal and pathogen complicate research and pose significant health risks. Knowledge about C. albicans’s different forms helps medical scientists create powerful methods to combat and stop candidiasis infection. [5,13,14]. 1.2. Global prevalence and clinical significance

Many parts of the world experience widespread Candidiasis infections that harm people throughout different age brackets across all geographical areas [6,7,15]. A wide range of infections occur because of Candida albicans in human populations from mild mucosal ailments affecting the mouth and female genital system to fatal systemic infections known as candidemia [3,6,16] Research demonstrates that systemic infections linked to candidiasis prove deadly for immunocompromised patients with numerous fatality possibilities [1,8,17]. Antifungal-resistant strains have become increasingly prevalent leading to greater barriers in effective treatment methods and infection control strategies [6,15,18] Given the clinical burden of candidiasis, a complete comprehension of the pathogen biology, its interactions with the host immune system lays importance for discovery of novel therapeutic approaches [1,19,20].

1.3. Metal-organic frameworks

Metal-Organic Frameworks (MOFs) are a group of porous materials composed of metal ions or clusters linked to organic linkers, yielding a crystalline structure with large surface area and adjustable porosity. These features offer MOFs a very high degree of versatility for various applications such as drug delivery, biosensing and environmental cleaning. MOFs have several benefits over conventional porous materials, for example, zeolites and mesoporous silica, as a result of their impactable pore shading and compound employability, which empower particular control over the molecular communications and motion. Advances have also been made to improve MOF functionality notably by AI driven discovery, green synthesis and fabrication of hybrid nanomaterials.

They are composed of metal nodes and organic linkers which when combined broaden their structure and result into molecules having porous and crystalline system that develop them to be employed in molecular adsorption and storage [21,22]. They possess high surface properties, in ordinary exceeding the traditional materials, enabling us reach higher occurrences of adsorption and selectivity in numerous applications [23]. The porosity of MOFs is also easily adjustable by changing the metal and organic components within them, which is especially useful in drug delivery and catalysis as this can be tailored to interact with specific molecules [22].

As opposed to common porous materials, MOFs offer higher tunability in terms of pore dimension and chemical functionality and, as a result of that, are suited in advanced drug delivery and biosensing applications [23]. Their one-form structure permits the positioning and drug carrier controlled market, thereby they were considered as benzene require for customization therapy approaches [24]. Moreover, MOFs can be designed to demonstrate unique optical or electric properties to implement them in the advanced biosensing technologies for disease diagnosis and monitoring [23].

In recent developments in MOF research, further enlarged the field of potential uses. The use of artificial intelligence (Al-var methods has speeded up the search of MOF’s designs, and led us to find superior configurations for particular requirements, like targeted drug discharge and pollutant capture [25]. Green synthesis strategies have been created to reduce the environmental effects of MOF generation, consistent with sustainability aims and decrease toxic web-site generation [25]. Additionally, hybrid nanomaterials integrating MOFs with other material components have been exploited in order to optimize their efficiency and long-term stability for different industrial and medical applications [25].

Nevertheless, MOFs continue to have obstacles regarding toxicity, biodegradability and long-lived stability that need to be conquered for effective clinical and industrial translation [21,23]. However, economy production is a main downside, needed progress of synthesis technology to gain economy and accumulate [25]. Though detailed reviews on MOFs in different applications already exist, only a few aimed at comparing actual clinical as well industrial experiences in real-world settings. Building on recent developments, challenges and implementations strategies this review uniquely aims to fill that gap and offers an MOF research roadmap for future.

2. Pathogenesis and mechanisms: from commensal to pathogen

2.1. Morphological plasticity: yeast to hyphal transition

C. albicans demonstrates an outstanding morphological adaptability because it can transform between yeast cells and hyphal forms [10,11]. The fungus expresses dimorphic features which serve as a key virulence factor because these distinct morphologies strongly relate to its ability to spread disease throughout the body. As a commensal, C. albicans colonizes mucosal surfaces in its yeast form, which is small and ovoid in shape [1,19]. Hyphae represent essential fungal features for tissue penetration since they appear as filamentous elongated structures [11,20,26]. Multiple signaling pathways along with transcription factors Efg1 and Cph1 [26,27] accurately regulate this form transition in response to environmental cues which include temperature pH and nutrients [28]. Morphological switching enables C. albicans to adapt to diverse host niches and transition from commensalism to pathogenicity. Comprehensive understanding of these mechanisms is essential for developing targeted antifungal strategies.

2.2. Biofilm formation: a fortress of resistance

Biofilm development elevates C. albicans pathogenic capabilities by creating new protective strategies [6,12]. C. albicans builds biofilms as structured communitites of cells which create their own self-produced extracellular matrix while binding to surfaces of both human tissues and medical equipment. The protective extracellular matrix facilitates biofilm resistance against antifungal medications while blocking the host’s immune response defense mechanisms [29]. Multi-step biofilm development occurs through three key steps consisting of cell adherence followed by hyphal elongation and extracellular polymeric substance production by microbial cells [30]. C. albicans biofilms serve as a component in multiple disease manifestations since such structures establish during medical device-related infections while also contributing to chronic mucosal disease formation [31]. The antifungal resistance shown by biofilms highlights an immediate requirement for new therapeutic approaches to prevent biofilm formation and destroy biofilm structures.

2.3. Key virulence factors: enzymes and adhesins

C. albicans achieves increased pathogenic potential through multiple virulence factors that include secreted enzymes together with cell surface adhesins [19,32]. The secreted enzyme family of Aspartyl proteinases (Saps) functions fundamentally to target host proteins while enabling intestinal tissue penetration [32,33]. The proteases active in these fungal cells disrupt host tissue stability and enable the spreading of fungal infection. Secreted enzymes known as phospholipases damage host cell membranes while boosting invasiveness by causing cell damage. Throughout dissemination of C. albicans cells throughout the body the adhesins Als proteins and Eap1p play a key role in cellular and surface adhesion [12,14,30]. The successful colonization and biofilm development alongside persistent infections depend upon strict adherence to host surfaces by the fungal cells. 3. Immune system interaction: a dynamic battle

3.1. Interactions with the Innate Immune System

When C. albicans meets its host it activates a rapid immune system response through multiple pathways [34–36]. Phagocytic cells together with macrophages and neutrophils function as frontline defenders of the innate immune response through path recognition followed by path clearance. The C-type lectin receptors (CLRs) Dectins accompanied by galectins within Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) detect particular cell-wall components of C. albicans which subsequently activates immune response signaling and fungus removal [35,37]. C. albicans manages to evade these innate immune responses through multiple evading mechanisms despite the fact that its immune targeted [38–40].

3.2. Immune evasion strategies: masking and deception

The pathogenicity of Candida albicans relies on its highly evolved ability to avoid detection and clearance by the host immune system [38–40]. A central strategy involves masking β-glucan, a potent activator of the Dectin-1 receptor. By reducing or concealing β-glucan exposure, C. albicans limits recognition by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), thereby suppressing host immune activation [37,39,40].

Additionally, C. albicans acquires host-derived complement regulatory proteins, including Factor H and C4b-binding protein, which inhibit complement-mediated opsonization and phagocytosis [36–38]. Through surface binding of these regulators, the fungus effectively disrupts the complement cascade and prevents efficient immune clearance [38,41].

Furthermore, the pathogen secretes various inhibitory molecules that directly target immune effector cells, reducing their capacity to mount an effective antifungal response. These multifaceted masking and deception strategies allow C. albicans to persist within the host and evade immune surveillance.

3.3. Host-pathogen dynamics: a complex interplay

C. albicans engages in an elaborate host immune response that surpasses basic pathogen-host interactions according to research [17,36]. The infection outcome results from a precise correlation between fungal virulence factors while considering both host immunity level together with environmental influences [17,42]. C. albicans intraspecific diversity directly affects pathogenic disease capabilities together with immune detection avoidance mechanisms [43]. Throughout fungal colonization processes and host immune response activation microbial compositions act as key determinants [44]. Extensive research reveals the essential role of ecological elements in understanding the pathogenic behavior of C. albicans.

4. Resistance to antifungal and treatments

4.1. Antifungal resistance-emergence

The increasing number of antifungal resistant C. albicans strains presents an expanding danger to medical treatment options. The resistance of C. albicans develops through three main mechanisms which include mutated drug target genes and elevated fungal efflux pumps while having modified cell wall integrity effects drug penetration into the cell [45]. The rise of antifungal resistance in the medical field requires physicians to create new antifungal therapies alongside discovering drugs that can overcome existing resistance mechanisms [6,18,45].

4.2. Current and emerging antifungal therapies

Current antifungal treatments include azoles with echinocandins and polyenes [18,29]. Interactions between polyenes and ergosterol result in membrane structural damage throughout the fungal cells. The therapeutic impact of these drugs faces challenges because resistant fungal strains develop frequently [18,45]. Researchers investigate new antifungal medications which target specific fungus virulence determinants and signaling pathfinder complexes. Research focuses on creating new medications to block fungal cell wall synthesis while finding potential disruptors of pill formation together with substances that specifically target pathogenic agents. Natural antifungal compounds extracted from plants show substantial potential for antifungal applications as confirmed by studies [31,46,47].

5. Genetic diversity of Candida albicans and virulence

5.1. Genomic plasticity and adaptation

As a pathogenic microbe C. albicans achieves its significant success through its notable genomic flexibility [27,48]. The adaptive capabilities of C. albicans result from its flexible genomic structure which enables appropriate responses to different host environments while enabling drug resistance development. Plasticity within C. albicans genome develops through three sequential mechanisms that involve changes in chromosome number termed “aneuploidy,” “loss of heterozygosity” processes and “mitotic recombination” functions during cell division. Genomic alterations alter gene expression patterns which have multiple impacts on virulence and drug resistance abilities and the capacity to evade host immune responses [43,48]. The adaptive qualities of the C. albicans genome reflect both its ability to colonize new environments and its opportunity to resist therapeutic decisions.

5.2. Strain-specific differences in virulence

Different strains of C. albicans demonstrate important fluctuations in disease-causing properties. The numerous genetic makeups together with epigenetic variations that exist across C. albicans populations determine this variability. Environmental elements modify virulence phenotypes by controlling gene expression and affecting microbial virulence characteristics [17,42,43]. Knowledge of strain-specific virulence differences helps scientists understand the complex way fungal genetic makeup affects relationship between fungal genes and environmental factors in C. albicans disease outcomes. The requirement to understand C. albicans population heterogeneity directly guides therapeutic development efforts which create specifically targeted therapeutic approaches. Researchers have used C. elegans model organisms to study how genetic background and ploidy levels affect virulence based on multiple studies [42,45,46].

6. Novel strategies in Candida management

6.1. Phytochemicals as antifungal agents

Plant-derived phytochemicals have emerged as promising candidates for the development of novel antifungal agents, particularly in the treatment of Candida albicans infections. Numerous studies have demonstrated that various phytochemicals exhibit significant antifungal activity [49–52], positioning them as potential alternatives to conventional antifungal drugs. These compounds often exert their antifungal effects through multi-targeted mechanisms, including the disruption of fungal cell wall integrity, interference with plasma membrane permeability, and induction of cellular apoptosis. For example, several phytochemicals compromise the fungal cell wall and membrane, leading to increased permeability and eventual cell death.

In addition, phytochemicals have been shown to suppress the expression of virulence factors, such as secreted aspartyl proteinases (SAPs), which play a critical role in C. albicans pathogenesis [51,53]. These agents may also impair fungal metabolism by inhibiting essential enzymatic pathways, thereby stalling cellular growth and proliferation [52].

Among notable examples, Syzygium aromaticum (clove) essential oil demonstrates potent anti-biofilm activity, effectively disrupting mature biofilms formed by C. albicans [49]. Similarly, extracts from Piper nigrum (black pepper) have been shown to inhibit hyphal formation, biofilm development, and the secretion of phospholipases and proteinases―key virulence traits in fungal infection [51]. These findings suggest that Piper nigrum extract holds considerable potential as a safe, natural antifungal therapy.

Further investigations into phytochemical-based antifungal strategies continue to expand the repertoire of plant-derived candidates [15]. Beyond single-agent use, combinatorial approaches involving phytochemicals and conventional antifungals (e.g., fluconazole or nystatin) are being explored to overcome drug resistance and enhance efficacy [54]. For instance, essential oils such as thyme and oregano, when used in conjunction with antifungal agents, have shown synergistic effects against resistant fungal strains. However, the pharmacodynamics, toxicity profiles, and mechanisms underlying these combinations require more detailed investigation before clinical application. Several phytochemicals, such as piperine and cinnamaldehyde, exhibit potent anti-biofilm properties by inhibiting adhesion, hyphal transition, and extracellular matrix synthesis in C. albicans. These compounds can work synergistically and may serve as adjuncts to standard antifungal therapy for biofilm-associated infections.

6.2. Nanotechnology in antifungal delivery

Nanotechnology enables scientists to develop new techniques that improve how antifungal drugs perform and reach their target location [55,56]. Covering antifungal drugs within nanoparticle structures helps eliminate the current challenges of standard antifungal medicinal products. Through their ability to enhance drug solubility and improve bioavailability nanotechnology research provides more efficient targeted delivery of drugs to infected tissues. Drug delivery systems based on nanoparticles achieve targeted delivery which limits adverse side effects while decreasing medication toxicity to systemic body processes [55].

Silver-copper-cobalt trimetallic nanoparticles represent a significant example of how nanotechnology enables antifungal therapy. Nanoparticles demonstrate exceptionally strong fungicidal capabilities against C. albicans which includes resistance to multiple drugs. These metal nanoparticles execute their antifungal action by damaging cell membranes possibly through combined metal action and producing oxidative stress that ends in fungal cell mortality [56]. The synthesis methods described in Ref. [57] utilize plant extracts to produce nanoparticles which provide environmentally friendly nanomaterial manufacturing solutions and advance their clinical utility prospects.

Nanotechnology applications for antifungal therapy remain in an initial development phase. Scientists require additional investigation to understand how these nanomaterials affect vivo safety and performance along with enduring consequences. The evaluation of nanomaterial safety for the environment must also consider toxic risks during synthesis and disposal stages. Such limitations do not detract from the substantial beneficial effects nanotechnology offers for improving drug delivery and antifungal efficiency thus demanding further research. Nanotechnology-based systems, including MOFs, have shown promising ability to penetrate C. albicans biofilms, overcoming the extracellular matrix barrier that limits drug access. By improving antifungal delivery and disrupting biofilm structural integrity, these systems enhance treatment efficacy against otherwise persistent infections. Research [58] shows how nanotechnology combines with antifungal peptides to expand the application range of this approach. In addition to nanoparticles, MOFs have shown significant promise as novel nanocarriers that improve the efficacy of antifungal agents against Candida species.

6.2.2. MOFs in drug delivery

MOFs are porous, crystalline materials with exceptionally high surface areas (3000–6000 m2/g), tunable pore size, and adjustable chemical functionality. These features enable them to encapsulate diverse therapeutic agents―small molecules, nucleic acids, and proteins―and release them in a controlled and targeted manner, enhancing efficacy while minimizing side effects. MOFs can be engineered for specificity toward disease sites, stimuli-responsive drug release, and multifunctional delivery (e.g., combined imaging and therapy). Despite these advantages, challenges such as potential cytotoxicity, limited large-scale production, and stability under physiological conditions remain key barriers to clinical translation.

6.2.1.1. Applications in drug delivery

(a) Cancer Therapy

Zr-based MOFs such as UiO-66 have been used to deliver small-molecule inhibitors like PDZ1i to hepatocellular carcinoma, targeting pro-metastatic genes and improving therapeutic outcomes [61]. MOFs have also enabled the co-delivery of cisplatin, siRNA, and photosensitizers for photodynamic therapy, effectively overcoming drug resistance in non-small cell lung cancer [62].

(b) Gene Delivery

Zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) has been applied to load siRNA for efficient gene silencing in oncogene targets such as Plk1, demonstrating high knockdown efficiency [63].

(c) Controlled Release Mechanisms

MOFs can be tailored for precision release triggered by environmental cues such as pH or light, allowing site-specific drug activation and reduced systemic toxicity [23].

6.2.3. Stimuli-responsive platforms

(a) pH-Sensitive Mechanisms

ZIF-8 remains stable under physiological conditions [64] but degrades in acidic environments, making it suitable for tumor microenvironment–responsive release [65].

(b) Enzyme-Activated Mechanisms

Functionalized MOFs incorporating enzymes can exploit tumor-associated enzymatic activity to trigger local drug release [66], enhancing therapeutic selectivity [67].

(c) Light-Triggered Mechanisms

Photo-responsive MOFs integrate light-sensitive ligands that change conformation upon irradiation, enabling precise spatiotemporal control over drug release [65].

MOFs and other nanocarriers like liposomes, dendrimers, and mesoporous silica are central in drug-delivery systems with their own pros and cons. MOFs have high capacity for drug loading, excellent biocompatibility and can be modulated for controlled release, which endows them with high adaptability. Liposomes, dendrimers and mesoporous silica in turn have also shown their own merits and limitations with respect to the drug delivery efficacy, targeting and scalability. 6.3. Host-directed therapies (HDTs)

The antifungal treatment approach of Host-directed Therapies (HDTs) represents a promising strategy that aims to improve the body’s immune function to eliminate Candida albicans infections by modulating host immune responses, as documented in recent studies [68,69]. These therapeutic methods seek to enhance the host’s immune defense mechanisms, thereby reducing reliance on antifungal drugs and minimizing the potential for resistance development. Such combined approaches have shown potential to improve patient outcomes by augmenting the effectiveness of conventional antifungal treatments.

Research in the field of HDTs has progressed in identifying specific immune regulatory factors that help protect against C. albicans immune evasion [68,69]. A primary focus has been on Hypoxia-Inducible Factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α), a transcription factor that plays a central role in cellular adaptation to low oxygen tension and in coordinating antimicrobial immune responses. Under normoxic conditions, HIF-1α is hydroxylated by prolyl hydroxylase domain (PHD) enzymes, marking it for ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. In hypoxic or inflammatory conditions―or upon pharmacological inhibition of PHD enzymes (e.g., by dimethyloxalylglycine [DMOG] or Roxadustat)―HIF-1α becomes stabilized, translocates to the nucleus, and forms a heterodimer with HIF-1β. This complex binds to hypoxia response elements (HREs) in target genes, inducing the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α), antimicrobial peptides, and metabolic enzymes that shift immune cells toward glycolysis, thereby enhancing effector functions.

In the context of C. albicans infection, stabilization of HIF-1α has been shown to augment neutrophil activity, particularly through increased production of reactive nitrogen species (RNS) and reactive oxygen species (ROS), which facilitate fungal killing in mucosal infection models [69]. Moreover, HIF-1α activation enhances neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, further aiding pathogen clearance. However, the therapeutic modulation of HIF-1α in systemic candidemia remains poorly characterized; excessive or prolonged activation may risk collateral tissue injury through hyperinflammation [70], emphasizing the need for targeted delivery strategies.

In addition, long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) have emerged as potential immunomodulatory targets for HDT development against C. albicans [68]. LncRNAs can regulate gene expression at transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and epigenetic levels, influencing dendritic cell maturation, macrophage polarization, and cytokine secretion. For example, specific lncRNAs are upregulated during fungal infection to enhance pro-inflammatory pathways, while others suppress excessive inflammation to prevent host damage. The dual role of lncRNAs underscores their potential as precision tools for balancing antifungal immunity and tissue preservation; however, their mechanistic roles in human C. albicans infections remain incompletely defined. HDT approaches may enhance the host immune system’s ability to recognize and clear biofilm-embedded fungal cells. By modulating immune pathways, these strategies can improve the penetration of immune effectors into the biofilm microenvironment, potentially overcoming one of the major obstacles in treating persistent C. albicans infections.

Despite encouraging preclinical results, the translation of HDTs to clinical practice faces significant challenges. Immune overstimulation through HDTs can cause host tissue damage, indicating the necessity for tightly regulated immune modulation [71]. Furthermore, most mechanistic insights are derived from in vitro and animal models, and robust clinical trials are essential to evaluate their safety, dosing parameters, and therapeutic windows in humans [72]. Future strategies should focus on integrating HDTs with pathogen-targeted antifungal agents, leveraging synergistic effects while minimizing the risks of immune dysregulation.

However, several limitations currently hinder the clinical application of HDTs against fungal infections:

Risk of immune overactivation: Excessive stimulation of immune pathways can cause collateral host tissue damage or inflammatory disorders, especially in patients with pre-existing immune dysregulation.

Variable efficacy: HDTs may work well in localized mucosal infections but show reduced effectiveness in systemic infections, where immune evasion strategies of C. albicans are more complex [70].

Patient-specific variability: Differences in age, comorbidities, and genetic immune profiles can influence HDT outcomes, complicating standardization.

Limited translational data: Most HDT findings are from in vitro and animal studies, with very few human clinical trials to confirm safety, dosage, and long-term effects [72].

6.4. CRISPR-Cas9 technology for antifungal development

CRISPR-Cas9 technology has significantly advanced the understanding of Candida albicans biology by enabling precise genome editing. This revolutionary system facilitates functional genetic studies by allowing targeted disruption or modification of specific genes, thereby uncovering their roles in virulence, drug resistance, and pathogenicity stages [73,74]. Its application offers a powerful platform for identifying novel antifungal targets and accelerating the development of targeted therapies [75].

Unlike traditional mutagenesis techniques, CRISPR-Cas9 provides accurate and efficient gene editing, which has been instrumental in elucidating the function of essential genes during host-pathogen interactions [76]. For instance, the biofilm regulator Sdd3, identified through CRISPR-based genetic screens, was found to modulate the Rho1–PKC–MAPK signaling cascade, a pathway crucial for biofilm formation and cell wall integrity. This makes Sdd3 a promising antifungal target [74]. CRISPR-Cas systems offer the potential to target and disrupt genes critical for biofilm development in C. albicans (e.g., BCR1, HWP1, ALS family), thereby reducing biofilm formation and restoring antifungal susceptibility.

The strategy of targeting virulence-associated genes―rather than genes essential for viability-―offers a potential advantage in reducing the development of resistance. Moreover, CRISPR-Cas9 can be employed to construct strain libraries and conduct high-throughput functional genomics in C. albicans. However, challenges such as off-target effects, delivery system limitations, and ethical considerations still need to be addressed before clinical translation can be achieved.

However, several limitations hinder the direct clinical translation of CRISPR-based antifungal strategies.

Delivery challenges: Efficient in vivo delivery of CRISPR components into fungal cells is difficult due to the rigid fungal cell wall and lack of well-established fungal-specific vectors. Current delivery systems (plasmid-based, ribonucleoprotein complexes, and nanoparticle carriers) often show low transformation efficiency in Candida, especially in clinical isolates [76].

Off-target effects: Unintended edits in non-target genomic regions can lead to unpredictable phenotypes, potentially compromising both safety and efficacy. This risk is exacerbated in fungal genomes with high sequence similarity in gene families.

Genetic background variability: Differences in ploidy, karyotype instability, and homologous recombination efficiency across Candida strains (and in non-albicans species such as C. auris) limit reproducibility and scalability [74].

Immune and safety concerns: For therapeutic applications, CRISPR delivery vehicles (viral or non-viral) may trigger host immune responses, while unintended genome modifications raise biosafety and ethical considerations.

Regulatory and scalability barriers: There are currently no established regulatory pathways for gene-editing therapies targeting fungal infections, and large-scale production of safe, standardized CRISPR systems remains a challenge [74].

7. Candida albicans biofilms: resistance and management

The antifungal therapy against C. albicans biofilms represents an obstacle since these structures consist of fungal communities protected by an extracellular matrix. Because biofilms provide a sessile lifestyle C. albicans develops resistance against standard antifungal treatments and host defense responses [12,76]. The biofilm environment protects fungal cells through multiple mechanisms such as limited drug access and gene expression modifications and through the development of persistent cells that naturally resist antifungals. Biofilm formation through C. albicans enables persistent infections to build up strongly which complicates treatment outcomes and increases the risk of reinfection [12]. Candida albicans biofilms are structured communities of fungal cells embedded within an extracellular matrix that restricts antifungal penetration and protects against host immune defenses. These biofilms contribute to persistent infections and high recurrence rates. Biofilm-specific mechanisms and their targeted management strategies are integrated throughout the therapeutic sections of this review.

The treatment of C. albicans biofilms demands effective strategic development to enhance therapeutic results. Successful counteraction of C. albicans biofilms demands simultaneous involvement of prevention measures alongside biofilm elimination methods. Treatment of established biofilms with conventional antifungal drugs normally fails to work therefore pushing the research direction toward alternative therapeutic strategies. Research active investigation combines novel antifungal drugs effective for biofilm penetration with targeting multiple biofilm formation processes and developing enhanced drug delivery systems [77,78].

Phytochemicals and Nanotechnology: Promising Anti-biofilm avenues [50,77,78]. Piperine and cinnamaldehyde phytochemicals appeared capable of working synergistically to inhibit C. albicans biofilms according to researchers [78]. This discovery points toward their future use as treatment adjuncts. The application of nanotechnology to create drug delivery systems enhanced by nanoparticles while simultaneously working to prevent biofilm structural integration remains under active investigation [77]. Additional research needs to explore improved implementation of these methodologies for clinical practice and evaluate their practical effect.

8. Role of gut microbiota and dysbiosis

Human health depends heavily on the complex microbial community that inhabits the gastrointestinal tract known as human gut microbiota. Within the complex gut microbiota environment C. albicans retains its association with native bacteria along with fungi and additional microscopic organisms [79,80]. The mutual and opposite influences among microorganism’s shape C. albicans colonization by affecting its ability to survive while influencing pathogenic potential. Drastically altered interactions maintain the gut’s health by preventing C. albicans from spreading too much.

Some bacterial strains hold anti-C. albicans properties which stop this fungus from growing while suppressing its multiplication within the body. Laboratory studies demonstrate Bifidobacterium adolescentis inhibits C. albicans growth through two mechanisms: it produces antimicrobial organic acids that fight candida along with lowering physical gut environment pH [79,80]. Studies demonstrate that probiotic strains including B. adolescentis could serve as a form of prevention against C. albicans overgrowth along with resultant infections. Certain bacterial species boost C. albicans growth potential while boosting its pathogenicity which causes microbiota disruption in the gut community.

Within mouse models of disease-associated C. albicans colonization depends to a great extent on gut microbiota structure its biological functions as well [81]. When the gut microbiota undergoes dysbiosis it creates areas beneficial for the undesired growth of C. albicans which raises the chance of potential infections [81,82]. The consumption of antibiotics can damage the gut microbiota then leads to a bacterial imbalance which enables C. albicans along with other invasive pathogens to multiply out of control [30]. The human colon environment simulation models in vitro studies provide indispensable information about how C. albicans interacts and coexists with microbiota which explains the processes that sustain colonization and disease development.

9. Vaccinations and prevention

Research aimed at creating efficient vaccines for C. albicans infections continues to be an ongoing major scientific hurdle [83,84]. The variable phenotypes along with a wide spectrum of genomic diversity together with challenges in generating protective immunity in compromised immune system patients present roadblocks during vaccine development processes. C. albicans makes vaccine design difficult because of its capacity to switch morphologies along with its effective evasion of immune system responses.

Researchers continue to make advances toward Candida vaccine development according to the literature [84]. The development of new vaccine candidates proceeded through clinical trials which evaluated two distinct vaccine strategies: Medical researchers are exploring two vaccine types inclusive of subunit vaccines targeting fungal antigens while expanding the immune response by using various epitopes in multivalent vaccines [85,86]. These vaccine programs deliver inconsistent outcomes because researchers agree there must be more improvements in their design.

Building effective vaccines for C. albicans depends entirely on complete understanding of protective immune responses directed against the organism. Scientists should identify essential fungal antigens which activate lasting immune responses while determining best vaccine designs for delivery and studying immune challenges in targeted groups. Two emerging vaccine technologies include nanoparticle-based platforms and synthetic glycans which researchers are developing to improve vaccine performance [58,87]. Researchers hope to achieve lasting immune response improvements through vaccine designs which incorporate T-cell epitopes [86]. The development of excellent Candida vaccines maintains its status as a top priority because it could stop invasive candidiasis and lower mortality rates [58].

9.1. Microbiome-based approaches

In addition to vaccination strategies, modulation of the host microbiome has emerged as a promising avenue for the prevention and management of Candida albicans infections. The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in colonization resistance, where commensal bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species inhibit fungal overgrowth through competitive nutrient utilization, production of antifungal metabolites (e.g., short-chain fatty acids), and immune modulation [85,86]. Dysbiosis caused by antibiotics, immunosuppressants, or dietary changes has been associated with increased susceptibility to candidiasis. Probiotic supplementation has shown potential in reducing Candida colonization in both gastrointestinal and oral mucosal sites, while prebiotics that selectively stimulate beneficial bacteria may indirectly limit fungal expansion. Furthermore, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has been explored in experimental models as a method to restore microbiome homeostasis and reduce fungal burden. However, microbiome-targeted therapies face challenges, including inter-individual variability, strain-specific effects, and the need for long-term safety data. Combining microbiome modulation with vaccination may provide synergistic protection against Candida infections, particularly in high-risk immunocompromised populations [83,84].

10. Non-albicans Candida species: emerging challenges

Global health faces increasing risks from the growing number of non-albicans Candida species which appear with greater regularity [85,87]. The species C. auris C. tropicalis and C. glabrata reveal better drug resistance capacities than C. albicans against conventional antifungal treatments. The appearance of C. auris multidrug resistant strains represents a dangerous clinical management obstacle and sets the stage for developing alternative therapeutic solutions.

Antifungal resistance in non-albicans Candida species arises from target alterations, drug efflux mechanisms, and cell wall modifications [87]. Identifying antifungal resistance mechanisms remains vital for creating drugs that exploit unexplored drug targets. The new antifungal medicines rezafungin ibrexafungerp and oteseconazole demonstrate potential to treat Candida non-albicans infections even when these organisms resist traditional antifungal drug treatments [87]. Research efforts in antifungal drug development must continue because the creation of new medicines demands an extended complex process.

11. Discussion

This review highlights several innovative antifungal strategies against Candida albicans, including MOFs, CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing, HDTs and phytochemicals. While each approach offers unique advantages, they also present specific challenges that limit immediate clinical application. MOFs, with their high surface area and tunable structures, show great promise for targeted antifungal delivery and biofilm penetration. Dual-functional MOFs, such as copper-based frameworks, can provide both drug delivery and intrinsic antifungal activity. However, safety concerns, large-scale synthesis challenges, and incomplete understanding of them in vivo behavior remain key barriers. Gene-editing tools like CRISPR-Cas9 enable precise targeting of fungal virulence and resistance genes, but delivery into fungal cells and off-target effects remain unresolved. HDTs, while enhancing host immune response, require careful modulation to avoid tissue damage. Similarly, phytochemicals offer multitargeted activity but often face issues of stability, bioavailability, and variability in potency. A major cross-cutting challenge across all strategies is the ability to translate laboratory success into clinical practice, considering regulatory approval pathways, manufacturing scalability, and cost-effectiveness. Addressing these limitations will be essential to advance these strategies from experimental models to patient care.

12. Future directions

The investigation of C. albicans requires multiple research avenues as future studies should combine specific essential fields. Science is exploring new antifungal compounds which attack virulence factors specifically and avoid assaulting essential cellular functions [88,89]. By implementing these approach scientists aim to prevent pathogenic microbes from using any debilitating capabilities and block gene mutations that confer resistance. Research into new therapy options shows promise through the combination of nanotechnology [90,91] and photodynamic therapy. MOFs hold potential for next-generation antifungal strategies, especially when combined with gene editing tools like CRISPR or microbiome-targeted therapies. Their ability to co-deliver antifungals and immune modulators may transform the treatment of resistant Candida albicans strains. Nanotechnology provides effective methods for targeted antifungal delivery to treatment sites which demonstrates both better outcomes and decreased toxic effects [90,91]. Photosensitizers activated by light work to eradicate fungal cells as an effective treatment method which preserves microbial ecosystems [92]. Understanding the intricate relationships between C. albicans and both immune system responses and resident microbial populations requires greater attention. The understanding of these C. albicans-host interactions supports therapeutic development of treatments which manipulate these interactions to prevent or treat candidiasis [93]. The development of vaccines shows potential as an emerging prevention method because it enhances immunity against C. albicans infections. The pathogen’s extensive variability of phenotypes and antigens poses ongoing difficulties for vaccine designers to create successful products that target fungus infections. Further study should evaluate Live Biotherapeutic Products’ novel potential as a treatment method for candidiasis [93]. Live Biotherapeutic Products exhibit therapeutic effects through their microorganisms while presenting a potential safe alternative to traditional antifungal treatments. Direct multidisciplinary scientific collaboration between fungal biologists and immunologists along with nanotechnologists and drug developers must develop new approaches to effectively combat growing C. albicans infections.

12.1. Practical challenges for clinical translation

Despite these promising directions, the translation of innovative antifungal strategies into clinical practice faces significant real-world hurdles. Clinical scalability is a primary concern, as large-scale production of nanomaterials such as MOFs demands strict control over particle size, crystallinity, and purity, which is technically challenging and cost-intensive. Similarly, CRISPR-based systems require reproducible manufacturing under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) conditions, along with long-term stability, to ensure clinical viability. Regulatory pathways for such advanced therapeutics remain underdeveloped, with agencies like the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) requiring extensive safety, toxicology, biodistribution, and clearance data before approval. For CRISPR-based interventions, ethical considerations and genomic stability concerns further complicate approval timelines. Antifungal vaccines and microbiome-based therapeutics also face prolonged regulatory review due to the lack of established precedents. Delivery systems constitute another critical barrier, as achieving targeted and sustained drug delivery to fungal infection sites, particularly in systemic infections, remains technically difficult. MOF-based delivery systems may be affected by factors such as particle charge, pore size, and in vivo stability, while CRISPR delivery into fungal cells remains inefficient. Host-directed therapies additionally carry the risk of off-target immune responses that may cause unintended inflammation or tissue damage. Overcoming these challenges through coordinated research, manufacturing innovation, and adaptive regulatory frameworks will be crucial for successfully moving these strategies from bench to bedside.

13. Conclusion

Candida albicans remains a formidable challenge due to its adaptive virulence, immune evasion, and rising antifungal resistance. Traditional antifungal therapies, while essential, are increasingly limited in efficacy, especially against biofilm-associated infections and resistant strains. This review highlights the potential of emerging therapeutic strategies that move beyond conventional paradigms.

MOFs offer customizable platforms for targeted and stimuli-responsive antifungal delivery, with promising results in both in vitro and preclinical models. Phytochemicals present multi-targeted, low-toxicity antifungal options, while CRISPR-Cas9 allows precise interrogation and targeting of fungal virulence genes. Host-directed therapies enhance endogenous immune responses and could mitigate resistance by avoiding direct fungal targeting.

However, the clinical translation of these technologies faces challenges including toxicity, scalability, and host-specific variability. Future research should focus on infection-type-specific strategies (e.g., oral vs. systemic), integration of antifungal and immunomodulatory modalities, and leveraging microbiome-fungus interactions for prevention.

A multi-disciplinary and mechanistic approach is crucial to developing next-generation antifungal therapies that are effective, targeted, and resistant to resistance.
