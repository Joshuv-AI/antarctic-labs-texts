Visualization of Unconjugated Bilirubin In Vivo with a Novel Approach Using Chemical Exchange Saturation Transfer Magnetic Resonance Imaging in a Rat Model
Authors: Lin Yang, Yan Cheng, Yanlong Jia, Zhen Cao, Zerui Zhuang, Xiaolei Zhang, Jitian Guan, Rongzhi Cai, Yan Lin, Renhua Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Unconjugated bilirubin (UCB) visualization is valuable for early bilirubin encephalopathy (BE) diagnosis and management. UCB neurotoxicity is a challenge, necessitating improved imaging modalities for precise localization and characterization. This study developed a noninvasive method for UCB imaging in the brain using chemical exchange saturation transfer (CEST) magnetic resonance imaging, which visualizes UCB distribution through amide–bulk water proton exchange, a process termed bilirubin CEST (Bil-CEST) imaging. Bil-CEST imaging parameters were initially optimized; the exchange rate of the amide protons of UCB was calculated. Bil-CEST imaging characteristics and specificity were assessed using in vitro images of UCB solutions under different conditions and images of other brain metabolites. Bil-CEST maps of the rat brain were collected at the baseline and dynamically, postinjection of the UCB solution or vehicle into lateral ventricles of Sprague–Dawley rats. The model was validated using a water maze and pathological staining. In vitro, the Bil-CEST effect was observed at approximately 5.5 ppm downfield from bulk water. This effect was proportional to the UCB concentration and B1 amplitude. In vivo, Bil-CEST imaging revealed a progressive enhancement following a lateral ventricular UCB injection. Conversely, no significant imaging changes were observed in the vehicle group. Compared with the vehicle group, the UCB group had more hippocampal neuronal apoptosis and worse cognitive function. These findings highlight the utility of Bil-CEST in direct UCB imaging, indicating its potential as a clinically valuable biomarker for BE diagnosis and management.

Introduction

Bilirubin is a byproduct of the metabolism of the heme group in red blood cells1 and is categorized into conjugated bilirubin (CB) and unconjugated bilirubin (UCB), depending on whether or not it binds to glucuronic acid in the hepatocytes. UCB accounts for 85% of the total bilirubin (TB). UCB is lipid-soluble and can cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) to produce cytotoxicity in neurons and astrocytes. In severe cases, it can cause bilirubin encephalopathy (BE),2 which has a mortality rate of 50–75%, and 75–90% of BE survivors are left with severe sequelae.3 Therefore, early and noninvasive detection of UCB is critical for preventing and managing BE. However, circulating UCB tends to bind tightly and reversibly to blood albumin. Methods such as direct spectrophotometry or colorimetric assays usually rely on the substance to be quantified being in a free, unbound state for accurate quantification.4 Therefore, the direct and accurate quantification of UCB by these methods is difficult. Moreover, the ratio of serum TB (mg/dL) to serum albumin (g/dL) has been utilized clinically as an approximate surrogate measure for UCB to predict the risk of bilirubin-induced central nervous system damage. Although this method was once recommended by the American Academy of Pediatrics, its indirectness and imprecision have led to its discontinuation in recent reports.5

Meanwhile, uniform international diagnostic criteria for BE are lacking. The clinical diagnosis of BE primarily depends on hyperbilirubinemia, neurological clinical manifestations, brainstem auditory evoked potentials, and cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) abnormalities. Hyperbilirubinemia is defined as a serum TB level of ≥5 mg/dL and a serum direct/CB level of >1.0 mg/dL.6 A TB level of ≥25 mg/dL and/or an increasing level of >0.5 mg/dL can be defined as acute BE (ABE). Phototherapy is an effective treatment for BE because it converts bilirubin into a water-soluble form through the use of blue light, making it easier for bilirubin to be excreted from the body.1 Without timely treatment, ABE may progress to kernicterus (chronic BE) which is characterized by permanent neurological sequelae, manifesting in the first year of life with hearing loss, upward gaze paresis, and movement disorders of dystonia or choreoathetosis.7 In the future, direct detection of UCB will be important for the establishment of new diagnostic criteria for BE.

Previously, no medical imaging was available for imaging UCB in the brain. Clinically, brain parenchymal changes resulting from UCB toxicity can only be evaluated indirectly using structure MRI. In neonates with ABE, high signal intensity on T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) at the posterior medial borders of the globus pallidi, subthalamic nuclei, hippocampus, and cerebellum (cortical and dentate nuclei) and iso- to hyper-intense signals in these areas on T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) can be observed. In contrast, kernicterus usually presents with a high signal intensity in these regions on T2WI.8 However, this method is subjective and poses challenges in differential diagnosis.9 Radiologists have made considerable efforts in advancing diffusion-weighted imaging,10 diffusion tensor imaging,11 proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy,12 and MRI radiomics13 to identify and diagnose ABE at an earlier stage. However, these imaging studies reveal only microstructural or metabolic brain tissue abnormalities caused by UCB, leading to issues of insufficient sensitivity and indirectness. These findings indicate the need to explore MRI techniques and develop a method that can directly visualize UCB with reliable temporal and spatial resolution.

Chemical exchange saturation transfer (CEST) imaging is an MRI technique that employs selective radiofrequency pulses to saturate exchangeable hydrogen protons.14 These saturated protons are transferred to bulk water protons via chemical exchange, resulting in a decreased signal from the bulk water pool, thereby providing insights into the solute pool and its surrounding environment.15 Compared with proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy, CEST imaging offers better spatial resolution and heightened sensitivity, enabling the imaging of micromolar metabolites such as glutamate, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and creatine.16 The chemical structure of UCB contains numerous exchangeable hydrogen protons, prompting us to investigate this characteristic in conjunction with CEST imaging.

In this study, we aimed at developing a noninvasive method for imaging UCB in the brain using CEST MRI (Bil-CEST). Additionally, we aimed at exploring the feasibility of Bil-CEST imaging on a 7T MRI scanner in vivo using a rat model and discussing the specificity, sensitivity, objectivity, and spatial resolution of Bil-CEST imaging.

Animal Experiments

MRI Data Acquisition

To investigate the Bil-CEST effect of bilirubin in the brain, we established a rat model of hyperbilirubinemia-induced neuronal damage. Figure 4 displays Bil-CEST intergroup comparison images at baseline and following the administration of PBS or varying bilirubin concentrations into the lateral ventricle over a period of 30–180 min (step size, 30 min). In the bilirubin group, the substance diffused rapidly throughout the brain upon injection into the lateral ventricle, causing a progressive increase in the intensity of the Bil-CEST signal. Histopathological Staining Results

Figure 5 displays transverse TUNEL panoramic scans of rat brains 2 weeks postmodeling, illustrating that the bilirubin group exhibited not only a higher number of TUNEL-positive neurons, indicative of apoptotic neurons, within the brain parenchyma compared with the PBS group but also a more pronounced enlargement of the lateral ventricles. Figure 6 presents the intergroup control images of H&E and Nissl staining in the hippocampus of rats 2 weeks postmodeling. H&E staining revealed bilirubin-induced yellow staining and numerous neuronal morphological abnormalities in the hippocampal region of the bilirubin group (p = 0.0006, Figure 6B), such as apoptotic necrosis, nuclear pyknosis, atrophy, and degeneration, confirming the successful establishment of the bilirubin rat model. In contrast, the brain tissue in the PBS group exhibited no yellow staining, and neuronal morphology remained typical. Nissl staining indicated a significantly higher incidence of neuronal morphological abnormalities and apoptosis in the hippocampal region of the bilirubin group compared with that of the PBS group, indicating that bilirubin treatment facilitates neuronal apoptosis in this region (p = 0.0022, Figure 6D).

Discussion

This study demonstrates for the first time that Bil-CEST enables visualization of the spatial distribution of UCB in the brain with a relatively high spatial resolution. Bil-CEST imaging can specifically detect the distribution of unconjugated bilirubin through proton exchange between the amide and bulk water in a concentration-dependent manner. Our study provides a promising basis for future clinical applications. Besides illustrating the spatial distribution of UCB, assessing changes in its relative concentration provides additional diagnostic information. Furthermore, measuring the bilirubin distribution may present an opportunity to investigate the photochemical interactions of bilirubin during phototherapy.

Phantom Experiments: Specificity of Bil-CEST Imaging

Groups with exchangeable hydrogen protons form the foundation of a contrast agent’s ability to facilitate CEST imaging, such as amide, imino, or hydroxyl groups. Zhou et al. were the first to exploit the exchange between bulk water protons and the amide protons of endogenous mobile cellular proteins and peptides for intracellular pH level assessment.18 5-Methyl-5, 6-dihydrothymidine enables the use of CEST contrast agents to monitor herpes simplex virus type-1 thymidine kinase expression through the presence of imino protons.19 Myo-inositol and glycogen take advantage of the slow to intermediate exchange rate of hydroxyl protons to produce the CEST effect.20,21 For an efficient CEST effect, a slow to intermediate exchange condition (Δω > k) is necessary, where Δω denotes the chemical shift (or offset frequency) of the exchanging spins and k represents the exchange rate. In our study, the amide proton exchange rate of UCB was 1031 Hz. Compared with the chemical shift difference at 7T, the exchange rate of the −NH protons has a slow to intermediate exchange rate and satisfies the condition (Δω > k). The exchangeable −NH protons from bilirubin were further verified in hydrogen–deuterium exchange experiments, evidenced by the complete line broadening of the three resonances near 5.5 ppm.22,23

In the in vitro phantom study, a concentration-dependent Bil-CEST effect was noted. With each 1 mM increment in bilirubin concentration, the Bil-CEST effect increased by approximately 1.4%. This finding indicates that the Bil-CEST effect correlates positively with bilirubin concentration, suggesting that UCB’s CEST effect can serve as a biomarker for its concentration. The B1 amplitude significantly influences the CEST effect. In our study, the Bil-CEST signal increases with the B1 field at lower intensities, showing a linear relationship. However, when the B1 intensity reaches 5 μT, the signal’s growth tends to slow due to saturation efficiency and spillover effects, eventually plateauing. The direct water saturation line width is proportional to the saturation pulse amplitude and duration. The saturation time had a smaller effect on the increase of the Bil-CEST effect after >3 s. Therefore, we chose a saturation time of 3 s as the pulse parameter for in vivo Bil-CEST scanning. Appropriate B1 amplitude and pulse duration lead to complete saturation. Therefore, we chose 5.0 μT as the optimal B1 amplitude in CEST imaging experiments, which also helps minimize the asymmetry of the asymmetric magnetization transfer effect and strengthens the specificity of Bil-CEST for bilirubin visualization.24

It has been reported that the CEST signal has a complex dependence on T1.25,26 Supporting Information Figure S2 shows that the z-spectra of samples 1 and #2 do not show significant differentiation due to the difference in T1W, especially near 5.5 ppm. We hypothesize that the Bil-CEST signal is relatively insensitive to T1 at high fields and under high saturation power in which T1 recovery and direct water saturation effect can be canceled.

Under the conditions of 5 μT and 3s (same as in vivo scanning), we observed a contribution of serum to the Bil-CEST effect. This may be attributable to the presence of a minor quantity of UCB in serum or to the spectral width, which is difficult to distinguish in the current technique. Given that bilirubin exhibits pronounced chemical shift differentials relative to common brain metabolites, the quantification of the Bil-CEST effect was unaffected by contamination from other small chemical shift metabolites, such as glucose (1.2, 2.1, and 2.9 ppm),27 glutamate (3.0 ppm),28 GABA (2.75 ppm),16 and creatine (1.8 ppm).29 This characteristic enhances the specificity of Bil-CEST for in vivo imaging.

Sensitivity and Objectivity of Bil-CEST Imaging

Structural MRI is the most commonly used adjunctive test for the clinical diagnosis of ABE and kernicterus, with signal alteration serving as a crucial indicator for BE diagnosis, as confirmed by numerous studies.13,30 MRI signal alterations may be associated with UCB-induced microglial activation or neuronal damage.31,32 This is supported clinically by the presence of bilaterally symmetric abnormally high signals in the globus pallidus and subthalamic nucleus of infants with kernicterus on T2WI, which aligns with autopsy neuropathological findings in kernicterus.33 Although early MRI in at-risk infants often reveals increased T1WI signal in these regions, it may yield false-positive results owing to the presence of myelin in these structures. Furthermore, T1 contrast in these brain regions can vary with the type of pulse sequence used.34 Most importantly, the diagnosis of BE using structural MRI is subject to subjective interpretation by the diagnosing physician. For instance, some infants with kernicterus do not exhibit any signal abnormalities on structural MRI.35

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) bilirubin levels correlate closely with clinical symptoms in patients with ABE, potentially providing a more accurate reflection of the BBB’s functional status and intracerebral bilirubin levels.36 UCB can cross the BBB in rat models due to its lipid solubility. Injecting bilirubin into the lateral ventricle serves as an effective method to demonstrate the concentration-dependent Bil-CEST signal in the rat brain, both before and after bilirubin administration, using 7.0 T MRI.37,38 In our study, bilirubin rapidly diffused through the CSF to the entire brain postinjection, with the Bil-CEST effect intensifying over time, aligning with the phantom study results. The in vitro and in vivo UCB concentration-dependent Bil-CEST effects substantiate the potential for imaging the UCB distribution in the brain under pathological conditions. The study findings suggest that Bil-CEST imaging may serve as an imaging biomarker for early BE diagnosis given its specific ability to visualize UCB and detect signal changes at an earlier stage. T2WI maps and TUNEL results from rat brains 2 weeks postmodeling reveal that UCB, having diffused extensively through the CSF in the lateral ventricles, induces neuronal apoptosis, leading to parenchymal atrophy and lateral ventricle volume expansion. These findings are consistent with the results of previous studies,39,40 confirming the success of the modeling and elucidating the altered signaling observed throughout the brain in Bil-CEST imaging.

Spatial Resolution of Bil-CEST

Current bilirubin testing techniques exhibit numerous limitations. Traditional blood tests, while capable of quantifying bilirubin, are invasive, demonstrate poor specificity by quantifying only total serum bilirubin levels, and lack spatial resolution. UCB concentrations in the brain are directly related to BE development. Diffuse reflectance spectroscopy detects bilirubin by analyzing the reflected light spectra from the skin surface, providing qualitative information based on spectral characteristics. However, its detection depth is only a few millimeters, which does not allow it to reach the depth of brain tissue, and it is susceptible to light interference from tissue components.41 Hyperspectral imaging maps the relative distribution of bilirubin without providing quantification.42 Photoacoustic microscopy remains susceptible to interference from other light-absorbing substances, and its spatial resolution is confined to the subcutaneous area.43 However, Bil-CEST offers the advantages of deep detection depth and high sensitivity inherent in CEST, enabling it to surmount the limitations of previous bilirubin detection methods. It can noninvasively visualize the distribution of UCB in deep brain tissues at the molecular level in vivo.

Limitation

The effects of insufficient repetition time and RF were not addressed in calculating UCB exchange rates in this study. Since the model-based fitting of quasi-steady-state (QUASS) CEST Z-spectra enables consistent and accurate quantification of exchange parameters through omega plot construction by reducing error due to signal overlap and nonequilibrium CEST effects.44 In the next work, we would like to use advanced QUASS Z-spectra reconstruction to standardize in vivo amide exchange quantification across acquisition conditions and further determine it in vivo. The in vivo experiments in this study were conducted at a B1 of 5 μT for 3 s, likely resulting in a high RF energy deposition, such as the high specific absorption rate. Particularly, Sun developed a QUASS CEST MRI technique to reconstruct the steady state from CEST scans collected with an inadequate saturation time. This method involved a QUASS CEST analysis that accounted for the effects of limited saturation duration (Ts) and relaxation delay (Td) by determining the labile proton fraction ratio and exchange rate using simulated CEST data.45 In future studies, we will employ advanced postprocessing to reconstruct the quasi-equilibrium CEST effect from that of reduced saturation power and shorter RF saturation duration, similar to our deep learning-based QUASS CEST reconstruction strategy.46 It is critical for future clinical translation.

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate the feasibility and potential of Bil-CEST MRI for mapping UCB changes with an optimal B1 amplitude, free from contamination by other common metabolites. UCB’s exchangeable amide protons produce a Bil-CEST effect at approximately 5.5 ppm in Z-spectra. In a rat model, the bilirubin group exhibited a Bil-CEST effect significantly higher than that of the control group. The study results show that Bil-CEST has the potential to be a clinically valuable and cost-effective biomarker for the early diagnosis and treatment assessment of BE. This noninvasive technique enables in vivo imaging of UCB and may prove valuable for the prevention and early diagnosis of BE. Further studies should investigate its potential as a biomarker for diagnosing and treating other diseases associated with elevated UCB and assess its clinical application at lower field strengths.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Bilirubin, glucose, GABA, and creatine were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. Dimethyl sulfoxide-d6 was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Deuterium oxide (D2O) was purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc. (Andover, MA 01810, USA). Fetal bovine serum was purchased from Gibco (USA). The terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay kit was purchased from Servicebio (Wuhan, China).

Adult male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (grade SPF) (age, 8–12 weeks; weight, 250 ± 50 g) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. The rats were housed in a controlled environment with a constant temperature (24 ± 2 °C), consistent humidity, and a 12 h light–dark cycle and provided with standard laboratory water and food. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines from the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (US National Institutes of Health). All experiments were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research Reporting In Vivo Experiments) guidelines and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shantou University Medical College (approval ID: SUMC2023–478). The rats were anaesthetized with a mixture of isoflurane (4% for induction and 2.5% for animal model construction and MRI), oxygen, and air (gas flow rate, 0.5 L/min).

Phantom Experiments

Phantom Experiment at 9.4 T

Three 15 mM bilirubin phantoms with varying D2O concentrations (0%, 3%, and 9%) were prepared and subsequently scanned using proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy. The 1H NMR phantom experiments were conducted by using a Bruker AVII 400 MHz NMR spectrometer (Bruker Biospin, Germany) at a 1H frequency of 400.13 MHz. Before acquisition, the magnetic field was shimmed using either gradients or manual shims, and the D2O signal was locked. The 1H NMR spectra of the bilirubin were recorded with the following parameters: the number of dummy scans, 4; recycle delay, 1.5 ms; mixing time, 100 ms; t1, 3 μs; 90° pulse width, 7.3 μs; the number of points, 32,760; the number of scans, 64; acquisition time, 2.05 s; and spectral width, 8000 Hz. Water suppression was achieved by irradiating the water peak during recycle delay and mixing time. The resulting 1H NMR spectral data were postprocessed using MestReNova (version 8.1.0, Mestrelab Research, Santiagode Compostela, Spain).

Phantom Experiment at 7 T

Bilirubin was prepared at varying concentrations (4, 6, 8, and 10 mM) for CEST scanning. Different saturation powers (1.0–7.0 μT) and saturation times (2–5 s) were also used to determine the optimized CEST scanning conditions and calculate the exchange rate (k) of UCB’s amide protons. To evaluate the influence of other common metabolites on Bil-CEST, phantoms of serum, glucose, GABA, and creatine (all at 10 mM) were prepared and subjected to Bil-CEST imaging by using the same optimal scanning parameters (5 μT, 3 s). All in vitro experiments at 7 T were conducted on an Agilent MR system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). For phantom CEST scanning, the continuous wave echo planar imaging (CW-EPI) sequence was utilized, as previously described,47 with the following parameters: repetition time (TR), 6000 ms; echo time (TE), 26.83 ms; the number of dummy scans, 10; shots, 1; repetitions, 1; averages, 1; slice thickness, 2 mm; Kzero, 32; field of view, 40 mm × 40 mm; matrix size, 64 × 64; and 102 frequency offsets unevenly distributed from −10 to 10 ppm relative to water resonance. The total scanning duration was 13 min, 31 s.

To explore the dependence of the Bil-CEST signal on T1 under high field and high saturation power (5 μT, 3s) conditions, we added 0.05 mM (sample no. 1) and 0.075 mM (sample no. 2) of MnCl2 to the same concentration (6 mM) of bilirubin solution to change T1w. T1 mapping, T2 mapping, and Bil-CEST (5 μT, 3s) scans were performed on a 7T MRI scanner for both samples. T1w, T2w, and z-spectra were recorded separately for two bilirubin samples with different T1w.

Animal Experiments

Experimental Design

All experiments were conducted in a blinded manner, with the rats being coded and revealed only after completion of histological and behavioral analyses. Furthermore, these rats were randomly assigned to the experiments described subsequently. 36 adult SD rats were included.

Experiment design 1: MRI and histopathological examinations were performed to investigate brain tissue damage following the UCB treatment. A total of 24 rats were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 8/group): a phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) group and two bilirubin groups (4 and 6 mM). MRI was performed to monitor structural changes and Bil-CEST at various time points postmodeling. Two weeks after modeling, the rats’ brain tissues were collected for histopathological analysis following a second MRI scan.

Experiment design 2: To assess the effects of UCB treatment on neurological and cognitive functions in rats, the Morris water maze (MWM) test was conducted 28 days postmodeling by randomly dividing 12 rats into 2 groups: the 4 mM bilirubin and PBS groups (n = 6/group). No rats died.

Bilirubin Solution Preparation and Animal Model Construction

A bilirubin solution for injection into the rat’s lateral ventricle was prepared as follows: bilirubin was dissolved in a 0.5 M sodium hydroxide solution and diluted with 1× PBS to achieve a final concentration of 10 mmol/L. Fresh bilirubin solutions were prepared prior to each experiment.

A model of hyperbilirubinemia-induced brain injury was established by injecting bilirubin into the lateral ventricle, as described previously.37,38 Adult male rats were randomly assigned to either the bilirubin or PBS group and placed in a stereotaxic apparatus (SR-6 N, Japan) with a mask under maintenance anesthesia following induction. A midlongitudinal incision was made in the rats’ scalp to expose the occipital bone, and a dental microdrill created a hole at a point 1 mm posterior to and 1.5 mm to the right of bregma. A 10 μL microinjector was slowly inserted 4.2 mm deep into the lateral ventricle through the hole, followed by slow injection of 10 μL of either bilirubin solution or PBS at the appropriate concentration. After the microinjector was withdrawn, the bone hole was sealed with bone wax, and the scalp incision was closed with sterile sutures. The rats were subsequently transferred to an MRI scanner for imaging.

MRI Data Acquisition

First, baseline MRI was obtained. Next, after establishing the animal model (i.e., injecting the bilirubin solution), six additional MRI scans were performed at 30 min intervals, spanning a duration of 3 h. T1WI imaging parameters were as follows: TR, 800 ms; TE, 8.64 ms; segments/echo train length, 24/8; average, 3; slice thickness, 2.0 mm; intersection gap, 0.02 mm; Kzero, 1; field of view, 45 × 45 mm2; and imaging matrix size, 192 × 192. T2WI parameters were as follows: TR, 2000 ms; TE, 34 ms; segments/echo train length, 24/8; average, 3; slice thickness, 2.0 mm; intersection gap, 0.02 mm; Kzero, 4; field of view, 45 × 45 mm2; and imaging matrix size, 192 × 192. The sequence type and acquisition parameters for in vivo Bil-CEST imaging matched those of the phantom. A saturation power of 5 μT and a saturation time of 3 s were selected for all in vivo Bil-CEST scans.

TUNEL Staining

After 2 weeks of modeling, apoptotic cells in rat brains were identified by using the TUNEL assay. The rats were deeply anesthetized, followed by transcardial perfusion with saline and then 4% paraformaldehyde. The brains were carefully excised and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24–48 h. Brain tissue surrounding the pinhole region was processed and paraffin-embedded. Subsequently, 4 μm-thick paraffin coronal sections were cut from the brain tissue at the pinhole location and placed on slides to dry at 37 °C overnight. Apoptotic cells were stained following the manufacturer’s instructions for the TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (Servicebio), with apoptosis-positive neurons labeled with green fluorescence. Images were captured using a pathology slide scanner (3DHISTECH Pannoramic, model: Pannoramic MIDI). Apoptosis-positive neurons in the brains of rats from each group were then observed by using CaseViewer 2.3.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Nissl Staining

The preparation of rat brain tissue at this stage was identical to that for TUNEL staining. The slices were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with H&E for neuropathological analysis and Nissl solution for assessing neuronal apoptosis (all from Beyotime Biotech Inc., China). Histopathological alterations in the rat brain tissue were examined using an Axio Imager A2 light microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). For quantitative analysis, five random, nonoverlapping fields of view were captured at 400× magnification. Injured neurons were identified by their shrunken cell bodies, vacuolation, and deeply stained nuclei, in contrast to normal neurons.

Cognitive Function Testing

The MWM test was used to evaluate the spatial learning ability and memory capacity of rats 28 days postmodeling, as delineated in prior studies. The rats were placed in the experimental room 1 day before each test to acclimate to the environment and temperature. During the spatial learning phase, the platform remained submerged and stationary in the same quadrant for 5 consecutive days. The rats underwent training four times daily at 30 min intervals to locate the platform, guided by tank wall markings, with randomized launch points. Rats that failed to locate the platform within 60 s were directed to it and stayed atop for 20 s, thereby acquiring spatial memory of the platform’s location. A camera and video analysis system (Zhongshidichuang Science and Technology Development Co., Ltd., China) recorded and analyzed the rats’ swimming paths and escape latencies. 24 h after the final training, the platform was removed, and the rats swam freely for 60 s. The number of platform crossings and time spent in the safety quadrant were metrics used to assess the rats’ spatial memory capacity.

All CEST data processing and analysis were performed using MATLAB R2012b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) with custom-written scripts. Statistical Analyses

All data are presented as means ± standard deviations and were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA) with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. The Shapiro–Wilk test assessed data normality, whereas Bartlett’s test evaluated the homogeneity of variances. Between-group comparisons of MWM test results were conducted using an unpaired Student’s t-test. For histopathological staining data, an unpaired Student’s t-test with Welch’s correction was used. One-way analysis of variance, followed by the Benjamini–Hochberg method for false discovery rate correction,50 was used for multiple comparisons of MTRasym data from more than two groups. Pearson’s correlation test was used for correlation analyses. For all analyses, p < 0.05 (two-tailed) values were considered statistically significant.
