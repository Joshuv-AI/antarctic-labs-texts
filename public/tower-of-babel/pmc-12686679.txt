A modular, single-protein envelope for targeted gene delivery
Authors: Xiaozhe Ding
Published: 2025

Main text

Gene and cell therapies increasingly rely on targeted delivery: getting the right payload to the right cell while sparing everything else. Vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein (VSV-G) is the most used envelope protein in viral delivery platforms. However, one obstacle has been VSV-G’s broad affinity for the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) family members, ubiquitous receptors that can erode specificity and drive off-target toxicity.1 In this issue, Duquénois et al. report an elegant solution: engineering the VSV-G protein itself to accept an N-terminal nanobody while abolishing native LDLR binding, thereby consolidating receptor recognition and membrane fusion in a single, chimeric envelope protein.2 Through experimental evolution, they identify two generalizable backbone mutations that restore folding/trafficking and markedly improve pseudotyping when a nanobody is fused to VSV-G’s N-terminus. When paired with two previously known mutations to eliminate LDLR binding,3 these chimeric envelope proteins retarget both VSV and lentiviral vectors specifically to HER2-positive cells. The work converts the long-sought conceptual advance of one-protein, retargetable VSV envelopes, into a practical tool for the field.

VSV-G is the workhorse envelope for in vitro and ex vivo gene transfer and is a component of several oncolytic VSVs. Yet the ability to use broadly expressed LDLR family members for entry compromises the vectors’ in vivo specificity and can contribute to neurotoxicity or inadvertent sequestration by non-target tissues.1 Structure-guided protein engineering identified VSV-G mutations (K47Q or R354Q) that abolish LDLR binding while preserving low-pH fusion, resulting in a receptor-blind scaffold with no entry that can serve as a blank canvas for tissue targeting.3 Targeted entry can then be achieved by displaying a ligand, typically an antibody or antibody fragment that binds to tissue-enriched receptors (Figure 1). Prior strategies to retarget vectors typically followed a bipartite approach: retaining fusion-competent, receptor-blind VSV-G and co-displaying a second membrane protein that binds the desired receptor. These approaches can work, but they produce particles with heterogeneous stoichiometry of the two proteins, adding manufacturing complexity.4,5,7 A monopartite envelope that combines receptor binding and pH-triggered fusion in a single protein would simplify vector assembly and likely improve particle-to-infectious-unit ratios.2,6

From broad-tropism VSV-G to targeted entry: antibody-based retargeting strategies

Wild-type VSV-G binds to LDL receptor (LDLR) family members, yielding ubiquitous entry (left). Introducing K47Q or R354Q mutation abolishes LDLR binding while preserving low-pH fusion, resulting in a receptor-blind scaffold with no entry (center).3 Targeted entry can then be achieved in two ways (right): bipartite designs co-display a second membrane protein (e.g., an antibody or antibody fragment) alongside detargeted VSV-G4,5; monopartite designs incorporate the binding domain into the G protein itself,2,6 improving homogeneity and manufacturability of resulting particles, but the fusion protein often requires customized adaptation to identify mutations (red dots) that restore folding/trafficking.6 In this issue, Duquénois et al.2 report an optimized VSV-G backbone (H22N + hydrophobic 422, e.g., S422I) that tolerates N-terminal nanobody (VHH) insertion; combined with K47Q/R354Q, this yields HER2-dependent infection for both VSV and lentiviral vectors and modularly generalizes across multiple VHHs—thereby achieving targeted expression while maintaining a single-protein envelope.

Two facts make such monopartite envelopes feasible. First, structural work has decoupled VSV-G’s receptor-binding and fusion functions and mapped LDLR-critical residues (K47, R354).3,8 Second, nanobodies (VHHs) offer compact, stable binding domains that can be grafted into viral glycoproteins—if the backbone tolerates the insertion.9

The authors start with a deliberately neutral testing ground: an N-terminal fusion of a nanobody against mCherry (C11), inserted between the VSV-G signal peptide and ectodomain via a flexible linker. Engineering with this non-targeting chimera (GC11) avoids selection on a cellular receptor during backbone optimization. The initial chimera (GC11) exhibits reduced surface expression (∼50% of wild-type), less efficient incorporation into VSVΔG particles, and approximately 20-fold lower infectivity, underscoring the cost of N-terminal grafting. Experimental evolution of this chimera yields two adaptive mutations: S422I (by passage 4) and H22N (by passage 7). When reintroduced into the chimera, the double mutant (“optimized,” GC11-opt) restores surface expression (to ∼80% of wild-type) and boosts pseudotyping titers ∼7-fold versus the non-optimized chimera. Electron microscopy confirms that the chimera still executes the canonical low-pH structural transition, and the nanobody remains accessible in both pre- and post-fusion states.

Mechanistically, S422I mutation likely stabilizes the β-hairpin of the C-terminal domain through hydrophobic stacking; substituting any hydrophobic residue at 422 (I, L, V, F, or M) reproduces the optimization, consistent with the hypothesis. The contribution of H22N is less obvious structurally, but functionally, it works synergistically with S422I to improve the titer of the pseudotyped vector.

Crucially, the optimized backbone generalizes to other VHH insertions. In head-to-head tests across 11 distinct nanobodies—including eight anti-HER2 VHHs—the optimized VSV-G improved pseudotyping in 9 out of 11 cases, with a geometric mean ∼11-fold enhancement; two HER2-binding constructs reached wild-type-like titers. To create targeted envelopes, the authors introduced K47Q or R354Q mutations to ablate LDLR recognition and fused HER2-specific nanobodies (H1–H4). The resulting chimeras supported the infection of HER2-positive HEK293T cells, while losing activity in an ERBB2-knockout derivative (HEK293T-HER2KO), and they strongly outperformed LDLR-blind VSV-G alone. The same pattern was observed for lentiviral vectors and SKBR3 breast cancer cells, which express high levels of HER2. In mixed co-cultures of HER2+ and HER2– HEK293T cells, VSVΔG pseudotyped with the HER2-targeted chimeras preferentially infected the HER2+ subpopulation (selectivity index ∼2–4), demonstrating genuine receptor-dependent tropism in a competitive setting.

Compared to other conceptually similar envelope designs for achieving antibody-based retargeting (Figure 1), the design presented in this study offers several major advantages. First, a single-protein envelope eliminates the particle-to-particle compositional heterogeneity inherent to bipartite systems,4,5,7 thereby enhancing manufacturability and reproducibility. Second, the solution is modular: most nanobodies tested benefited from the optimized scaffold, opening a path to a menu of tissue-specific envelopes.9 Third, the same chimera works on both VSV and lentiviral particles, bridging oncolytic virotherapy and vector-based gene delivery within a single engineering framework.6

Three issues deserve attention for future development and application of this technology. (1) Specificity headroom. The selectivity indices in HER2+ and HER2– HEK293T co-cultures are modest (∼2–4), highlighting that rigorous in vivo head-to-head studies will be necessary to benchmark on-target efficiency and off-target sparing across organs, particularly organs with high LDLR expression (such as the liver) and Fc-receptor-rich compartments. (2) Receptor selection. Receptor-mediated delivery depends on the chosen receptor’s ability to internalize and traffic to acidic endosomes compatible with VSV-G fusion,8 but not all clinically appealing receptors share that feature, so receptor selection (and linker engineering to set nanobody reach) will be critical. (3) Immunogenicity and durability. Repeated or systemic dosing with chimeric envelopes may raise anti-nanobody or anti-VSV-G immunity; humanization of VHHs and de-immunization of G surfaces will likely be required, particularly for chronic indications.9

The most immediate applications are oncolytic VSVs6 and in vivo lentiviral delivery for solid-organ or hematologic indications, where LDLR-rich off-targets trap conventional VSV-G.5 Because the optimized backbone accommodates multiple nanobodies, multiplexed, or bispecific versions (e.g., HER2 and EGFR, or tumor antigen and stromal marker) could further increase selectivity and mitigate antigen escape.4,7 Pairing this envelope technology with appropriate cargo engineering, such as the use of logic-gated expression cassettes or microRNA-detargeting, could add further layers of control. Looking beyond HER2, the same design could be used to build cell-type-tuned envelopes for HSCs, T cells, or hepatocytes, addressing VSV-G’s known limitations on quiescent lymphocytes by bypassing LDLR dependence.10
