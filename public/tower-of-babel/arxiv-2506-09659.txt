Intent Factored Generation: Unleashing the Diversity in Your Language Model
Eltayeb Ahmed Uljad Berdica, Martha Elliott Danijela Horak Jakob N. Foerster

Abstract
Obtaining multiple meaningfully diverse, high quality samples from Large Language Models (LLMs) for a fixed prompt remains an open challenge. Current methods for increasing diversity often only operate at the token-level, paraphrasing the same response. This is problematic because it leads to poor exploration on reasoning problems and to unengaging, repetitive conversational agents. To address this we propose I ntent F actored G eneration ( IFG ), factorising the sampling process into two stages. First, we sample a semantically dense intent that anchors the sample, e.g., a summary or keywords. Second, we sample the final response conditioning on both the original prompt and the intent from the first stage. This factorisation allows us to use a higher temperature during the intent step to promote conceptual diversity, and a lower temperature during the final generation to ensure the outputs are coherent and self-consistent. Additionally, we find that prompting the model to explicitly state its intent for each step of the chain-of-thought before generating the step is beneficial for reasoning tasks. Empirically, we demonstrate that our simple method is highly effective across a diverse set of tasks. We show this method improves both pass@k and Reinforcement Learning from Verifier Feedback (RLVF) on maths and code tasks. For instruction-tuning, we combine IFG with Direct Preference Optimisation to increase conversational diversity without sacrificing reward. Finally, we achieve higher diversity while maintaining the quality of generations on a general language modelling task, using a new dataset of reader comments and news articles that we collect and open-source. In summary, we present a simple method of increasing the sample diversity of LLMs while maintaining performance. This method can be implemented by changing the prompt and varying the temperature during generation, making it easy to integrate into many algorithms for gains across various applications.



1
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are becoming more pervasive, with applications spanning from chatbots to programming agents
(
Schick et al. 2023
)
and creative writing aides
(
Toplyn 2023
;
Lee et al. 2022
)
. Although LLMs show considerable performance at many of these tasks, one clear limitation is the lack of meaningful diversity in their responses
(
Peeperkorn et al. 2024
)
.

There are many situations where generating a
semantically diverse

(
Kuhn et al. 2023
)
set of responses is desirable. For example, in verifiable reasoning domains such as maths and code, sampling a wide range of diverse candidate solutions increases the likelihood of finding a correct solution
(
Ehrlich et al. 2025
)
. Combining this with a reliable verifier at test-time can result in a strong problem-solving agent
(
Chervonyi et al. 2025
)
. Furthermore, semantic diversity is beneficial for exploration when doing Reinforcement Learning from Verifier Feedback (RLVF) to increase reasoning capabilities on tasks like maths and code
(
Havrilla et al. 2024
)
.

Raising the token selection temperature is a common practice that increases token-level diversity, but frequently fails to significantly increase semantic diversity without leading to a breakdown of coherence
(
Peeperkorn et al. 2024
)
.
Diverse Beam Search (DBS)
(
Vijayakumar et al. 2016
)
is an alternative, but it is slow due to its sequential nature. Similar to increasing temperature, DBS only forces an increase in token-level diversity, which does not always increase semantic diversity. Lastly, DBS is a deterministic decoding procedure that yields identical generations across seeds, further limiting its utility.
This lack of semantic diversity is more pronounced in instruction-tuned models
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
, including popular closed models served by high-end API providers.

:

This is in contrast to standard direct generation. We see that by raising the temperature during the intent phase, we obtain responses that are more semantically diverse. The generations lose coherence when we use similarly high temperatures for direct generation.

In this paper, we present
I
ntent
F
actored
G
eneration (
IFG
), a simple method that makes semantic diversity controllable, as depicted in Figure
1
. At its core, IFG separates the sampling process into two stages. In the first stage, we sample a short
intent
, such as keywords or a dense summary, that describes the sample to be generated. In the second stage, we sample the final
response
conditioned on this intent and the original prompt. Factorising sampling into these two stages allows us to independently control the temperature for the intent and the final response.
A high temperature for the intent increases semantic diversity, because the intent compactly represents the concepts in the response; changing even a single token in the intent can cause large semantic changes to the response. The use of a lower temperature for the final response keeps it coherent and self-consistent. Furthermore, we find that eliciting the model to output summaries of each intended step before outputting the step is beneficial for certain tasks, and we refer to this as IFG-prompting.
Similar to Chain-of-thought prompting
(
Wei et al. 2023
)
, IFG-prompting is most beneficial on complex reasoning tasks and can be combined with Chain-of-thought to achieve further gains.
We implement IFG at inference time with off-the-shelf LLMs through prompting. We also implement IFG by finetuning LLMs on datasets annotated with intents. This annotation is straightforward and can be automated with LLMs. We show that both IFG implementations lead to improvements in performance.

Our method is conceptually simple, and displays empirical gains on a wide range of tasks from maths and code, to chatbots and language modelling tasks out-of-the-box. Specifically, IFG results in better exploration for reasoning tasks, leading to significant improvements in pass@k on maths and coding problems on the MATH
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021a
)
and LiveCodeBench
(
Jain et al. 2024
)
benchmarks respectively. We show that IFG leads to better RLVF (RL from Verifier Feedback) performance on maths tasks. We also demonstrate that IFG increases diversity of responses without compromising on their quality. We do this by first defining a quantitative measure of semantic diversity,
R
elaxed
S
emantic
E
ntropy (
RSE
), which adapts Semantic Entropy
(
Kuhn et al. 2023
)
to longer and more complex sequences. Second, we collect a dataset of comments from
Reddit
and show that IFG pushes up the quality-diversity Pareto frontier for models finetuned on this data. Additionally, we study IFG in the instruction tuning setting using Direct Policy Optimisation (DPO). Here we also find an improvement in the quality-diversity Pareto frontier.

We open-source our code and provide a
diversifier wrapper
that adds IFG to any API-provided LLM.

To summarise, our contributions are:

1.

Intent Factored Generation:
A method to sample meaningfully diverse high quality responses from LLMs (
Section 4.1
).

2.

Relaxed Semantic Entropy:
An instantiation of the Semantic Entropy metric
(
Kuhn et al. 2023
)
modernised with a more lenient definition of equivalence that is robust to longer sequences (
Section 4.2
).

3.

Exploration for Reasoning:
We empirically demonstrate the utility of IFG in improving exploration. We show the benefits of this exploration both stand-alone and as a component of RL with Verifier Feedback (RLVF) (
Section 6.1
,
Section 6.2
).

4.

Diverse Aligned Models:
IFG applied to instruction tuning results in aligned models with higher semantic diversity (
Section 6.3
).

5.

Reddit News Comments:
Our open-source dataset of news articles from various websites and Reddit user comments on these articles. We use this dataset to show that IFG results in diverse comments at test time (
Section 6.4
).

Background

Causal Language Modelling

Given a corpora
\mathcal{D}
of natural language text documents
(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})
, language modelling is the process of learning an estimated distribution
p(x)
from the dataset. Since natural language is usually intended to be read sequentially, it is common to factorise
p
as follows:

\displaystyle p(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(s_{i}|s_{1},\cdots,s_{i-1})

(1)

where
s_{1},\cdots,s_{t}
are the individual tokens that make up the text document
x
. This factorisation is known as Causal Language Modelling
(
Bengio et al. 2000
)
. One of the benefits of this formulation is that it facilitates generating text sequentially, both from scratch or in continuation from a given prefix, known as a
prompt
. In recent years, the Causal Transformer architecture has been applied to Causal Language Modelling with great success
(
Radford et al. 2019
)
, and shows continually increasing performance with model scale.

Semantic Entropy

Given an LLM
M
, a prompt
p_{i}
and an induced distribution of responses
r\sim M(p_{i})
, the Semantic Entropy
\mathrm{SE}_{M}(p_{i})
measures the conceptual uncertainty of
M
given the prompt
p_{i}

(
Kuhn et al. 2023
)
. To compute
\mathrm{SE}_{M}(p_{i})
, we define a set of equivalence classes
c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
where
r_{j},r_{k}\in c_{i}
if and only if
r_{j}
and
r_{k}
bidirectionally entail each other, i.e., they are paraphrases of the exact same statement. We then compute entropy of the distribution over equivalence classes induced by the LLM. We provide a more detailed definition in Appendix
A
.

RL from Verifier Reward

Reinforcement Learning is a training paradigm where a model
M
interacts with an environment to learn a mapping from states
s
to actions
a
that maximises the reward function
R(s,a)

(
Sutton 2018
)
. With LLMs, we can model reasoning questions as states, the step-by-step solutions as actions and the accuracy of the final answers as the reward. This allows us to apply RL to improve the LLM’s ability to reason in domains such as maths and code
(
Havrilla et al. 2024
;
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
;
Kazemnejad et al. 2024
)
. In this work, we leverage the Self-Taught Reasoning algorithm (STaR)
(
Zelikman et al. 2022
)
that iteratively builds a dataset by sampling solution attempts from the model under training. This is equivalent to a policy gradient algorithm
(
Sutton 2018
)
with resets
(
Nikishin et al. 2022
)
. In this work we will use STaR to refer to the version of the algorithm in
Zelikman et al. 2022
which does not employ
post-rationalisation
.

Instruction Tuning

Pre-trained language models are usually not adept at following instructions or responding in a conversational manner. This can be remediated with a post-training phase called instruction-tuning
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
.
Although supervised fine-tuning leads to an improvement in instruction following, further gains can be obtained through RLHF
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
, or using off-policy equivalent algorithms such as Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
.

3
Related Work

Methods for Diverse Generation

Raising the temperature during the sequential sampling with an LLM is the most widely used method for increasing the output diversity. This is implemented by dividing the final logits by a constant
t
before applying the softmax function. As
t
approaches infinity, the entropy of the softmax distribution increases. This increases token-level diversity but often does not lead to higher semantic diversity
(
Peeperkorn et al. 2024
)
.
Similarly, Diverse Beam Search
(
Vijayakumar et al. 2016
)
penalises repeated tokens or n-grams across sequentially generated sequences and suffers the same limitation. Another drawback of DBS is the high generation latency resulting from it sequential nature.

Zhu et al. 2025
increase diversity by combining base and instruction-tuned models. Their methods requires loading both a base and an instruction-tuned model into memory, effectively doubling the GPU memory requirements. Fundamentally, this method is bounded by the diversity of the base model. Rainbow Teaming follows a different approach
(
Samvelyan et al. 2024
)
by forcing generations to fit into pre-determined categories using the MAP-Elites algorithm
(
Mouret and Clune 2015
)
. This algorithm is computationally expensive and relies on manually defining the categories and axes of variation for a given problem, whereas our method achieves significant semantic diversity requiring only a few-shot prompt and a minor change to the sampling implementation.

Structured Generations

Prior work such as Control Tokens
(
Keskar et al. 2019
)
incorporate intermediate representations by prepending control tokens to document sequences. The coarse-grained control tokens provide only high-level indicators of document type and are insufficient for inducing diverse generations within a single type.
Wang et al. 2024
introduce fine-grained planning tokens that can be used to guide and diversify responses to a given prompt. These planning tokens are not interpretable or grounded in language. Furthermore, these tokens need to be trained from scratch to represent general purpose language. This can be as expensive as pretraining the language model itself.
Other lines of work by
Tan et al. 2020
and
Yao et al. 2019
investigate generating sequences in two or more stages, starting with a scaffolding of keywords and then further filling in with subsequent stages. However, both of these methods train different language models for each stage and do not train a single consolidated model. We note that both of these methods operate in the same regime where planning is carried out to completion before generating the entire sequence, and do not investigate the utility of interspersing planning with generation, nor do they apply their methods to any reasoning problems.

4
Method

4.1
Intent Factored Generation

When sampling a response
r
to a prompt
p
from an LLM
M
the response can be described by an
intent
which describes the conceptual meaning of the response, and
phrasing
which describes the wording and formatting. As shown in Figure
2(a)
, when sampling from LLMs,
intent
and
phrasing
are latent variables, and although increasing temperature will increase the entropy of both, the increase tends to be dominated by changes in
phrasing

(
Peeperkorn et al. 2024
)
. Instead, our method Intent Factored Generation explicitly first samples the intent
i
, a “summary representation“ of the response, represented as keywords or a brief summary. We then sample the final response,
r
conditioning on both the prompt
p
and intent
i
. Some examples of IFG
intents
and
responses
are shown in Figure
5(b)
.
Applying IFG changes the sampling process from
\textbf{r}\sim M(\textbf{p})

to

\displaystyle\textbf{i}\sim M(\textbf{p})\Rightarrow\textbf{r}\sim M(\textbf{p},\textbf{i})

(2)

Algorithm 1

Intent Factored Generation

LLM
M
, prompt
P
,intent temperature
t_{i}
,response temperature
t_{r}

1:

\mathrm{Intent}\leftarrow M(P,t_{i})

High temp. for intent

2:

P\leftarrow P+\mathrm{Intent}

Append to prompt

3:

\mathrm{Response}\leftarrow M(P,t_{r})

Lower temp.

4:

return

\mathrm{Response}

This two-stage sampling process is illustrated in Figure
2(b)
. The goal of explicitly sampling the
intent

i
is to allow us to directly control its sampling temperature
t_{i}
. We then use a lower temperature
t_{r}
to sample the final response to maintain coherence. We show this increases semantic diversity of the final responses. We provide a description of the overall IFG sampling process in Algorithm
1
.

We note that to sample an
intent
from an LLM before sampling a
response
for a given prompt, we must prime the LLM to do so. This can be done by few-shot prompting the LLM and we refer to this as
Few-shot Prompted IFG
. Alternatively, if we have a dataset from a distribution we want to sample from, we can finetune the LLM to use IFG on the dataset. We refer to this as
Finetuned IFG
. To produce an IFG-compatible dataset, we use an LLM to annotate each sequence with an intent. This can be at the level of one intent per response, or at a more granular level with an intent for each segment of a longer response. The granular approach enables us to apply IFG sampling to each segment. Applying IFG at the segment level involves alternating temperatures between
t_{i}
and
t_{r}
as we alternate between sampling intents and responses. A more formal description of the procedure with several examples of intent annotation can be found in Appendices
B
and
I.2
respectively.

4.2
Relaxed Semantic Entropy

To estimate the diversity of a set of generations
\mathcal{G}
, we use an instantiation of Semantic Entropy
(
Kuhn et al. 2023
)
which we refer to as
R
elaxed
S
emantic
E
ntropy (
RSE
). We differ from
Kuhn et al. 2023
’s Semantic Entropy in two key ways: (1) We use a more flexible similarity criteria using an LLM and (2) We adopt a clustering method that handles the possible intransitivity of the LLM-defined similarity metric we use.

For (1), instead of using bidirectional entailment as our pairwise metric for similarity, we ask a prompted LLM to judge similarity instead:
\mathrm{sim}(\mathcal{G}[i],\mathcal{G}[j])\in\{0,1\}
. Through prompting we have per-task flexibility in defining what constitutes similarity between responses.

For (2), we use connected components to assign clusters. For a set of generations
\mathcal{G}
, we construct an adjacency matrix
A
where
A[i,j]=\mathrm{sim}(\mathcal{G}[i],\mathcal{G}[j])
. We then take each connected component of the graph defined by
A
to be a cluster. Further implementation details can be found in Appendix
A.2
.

5
Experimental setup

5.1
Mathematical Reasoning

To investigate the efficacy of IFG in finding solutions to mathematical problems, we conduct experiments on the MATH dataset of maths questions
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021a
)
, using the
Qwen-2.5

(
Yang et al. 2024
)
family of models. We few-shot prompt the model to solve the questions with traditional Chain-of-Thought reasoning
(
Wei et al. 2023
)
as a baseline. We compare against
Few-shot Prompted IFG
, with the unit of IFG granularity being each reasoning step. Prompts are listed in Appendix
I
. To evaluate the efficacy of IFG at exploration, we evaluate test “pass@k” for various values of
k
. We then investigate if improved exploration demonstrated by a high “pass@k” can be translated to higher “pass@1” accuracy through RL from Verifier Feedback. To do this, we use STaR to train our model on the train split of MATH. We compare baseline models trained with STaR to models trained with STaR+IFG. To investigate the effect of scale on our method we run STaR experiments on models sizes ranging from 3B to 14B parameters.

For all experiments, we tune temperatures—
t_{i},t_{r}
for IFG, or temperature
t
for the baseline—with random search to maximise pass@8, as we do STaR without 8 rollouts per problem. We conduct this hyperparameter tuning on a subset of the training dataset, with a budget of 10 runs.

5.2
LiveCodeBench

We evaluate the utility of IFG for coding tasks on the LiveCodeBench benchmark
(
Jain et al. 2024
)
—a benchmark of algorithmic competitive programming problems—using the model
Qwen-2.5-Coder-32B

(
Hui et al. 2024
)
. We compare the “pass@k” metric for different values of
k
. For the baseline, we evaluate using the standard prompts from the LiveCodeBench codebase. We then modify the prompts to use
Few-shot Prompted IFG
. To do so we add
comments
to the code to contain the intents; we use one intent for every 1–-3 closely related lines of code. Prompts can be found in Appendix
I
. For both the baseline and our method, we tune the sampling temperatures
t_{i},t_{r}
or
t
, on a separate validation split of the dataset, optimising for “pass@10” using random search with a budget of 10 runs.
Both the validation set used for tuning and the test set we report the final results on are explicitly
limited to problems from competitions that occurred after the release of the
Qwen-2.5-Coder-32B
model.

5.3
Instruction-Tuning with DPO

The collapse of diversity of LLMs after instruction-tuning is a widely observed phenomena
(
Zhu et al. 2025
)
. Hence, we combine IFG with Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
to train instruction-tuned models with higher diversity while maintaining alignment with the preference dataset. We conduct these experiments on
Qwen-2.5-7B
using the Helpful and Harmless Golden dataset
(
Cai et al. 2024
)
that contains pair-wise examples of preferred and rejected conversational responses. We compare against vanilla DPO as a baseline. For DPO+IFG, we do
Finetuned IFG
, using
Llama3.1-8B-Instruct
to annotate every assistant turn in the original dataset with keyword intents. Annotation prompt and examples of keyword intents are in Appendix
I
.

Both models are trained by first performing SFT on the chosen responses from the dataset for one epoch. This is then followed by DPO instruction-tuning on the full pair-wise preference dataset.

To find the Pareto frontier of quality vs diversity, we collect samples from both models across different sampling temperatures
t
for the DPO model and
t_{i},t_{r}
for DPO+IFG model. To measure diversity we use Relaxed Semantic Entropy (RSE). To evaluate quality, we train a Bradley-Terry reward model
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
on the same HH-Golden dataset.

Additionally, we use the Perspective API
(
Lee et al. 2022
)
to measure undesirable traits of model responses such as toxicity, profanity, incoherence, etc. and we examine how they trade off with diversity for the DPO model vs the DPO+IFG model. More details on the API in Appendix
C
.

5.4
Diverse Comment Generation

We curate a dataset of news articles and reader comments from
Reddit
. We gather over
556
k comments on
14
k news articles. We collected this data from the
r/news
subreddit. Further details on the composition of the dataset and the preparation procedure are described in Appendix
D.2
. As part this work’s contribution, we open source this dataset for future use by the research community.

We proceed to finetune models on this dataset annotated with IFG intents, and refer to this as
Finetuned IFG
. We use keywords that summarise the comment as intents. We produce these keywords using a few-shot prompted
Llama3-8B-Instruct
model, similar to
Section 5.3
.
As a baseline, we finetune on the dataset without IFG intent annotation, and refer to it as
Direct
sampling.

We evaluate the direct model using both plain sampling and Diverse Beam Search (DBS)
(
Vijayakumar et al. 2016
)
, a test-time method for increasing diversity through similarity penalties among deterministic parallel generations. We measure how these models trade off quality with diversity as we vary temperature
t
for the direct model,
t_{i}
and
t_{r}
for the IFG model, and the hyperparameters of DBS as defined in
Vijayakumar et al. 2016
. To measure diversity we use RSE and for quality we use a
\mathrm{coherence}
metric, which is computed as
1-\mathrm{incoherence}
. We obtain
\mathrm{incoherence}
through the Perspective API
(
Lee et al. 2022
)
.

LiveCodeBench

7
Discussion

The results described in Section
6
show that IFG sampling leads to stronger performance, or higher diversity for a given level of performance across mathematical reasoning, code generation, instruction following, and comment generation tasks. There are two differences between IFG sampling and standard sampling from LLMs. The first difference is that we elicit the LLM to generate a dense intent defining the gist of the response up-front. The second difference is that we vary the temperature during generation, using separate temperatures
t_{i}
for intents and
t_{r}
for the final responses.

In Appendix
G
, we conduct an ablation to determine the effect of each change individually.
In the ablation we constrain
t_{i}=t_{r}
when tuning temperature and we refer to this as “IFG - Equal”. For code generation, instruction following and comment generation, we find that IFG outperforms
IFG-Equal
. On code generation, we see that
IFG-Equal
outperforms the baseline but underperforms IFG for pass@5 and pass@10. This confirms the benefit of the two temperature approach.

On mathematical reasoning, Figure
15
shows that
IFG - Equal
matches IFG at pass@k when sampling from a base
Qwen-2.5-3B
, with both outperforming the non-IFG baseline. This is corroborated by Figure
3
(b) where IFG outperforms the baseline at pass@1 when using greedy-decoding (
t_{i}=t_{r}=t=0
), demonstrating that IFG-prompting improves performance on given tasks.

Qualitatively comparing the greedy decoding IFG generations to the baseline generations on MATH, we see that IFG sampling encourages the model to take more fine-grained steps when trying to solve maths problems. This can be seen in Appendix
J.1
, particularly samples
4
,
5
and
9
.
Although the substance of the solutions for both the vanilla and IFG prompts is identical, in IFG-prompting we explicitly delineate each step of the solution by annotating it with an intent. This encourages the model to use clear granular steps in its solution.

In aggregate, these findings indicate that both components of our method synergise to improve the quality-diversity trade-off for LLM generations.

8
Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that Intent Factored Generation increases sample diversity while preserving quality. We verify this method’s effectiveness on a wide range of tasks such as maths and code reasoning, as well as instruction-tuning and language modelling. We show that IFG increases the probability of finding a correct answer on reasoning tasks, and we use this to improve the model’s reasoning ability with Reinforcement Learning from Verifier Feedback (RLVF).

A notable limitation of our method is that when applied to reasoning, it is only useful when a reliable verifier exists. If the verifier is noisy then it is not possible to accurately identify the correct solution and our method can lead to reward hacking. We tried to replicate our STaR experiments on the MMLU dataset
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021b
)
; however, due to MMLU’s multiple choice nature, random answers have a 25% chance of being correct. This led to us not being able to achieve performance gains with STaR, neither with vanilla STaR nor with STaR+IFG.

In conclusion, we present IFG, our simple method for semantically diversifying LLMs by sampling higher temperature intents that summarise responses before sampling them. We empirically demonstrate that out-of-the-box IFG significantly improves performances across many different use-cases, spanning from reasoning tasks such as maths and code to conversational chatbots. We hope this robust performance, paired with the ease of implementation and the low computational overhead, leads to future endeavours applying IFG to algorithms or methods that benefit from diversity such as other RLHF and RLVF variants, language-based tree search and evolutionary algorithms.

For a given set of generations

we want to define a metric that measures semantic diversity. Semantic Entropy
(
Kuhn et al. 2023

which does so, uses bidirectional entailment as the equivalence notion that determines if generations are semantically similar. As this is a strict equivalence relationship Semantic Entropy will deem as semantically distinct two generations that are largely similar with minor differences. For longer generations, this means that even most similar generations would be marked as distinct. In our proposed metric Relaxed Semantic Entropy (RSE) we relax bidirectional entailment to bidirectional similarity, with the similarity of two generations being judged by a few-shot prompted LLM. By tuning the prompt of this LLM we can adjust the equivalence notion to suit the task at hand. n

c

However, using this estimate has the advantage of not depending on the probabilities of the generations, and can be used when they are not available, such as for data collected from humans. D.3
Impact Statement

Although our method IFG is generally applicable, we demonstrate its performance on the task of generating synthetic comments in response to news articles. It is important that these comments are not taken to represent the views of any communities or demographic slices, even if the generated comments purport to do so, as they might not be accurate. Furthermore, models trained to mimic user comments could be used to generate fake comments that could be automatically posted to websites and forums, with nefarious goals such as manipulating public opinion or scamming. Although our method enables generating comments with greater diversity, it is our view that it does not fundamentally increase the risk of fake user engagement generation, given the already high capability of SoTA language models at doing so.

Furthermore, we collected data from Reddit. Data from reddit is already publicly available and is included in machine learning datasets such as Common Crawl
(
Smith et al. 2013

E.3
Self-BLEU and Perplexity

Self-BLEU
We consider the metric as defined by
Zhu et al. 2018
as an alternative to RSE in measuring the diversity of a group of responses. For every group of
k
parallel responses corresponding to a single prompt, we calculate the mean of every single response’s BLEU score with the rest of
k-1
responses serving as reference. By definition, identical sentences get a Self-BLEU score of 1 and completely different ones get a score of 0, i.e. a very diverse set of responses has a low Self-BLEU score. We plot 1 - self-BLEU for the Pareto fronts so that the higher values of the plotted metric correspond to higher diversity, similar to our own RSE metric. We use the Natural Language Toolkit package (
nltk
) for the BLEU score calculations. We use the same tokenizer as the base model we trained, namely
Qwen2.5-7B
, to get the n-grams required for the BLEU score. We did not do word-level separation since in many cases the high-temperature samples feature decoded tokens that are not part of the natural language and are not always separated by spaces (see
Appendix J
).

Perplexity
We consider perplexity as an alternative metric for quality. Intuitively, low-quality and incoherent responses are more likely to have a higher perplexity score than coherent ones. This means that low perplexity corresponds to high-quality. In order to maintain the same preference polarity with regards to metrics and Pareto frontiers, we plot the negative log of the mean perplexity for a temperature profile. To have an unbiased calculation of perplexity, we used a
different
base model than the one we trained, namely
Llama3.1-8B
. We explore another quality metric alternative in
Section E.4
, by using the win rate at which a SOTA LLM chooses our method’s responses over the baseline, commonly referred to as win rate.
