Prenatal ultrasound diagnosis of sirenomelia: A case report
Authors: Sana Ghades, Chaima Ouhibi, Mohamed Ridha Fatnassi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSirenomelia is an extremely rare and almost invariably lethal congenital malformation characterized by variable fusion of the lower limbs and severe associated visceral anomalies. We report a case prenatally diagnosed at 20 weeks of gestation in a 25-year-old nondiabetic, nonconsanguineous woman referred for suspected polymalformative fetal syndrome. Prenatal ultrasound demonstrated severe oligohydramnios, bilateral small hyperechoic kidneys suggestive of renal dysplasia, fused lower limbs, absent external genitalia, and a single umbilical artery, findings strongly supporting the diagnosis of sirenomelia. A suspected conotruncal cardiac anomaly was noted, though limited by the severity of oligohydramnios. Postnatal examination confirmed the prenatal findings; fetal karyotype was normal. Medical termination of pregnancy was performed following multidisciplinary counseling. This case highlights the central role of prenatal ultrasound in the early recognition of sirenomelia, and the importance of careful radiologic description, cautious classification, and multidisciplinary counseling in the management of this rare lethal condition.Keywords: Sirenomelia, Mermaid syndrome, Prenatal diagnosis, Oligohydramnios, Renal dysplasia, Caudal dysgenesis

Introduction

Sirenomelia, also known as mermaid syndrome, is an uncommon congenital malformation that is usually incompatible with life, defined by different degrees of fusion involving the lower extremities and severe associated visceral anomalies, particularly renal and genitourinary defects [1]. Early descriptions of sirenomelia date back to the 16th century, notably those of Rocheus in 1542 and Palfyn in 1553. The estimated prevalence is approximately 1 in 100,000 live births [1]. Due to severe renal agenesis or dysgenesis, which results in oligohydramnios and subsequent pulmonary hypoplasia, the prognosis is usually unfavorable

Prenatal ultrasound is essential for early diagnosis, especially when lower limb fusion is associated with severe oligohydramnios, renal abnormalities, abnormal or absent external genitalia, anorectal malformation, and a single umbilical artery [2]. First-trimester ultrasound can be particularly valuable for early recognition of lower limb fusion, renal anomalies, and abnormal umbilical vascularization. In the second trimester, severe oligohydramnios may limit sonographic visualization making prenatal diagnosis challenging.

The etiopathogenesis of sirenomelia remains debated. Several mechanisms have been proposed, including the vascular steal phenomenon, abnormalities of caudal blastogenesis, maternal diabetes, environmental exposures, and possible genetic susceptibility [3].

We describe a Tunisian case of sirenomelia diagnosed prenatally at 20 weeks of gestation in a 25-year-old woman with no known diabetes or consanguinity. We discuss its clinical, ultrasound, and postnatal features in the context of the current literature.

Case presentation

A 25-year-old pregnant woman was referred to our department at 20 weeks of gestation for detailed evaluation of suspected multiple fetal anomalies. Her medical history was unremarkable, with no known diabetes and there was no consanguinity with her 31-year-old partner. No family history of diabetes, congenital anomalies, or genetic disorders was reported.

A detailed specialist-performed ultrasound examination at 20 weeks of gestation revealed a viable singleton pregnancy with fetal biometry consistent with gestational age. Severe oligohydramnios was directly visualized and significantly limited the overall anatomical assessment. Lower limb fusion was directly identified, with visualization of a single femur-like long bone (Fig. 1A). and partial visualization of a tibial segment (Fig. 1B), suggestive of a severe form of sirenomelia. Both kidneys appeared small and hyperechoic, with loss of corticomedullary differentiation, suggestive of bilateral renal dysplasia (Fig. 1D). The external genitalia were not visualized. A single umbilical artery was suspected prenatally and confirmed postnatally. Cardiac assessment showed a suspected ventricular septal defect (Fig. 1C). However, evaluation of the outflow tracts was limited by fetal position and severe oligohydramnios, and no definitive conotruncal anomaly could be established.

Prenatal ultrasound findings in a fetus with suspected sirenomelia. (A and B) Ultrasound images showing fusion of the lower limbs with visualization of a single femur-like long bone (white arrow) and partial visualization of a tibial segment, suggestive of a severe form of sirenomelia (blue arrow). (C) Fetal cardiac ultrasound showing a suspected discontinuity of the interventricular septum, suggestive of a ventricular septal defect (arrow). (D) Prenatal abdominal ultrasound showing a small hyperechogenic fetal kidney with abnormal parenchymal appearance, suggestive of renal dysplasia (triangles).

Amniocentesis was performed and showed a normal fetal karyotype. Given the severity of the malformations and the unfavorable prognosis, the parents consented to medical termination of pregnancy, resulting in the delivery of a stillborn fetus weighing 350 g.

Postnatal examination showed a single umbilical artery, absence of external genitalia and an imperforate anus. The lower limbs were fused from the pelvic region to the knee (Fig. 2). Based on prenatal ultrasound and postnatal palpation, precise skeletal typing could not be established with certainty in the absence of radiographic confirmation. The findings were most suggestive of a severe form of sirenomelia, possibly Stocker type VI or VII, although definitive skeletal classification was not possible without radiographic confirmation.

Postnatal macroscopic examination. (A) Lateral view of the fetus showing fusion of the lower limbs (circle), consistent with sirenomelia. (B) Anterior view showing proximal fusion of the lower limbs extending from the pelvic region to the knees (circle). (C) Close-up view showing fused lower limbs forming a single tapered distal extremity (arrow).

Additional investigations, including radiographic evaluation of the lower limbs in the stillborn fetus and maternal laboratory testing for heavy metals and blood glucose, could not be performed because parental consent was not obtained.

Discussion

Sirenomelia, also known as mermaid syndrome, is an uncommon congenital malformation that is usually incompatible with life, defined by different degrees of fusion involving the lower extremities. It belongs to the spectrum of caudal dysgenesis and is often accompanied by severe genitourinary, gastrointestinal, skeletal, cardiovascular, and vascular anomalies [4]. The literature reports a male predominance (ratio 2.7:1) and a more frequent association with monozygotic twin pregnancies [[5], [6], [7]]. The diagnosis is usually established based on the presence of lower-limb fusion associated with severe renal anomalies, oligohydramnios, absent or abnormal external genitalia, anorectal malformation, and a single umbilical artery.

In 1987, Stocker and Heifetz proposed a 7-type classification of sirenomelia based on the number, arrangement, and degree of fusion of the long bones in the lower limbs [8]. Type I presents 2 separate femora, tibiae, and fibulae; Type II shows fused femora with separate tibiae and fibulae; Type III includes fused femora and tibiae with separate fibulae; Type IV shows fused femora, tibiae, and fibulae; Type V shows absent femora with fused tibiae and separate fibulae; Type VI shows absent femora with fused tibiae and fibulae; and Type VII represents the most severe form, with absent femora and tibiae and fused fibulae. This classification has prognostic and descriptive value, though radiographic confirmation is required for accurate typing [8,9].

In the present case, ultrasound examination at 20 weeks of gestation revealed severe oligohydramnios, bilateral renal abnormalities, a suspected cardiac defect, and major lower limb malformations. Postnatal examination confirmed the diagnosis by showing fused lower limbs extending from the pelvis to the knee, absence of external genitalia, imperforate anus, and a single umbilical artery.

Prenatal diagnosis can be difficult when severe oligohydramnios limits fetal visualization, particularly in the second trimester. Advances in prenatal ultrasonography have improved the early detection of sirenomelia and other severe congenital malformations. High-resolution 2-dimensional ultrasound enables detailed fetal anatomical assessment from the first trimester, particularly for lower limb fusion, renal anomalies, oligohydramnios, and abnormal umbilical vascularization [10,11]. Early diagnosis enables timely parental counseling, comprehensive evaluation of associated anomalies, and appropriate use of additional diagnostic investigations when indicated. Doppler assessment of the umbilical vessels and fetal abdominal vasculature may also be useful, as a single umbilical artery and abnormal vascular pattern are frequently reported [2,3,12]. Fetal MRI can be considered as an adjunct to ultrasound in selected cases, particularly when severe oligohydramnios limits sonographic visualization. It may help better delineate associated visceral, spinal, and lower limb anomalies, although prenatal ultrasound remains the primary diagnostic tool for sirenomelia [13].

The etiopathogenesis of sirenomelia remains controversial. Several mechanisms have been proposed, including maternal diabetes, environmental factors, abnormal caudal blastogenesis, vascular steal phenomenon, and genetic susceptibility [7,14].

Maternal diabetes has historically been considered a potential risk factor, particularly because caudal developmental defects are more frequent in diabetic pregnancies (10%-15% of affected children are born to diabetic mothers) [1]. However, maternal diabetes has been reported in only 0.5%-3.7% of sirenomelia cases, indicating that it cannot fully explain the pathogenesis of this malformation suggesting that diabetes alone cannot explain the condition [7,14].

In our case, maternal diabetes was not known, and there was no personal or family history suggestive of diabetes.

The vascular steal hypothesis remains one of the most widely accepted hypotheses. According to this theory, an abnormal large vitelline artery diverts blood flow from the caudal part of the embryo toward the placenta, resulting in hypoperfusion of the caudal mesoderm [9,13]. This mechanism may explain the association of lower limb fusion, renal agenesis or dysgenesis, anorectal malformations, genital anomalies, and single umbilical artery. However, this theory does not fully explain all associated anomalies, particularly craniofacial and cardiac defects [4].

Another hypothesis is the defective blastogenesis theory. According to this concept, sirenomelia results from an early developmental disturbance occurring during the third week of embryogenesis, at the end of gastrulation. This early defect may affect multiple caudal structures simultaneously, including the lower limbs, kidneys, genital tract, anorectal region, and vascular system. Other teratogenic factors such as vitamin A, cadmium, and lead have been associated with sirenomelia in animal and human models [8].

Current understanding suggests that sirenomelia may result from an interaction between vascular abnormalities, early embryonic developmental defects, environmental exposures, and genetic factors [1,8,13].

Sirenomelia is commonly associated with severe renal malformations, including bilateral renal agenesis, renal dysplasia, or hypoplastic kidneys. Renal and urinary tract anomalies frequently impair fetal urine excretion, resulting in severe oligohydramnios. This critical lack of amniotic fluid causes prolonged compression on the fetal chest and prevents essential fluid from expanding the airways, directly leading to pulmonary hypoplasia, one of the main causes of neonatal death [2,3,12].

In the present case, prenatal ultrasound demonstrated bilateral small, hyperechoic kidneys with loss of corticomedullary differentiation, severe oligohydramnios, complete fusion of the lower limbs, imperforate anus, absent external genitalia, and a single umbilical artery. These findings were highly consistent with sirenomelia.

Caudal regression syndrome (CRS) is the main differential diagnosis, as both conditions share caudal developmental defects. However, several features in our case allowed distinction from CRS: complete fusion of the lower limbs into a single structure (whereas lower limbs in CRS are typically hypoplastic but remain separate), the presence of a single umbilical artery (highly characteristic of sirenomelia), and severe bilateral renal dysgenesis/agenesis with consequent oligohydramnios. In contrast, renal involvement in CRS is usually less severe, and lower limb fusion is absent or minimal [13].

Other differential diagnoses included severe limb-body wall complex and VACTERL association. Limb-body wall complex was excluded due to the absence of abdominal wall defects, thoraco-abdominal involvement, and the presence of a normally inserted umbilical cord. VACTERL association was considered because of the cardiac and anorectal anomalies; however, the complete lower limb fusion and single umbilical artery are not typical features of VACTERL and strongly favored sirenomelia.

Postnatal clinical examination, skeletal radiography, and fetopathological assessment, when available, remain essential to confirm the diagnosis and differentiate sirenomelia from other caudal dysgenesis syndromes.

The prognosis of sirenomelia is extremely poor. Most affected fetuses die in utero or shortly after birth. Survival is primarily determined by the degree of renal function preservation, as renal agenesis or severe dysplasia leads to oligohydramnios and secondary pulmonary hypoplasia. Fewer than 1% of affected neonates survive beyond the first week of life. Long-term survival is exceptional and depends on the presence of functional renal tissue and the feasibility of complex surgical management [1].

Once the diagnosis is suspected, multidisciplinary counseling is essential. Parents should be informed about the severity of the condition, the high risk of fetal or neonatal death, the associated malformations, and the available management options. Depending on gestational age, local legislation, and parental wishes, management may include continuation of pregnancy with neonatal palliative care or medical termination of pregnancy [15,16].

In our case, because of the severity of the malformations and the poor prognosis, medical termination of pregnancy was proposed and accepted by the parents. Unfortunately, additional investigations, including fetal radiography, fetopathological examination, and maternal laboratory testing, could not be performed because parental consent was not obtained. This limited the anatomical and etiological assessment.

Clinical significance and contribution

This case contributes to the limited North African literature on sirenomelia by documenting a Tunisian case diagnosed after late referral at 20 weeks of gestation. Sirenomelia may be suspected during the first trimester and the absence of early prenatal follow-up delayed diagnosis. This observation emphasizes the public health importance of early antenatal screening and timely referral for fetal anomaly assessment. In addition, the associated cardiac anomaly in our case is notable, as such findings are not consistently reported in sirenomelia and suggest that the pathogenesis may involve more than the vascular steal phenomenon alone, supporting the role of an early blastogenesis defect. Finally, the parental refusal of additional investigations highlights the importance of culturally sensitive counseling in contexts where severe congenital malformations may be associated with misconceptions and stigma.

Limitations

However, this case has several limitations. First, the absence of fetal radiography prevented precise classification of the skeletal anomalies and the type of lower limb fusion. Second, fetopathological examination was not conducted as parental consent was not obtained. Third, maternal testing for glucose metabolism and heavy metals exposure could not be completed. Finally, genetic testing was unavailable, which limited etiological assessment and recurrence-risk counseling.

Although these limitations were present, the association of severe oligohydramnios, renal anomalies, lower limb fusion, imperforate anus, absent external genitalia, and single umbilical artery was strongly suggestive of sirenomelia.

Conclusion

Sirenomelia, or mermaid syndrome, is an almost invariably lethal congenital malformation characterized by variable fusion of the lower extremities and severe associated visceral anomalies, particularly renal and genitourinary defects. This case illustrates the crucial role of detailed prenatal ultrasound in the early recognition of sirenomelia, particularly when severe oligohydramnios is accompanied by renal abnormalities, abnormal or absent external genitalia, anorectal malformation, and a single umbilical artery. Early detection permits appropriate multidisciplinary counseling and informed parental choice.

While the absence of additional postnatal and maternal investigations limited the etiological assessment, the prenatal and postnatal findings were concordant with a definitive diagnosis of sirenomelia. The present case further revealed the importance of addressing cultural misconceptions surrounding severe congenital malformations in the Tunisian context, and genetic counseling should be considered for couples following such a diagnosis.
