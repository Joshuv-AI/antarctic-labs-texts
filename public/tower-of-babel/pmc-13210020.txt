Prediction of polycystic ovary syndrome using machine learning models: Addressing class imbalance and high dimensionality
Authors: Sachin Acharya, Satyanarayana Poojari, Asha Kamath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a hormonal disorder that affects fertility and long-term health issues such as metabolic syndrome. Class imbalance is prevalent in PCOS datasets and leads to bias toward the majority class, resulting in poor identification of the minority class. This study evaluated the impact of varying levels of class imbalance and high dimensionality on the performance of various machine learning (ML) models through a simulation study.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This study involved an imbalanced PCOS dataset consisting of 41 clinical and physical parameters of 541 patients gathered from 10 hospitals across Kerala, India. This study evaluated the performance of ML models, namely k-nearest neighbor, decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), support vector machine, and XGBoost, under varying imbalance ratios (IRs). Stratified resampling was used to generate multiple imbalanced scenarios. Model performance was assessed using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1 score.RESULTS:For IRs up to 3, RF outperformed other methods, based on performance measures. For IR >3, XGBoost emerged as the top-performing method, followed by DT. In the case of IR exceeding 5, the accuracy of the minority class fell below 50%. Based on simulation results, RF was used to identify significant variables.CONCLUSION:For original data (IR = 2.03), the RF model is a better model for predicting PCOS, with a sensitivity of 94% and a specificity of 81%. This study contributes to making informed decisions on selecting the most appropriate classification method based on a specific proportion of imbalance in healthcare datasets.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common hormonal disorder that mainly affects individuals with ovaries, typically during their reproductive years. PCOS is one of the most common endocrine disorders among women of reproductive age and is characterized by hormonal imbalances, particularly an excess of androgens (male hormones like testosterone) and insulin resistance. These hormonal imbalances often result in symptoms such as irregular menstrual cycles, ovulatory dysfunction, and increased levels of androgens, which can lead to symptoms such as acne, excessive hair growth, and male-pattern baldness. If not treated well, PCOS can lead to fertility challenges and long-term health problems such as metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease.[1] Effective management of PCOS includes hormonal contraceptives, anti-androgen medications, insulin-sensitizing drugs, and lifestyle modifications. In India, the prevalence of PCOS ranges between 2% and 35%, reflecting its widespread occurrence.[2] Factors such as age, weight, lifestyle habits, and hormonal imbalances play a critical role in identifying and diagnosing PCOS. Early detection and management are crucial to mitigate long-term complications of the syndrome, including infertility, metabolic issues, and cardiovascular risks.

Classification using machine learning (ML) has demonstrated significant potential in healthcare, particularly for tasks such as disease diagnosis, survival prediction in critical care, and identifying stages of illnesses. These models help in decision-making processes by providing accurate and timely predictions. Data characteristics, complexity, and the heterogeneous nature of health-related data pose significant challenges to the effective application of ML models in the healthcare sector.[345] Recent studies highlight the potential of ML models to accurately diagnose diseases such as cardiovascular disease,[6] breast cancer,[7] diabetic retinopathy,[8] and sepsis,[9] as well as to predict the success of infertility treatment.[10] These advancements contribute to early intervention, personalized medicine, and improved patient care.

Datasets related to PCOS often exhibit class imbalance, where positive and negative cases are unequally distributed. This is a common issue in healthcare datasets, as rare diseases or uncommon medical conditions tend to occur less frequently than more common ones. As a result, ML models are typically biased toward the majority class, leading to reduced predictive accuracy for the minority class. Addressing class imbalance and selecting appropriate classification models is essential to improve predictive accuracy and support effective decision-making in clinical practice.

Several techniques have been developed to address class imbalance in classification tasks. This includes balancing techniques such as random undersampling (RUS), which removes majority class observations, and random oversampling (ROS), which duplicates minority observations to balance the dataset. Other oversampling techniques include the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) and ADAptive SYNthetic (ADASYN), which generate synthetic data based on the nearest neighbor approach.[11] Rezvani and Wang[12] tackled the broader challenge of imbalanced data learning, highlighting open research questions and potential future directions. Bajer et al.[13] provided an in-depth analysis of SMOTE, discussing its progress and challenges. Pandey et al.[14] focused on addressing the class imbalance in heart disease datasets by using balancing techniques such as ROS, RUS, SMOTE, and cost-sensitive learning and observed that ROS performed better than other methods. Salmi et al.[15] conducted a comprehensive review of imbalanced learning techniques within healthcare data analysis. Yang et al.[16] evaluated ROS and RUS on prediction models in large observational health databases, advising against their use due to class imbalance issues. Avasthi et al.,[17] Panda et al.,[18] Jaiswal et al.,[19] and Ahmad et al.[20] employed balancing techniques such as SMOTE and ADASYN to handle the PCOS dataset.

Most of the balancing techniques generate synthetic data, which may not accurately reflect the real-world characteristics of the minority class. While this can enhance model accuracy, it may lack clinical relevance and lead to overfitting or loss of crucial information. Given the prevalence of imbalanced class distributions in real-world healthcare scenarios and the limitations of balancing techniques, it is critical to evaluate the effectiveness of classification models without artificially balancing the PCOS dataset. Identifying significant risk factors for PCOS is crucial for effective prevention and improved health outcomes. However, this process is influenced by factors such as the choice of model for selection, class imbalance, and the dimensionality of the dataset. In high-dimensional data, classical logistic regression models are prone to overfitting, making methods such as the Wald test or likelihood ratio (LR) test potentially unreliable for variable selection. To improve the identification of significant variables, it is essential to choose an appropriate model while considering class imbalance and data dimensionality. Balancing model performance with effective variable selection is crucial for understanding the risk factors for PCOS and enhancing healthcare outcomes.

The novelty of the study lies in the simulation-based evaluation of the impact of varying levels of class imbalance and high dimensionality on the performance of various ML models. Using a stratified sampling technique, different class imbalance scenarios covering moderate-to-severe imbalance are generated from the original PCOS dataset. Classifier performance is evaluated for robustness and predictive accuracy across these scenarios by repeating each scenario 1000 times with varying observations, effectively capturing a wide range of real-life class imbalance situations. The better-performing model identified through simulation is subsequently applied to the original dataset for feature selection and prediction, enhancing both interpretability and predictive performance. Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

The study adopted a simulation-based approach to identify an effective ML model for handling an imbalanced, high-dimensional PCOS dataset. Various imbalance scenarios were generated using stratified sampling, and models were evaluated across these IRs. Each scenario was repeated 1000 times to ensure generalizability. The better-performing model was then applied to the original PCOS dataset for feature selection and prediction.

Study participants and sampling

A simulation study was conducted to evaluate the performance of various classification models, including KNN, SVM, DT, XGBoost, and RF, using the original PCOS dataset. While checking for multicollinearity, redundant variables were identified (FSH to LH Ratio, waist to hip ratio, weight, and height), and were removed from the original dataset. Later, multicollinearity was reassessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and it was found that all VIF values were below 5, indicating no significant multicollinearity.

Initially, an equal proportion of samples (50:50) was randomly selected from each class through a stratified resampling approach, with each class treated as a separate stratum. Later, different IRs such as 1.5 (60:40), 1.857 (65:35), 2.33 (70:30), 3 (75:25), 4 (80:20), 5.67 (85:15), 9 (90:10), and 19 (95:5) were considered to represent varying degrees of class imbalance. These IR configurations were adapted from Hasanin et al.[11] This study also extended the simulation by including additional imbalance levels (60:40, 70:30, 80:20, 85:15, and 95:5).

Prior to model evaluation, hyperparameter tuning was performed for each classifier by using grid search. For KNN, the optimal number of neighbors (k) was selected; for SVM, the cost (C) and kernel width (sigma) were tuned. The DT model used the complexity parameter (cp) to control pruning. In the XGBoost model, the maximum tree depth (max_depth), learning rate (eta), and regularization parameter (gamma) were optimized. For the RF model, the number of variables randomly sampled at each split (mtry) and the minimum node size (min.node.size) were fine-tuned. This systematic tuning ensured that each model operated under optimal conditions during the simulation.

The predictive performance was assessed using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1 score, with five-fold cross-validation employed to ensure generalizability and reduce the risk of overfitting. The simulation experiment was replicated 1000 times, and the average results are reported to ensure robustness and reliable assessment of model performance in diverse scenarios. The simulation was carried out in R Studio (Version 2024.09.1+393).

Data collection tool and technique

For this study, data on PCOS were sourced from the Kaggle website, consisting of information gathered from 10 distinct hospitals across Kerala, India.[21] This dataset included 41 wide range of physical and clinical parameters aimed at discerning PCOS. The PCOS dataset contained 541 records of female patients, with 177 (33%) diagnosed with PCOS and 346 (67%) not diagnosed.

Ethical consideration

The study utilized the publicly available secondary dataset that contained no personally identifiable information about the patients. As the data were used in anonymized form and without direct human involvement, ethical approval was not required.

Model development and evaluation

Classification models

Classification models such as KNN, SVM, DT, RF, and XGBOOST were considered for predicting PCOS. The details of these classification models are given below.

K-nearest neighbors (KNN) is a simple ML algorithm that classifies data based on the similarity of test observations to the k nearest training observations. It uses a distance metric, Euclidean distance, to identify neighbors, with the optimal k value chosen to minimize classification error.[17]

Support vector machines (SVMs) are used for both linear and nonlinear classification tasks. SVM works by mapping the original data into a higher-dimensional space by using a nonlinear transformation. It aims to maximize the margin between the classes by utilizing support vectors and ensures that the model generalizes well by finding an optimal hyperplane.[17]

Decision trees (DTs) are hierarchical models used for both classification and regression tasks. The DT algorithm partitions the dataset into subsets based on the most significant variables, with each node representing a test on a feature, branches showing the possible outcomes, and leaf nodes holding the final class labels. The process starts with the complete dataset and splits based on the feature that provides the maximum information gain, measured by the reduction in uncertainty of class labels.[17]

Random forest (RF) is an ensemble learning technique that improves predictive performance by combining multiple DTs. Each tree is trained using a random subset of features, and the final prediction is obtained by aggregating the votes of all the trees. This method enhances model robustness by leveraging the power of multiple weak classifiers.[17]

Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) builds trees sequentially, with each tree correcting the errors made by the preceding tree. XGBoost incorporates several optimizations, including regularization to prevent overfitting and improved computational efficiency, making it well-suited for large datasets. By minimizing both the loss function and the regularization term, XGBoost enhances performance and accuracy.[17]

These models differ in complexity and application, with KNN being simple yet effective for smaller datasets, SVM excelling in high-dimensional spaces, DT offering interpretability, and RF and XGBoost providing robust performance for large, complex datasets through ensemble learning.

Performance evaluation metrics

A confusion matrix provides a detailed comparison between the model’s predictions and actual results. The effectiveness of classification models in predicting class labels is assessed using various evaluation metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1 score.

Feature selection

The PCOS dataset under consideration showed an IR of 2.03 (67:33). Based on the simulation results and the results on the original dataset, the RF was found to be better in this scenario. Hence, significant factors for the prediction of PCOS were identified using the mean decrease accuracy (MDA) approach derived from the RF model. The variable importance plot, based on MDA, was employed to identify the most influential predictors for PCOS classification. MDA measures the reduction in model accuracy when a specific variable is permuted, thereby indicating its contribution to predictive performance. A threshold value of 1 was applied, retaining only those variables that caused more than a 1% decrease in accuracy. Variables with MDA values below this threshold were excluded due to their limited impact on prediction. This threshold-based feature selection approach allowed the model to maintain comparable accuracy using fewer variables, while also reducing model complexity and enhancing interpretability.

Figure 2 illustrates the variable importance plot, where the x-axis represents MDA values and the y-axis represents variables. The red line indicates the reference threshold MDA used to distinguish informative variables. Based on the plot, 14 variables out of 41 were found to be significant in predicting PCOS. The most important variables include Follicle_No_R, Follicle_No, Skin_darkening, hair_growth, Weight_gain, Cycle, Fast_food, and Body Mass Index (BMI). Moderately influential features were Cycle Length, Anti-Mullerian Hormone (AMH), Hip, Pimples, Waist, and Regular_Exercise. Overall, the MDA-based variable ranking supports effective feature selection and model refinement in high-dimensional, imbalanced clinical datasets.

Discussions

Descriptive statistics provide various important insights based on demographic, lifestyle, and clinical parameters. The study analyzed health records of female patients from Kerala, India, focusing on numerical and categorical variables. Findings indicated that most participants had normal-to-slightly overweight body profiles, with variations in menstrual cycle characteristics and ovarian reserve markers. The high prevalence of PCOS aligns with global trends, emphasizing the need for attention in reproductive health clinics.

Many patients reported symptoms of hormonal imbalance, such as weight gain, hair growth, and skin darkening. Furthermore, frequent fast-food consumption (51.5%) and lack of regular exercise (75.2%) were reported, which are lifestyle factors that contribute to metabolic and hormonal issues. The presence of regular menstrual cycles in 72.1% of patients provides a useful baseline for understanding PCOS’s impact. These findings emphasize the importance of integrating demographic, lifestyle, and clinical data for improved diagnosis and management of reproductive health.

The study employed five-fold cross-validation to evaluate the performance of classification models in predicting PCOS, with average results computed across the five folds. This process was repeated 1000 times, and the overall average performance across all repetitions was reported to ensure comprehensive validation and generalizability of the model. The results highlight the critical insights into the effect of class imbalance on model performance. In cases of a balanced or nearly balanced scenario (IR: 1 and 1.5), RF demonstrated better performance, achieving high accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. XGBoost followed closely, showing robust results with slightly lower performance than RF. KNN and SVM lagged, particularly in specificity, making them less favorable for balanced datasets.

In cases of moderate imbalance (IR between 1.8 and 3), RF and XGBoost continued to perform better, with RF slightly edging out in accuracy and sensitivity. XGBoost maintained high performance, showcasing its adaptability to handle moderate imbalances. DT and SVM showed reasonable accuracy, but their sensitivity and specificity were not as high as RF and XGBoost. These models remain suitable for health science applications where minority class detection is crucial but not hindered by extreme imbalance.

When the IR exceeded 3, XGBoost and DT outperformed other models. XGBoost showed superior specificity (41%), indicating its robustness in identifying the minority class under severe imbalance conditions. DT, although showing slightly lower specificity (38%) than XGBoost, continued to deliver strong overall performance with high accuracy and sensitivity across classes. These results highlight the need to tailor the model selection to the IR, using RF and XGBoost for balanced to moderately imbalanced scenarios and XGBoost and DT for severely imbalanced scenarios. The simulation study further revealed that as the IR increased, the accuracy of the minority class decreased drastically across the models. Notably, the KNN model was most affected by class imbalance, whereas XGBoost and tree-based models such as DT and RF were less affected.

Further, evaluation of the classifiers on the original dataset, which included all features with an IR of 2.03, revealed that the RF model outperformed others, followed by XGBoost [Table 4]. This finding aligns with the results of the simulation study. Among the 41 features, 14 significant predictors of PCOS were identified using the MDA approach of the RF model, reducing the dataset’s dimensionality by 34%. The classifiers were re-evaluated using only the selected features, and the results indicated that the RF model remained as the best-performing model, demonstrating improved performance metrics compared to the model trained on all features [Table 5]. By identifying 14 significant predictors among 41 features, the study highlights the importance of selecting only the most relevant attributes for PCOS prediction. This dimensionality reduction facilitates better model performance and reduces the risk of redundancy, overfitting, and computational complexity, making the model more applicable in real-world clinical settings.

Proper management and control of risk factors can enhance early detection, prevention, and control of PCOS, leading to better health outcomes. The selected features include several clinical and lifestyle-related factors that play a critical role in the identification and understanding of PCOS.

Maintaining a healthy BMI through a balanced diet and regular exercise is essential, as overweight and obesity are common among individuals with PCOS, which exacerbate insulin resistance and hormonal imbalances that are central to PCOS.[2223] Unhealthy dietary habits increase the severity of PCOS, which impacts the metabolic and fertility outcomes.[24] Limiting fast food intake and focusing on calorie-restricted and DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) diets appear effective in improving insulin resistance and enhancing body composition among women with PCOS.[25] Skin darkening and hair growth are the key symptoms of PCOS and occur due to insulin resistance and excess androgens caused by a hormonal imbalance, respectively.[2627] Managing these symptoms through corrected metabolic and endocrinal disturbances, along with lifestyle management, significantly improves the quality of life for individuals with PCOS.[28]

Menstrual irregularities, caused by excess androgens, a symptom of PCOS, and regular monitoring of menstrual cycle patterns enable early identification of PCOS.[23] Elevated AMH levels, which correlate with increased follicle count, are indicative of PCOS.[2627] Therefore, hormonal evaluation through regular medical check-ups is critical for understanding the underlying hormonal imbalances and tailoring treatment strategies. Additionally, an increase in hip size is associated with a higher likelihood of developing PCOS.[29]

Lifestyle changes such as physical exercises, a balanced diet, and behavioral changes play a key role in managing PCOS and are suggested by international evidence-based guidelines.[30] These lifestyle changes not only help reduce the impact of PCOS but also reduce the associated risks, such as metabolic and cardiovascular diseases.[31] Further, awareness programs and educational modules improve knowledge about PCOS.[32]

Healthcare providers should offer comprehensive care that includes nutritional counselling, psychological support, and routine health assessments to address all aspects of PCOS. By adopting these holistic measures, the early detection, prevention, and management of PCOS can be significantly improved, enhancing overall health and well-being.

Limitations and recommendations

Future studies can focus on varying the number of features, sample sizes, along with IR. This study used data collected from 10 different hospitals in parts of Kerala, India. This geographic limitation to hospitals in Kerala may introduce selection bias, thereby affecting the generalizability of the study findings. Although XGBoost emerged as a better method under severe IR, the minority class accuracy remained below 50%, which is not satisfactory. This underscores the need for further research on evaluating cost-sensitive learning and ensemble-based balancing methods to improve minority class accuracy in the case of extreme class imbalance. The interaction between variables was not explored in this study. Additionally, efforts should focus on developing classification models that are more robust to data characteristics such as IR and high dimensionality, to achieve accurate and clinically interpretable predictions.

Conclusion

This study assessed the performance of various ML models in predicting imbalanced PCOS datasets under high-dimensional conditions and varying IR. The study findings provide critical insights and practical recommendations for their application in improving PCOS diagnosis and management. Results showed that RF was the most effective model for balanced and moderately imbalanced datasets (IR ≤3), yielding high accuracy, followed by XGBoost. In cases of severe imbalance (IR >3), XGBoost and DT exhibited superior performance, with XGBoost outperforming DT. Using the RF model, 14 significant risk factors among 41 features were identified as major contributors to PCOS prediction, and these features yielded a better prediction model.

The results of this study support clinical decision-making, with potential benefits in identifying key risk factors for PCOS, facilitating early detection, and enabling individualized patient care. By identifying the better-performing model that effectively handles imbalanced and high-dimensional clinical data, the study contributes to evidence-based decision-making and aligns with public health policy benchmarks aimed at improving women’s health outcomes, promoting early intervention, and optimizing resource allocation in reproductive healthcare.
