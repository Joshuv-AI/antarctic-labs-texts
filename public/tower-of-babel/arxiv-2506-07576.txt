Super Encoding Network: Recursive Association of Multi-Modal Encoders for Video Understanding
Boyu Chen, Siran Chen, Kunchang Li, Qinglin Xu, Yu Qiao, Yali Wang

Abstract
Video understanding has been considered as one critical step towards world modeling, which is an important long-term problem in AI research. Recently, multi-modal foundation models have shown such potential via large-scale pretraining. However, these models simply align encoders of different modalities via contrastive learning, while lacking deeper multi-modal interactions, which is critical for understanding complex target movements with diversified video scenes. To fill this gap, we propose a unified Super Encoding Network (SEN) for video understanding, which builds up such distinct interactions through recursive association of multi-modal encoders in the foundation models. Specifically, we creatively treat those well-trained encoders as “super neurons" in our SEN. Via designing a Recursive Association (RA) block, we progressively fuse multi-modalities with the input video, based on knowledge integrating, distributing, and prompting of super neurons in a recursive manner. In this way, our SEN can effectively encode deeper multi-modal interactions, for prompting various video understanding tasks in downstream. Extensive experiments show that, our SEN can remarkably boost the four most representative video tasks, including tracking, recognition, chatting, and editing, e.g., for pixel-level tracking, the average jaccard index improves 2.7%, temporal coherence(TC) drops 8.8% compared to the popular CaDeX++ approach. For one-shot video editing, textual alignment improves 6.4%, and frame consistency increases 4.1% compared to the popular Tune-A-Video approach.

IV
Our Super Encoding Network (SEN)

In this section,
we introduce our SEN in detail.
As shown in Fig
2
,
there are two distinct characteristics.
First,
it is a meta encoder network,
since each neuron refers to an encoder network of one modality that comes from a multi-modal foundation model.
This allows it to inherit the well-pretrained knowledge of these frozen foundation models.
Second,
it is a recursive structure,
where we cascade these super neurons via a Recursive Association (RA) block.
This enables it to learn deeper interactions of multi-modal knowledge, thereby enhancing various complex video tasks in the downstream.

As shown in Fig
2
,
we first feed the input multi-modal data
\{\mathbf{X}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
into the first layer of super neurons,
for extracting the latent feature of each modality,

\mathbf{F}^{(1)}_{m}=\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathbf{X}_{m}).

(1)

where
\mathbf{F}^{(1)}_{m}
is the latent feature of modality
m
in the 1st layer.
Notice that,
these super neurons are actually the encoders in the frozen foundation models,
e.g.,
Imagebind
[
54
]
.
Hence,
the latent features
\{\mathbf{F}^{(1)}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
inherit semantic knowledge of multi-modalities,
which are memorized in the frozen foundation models via large-scale data pre-training.

However, one layer of such neurons is not sufficient to encode deeper interactions between modalities,
leading to the limited capacity of modeling complex dynamics in open scenarios.

To this end,
we propose a concise Recursive Association (RA) block,
which can progressively fuse multi-modalities by knowledge integrating, distributing and prompting with recursive usage of these super neurons in our SEN.

IV-A

Recursive Association (RA) of Super Neurons

As mentioned in the introduction,
we are partially inspired by multi-modal association procedure of human brain
[
19
,
20
]
.
When a human receives the input signal of one modality,
the brain area of this modality will produce neurotransmitters.
Then,
these neurotransmitters are delivered to other brain areas that refer to other modalities.
Such a process is repeated many times to achieve multi-modal association.
To mimic such a human-like process,
we introduce a Recursive Association (RA) block for our SEN.
As shown in Fig
2
,
it consists of three key components,
including knowledge integrating, distributing, and prompting of super neurons.

Knowledge Integrating
.
Suppose that,
we obtain the latent features
\{\mathbf{F}^{(i)}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
(i.e., knowledge) from the
i
-th layer of our SEN.
To start with,
we first fuse all the latent features together as a multi-modal knowledge
\mathbf{F}^{(i)}
,

\mathbf{F}^{(i)}=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{F}^{(i)}_{1},...,\mathbf{F}^{(i)}_{m},...,\mathbf{F}^{(i)}_{M}).

(2)

Note that,
all the latent features are extracted from encoders in the foundation model.
Since these encoders are aligned via multi-modal constratsive pretraining,
the extracted features have the same dimension.
Hence,
we use
\mathcal{R}
as a fusion operation (e.g., pooling or concatenation) to generate the multi-modal knowledge.

Fig. 3:
The details about how SEN be applied to representative video tasks in the downstream, from video tracking to video recognition, from video chatting to video editing.

Knowledge Distributing
. After obtaining multi-modal knowledge feature
\mathbf{F}^{(i)}
,
we distribute it to all the super neurons in the next layer,
in order to build up deeper multi-modal interactions.
Specifically,
by mimicking the human brain procedure above,
we transform
\mathbf{F}^{(i)}
as "neurotransmitters" for each individual modality.

\mathbf{G}^{(i)}_{m}=\mathcal{H}^{(i)}_{m}(\mathbf{F}^{(i)}).

(3)

where
\mathcal{H}^{(i)}_{m}
is a MLP operation to produce "neurotransmitter" of modality
m
at the
i
-th layer.

Knowledge Prompting
.
The next question is how to feed the "neurotransmitter" into the corresponding super neuron at the
i
-th layer.
Note that,
super neurons are encoders in the multi-modal foundation model.
Hence,
each neuron has already had the input data of the corresponding modality
\mathbf{X}_{m}
.
In this case,
we propose to convert the "neurotransmitter" as a contextual prompt
\mathbf{P}^{(i)}_{m}
for each modality,

\mathbf{P}^{(i)}_{m}=\mathbf{G}^{(i)}_{m}+\mathbf{Q}^{(i)}_{m}.

(4)

For each modality at the
i
-th layer,
we introduce the learnable prompt
\mathbf{Q}^{(i)}_{m}
to increase the adaptation capacity of our RA.
Finally,
we concatenate the input data
\mathbf{X}_{m}
and the contextual prompt
\mathbf{P}^{(i)}_{m}
,
subsequently feeding them into the corresponding neuron to extract the latent feature in the next layer,

\mathbf{F}^{(i+1)}_{m}=\mathcal{M}_{m}(\mathbf{X}_{m}\oplus\mathbf{P}^{(i)}_{m}).

(5)

As we can see,
the latent feature at the
(i+1)
-th layer does not only depend on the input
\mathbf{X}_{m}
,
but it also depends on
\mathbf{P}^{i}_{m}
that is summarized from all the modalities at the
i
-th layer (i.e., Eq. (
2
)-(
4
)).
In this case,
this latent feature at the current layer not only preserves semantic knowledge in this modality,
but it also integrates multi-modal contexts.
Since a single layer of such neurons is insufficient to encode the intricate interactions between modalities, it restricts the model’s ability to capture complex dynamics in open scenarios. To address this limitation, a recursive manner is introduced.
Via stacking multiple layers in a recursive manner,
our SEN can progressively achieve deep multi-modal interactions in videos.
In subsequent experiments, we demonstrate the necessity of this recursive process in enhancing the model’s performance for downstream video tasks.

IV-B

SEN for Downstream Video Tasks

To show the effectiveness of our SEN,
we next explain how to apply it for four most representative video tasks from video perception to understanding,
including tracking, recognition, chatting and editing.
Basically,
we choose the simplest way for downstream task adaptation,
where
we add or concatenate the final feature of SEN into the corresponding task model.
This makes our SEN a plug-and-play unified encoder network for video understanding.

Pixel-Level Tracking
.
This task is to estimate full-length motion trajectories for every pixel in every frame of a video.

First
,
we choose the popular models
[
72
,
73
]
for this task.
For convenience,
we take Omnimotion
[
72
]
as an illustration.
As shown in Fig
3
(a),
this model first specifies the location of a pixel
p_{i}
at frame
i
,
and concatenate it with a randomly initialized depth map to compose the 3D location of this pixel
x_{i}
.
Next,
this model maps
x_{i}
in frame
i
to its canonical 3D volume,
and leverages inverse mapping to find the corresponding
x_{j}
in frame
j
for pixel-level tracking.

Second
,
besides of video modality,
we choose depth and text as other modalities in our SEN.
The main reason is that,
depth can provide extra 3D information,
and text can provide extra motion description of target.
Both are important clue for this tracking task.
Hence,
we use GPT-4o
[
4
]
to generate the caption of this video,
and use GLPN
[
74
]
to generate the depth of this video.

Finally
,
we feed all these modalities into our SEN for deeper interactions,
and average the output features of all these modalities as a contextual feature.
Then,
we resize it as the shape of
x_{i}
in the Omnimotion model
and add it with
x_{i}
for tracking.
Note that,
all the encoders in SEN and the task model are pretrained.
We froze them and only fine-tuned the learnable RA in SEN for task adaptation.

Zero-Shot Video Recognition
.
This task is to identify the category of the query video from a number of given classes.
To validate the generalization capacity of our SEN,
we choose a zero-shot setting where the classes in the testing are not seen in the training data.

First
,
the task model is actually the multi-modal foundation model like ImageBind
[
54
]
.
Hence,
we can directly use multi-modal encoders of the task model as super neurons of our SEN.

Second
,
the input modalities of our SEN are video, text and audio for the VggSound benchmark
[
75
]
.
Zero-shot video recognition can be done by video-text and audio-text contrastive learning,
i.e.,
We compute video-text and video-audio cosine similarity respectively and add these two similarity matrices to get the overall similarity matrices for prediction as shown in Fig
3
(b).

Finally
,
all the encoders in SEN and the task model are pretrained.
We froze them and only fine-tuned the learnable RA in SEN for task adaptation.

Video-to-Text Chatting Task
.
This task is a Risk Object Localization and Intention and Suggestion Prediction (ROLISP) task in the auto-driving scenarios
[
76
]
, which is done by chatting with model.
The main reason we chose this task is that this task not only contains risk object localization (video perception), but also contains risk explanation (video reasoning).
Hence,
it is a perfect choice to validate our SEN for challenging video tasks.

First
,
we choose the popular Shikra
[
21
]
as the task model.
As shown in Fig
3
(c),
it is a typical style of Multi-modal Large Language Model (MLLM),
consisting of visual encoder, FC connector and LLM.

Second
,
besides of the given video and text modality,
we choose depth as another modality in our SEN.
The main reason is that,
depth can provide extra 3D information for better localization.
Hence,
we use GLPN
[
74
]
to generate the depth of this video. we feed all these modalities into our SEN
and average their output features as the final feature.

Finally
,
the final feature is added to the visual token as context for boosting this task.
Subsequently, the visual token is further processed by a FC connector.
Then we feed the processed feature and the question into LLM to obtain the final bounding box and answer.
Again,
We frozen all the models and and only fine-tune the learnable RA in SEN.

Text-to-Video Editing
.
This task is to edit a video according to the given text query.
To validate the generalization capacity of our SEN,
we choose one-shot editing setting,
where there are only one video-text sample in the training data.

First
,
we choose the popular Tune-a-video
[
41
]
as our task model.
As shown in Fig
3
(d),
it is a typical style of diffusion model,
consisting of DDIM inversion and denoising steps
[
41
]
.
Second
,
besides of video modality,
we choose depth and text as other modalities in our SEN for extra 3D information and detailed semantic caption.
We use GPT-4o
[
4
]
to generate the caption of this video,
and use GLPN
[
74
]
to generate the depth of this video.

Finally
,
we feed all these modalities into our SEN,
and average the output features as a contextual feature.
Then,
we resize it as the shape of the noisy video (after DDIM inversion) in the Tune-A-Video model,
and add it for editing.
We frozen all the encoders in SEN and the task model,
while only fine-tuning the learnable RA in SEN for task adaptation.

Metrics.

We follow the metrics of omnimotion
[
72
]
for the pixel-level tracking task.
<\delta^{x}_{\textrm{avg}}
denotes the average positional accuracy.
Average Jaccard(AJ) evaluates both occlusion and position accuracy.
Occlusion Accuracy(OA) evaluates the visibility/occlusion prediction accuracy at each frame.
Temporal Coherence(TC) is the
L_{2}
distance between the acceleration of ground truth tracks and predicted tracks. The lower TC, the better the model’s performance.
Top1 Acc is the evaluation metric for video-level recognition task.
The performance of the video-to-text chatting task is valued by BLEU-4 (B4), METEOR (M), CIDER (C), and SPICE (S), and Mean Intersection Over Union (mIoU) as
[

Different Feature Fusion Format:
We conduct experiment to verify the effectiveness of the RA block as shown in Table
V
.
Because the SEN-derived features often incorporate additional modalities (e.g., depth and text in pixel-level tracking) that the task models do not utilize.
Since the additional introduction of modalities may potentially bring about performance improvements. To ensure a fair comparison, we have established a baseline method that incorporates multimodal information based on the task model.

In other words, baseline in Table
V
serves as our fair comparison baseline method.

The baseline in the table means that we have deleted the RA block. Then we only concat the latent
\{\mathbf{F}^{(i)}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}

and directly input them to the next layer of the multi-modal encoders.
This process is repeated three times for fair comparison.
As shown in Table
V

The Baseline outperforms the pure task model with a slight margin.
We also attempted to replace the RA module with the attention module of the Transformer
[
38
]
. That is, we first pass the latent
\{\mathbf{F}^{(i)}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
of each modality through a transformer block.
Then the processed latents are concatenated and input into the next layer of the SEN.
As shown in Table
V
, our RA block obtains the most optimal outcomes compared to other designs.
RA performs better than the transformer attention module because,
the parameters of an attention block in transformer (e.g., 7.1M in ViT-B/16) are much larger than our RA (1.7M).
In the video tasks with limited training data (e.g., pixel-level tracking and one-shot video editing), the transformer structure faces overfitting, while our design tends to alleviate overfitting,
compared to the transformer attention module.

Necessity of Recursive Design and Upper Bound

To validate the necessity of our recursive design,
we experimented on the number of RA blocks as shown in Table
VI
.
The number of RA blocks means the number of multi-modal interactions executed within the SEN.
The more multi-modal interactions, the higher the task results can be obtained.
By conducting deeper multi-modal interactions, the model can better explore multimodal information and enhance its robustness.

We also validate the potential upper bound of the RA block number.

As shown in Table
VI
, the performance tends to converge when the number of RA blocks increases to 3.
The reason is that, a super neuron in our SEN refers to an encoder,
e.g.,
32 attention blocks for ViT-H/14
[
38
,
87
]
.
Hence,
3 RA Blocks equate to a network depth of 96 attention blocks,
which is sufficient to achieve deeper multi-modal interaction.
Adding more RA Blocks may bring overfitting and more inference time.
Hence,
we choose the setting of 3 RA blocks.

VI
Limitations

Although SEN achieves remarkable generalization capacity on representative video understanding tasks.
We have not tried more multi-modal foundation models for building meta encoders because of the limited computational resources.
In subsequent research explorations, we may add more modalities inputs to verify the effectiveness of this method on more tasks.
More in-depth research will be conducted in the future when the opportunity arises.

VII
Conclusion

In conclusion, we propose a brand-new Super Encoding Network(SEN) for multi-modal video understanding.
SEN creatively treats multi-modal frozen foundation models as super neurons. This flexible structure allows our SEN to effectively encode deeper multi-modal interactions and prompting various video understanding tasks in downstream.
The proposed RA blocks progressively associate foundation models for deeper multi-modal interaction via knowledge integrating, distributing, and prompting video-relevant knowledge in a recursive manner.
Extensive experiments show that SEN performs remarkably on four representative video tasks, from video tracking to recognition,
from video chatting to editing.
