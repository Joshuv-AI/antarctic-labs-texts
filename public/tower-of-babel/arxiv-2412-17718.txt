ℜ \mathfrak{R} obin Hood Reachability Bidding Games Full Version
Shaull Almagor, Guy Avni, Neta Dafni

Abstract
. Two-player graph games are a fundamental model for reasoning about the interaction of agents. These games are played between two players who move a token along a graph. In bidding games, the players have some monetary budget, and at each step they bid for the privilege of moving the token. Typically, the winner of the bid either pays the loser or the bank, or a combination thereof. We introduce Robin Hood bidding games, where at the beginning of every step the richer player pays the poorer a fixed fraction of the difference of their wealth. After the bid, the winner pays the loser. Intuitively, this captures the setting where a regulating entity prevents the accumulation of wealth to some degree. We show that the central property of bidding games, namely the existence of a threshold function, is retained in Robin Hood bidding games. We show that finding the threshold can be formulated as a Mixed-Integer Linear Program. Surprisingly, we show that the games are not always determined exactly at the threshold, unlike their standard counterpart.

1.
Introduction

A
reachability game
is a 2-player game played on a graph, by placing a token on one of the vertices and moving it along the edges according to some predefined rules, where the goal of Player 1 (denoted 🚺
1
) is to reach a set of target vertices, and the goal of Player 2 (denoted 🚹
2
) is to prevent that. Reachability games are fundamental in automated synthesis of systems
(
Pnueli and Rosner, 1989
)
, where a system plays against an environment (e.g., controller synthesis
(
Girard, 2012
)
, robotic planning
(
Fainekos et al., 2006
)
, network routing
(
Brihaye et al., 2019
)
, etc.).

In
bidding games

(
Lazarus et al., 1996
;
Lazarus et al., 1999
;
Avni et al., 2019
;
Avni and Henzinger, 2020
)
, each player has a
budget
(a real value in
[0,1]
, where the sum of the budgets is assumed to be normalized to
1
) at any given moment, and the movement of the token in each step is determined by an
auction
, resulting in the higher bidder moving the token.
We focus on
Richman
games
(
Lazarus et al., 1996
)
, where the winner of the auction pays their bid to the loser (see e.g.,
(
Avni et al., 2021
)
for other bidding mechanisms).

Bidding games are useful for modelling settings where agents compete for some resource (e.g., money, computational resources, etc.) and use these resources to direct the interaction.

A common phenomenon in bidding games is that if one of the players accumulates a high-enough portion of the budget, that player can
force the game to reach any desired location. In loose terms, “the rich can do whatever they want”.
In some settings this phenomenon is desirable, e.g., when modelling the interaction between an attacker and defender of a security system, and the budget is computational resources – nothing prevents either party from hogging resources in order to win.
In many other settings, however, the players operate under some regulating entity (e.g., a scheduler in an operating system, or monetary regulation), which prevents the accumulation of excessive wealth in order to achieve some fairness, or to inspire active participation in the game.

A standard means to regulate wealth is to redistribute some of the wealth of the rich to the poor, à la Robin Hood’s
steal from the rich and give to the poor

(
Poddar et al., 2012
;
Franko et al., 2013
)
. Note that this is not the same as taxation in that in standard taxation it is not at all clear that taxes go to the poor, nor is it the case that the poor are not taxed.

In this work, we introduce a variant of bidding games called
Robin Hood bidding games
which incorporates wealth regulations. In a Robin Hood bidding game, each auction is preceded by a
wealth redistribution
phase: the richer player pays the poorer player a constant fraction (denoted
\lambda
) of the difference between their budgets. The classical model of bidding games then corresponds to
\lambda=0
. We only consider
0\leq\lambda<\frac{1}{2}
, as
\lambda\geq\frac{1}{2}
would mean that the richer player becomes the poorer (or equal, for
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}
), which is of little motivation.

The central question in the study of bidding games is the existence of a
threshold
function for the game: a function that assigns for each vertex
v
a value
\mathsf{Th}(v)
, such that if 🚺
1
starts with budget more than
\mathsf{Th}(v)
then she wins, and if she starts with less than
\mathsf{Th}(v)
, she loses.
It is shown in
(
Avni et al., 2021
)
that every reachability bidding game has a threshold, and finding its value is in
\mathrm{NP}\cap\mathrm{coNP}
.

We extend the known results regarding the existence of thresholds for reachability bidding games, and show that every Robin Hood reachability bidding games has a threshold (
Section

4
). We further show that computing this threshold can be done using Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP).
Additionally, unlike previous works, we discuss what happens when the initial budget equals exactly the threshold (
Section

5
). We find, surprisingly, that the game might not be determined at the threshold (i.e., neither player has a winning strategy), a behavior that does not occur in standard bidding games where 🚺
1
wins ties
(nor in general turn based games without concurrent biddings
(
Martin, 1975
)
).
Apart from the result itself, we believe it is important to draw attention to discussions about the behavior at the threshold. Indeed – it is often the case that optimal strategies work by reaching certain vertices exactly at their threshold. Still in
Section

5
, we show that given the threshold function and a vertex
v
, we can decide in polynomial time if the game is undetermined at
v
with budget
\mathsf{Th}(v)
, and if not – who the winner is.

In addition to these contributions, we observe curious behavior of the threshold function when
\lambda
is treated as a parameter. Specifically, in
Section

3
we conduct an elaborate analysis of the example in
Figure

1
and show that this function might be discontinuous (see
Figure

2
). We also demonstrate a toolbox for analyzing specific games when
\lambda
is a parameter.

Preliminaries

A
graph
is
G=(V,E)
where
V
is a set of vertices and
E\subseteq V\times V
is a set of edges. For
v\in V
we denote by
\Gamma(v)=\{u\mid(v,u)\in E\}

of

A
Robin Hood reachability bidding game

is the

is a set of
target
vertices for 🚺
1

We assume for convenience that the vertices in
T
are sinks.
We sometimes omit
v_{0}
and
x_{\mathrm{init}}
, when the discussion is not limited to specific initial vertex and budget.

The game is played between 2 players as follows. At each step, a token is placed on a vertex
v\in V
(initially
v_{0}
), and each of the players has a
budget
, the budgets being described by a vector
\boldsymbol{w}\in[0,1]^{2}
(initially
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x_{\mathrm{init}}\\
1-x_{\mathrm{init}}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
).
For clarity, we denote vectors in bold (e.g.,
\boldsymbol{v}
).

The game proceeds in steps, each consisting of the following phases:

(1)

Wealth Redistribution (abbreviated WR and denoted

\mathfrak{R}

): Each player’s budget is updated using the operator

\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}(x)=(1-2\lambda)x+\lambda

(2)

Bidding: Each player (concurrently) makes a bid within their budget. The player with the higher bid
b
wins the bidding (a tie is broken in favor of 🚺
1
) and pays
b
to the other player. The budgets are updated accordingly.

(3)

Moving: The player who wins the bidding moves the token to a neighbor of
v
of their choice.

We remark that

\mathfrak{R}

can be viewed as the linear operator on vectors given by the matrix

\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}=\begin{pmatrix}1-\lambda&\lambda\\

\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
1-x\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}(1-2\lambda)x+\lambda\\
(1-2\lambda)(1-x)+\lambda\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. We abuse notation and use either view as convenient.

An Enlightening Example

In this section we expand the discussion regarding the game in

We are interested in finding a threshold for

. We denote this threshold by

For

In the following, we show the existence of a threshold

for every

A play can end by either reaching

or

(and 🚺
1
wins or loses respectively), or oscillate infinitely between

, in which case 🚺

loses. Regardless, a play can be described by a finite or infinite sequence of iterations, each comprising four phases: (1) WR in

; (2) bidding in

; (3) WR in
v_{\mathrm{right}}
; and (4) bidding in
v_{\mathrm{right}}

which ends the play, or be infinite.
For the

\boldsymbol{w}^{(n)}_{\mathrm{wr},\mathrm{left}}

and
\boldsymbol{w}^{(n)}_{\mathrm{wr},\mathrm{right}}
for

.
We also denote the first entries of these vectors, that is, 🚺
1

3.1.
Alternative Tie Breaking

It is useful to first consider the case that ties are not always broken in favor of 🚺

We describe optimal strategies

At any iteration

.

Recall that the budget vectors belong to the affine subspace
W=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid x+y=1\}
, which is invariant under
M
. Every vector
\boldsymbol{w}\in W
can be written as a linear combination of the form
\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{E^{\mathtt{vec}}_{1}}+c\boldsymbol{E^{\mathtt{vec}}_{2}}
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Indeed, since
\boldsymbol{E^{\mathtt{vec}}_{1}}\in W
and
\boldsymbol{E^{\mathtt{vec}}_{2}}
is the slope of
x+y=1
, we have that
W=\{\boldsymbol{E^{\mathtt{vec}}_{1}}+c\boldsymbol{E^{\mathtt{vec}}_{2}}\mid c\in\mathbb{R}\}
.

We then have
M\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{E^{\mathtt{vec}}_{1}}+cE^{\mathtt{val}}_{2}\boldsymbol{E^{\mathtt{vec}}_{2}}=\boldsymbol{E^{\mathtt{vec}}_{1}}+E^{\mathtt{val}}_{2}(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{E^{\mathtt{vec}}_{1}})
. Projecting this on the first coordinate and denoting
x_{\mathrm{fix}}=\frac{2\lambda-2}{4\lambda-3}
, for every
n
we have
x^{(n+1)}_{\mathrm{left}}=\mathtt{next}\left(x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}\right)
, where
\mathtt{next}(x)=x_{\mathrm{fix}}+E^{\mathtt{val}}_{2}(x-x_{\mathrm{fix}})
, and overall

x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}=x_{\mathrm{fix}}+\left(E^{\mathtt{val}}_{2}\right)^{n}(x_{\mathrm{init}}-x_{\mathrm{fix}})

If the condition
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{wr},\mathrm{right}}>\frac{1}{2}
is met for some
n
(and the play does not end before that), 🚺
1
wins. Similarly, if
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{wr},\mathrm{left}}<\frac{1}{2}
(equivalently,
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}<\frac{1}{2}
) then 🚺
1
loses. If neither of these occurs for any
n
then the play is infinite and 🚺
1
loses.

It is convenient to phrase the win condition
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{wr},\mathrm{right}}>\frac{1}{2}
as a condition on
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}
, which allows the analysis to focus on
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}
only. Note that
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{wr},\mathrm{right}}
is an injective function of
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}
, obtained by projecting the operator
\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}B_{\mathrm{left}}\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}
on the first coordinate. Its reverse function, denoted
f^{\mathrm{rev}}(x)
, satisfies
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}=f^{\mathrm{rev}}\left(x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{wr},\mathrm{right}}\right)
. It is easy to verify that
f^{\mathrm{rev}}(x)=\frac{4\lambda^{2}-5\lambda+x+1}{2(2\lambda-1)^{2}}
, and it is increasing for all
x
. Therefore, 🚺
1
wins in the
n
’th iteration if and only if
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}>f^{\mathrm{rev}}(\frac{1}{2})
. It follows that 🚺
1
wins the play if and only if there exists
n
such that
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}>f^{\mathrm{rev}}(\frac{1}{2})
and
x^{\left(n^{\prime}\right)}_{\mathrm{left}}\geq\frac{1}{2}
for all
n^{\prime}<n
.

We now split the analysis according to the value of
\lambda
. Specifically, according to whether
E^{\mathtt{val}}_{2}
is the dominant eigenvalue, i.e., whether

\lambda<\frac{1}{4}
or
\lambda\geq\frac{1}{4}
.

The case
\frac{1}{4}\leq\lambda<\frac{1}{2}

In this case,
f^{\mathrm{rev}}(\frac{1}{2})\geq 1
and therefore 🚺
1
’s winning condition is never met, and she loses for every initial budget.

The case
0<\lambda<\frac{1}{4}

In this case, we have
E^{\mathtt{val}}_{2}>1
. Recall that
\boldsymbol{E^{\mathtt{vec}}_{1}}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x_{\mathrm{fix}}\\
1-x_{\mathrm{fix}}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
1
, and we claim that it is the threshold vector, that is,
x_{\mathrm{fix}}=\frac{2\lambda-2}{4\lambda-3}
is a (2-strong) threshold. Note that
x_{\mathrm{fix}}
is increasing and continuous in
\lambda
, and equals
\frac{2}{3}
for
\lambda=0
and
\frac{3}{4}
for
\lambda=\frac{1}{4}
.

Recall that
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}=x_{\mathrm{fix}}+\left(E^{\mathtt{val}}_{2}\right)^{n}(x_{\mathrm{init}}-x_{\mathrm{fix}})
, and 🚺
1
wins if and only if this value goes above
f^{\mathrm{rev}}(\frac{1}{2})
(and does not go below
\frac{1}{2}
before that). We now have
\frac{1}{2}<\frac{2}{3}\leq x_{\mathrm{fix}}<f^{\mathrm{rev}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)<1
. It follows that:

•

For
x_{\mathrm{init}}=x_{\mathrm{fix}}
, we have
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}\equiv x_{\mathrm{fix}}<f^{\mathrm{rev}}(\frac{1}{2})
for all
n
, and so 🚺
1
loses.

•

For
x_{\mathrm{init}}>x_{\mathrm{fix}}
, the sequence
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}
increases unboundedly, eventually above
f^{\mathrm{rev}}(\frac{1}{2})
, at which point 🚺
1
wins.

•

For
x_{\mathrm{init}}<x_{\mathrm{fix}}
, the sequence decreases unboundedly, eventually below
\frac{1}{2}
, at which point 🚺
1
loses.

Remark 3.1.

In addition to the thresholds, an analysis of the eigenvalues of
M
gives us an insight regarding the behavior of the budget vectors throughout the play.

In the case
\lambda=\frac{1}{4}
,
M
is the identity matrix, meaning
x^{(n+1)}_{\mathrm{left}}=x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}
for all
n
, and the resulting sequence of configurations is periodic. We use the fact that a win condition for 🚹
2
is not met during the first iteration, since we only consider
x_{\mathrm{init}}\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

In the case
\frac{1}{4}<\lambda<\frac{1}{2}
,
v_{2}
cannot be reached symmetrically to
v_{1}
, and so the play is again infinite. In this case,
E^{\mathtt{val}}_{2}<1
. Since the dominant eigenvalue is
E^{\mathtt{val}}_{1}=1
, the sequence of the budget vectors
v^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}
converges to
\boldsymbol{E^{\mathtt{vec}}_{1}}
, which depends only on
\lambda
and not on the initial budget.

In the case
0<\lambda<\frac{1}{4}
, as mentioned above, the dominant eigenvalue is
E^{\mathtt{val}}_{2}>1
, and so the budgets would diverge if the play had stayed in
v_{\mathrm{left}},v_{\mathrm{right}}
.

3.2.
Correct Tie Breaking

We now return to the original tie breaking mechanism, where all ties are broken in favor of 🚺
1
. When bidding in
v_{\mathrm{right}}
, 🚹
2
must win the bidding in order to not lose instantly. The analysis must therefore be modified in the following ways:

•

🚺
1
can win from
v_{\mathrm{right}}
with a budget of exactly
\frac{1}{2}
. The winning condition is therefore changed to
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}\geq f^{\mathrm{rev}}(\frac{1}{2})
(instead of a strict inequality). Accordingly, we differentiate between the cases
\lambda=\frac{1}{4}
(where
f^{\mathrm{rev}}(\frac{1}{2})=1
) and
\lambda>\frac{1}{4}
(where
f^{\mathrm{rev}}(\frac{1}{2})>1
).

•

When in
v_{\mathrm{right}}
and
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{wr},\mathrm{right}}<\frac{1}{2}
, 🚹
2
must bid strictly more than
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{wr},\mathrm{right}}
, i.e.
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{wr},\mathrm{right}}+\epsilon_{n}
for some
\epsilon_{n}>0
. Note that thus far, our analysis considered fixed optimal strategies, and hence a single play. Now, however, each strategy of 🚹
2
may choose different values for the
\epsilon_{n}
’s, thus inducing multiple plays that need to be analyzed.

The case
\lambda=\frac{1}{4}

In this case
f^{\mathrm{rev}}(\frac{1}{2})=1
. With the updated winning condition, 🚺
1
wins if and only if
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}=1
for some
n
.

For
x_{\mathrm{init}}<1
, 🚺
1
still loses. The reason is 🚹
2
can choose values
\epsilon_{n}
so that the sequence
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}
is increasing but strictly below
1
.
Indeed, let
\{x_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be an increasing sequence, strictly bounded above by
1
, such that
x_{0}=x_{\mathrm{init}}
. We show that 🚹
2
can ensure
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}\leq x_{n}
for all
n
. Fix
n
and assume
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}\leq x_{n}
. The next value
x^{(n+1)}_{\mathrm{left}}
is an increasing, continuous function of
\epsilon_{n}
. Since
M
is the identity matrix, this function equals
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}
for
\epsilon_{n}=0
, and so a small enough
\epsilon_{n}
achieves
x^{(n+1)}_{\mathrm{left}}\leq x_{n+1}
.

If
x_{\mathrm{init}}=1
, however, 🚺
1
wins in the first iteration. Indeed, by bidding
\frac{1}{4}
, moving to
v_{\mathrm{right}}
, then bidding
\frac{1}{2}
and moving to
v_{1}
, the resulting play is:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right)}_{v_{\mathrm{left}}}\overset{\hbox{\hskip 3.51389pt\hskip-2.90279pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-2.90279pt\hskip-3.51389pt\raisebox{3.2764pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=6.02777pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-3.51389pt\hskip 3.51389pt}}{\longrightarrow}\underbrace{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0.75\\
0.25\end{smallmatrix}\right)}_{v_{\mathrm{left}}}\overset{\text{ bid }\frac{1}{4}}{\longrightarrow}\underbrace{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0.5\\
0.5\end{smallmatrix}\right)}_{v_{\mathrm{right}}}\overset{\hbox{\hskip 3.51389pt\hskip-2.90279pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-2.90279pt\hskip-3.51389pt\raisebox{3.2764pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=6.02777pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-3.51389pt\hskip 3.51389pt}}{\longrightarrow}\underbrace{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0.5\\
0.5\end{smallmatrix}\right)}_{v_{\mathrm{right}}}\overset{\text{ bid }\frac{1}{2}}{\longrightarrow}\underbrace{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right)}_{v_{\mathrm{1}}}

The case
\frac{1}{4}<\lambda<\frac{1}{2}

Here
f^{\mathrm{rev}}(\frac{1}{2})>1
, and so 🚺
1
loses for any initial budget (including exactly
1
).

The case
0\leq\lambda<\frac{1}{4}

In this case
E^{\mathtt{val}}_{2}>1
,
x_{\mathrm{fix}}<f^{\mathrm{rev}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)<1
and recall that in
Section

3.1
the threshold was
x_{\mathrm{fix}}
. We claim this remains the threshold, only it is now 1-strong instead of 2-strong.

First consider
x_{\mathrm{init}}\geq x_{\mathrm{fix}}
. Fix a strategy of 🚹
2
. Since
\epsilon_{0}=0
results in 🚹
2
losing in the first iteration, we assume for the rest of the analysis that
\epsilon_{0}>0
, and so
x^{(1)}_{\mathrm{left}}>x_{\mathrm{init}}\geq x_{\mathrm{fix}}
. Recall that in
Section

3.1
, such a value for the initial budget resulted in a winning play for 🚺
1
. Now, 🚹
2
can do no better; indeed, for all
n
, since
\epsilon_{n}>0
, we have by induction that
x_{\mathrm{fix}}+\left(E^{\mathtt{val}}_{2}\right)^{n-1}\left(x^{(1)}_{\mathrm{left}}-x_{\mathrm{fix}}\right)\leq x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}
(c.f., a similar equation but with equality in
Section

3.1
). We conclude that
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}
increases above
f^{\mathrm{rev}}(\frac{1}{2})
, and 🚺
1
wins.

Finally, consider
x_{\mathrm{init}}<x_{\mathrm{fix}}
. This previously resulted in a sequence
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}
with the (negative) differences
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}-x_{\mathrm{fix}}
being multiplied by the constant
E^{\mathtt{val}}_{2}>1
with each iteration, and the sequence
\epsilon_{n}
can now be chosen to retain a similar behaviour of the differences. Indeed, Fixing
n
and
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}
, we have that
x^{(n+1)}_{\mathrm{left}}
is an increasing, continuous function of
\epsilon_{n}
, that equals
x_{\mathrm{fix}}+E^{\mathtt{val}}_{2}\left(x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}-x_{\mathrm{fix}}\right)
for
\epsilon_{n}=0
. A small enough
\epsilon_{n}
can therefore achieve
x^{(n+1)}_{\mathrm{left}}<x_{\mathrm{fix}}+\frac{1}{2}E^{\mathtt{val}}_{2}\left(x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}-x_{\mathrm{fix}}\right)
. In conclusion, 🚹
2
can still force the sequence
x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{left}}
to decrease below
\frac{1}{2}
, and so 🚺
1
loses.

Concluding the three cases, we have that the thresholds are the following:

•

For
0\leq\lambda<\frac{1}{4}
:
\tau(\lambda)=\frac{2\lambda-2}{4\lambda-3}
(increasing from
\frac{2}{3}
to
\frac{3}{4}
, 1-strong threshold)

•

For
\lambda=\frac{1}{4}
:
\tau(\lambda)=1
(1-strong threshold)

•

For
\frac{1}{4}<\lambda<\frac{1}{2}
:
\tau(\lambda)=1
(2-strong threshold)

Note that
\tau(\lambda)
is discontinuous at
\lambda=\frac{1}{4}
, which gives us the following.

Corollary 3.1.

There exists a game
\mathcal{G}
and vertex
v
such that the threshold function of
v
is discontinuous as a function of
\lambda
.

Remark 3.2.

The behavior of
\tau
in this example can be given an economic interpretation: after a certain threshold (namely
\lambda=\frac{1}{4}
), the threshold (suddenly) becomes equivalent to that of any
\frac{1}{4}\leq\lambda<\frac{1}{2}
. This suggests that beyond a certain threshold, it no longer helps anyone to impose more tax. Naturally this does not fully extend to real-life economics, but it is a curiosity nonetheless.

Remark 3.3.

The analysis carried out in this section crucially depends on obtaining a characterization of the play resulting from optimal strategies as a linear dynamical system. Since analyzing such systems is notoriously difficult, especially in high dimensions
(
Ouaknine and Worrell, 2012
)
, automating this analysis algorithmically seems out of reach.
Nonetheless, the tools we develop in this section may be used in other examples. Specifically, starting with alternative tie-breaking to avoid sinks, and using the dominant eigenvalues as a guide to the long-run behavior.

4.
Existence of Thresholds

The analysis in
Section

3
demonstrates the threshold function for a specific game, but does not give a general technique for computing thresholds, nor shows that they always exist.
In this section we present our main result, namely that every game has a threshold function.

The section is organized as follows. In
Section

4.1
we describe an invariant dubbed
the average property
which gives a lower bound on the threshold.
In
Section

4.2
we restrict the discussion to games played on directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), in which case there exists a unique function satisfying the average property, and it constitutes a threshold.
In
Section

4.3
we turn to general graphs, and show the existence of a threshold by a reduction to the setting of DAGs. In
Section

4.4
we show that the threshold satisfies the average property, and is obtained as the point-wise maximum over all functions satisfying this property. Finally, in
Section

4.5
we use this characterization to compute the threshold using mixed-integer linear programming (MILP).

4.1.
The Average Property

Consider a game
\mathcal{G}=\langle G,\lambda,T\rangle
, and assume it has a threshold
\mathsf{Th}\colon{V\to[0,1]}
. For a sink
v\in T
, it holds that
\mathsf{Th}(v)=0
, since starting at the target, 🚺
1
instantly wins for any initial budget. For a sink
v\notin T
, we have
\mathsf{Th}(v)=1
, since 🚺
1
instantly loses for any initial budget.

For a non-sink
v\in V
,
\mathsf{Th}(v)
relates to the minimum and maximum values of
\mathsf{Th}
among
v
’s neighbors as follows. Intuitively, if 🚺
1
wins the bidding at
v
, it is optimal for her to choose the next vertex to have a minimal threshold. Similarly, 🚹
2
would choose the maximal threshold. As we show in this section, the budget needed
during the bidding phase
in
v
for 🚺
1
to win the game turns out to be exactly the average of these two values, and so the threshold (which is the budget before WR) is obtained by applying the reverse map

\mathfrak{R}^{-1}

on this average (where
\hbox{\hskip 7.27084pt\hskip-7.27084pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}^{-1}$}\hskip-7.27084pt\hskip-0.05554pt\raisebox{4.69559pt}{\hbox{$\hskip-8.5pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-0.05554pt\hskip 7.27084pt}(x)=\frac{x-\lambda}{1-2\lambda}
).
We remark that similar average properties typically arise in bidding games
(
Avni and Henzinger, 2020
)
.

Definition 4.1.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph,
T\subseteq V
a subset of the sinks, and
f\colon V\to[0,1]
. For a non-sink
v\in V
, let

v^{+}=\argmax_{u\in\Gamma(v)}f(u)\quad\text{ and }\quad v^{-}=\argmin_{u\in\Gamma(v)}f(u)

(we choose arbitrarily if the extrema are not unique).
Let

f_{\mathrm{avg}}(v)=\frac{f(v^{+})+f(v^{-})}{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\ f_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)=\hbox{\hskip 7.27084pt\hskip-7.27084pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}^{-1}$}\hskip-7.27084pt\hskip-0.05554pt\raisebox{4.69559pt}{\hbox{$\hskip-8.5pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-0.05554pt\hskip 7.27084pt}(f_{\mathrm{avg}}(v))\

We say that
f
satisfies the
average property
if for every
v\in V
:

•

If
v
is a sink, then

f(v)=\begin{cases}0&v\in T\\
1&v\notin T\end{cases}
.

•

If
v
is not a sink, then

f(v)=\begin{cases}0&f_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)<0\\
f_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)&f_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)\in[0,1]\\
1&f_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)>1\\
\end{cases}

Equivalently,
f(v)=\max(\min(f_{\mathrm{pre}}(v),1),0)

Note that the “artificial” introduction of
1
and
0
as limits makes sense both because
f
’s range is
[0,1]
, but also semantically. For example, if
f_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)<0
, it can be viewed as “🚺
1
does not even need
0
in order to win, so
0
is certainly enough”. Formally, since
\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}(x)
is an increasing function, we have in this case that
\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}(0)>\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}(f_{\mathrm{pre}}(v))=f_{\mathrm{avg}}(v)
. Therefore, starting with budget
0
, during the first bidding 🚺
1
has more than
f_{\mathrm{avg}}(v)
. In the following we show this is enough for 🚺
1
to win. The analysis for
f_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)>1
is similar.

The following lemma provides a clear view of the motivation for the average property and its relation to thresholds. Intuitively, it states that if
f
satisfies the average property, then starting from
x_{\mathrm{init}}>f(v_{0})
, 🚺
1
can guarantee that the current budget always remains above
f(v)
, and dually for 🚹
2
staying below
f(v_{0})
.
At first glance, this may seem to suggest that every function satisfying the average property is a threshold. This, however, is generally false: there may be multiple such functions, while the threshold is clearly unique.

Lemma 4.2.

Let
\mathcal{G}=\langle G,v_{0},\lambda,T\rangle
be a game, and let
f
be a function satisfying the average property.
There exist strategies
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
for Players 1 and 2, respectively, such that the following holds.

(1)

If
x_{\mathrm{init}}>f(v_{0})
then for every strategy
\sigma^{\prime}_{2}
of 🚹
2
, every configuration
(v,x)
in
\texttt{play}(\sigma_{1},\sigma^{\prime}_{2},v_{0},x_{\mathrm{init}})
satisfies
x>f(v)
.

(2)

If
x_{\mathrm{init}}<f(v_{0})
then for every strategy
\sigma^{\prime}_{1}
of 🚺
1
, every configuration
(v,x)
in
\texttt{play}(\sigma^{\prime}_{1},\sigma_{2},v_{0},x_{\mathrm{init}})
satisfies
x<f(v)
.

Proof.

Assume
x_{\mathrm{init}}>f(v_{0})
. We describe
\sigma_{1}
inductively. Let
(v,x)
be a configuration such that
v
is not a sink and
x>f(v)
. Then
f(v)<1
, and in particular
f(v)\geq f_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)
(as either
f(v)=f_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)
or
f(v)=0>f_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)
). After WR, 🚺
1
has budget

\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}(x)>\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}(f(v))\geq\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}(f_{\mathrm{pre}}(v))=f_{\mathrm{avg}}(v)

We now describe the bid of 🚺
1
. Let

f_{\mathrm{diff}}(v)=\frac{f(v^{+})-f(v^{-})}{2}

1
bids
f_{\mathrm{diff}}(v)
(note that
f_{\mathrm{diff}}(v)\leq f_{\mathrm{avg}}(v)
). If she wins the bidding, she moves to
v^{-}
, at which point her budget is
x^{\prime}
satisfying

As desired.
Dually, if 🚺
1
loses the bidding, then 🚹
2
bid more than
f_{\mathrm{diff}}(v)
, so 🚺
1
’s new budget is
x^{\prime}
satisfying

x^{\prime}>\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}(x)+f_{\mathrm{diff}}(v)>f_{\mathrm{avg}}(v)+f_{\mathrm{diff}}(v)=f(v^{+})

and the invariant is maintained regardless of the vertex 🚹
2
chooses to move to (since
v^{+}
has maximal value of
f
among the neighbors).

Next assume
x_{\mathrm{init}}<f(v_{0})
, we describe
\sigma_{2}
. Given
(v,x)
such that
x<f(v)
, we have in particular
f(v)>0
, and so
f(v)\leq f_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)
. After WR, 🚺
1
has budget

\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}(x)<\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}(f(v))\leq\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}(f_{\mathrm{pre}}(v))=f_{\mathrm{avg}}(v)=f(v^{+})-f_{\mathrm{diff}}(v)\leq 1-f_{\mathrm{diff}}(v)
.
Thus, 🚹
2
has budget at least
f_{\mathrm{diff}}(v)
. He bids that amount, and upon winning moves to
v^{+}
.
If 🚺
1

f

If 🚺
1

Consider a function
f
satisfying the average property, and recall that 🚺
1
wins in a play if and only if a vertex
v\in T
is reached. Such vertices satisfy

by
Definition

4.1
.
Thus, if every configuration

T

Corollary 4.3.

Let
\mathcal{G}

In this section we restrict attention to directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), and show the existence of thresholds in this case. We rely on these results in

Lemma 4.4.

Proof.

We start by constructing a function

and otherwise

on the sinks.
We proceed by defining

If

\mathsf{Th}(v)=1
. The strategy given by

4.2
maintains
\mathsf{Th}(v)<x\leq 1
, thus winning for 🚺
1
.
∎

We now turn our attention to what happens when the initial budget equals exactly the threshold.

Proposition 4.6.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a game played on a DAG. Then
\mathsf{Th}
is either 1-strong or 2-strong, and satisfies the following.

•

For a sink
v
, it is 1-strong if and only if
v\in T
.

•

For a non-sink
v
and
\lambda=0
, it is 1-strong if and only if
T
is reachable from
v
(equivalently,
\mathsf{Th}(v)<1
).

•

For a non-sink
v
and
\lambda>0
, it is 1-strong if and only if
\mathsf{Th}_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)\leq 1
.

Proof.

We consider the different cases mentioned.

The case that
v
is a sink

The thresholds
0
and
1
for
v\in T
and
v\notin T
respectively, are trivially 1-strong and 2-strong.

The case that
v
is not a sink and
\lambda=0

It is shown in
(
Avni et al., 2021
)
that
T
is reachable from
v
if and only if
\mathsf{Th}(v)<1
. If it is not, then the threshold is clearly 2-strong. Assume
T
is reachable from
v
, and we show that
\mathsf{Th}(v)
is 1-strong, by induction from the sinks backwards.

We claim that for every
u\in\Gamma(v)
, we have that
T
is reachable from
u
if and only if
\mathsf{Th}(u)
is 1-strong. Indeed, if
u
is a sink then
T
is reachable from
u
if and only if
u\in T
if and only if
\mathsf{Th}(u)
is 1-strong; otherwise, the equivalence follows from the induction hypothesis. Note that, since
v^{-}
is defined to be chosen arbitrarily among the neighbors of
v
with the lowest thresholds, we can choose it to have a 1-strong or a weak threshold when possible (intuitively, we consider a 2-strong thresholds “higher” than other thresholds of the same value). Under this convention, assume by way of contradiction that
T
is not reachable from
v^{-}
. Then
\mathsf{Th}(v^{-})=1
is 2-strong and so
\mathsf{Th}(u)=1
is a 2-strong threshold for all
u\in\Gamma(v)
. In this case,
T
is not reachable from any such
u
, in contradiction to it being reachable from
v
. Therefore,
T
is reachable from
v^{-}
, and so
\mathsf{Th}(v^{-})
is 1-strong. It remains to show that 🚺
1
has a winning strategy from
(v,\mathsf{Th}(v))
. Indeed, the strategy of bidding
\mathsf{Th}_{\mathrm{diff}}(v)
wins similarly to before: Upon winning the bidding she has exactly
\mathsf{Th}(v^{-})
, which wins from
v^{-}
. Upon losing the bidding she has strictly more than
\mathsf{Th}(v^{+})
and so she wins from any possible next vertex.

The case that
v
is not a sink and
\lambda>0

We show that
\mathsf{Th}(v)
is 1-strong if and only if
\mathsf{Th}_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)\leq 1
.

Assume
\mathsf{Th}_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)\leq 1
, and consider the configuration
(v,\mathsf{Th}(v))
. 🚺
1
’s budget during the first bidding, namely
\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}(\mathsf{Th}(v))
, is at least
\mathsf{Th}_{\mathrm{avg}}(v)
, and she wins by bidding
\mathsf{Th}_{\mathrm{diff}}(v)
similarly to before, provided that
\mathsf{Th}(v^{-})
is 1-strong. Indeed,
\mathsf{Th}(v^{-})\leq\mathsf{Th}_{\mathrm{avg}}(v)\ =\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}(\mathsf{Th}_{\mathrm{pre}}(v))<1
, the strict inequality holding due to
\lambda>0
. If
v^{-}
is a sink, it follows that
\mathsf{Th}(v^{-})=0
, and it is 1-strong. Otherwise,
\mathsf{Th}_{\mathrm{pre}}(v^{-})\leq 1
and so
\mathsf{Th}(v^{-})
is 1-strong by the induction hypothesis.

If
\mathsf{Th}_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)>1
, then
\mathsf{Th}(v)<\mathsf{Th}_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)
. During the first bid 🚺
1
has
\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}(\mathsf{Th}(v))<\hbox{\hskip 4.80554pt\hskip-3.68056pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}$}\hskip-3.68056pt\hskip-4.80554pt\raisebox{4.68054pt}{\hbox{$\hskip 1.0pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-4.80554pt\hskip 4.80554pt}(\mathsf{Th}_{\mathrm{pre}}(v))=\mathsf{Th}_{\mathrm{avg}}(v)
, and 🚹
2
wins as before.
∎

Note that if
\mathsf{Th}(v)<1
then
\mathsf{Th}_{\mathrm{pre}}(v)\leq 1
, so we have the following.

Corollary 4.7.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a reachability game played on a DAG. Whenever
\mathsf{Th}(v)<1
, it is 1-strong.

4.3.
Games Played on General Graphs

We are now ready for our main result.

Theorem 4.8.

Every game
\mathcal{G}=\langle G,\lambda,T\rangle
has a threshold function which satisfies the average property.

Proof.

We construct a function
\mathsf{Th}(v)
, show that it satisfies the average property, and finally show that it constitutes a threshold function.

The first step is to reduce the setting to that of DAGs. Denote
G=(V,E)
and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We turn to define a game that is played on a DAG (specifically, the unravelling of
G
for
n
steps) and, intuitively, corresponds to the objective of winning in
\mathcal{G}
within at most
n
steps.
Consider the DAG
\mathcal{D}_{n}=(V\times\{0,\ldots,n\},E_{n})
, where
E_{n}=\{(u_{i},v_{i+1})\mid(u,v)\in E,0\leq i\leq n-1\}
. As an example, the underlying graph of the game depicted in
Figure

5
yields the DAG depicted in
Figure

4
for
n=2
.

Next, we define the game
\mathcal{G}_{n}=\langle\mathcal{D}_{n},\lambda,T\times\{0,\ldots,n\}\rangle
. By
Lemma

4.4
we have that
\mathcal{G}_{n}
has a threshold function
\mathsf{Th}_{n}
.

Let
v\in V
. We consider the relation between
\mathsf{Th}_{n}((v,0))
and
\mathsf{Th}_{n+1}((v,0))
. Assume 🚺
1
has a winning strategy
\sigma_{1}
for
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, starting in configuration
((v,0),x_{\mathrm{init}})
. Following
\sigma_{1}
ensures, in particular, that the play does not reach
(V\times\{n\})\setminus(T\times\{n\})
, as those are sinks not belonging to the target.
Observe that
\mathcal{D}_{n+1}
is obtained from
\mathcal{D}_{n}
by (possibly) adding outgoing edges only from
(V\times\{n\})\setminus(T\times\{n\})
.
The strategy
\sigma_{1}
therefore wins, starting in
((v,0),x_{\mathrm{init}})
, in
\mathcal{G}_{n+1}
as well. Intuitively, winning
\mathcal{G}
in at most
n
steps particularly wins it in at most
n+1
steps. Thus,
\mathsf{Th}_{n}((v,0))\geq\mathsf{Th}_{n+1}((v,0))
, i.e., the sequence
\{\mathsf{Th}_{n}((v,0))\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is non-increasing. This sequence is also bounded from below by
0
, and therefore converges. We define the threshold
\mathsf{Th}
for
\mathcal{G}
as the pointwise-limit

\mathsf{Th}(v)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{Th}_{n}((v,0))

Next, we prove that
\mathsf{Th}
satisfies the average property.
For a sink
v\in T
, we have
\mathsf{Th}_{n}((v,0))=0
for all
n
(since
(v,0)\in T\times\{0,\ldots,n\}
), and so
\mathsf{Th}(v)=0
as needed. For a sink
v\notin T
, we have
\mathsf{Th}_{n}(v)=1
for all
n
(since
(v,0)
is a sink in
\mathcal{D}_{n}
and does not belong to the target), and so
\mathsf{Th}(v)=1
as needed.

For the following, fix a non-sink
v
. We need to show (as per
Definition

4.1
) that

(1)

\mathsf{Th}(v)=\max\left(\min\left(\hbox{\hskip 7.27084pt\hskip-7.27084pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}^{-1}$}\hskip-7.27084pt\hskip-0.05554pt\raisebox{4.69559pt}{\hbox{$\hskip-8.5pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-0.05554pt\hskip 7.27084pt}\left(\frac{\mathsf{Th}(v^{+})+\mathsf{Th}(v^{-})}{2}\right),1\right),0\right)

Note that
(v,0)
is not a sink in
\mathcal{D}_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
. For every
n\geq 1
, let

v^{+}_{n}=\argmax_{u\in\Gamma(v)}\mathsf{Th}_{n}(u)\quad\text{ and }\quad v^{-}_{n}=\argmin_{u\in\Gamma(v)}\mathsf{Th}_{n}(u)

For every
u\in\Gamma(v)
, consider the sub-DAG of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
starting in
(u,1)
. Observe that this sub-DAG is isomorphic to the sub-DAG of
\mathcal{D}_{n-1}
starting in
(u,0)
(with the difference only being the indices of the levels). It follows that
\mathsf{Th}_{n}((u,1))=\mathsf{Th}_{n-1}((u,0))
. By the average property for
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, we have

\mathsf{Th}_{n}((v,0))=\max\left(\min\left(\hbox{\hskip 7.27084pt\hskip-7.27084pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}^{-1}$}\hskip-7.27084pt\hskip-0.05554pt\raisebox{4.69559pt}{\hbox{$\hskip-8.5pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-0.05554pt\hskip 7.27084pt}\left(\frac{\mathsf{Th}_{n-1}((v^{+}_{n},0))+\mathsf{Th}_{n-1}((v^{-}_{n},0))}{2}\right),1\right),0\right)

Note that this is a continuous function of
\mathsf{Th}_{n-1}((v^{+}_{n},0))
and
\mathsf{Th}_{n-1}((v^{-}_{n},0))
.
In order to show
Equation

1
, it is therefore enough to show

(2)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{Th}_{n-1}((v^{+}_{n},0))=\mathsf{Th}(v^{+})

(3)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{Th}_{n-1}((v^{-}_{n},0))=\mathsf{Th}(v^{-})

We show that
Equation

2
holds, and the proof for
Equation

3
is analogous.
Note that
v^{+}_{n}
might be a different vertex for each
n
, and so the left hand side of
Equation

2
does not describe the limit of thresholds for a single vertex.
Intuitively, however, there is a set of vertices that appear infinitely often in this limit whose corresponding limits are all equal, which enables us to conclude the claim.

Formally, let
N^{+}(v)=\{u\in\Gamma(v)\mid\mathsf{Th}(u)=\mathsf{Th}(v^{+})\}
. Then for every
u^{+}\in N^{+}(v)
and
u^{\prime}\in\Gamma(v)\setminus N^{+}(v)
, we have that
\mathsf{Th}(u^{\prime})<\mathsf{Th}(u^{+})
. By the definition of
\mathsf{Th}
as a limit, it follows that there exists
n_{u^{+},u^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathsf{Th}_{n}((u^{\prime},0))<\mathsf{Th}_{n}((u^{+},0))
for every
n>n_{u^{+},u^{-}}
.
Taking
n_{0}
as the maximum over all such
n_{u^{+},u^{-}}
, it follows that
v^{+}_{n}\in N^{+}(v)
for every
n>n_{0}
. Since
N^{+}(v)
is finite, and
\mathsf{Th}_{n-1}((u,0))\overset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathsf{Th}(v^{+})
for every
u\in N^{+}(v)
,
we have that
\mathsf{Th}_{n-1}((v^{+}_{n},0))\overset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathsf{Th}(v^{+})
as well.

Finally, we show that
\mathsf{Th}
is a threshold function for
\mathcal{G}
.
Assume
\mathcal{G}
starts in
(v,x_{\mathrm{init}})
.

If
x_{\mathrm{init}}>\mathsf{Th}(v)
then there exists
n
such that
x_{\mathrm{init}}>\mathsf{Th}_{n}(v)
, meaning 🚺
1
has a strategy that wins in at most
n
steps, and in particular wins in
\mathcal{G}
.

If
x_{\mathrm{init}}<\mathsf{Th}(v)
, then since
\mathsf{Th}
satisfies the threshold property, it follows from
Corollary

4.3
that 🚹
2
has a winning strategy.
∎

4.4.
Characterization of Thresholds in Terms of the Average Property

Recall that for games played on DAGs, the threshold is the unique function satisfying the average property, and it can be computed inductively from the sinks. For general games with
\lambda=0
, it is known
(
Avni et al., 2021
)
that there is still a unique function satisfying the average property, and that finding the threshold is in
\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}
(and in
\mathsf{P}
for graphs with out-degree
2
).

In stark contrast, this uniqueness no longer holds in Robin Hood games for
\lambda>0
, as we now demonstrate.
Consider the game in
Figure

5
, with
\lambda=\frac{1}{4}
. It can be verified that the numbers on the vertices are the thresholds. However, for any
t\in[0,1]
, the function defined by
f(v_{0})=t
and
f(v)=\mathsf{Th}(v)
for
v\neq v_{0}
satisfies the average property. Indeed, this can be easily checked for
v\neq v_{0}
(and also follows from
Lemma

4.4
, since from without
v_{0}
the game is a DAG).
For
v_{0}
, note that
\hbox{\hskip 7.27084pt\hskip-7.27084pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}^{-1}$}\hskip-7.27084pt\hskip-0.05554pt\raisebox{4.69559pt}{\hbox{$\hskip-8.5pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-0.05554pt\hskip 7.27084pt}(x)=2(x-\frac{1}{4})=2x-\frac{1}{2}
, so

f_{\mathrm{pre}}(v_{0})=\hbox{\hskip 7.27084pt\hskip-7.27084pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}^{-1}$}\hskip-7.27084pt\hskip-0.05554pt\raisebox{4.69559pt}{\hbox{$\hskip-8.5pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-0.05554pt\hskip 7.27084pt}(f_{\mathrm{avg}}(v_{0}))=\hbox{\hskip 7.27084pt\hskip-7.27084pt\hbox{$\mathfrak{R}^{-1}$}\hskip-7.27084pt\hskip-0.05554pt\raisebox{4.69559pt}{\hbox{$\hskip-8.5pt\includegraphics[width=8.61108pt]{robinhood1.pdf}$}}\hskip-0.05554pt\hskip 7.27084pt}\left(\frac{t+\frac{1}{2}}{2}\right)=t=f(v_{0})

and since
f(v_{0})\in[0,1]
, the average property holds.

Theorem 4.9.

Consider a game
\mathcal{G}
. The threshold
\mathsf{Th}
is the point-wise maximum over the functions satisfying the average property.

Proof.

Let
A
be the set of functions satisfying the average property and let
m(v)=\sup_{f\in A}f(v)
. Since
\mathsf{Th}
satisfies the average property, we have
\mathsf{Th}(v)\leq m(v)
. We additionally have
f(v)\leq\mathsf{Th}(v)
for every
f\in A
; indeed, assume by way of contradiction that
\mathsf{Th}(v)<f(v)
, and let
\mathsf{Th}(v)<x_{\mathrm{init}}<f(v)
. By the threshold definition, 🚺
1
has a winning strategy, but by
Corollary

4.3
, 🚹
2
also does. Therefore,
m(v)\leq\mathsf{Th}(v)
, and overall
\mathsf{Th}(v)=m(v)
.
∎

4.5.
MILP for Computing the Threshold

While
Theorem

4.8

shows the existence of thresholds, it uses a limit and is therefore not constructive. However, using
Theorem

4.9
we can algorithmically compute the threshold using mixed-integer linear programming (MILP).

Theorem 4.10.

Given a game
\mathcal{G}
, we can efficiently construct a MILP instance
\mathcal{I}
whose solution is the threshold function for
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

Consider a game
\mathcal{G}
with vertices
V=v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
.
The average property can be readily expressed as a set of constraints on the variables
\mathsf{Th}(v)
for every
v\in V
containing linear and
\min
/
\max
expressions (as per
Definition

4.1
).
Observe that this complies with MILP, since e.g., the expression
\min\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
can be removed by introducing a new variable
X
, a variable
b\in\{0,1\}
and the following constraints:

X\leq x_{1}\wedge X\leq x_{2}\wedge X\geq x_{1}-2M(1-b)\wedge X\geq x_{2}-2Mb

Where
M

6.
Discussion and Future Research

Robin Hood bidding games incorporate a regulating entity into bidding games, allowing the simulation of realistic settings that cannot be captured with standard bidding games.
The introduction of wealth redistribution comes at a technical cost: analyzing the behavior of the game becomes much more involved (c.f.,
Section

3
). Nonetheless, we are able to show that the model retains the nice property of having a threshold function, albeit the game may become undetermined exactly at the threshold.

Apart from establishing the theoretical and algorithmic foundations of this setting, our results shed light on various properties of the optimal strategies of the players. In particular, we show that when starting above the threshold, 🚺
1
intuitively plays on an unwinding of the game to a DAG in order to reach
T
. However, when playing exactly from the threshold, 🚺
1
needs a path to
T
along which she can afford to win all the biddings.

A natural future direction is to extend our framework to infinite-duration games, e.g., Büchi and parity games. For standard bidding games, winning in infinite-duration games reduces to an analysis of strongly connected components
(
Avni et al., 2019
)
. In the Robin Hood case, however, this no longer applies, suggesting that showing the existence of a threshold function is nontrivial, if there even exists one.

One view of WR is as a mechanism for changing the budgets of players outside the bidding phase. A different mechanism for achieving this is, introduced in
(
Avni et al., 2024
)
, designates special vertices where agents can
charge
their budget. From an economical perspective, this can be seen as vertices where a player performs some “work” and receives a salary.
Thus, combining the models would allow us to specify a richer economic dynamics. It would be interesting to examine whether this model retains nice algorithmic properties.

A different research direction concerns viewing wealth redistribution as a form of
discounting

(
Broome, 1994
)
: in discounting, the value of future rewards decreases exponentially with time, according to some discount factor
\lambda
. Wealth redistribution can then be viewed as a discounting factor on the difference of the budgets of the agents. It may be of interest to consider other models of discounting in bidding games, e.g., a reward model for the agents where future-budgets are worth less than current budgets.
