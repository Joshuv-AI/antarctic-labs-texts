Antibiotics for Delirium in Older Adults With No Clear Urinary Tract Infection: A Multicentre, Pragmatic, Randomized Trial—Vanguard Results
Authors: Michael Fralick, Nathan M. Stall, Katarina Zorcic, Christina Reppas‐Rindlisbacher, Eric Kaplovitch, Amy S. Li, Derek R. MacFadden, Daniel Kobewka, Lucas Castellani, Patricia Parsons, Allison McGeer, Janine McCready, Bryant Lim, Emil Hodzic‐Santor, Christopher Kandel, Hania Siddiqui, Hania Siddiqui, Harshita Chaudhary, Afsaneh Raissi, Tamara Van Bakel, Maureen Taylor, Enoch Choo, Sharrmini Chandrasegaram, Natalie Walde, Irene Amadu, Logan Coccimiglio, Malaz Idris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIt is unclear whether antibiotics improve outcomes for older adults with delirium who have pyuria or bacteriuria in the absence of other signs or symptoms of a urinary tract infection.MethodsOur study is a multicenter, parallel‐group, single‐blinded, non‐inferiority pragmatic randomized trial. The trial included a vanguard phase of the first 30 participants, and herein we report the aggregate results from this phase. We included hospitalized adults aged 60 years and older who had delirium and pyuria and/or bacteriuria. We excluded patients with fever, upper or lower urinary tract symptoms, an indwelling foley for more than 72 h, more than 24 h of antibiotics at the time of eligibility, or another indication for antibiotics (e.g., pneumonia). The main outcomes of the vanguard phase were rates of participant recruitment, protocol adherence, and study withdrawal. Our initial goals were 4 patients randomized monthly per site, greater than 95% adherence to allocation, and less than 5% withdrawal rate.ResultsThe median age was 86 years (interquartile range [IQR] 81–92), 68% were female, and most (79%) had either minor or major neurocognitive impairment. Of the participating sites that recruited at least one participant, the recruitment rates ranged from 0.05 to 1.8 participants per month. Adherence to allocation was 100%. Two participants (6.7%) withdrew. For participants randomized to receive antibiotics, the median duration was 5 days (IQR 5–7). By Day 7, delirium had resolved in 39% of the participants who remained hospitalized; 25% of participants were discharged, 32% had ongoing delirium, and 4% did not have a delirium assessment available. By Day 7, 1 (3.6%) developed C. difficile , 1 (3.6%) died, and 1 (3.6%) developed bacteremia.ConclusionWe completed our vanguard phase with 100% adherence to allocation and withdrawal was near the target of 5%. The recruitment rate varied widely and overall was lower than anticipated.

Introduction

Delirium, an acute confusional state, afflicts approximately one‐third of hospitalized older adults and carries a 1‐year risk of mortality exceeding 50% [1, 2]. The work‐up for delirium includes a thorough evaluation for precipitating factors, as well as laboratory and imaging investigations to identify and treat potential underlying causes [3, 4]. A urinalysis and urine culture are frequently collected in this clinical scenario, and are often positive because bacteriuria and pyuria are common in older adults [5]. These positive findings often reflect bacterial colonization of the bladder rather than acute infection (i.e., asymptomatic bacteriuria) [5]. However, distinguishing between symptomatic and asymptomatic bacteriuria can be challenging as delirium can affect a patient's ability to communicate whether lower urinary tract symptoms are present [6]. The Infectious Diseases Society of America recommends against treatment with antibiotics for patients found to have pyuria or bacteriuria in the absence of lower or upper urinary tract symptoms; however, there is wide variability in clinical practice [7].

In one of the largest studies to date, the proportion of hospitalized patients with asymptomatic bacteriuria who received antibiotics exceeded 80% at 35 of the 46 included hospitals, with multiple hospitals having rates as high as 100% [8]. Factors associated with the use of antibiotics included older age, history of dementia, and altered mental status. There exists a crucial knowledge gap: Do patients with delirium and pyuria or bacteriuria, but no other signs of urinary tract infection, have an infection that is contributing to delirium? Or do the risks associated with antibiotic use outweigh any potential benefits in this clinical scenario?

A recent systematic review found a paucity of evidence to guide the management of delirium in patients with pyuria or bacteriuria. There was no evidence that treatment with antibiotics improved clinical outcomes for older adults with pyuria or bacteriuria and altered mental status without systemic signs of infection or urinary symptoms [9]. In two of the included studies, participants who received antibiotics had worse outcomes compared to those who did not receive antibiotics; however, both of these studies were observational, and the worse outcomes may reflect selection bias [10, 11]. Our objective was to conduct the Antibiotics for Delirium in Older adults with No clear Urinary Tract infection (A‐DONUT) trial, which is a pragmatic randomized trial to assess whether routine care without antibiotics is non‐inferior to routine care with antibiotics for resolution of delirium in hospitalized older adults with delirium and either pyuria or bacteriuria without overt symptoms or signs of a lower or upper urinary tract infection. We planned a priori to include a vanguard phase to assess for feasibility, given the anticipated challenges of recruiting older adults hospitalized with delirium.

Methods

Trial Design

We conducted a multicentre, parallel‐group, single‐blinded, non‐inferiority pragmatic randomized trial. The first 30 participants randomized were included in the vanguard phase (Figure 1). We followed the CONSORT guidelines for reporting results from feasibility trials [12]. The trial received research ethics board approval at all participating sites and was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov prior to initiation (NCT06004739).

CONSORT diagram for the first 30 participants randomized in vanguard phase of the A‐DONUT trial.

Participants

We included patients aged 60 years and older who were hospitalized, had delirium, and had pyuria (defined as white blood cells detected on urinalysis or dipstick) or bacteriuria (defined as bacteria grown on urine culture). A recent meta‐analysis demonstrated that the sensitivity of a urine dipstick for bacteriuria is 90% (95% CI 84%–94%) [13]. Patients were recruited from five participating hospital sites in Canada from May 18, 2024 (date of first vanguard patient randomized) to February 12, 2025 (date of last vanguard patient randomized). There were an additional two sites where zero patients were recruited. We excluded patients with fever (temperature > 37.9°C or > 100.2°F) in the preceding 48 h; lower urinary tract symptoms (new dysuria, urgency, polyuria, incontinence, suprapubic pain); upper urinary tract symptoms (such as costovertebral tenderness); indwelling urinary catheter for more than 72 h; receipt of more than 24 h of antibiotics at the time of eligibility assessment; another indication for antibiotics (e.g., pneumonia) that can also treat common urinary pathogens; or patients previously enrolled in the trial. When a substitute decision maker was unavailable, deferred consent was permitted with a 96‐h time window from enrollment during which consent could be obtained. Deferred consent is common for clinical trials of patients with conditions that affect capacity for decision making (e.g., stroke, critical illness) and/or low‐risk interventions [14, 15, 16].

Intervention and Comparator

Participants were randomized in a 1:1 allocation to antibiotics or no antibiotics. The antibiotic choice, dose, and duration were left to the discretion of the most responsible healthcare provider caring for the participant. When the treating healthcare provider solicited guidance for treatment duration, the suggested treatment duration was no more than 5 days [17].

Outcome

We had three main outcomes for our vanguard phase: rate of recruitment (target: four participants per hospital per month), adherence to allocation arm (target: at least 95%), and rate of withdrawal (target: less than 5%). Our target rate of recruitment was based on our collective experience on inpatient General Internal Medicine units at the participating hospitals. We estimated that there would be approximately 3–4 eligible patients per week, with successful consent obtained from 30% of eligible participants (based on prevailing rates in other clinical trials involving our team) [18, 19]. Our targets for adherence and study withdrawal were also informed by recently completed inpatient pragmatic trials [18, 19].

During the vanguard, the planned primary outcome for the full scale trial was a composite of discharge from hospital by Day 7 or absence of delirium based on an in‐person assessment by Day 7 for those who remained hospitalized. Delirium was assessed on Day 7 by trained research personnel using the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM). Presence of delirium was defined as a positive CAM score; absence was defined as either a negative CAM score at Day 7 or hospital discharge prior to Day 7. The Day 7 assessment was selected for two reasons. First, it was informed by a recently completed survey of 297 physicians that asked, “In your opinion, if an antibiotic was started in a patient with delirium and bacteriuria, what delay do you expect before seeing an improvement?” The vast majority (95%) of respondents indicated they would expect to see a response within 7 days, with 64% indicating as early as 3 days [20]. Second, a 7‐day timeframe is similar to the duration of treatment for a patient with cystitis, which is typically 3–5 days. A second follow‐up at Day 30 was conducted virtually or in person among patients who remained in hospital. The 30 day outcomes included readmission, death, C. difficile , subsequent antibiotics, or subsequent diagnosis of delirium.

Sample Size

The sample size for our vanguard phase was 30 patients, informed by the funding obtained.

Randomization

Randomization was performed centrally using REDCap to ensure allocation concealment. Randomization was stratified by hospital site with a 1:1 allocation ratio using permuted blocks of variable size.

Blinding

Our study was single‐blinded with the primary outcome (delirium) assessed by a study team member blinded to the participant's allocation arm. It was not feasible to include a placebo because there are many different antibiotics available to treat a potential urinary tract infection, and each antibiotic has varying routes of administration and dosing. Our approach of foregoing placebo aligns with recently published pragmatic trials of hospitalized patients related to infectious disease [19].

Statistical Analysis

We used descriptive statistics to summarize patient characteristics and study outcomes. We purposefully reported all results in the aggregate to minimize the risk of a Type I error (preserve alpha).

Results

Our vanguard phase included five sites that were able to recruit at least one participant between May 18, 2024 and February 12, 2025. Of the 226 potentially eligible participants, 52 were approached and 30 randomized (Figure 1). Across the five sites, the number of participants successfully recruited per month ranged from 0.05 to 1.8 (0.05, 0.5, 1.0, 1.2, and 1.8).

The median age was 86 years (interquartile range [IQR] 81–92), 68% were female, 68% were white, 7% were Black, 11% were Latin American, and 11% were Asian (Table 1). Regarding comorbid conditions, 39% had cardiovascular disease, 43% had a major neurocognitive disorder, 36% had a mild neurocognitive disorder, 21% had a prior history of delirium, and 18% had a previous stroke or TIA. Of the included participants, 89% were identified as frail, scoring at least 5 (mild frailty) on the Clinical Frailty Scale [21].

Discussion

We were able to complete the vanguard phase of our multicentre, parallel‐group, single‐blinded, non‐inferiority pragmatic randomized trial comparing antibiotics to no antibiotics for hospitalized older adults with delirium and pyuria and/or bacteriuria in the absence of overt signs or symptoms of a urinary tract infection. We were able to achieve our planned adherence to allocation (100%), and the study withdrawal rate was near our target of 5%. Our rate of recruitment was variable and lower than anticipated, though it does align with the AID‐ICU trial evaluating haloperidol in the Intensive Care Unit for the treatment of delirium, which recruited 1000 participants across 34 sites over a 4‐year period [22].

Before initiating our trial, we estimated (based on our collective clinical experience) that each hospital would identify approximately four eligible patients per week. This proved to be an overestimate, likely influenced by availability bias and the inherent difficulty in accurately gauging the true prevalence of delirium and bacteriuria or pyuria in hospitalized patients aged 60 years and older. Even when eligible patients were identified, often their substitute decision maker would decline enrollment because of a desire to treat with antibiotics. Prior studies have shown this desire is often driven by the perception that bacteriuria causes delirium, and that a short course of antibiotics is relatively safe [7]. While less common, there were also instances wherein the attending clinician would indicate they didn't want their patient to be considered for the trial because a decision had already been made to treat with antibiotics.

For the full scale trial, we will draw on insights from the vanguard phase to update our recruitment strategy and improve our rate of recruitment. Having a list of patients hospitalized in the past 24 h with a diagnosis of delirium proved the most effective way to identify potential participants. The hospital with the highest recruitment rate in the vanguard phase leveraged an existing resource that identifies patients hospitalized in the prior 24 h with suspected delirium; this approach was subsequently adopted at the other sites where technically feasible. Screening urine culture results to identify inpatients with bacteriuria was trialed at multiple study sites, but was ineffective because of the high number of abnormal urine cultures and the turnaround time for culture results often exceeding 24 h. The hospital with a recruitment rate of 0.05 participants per month lacked a research coordinator for nearly the entire vanguard period because of a staffing shortage, highlighting the importance of dedicated research coordinator support.

Our low rate of recruitment also necessitates expansion to additional hospital sites. We have identified an additional 10 hospitals committed to participating. Our vanguard phase reinforced the importance of having hospitals with engagement in the disciplines of general internal medicine, geriatric medicine, and infectious disease. New sites joining the trial will benefit from site leads in these specialties.

We have also made additional updates to our study protocol. First, we have added an exclusion criteria for patients who receive a single dose of a long‐acting antibiotic (i.e., fosfomycin), because a single dose is sufficient for treatment of a UTI. Second, moving forward we will use the 3D‐CAM tool to assess delirium (our primary outcome), because 3D‐CAM has been used in other randomized trials related to inpatient delirium, is validated in this setting, and provides a structured mental status assessment [23, 24]. Third, we have reframed our primary outcome to resolution of delirium by Day 7 and for patients discharged home prior to Day 7, we will have phone call with the substitute decision maker to assess for delirium resolution using an adaptation of the validated SQiD questionnaire (Sensitivity 80%, specificity 71%, negative predictive value 91%) [25]. Fourth, we plan to conduct more frequent delirium assessments with the goal of daily assessments for each participant while in hospital until discharge or Day 7, whichever occurs first. The CAM and the 3D‐CAM use similar assessments and provide a uniform output (delirium positive or negative), allowing us to subsume vanguard phase participants into our full trial.

For the full scale trial, the estimated sample size is based on a non‐inferiority margin of 10%, which was informed by the “Non‐inferiority Clinical Trials to Establish Effectiveness” Guidance document from the FDA [26]. To ensure a clinically important and acceptable margin is utilized, we conducted a survey study of healthcare providers that confirmed the use of a 10% margin [27].

Our study has several limitations. One limitation is that we restricted the population to hospitalized older adults, and thus it is unknown whether results will apply to outpatients, including long‐term care home residents. Another limitation is that our sample size of 30 participants for the vanguard phase is relatively small, and was not informed by a sample size calculation. While it can be argued that a feasibility trial should be powered for a main feasibility outcome (e.g., rate of recruitment), our reasons for pursuing the full trial despite the lower‐than‐anticipated recruitment rate is threefold. First, we are confident the changes made to our study protocol will improve the rate of recruitment, as we saw the highest recruitment rates at sites that leveraged lists of patients with delirium who were recently hospitalized. Second, we have identified an additional 10 hospitals that are committed to participating. Third, our trial is novel and has the potential for practice‐changing results, as it is enriched with patients who are typically excluded (e.g., hospitalized older adults) or underrepresented (e.g., older women, patients with cognitive impairment) from most clinical trials.

We were able to complete the vanguard phase of A‐DONUT with 100% adherence and minimal withdrawal, but a slower‐than‐anticipated recruitment rate. We are optimistic that with changes to the protocol and our improved approach for identifying potentially eligible participants, we can successfully complete the full trial. A‐DONUT will be the first randomized trial to provide high‐quality evidence to guide the management of older adults with delirium and pyuria or bacteriuria without overt signs of a lower or upper urinary tract infection.
