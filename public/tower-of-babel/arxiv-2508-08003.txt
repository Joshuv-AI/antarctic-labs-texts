Counting Salem numbers arising from arithmetic hyperbolic orbifolds
Michelle Chu, Plinio G. P. Murillo, Otto Romero, Lola Thompson

Abstract
. The relationship between Salem numbers and short geodesics has been fruitful in quantitative studies of arithmetic hyperbolic orbifolds, particularly in dimensions 2 and 3. In this article, we push these connections even further. The primary goals are: (1) to bound the proportion of Salem numbers of degree up to n + 1 n+1 in the commensurability class of classical arithmetic lattices in any odd dimension n n ; (2) to improve lower bounds for the strong exponential growth of averages of multiplicities in the geodesic length spectrum of non-compact arithmetic orbifolds. In order to accomplish these goals, we bound, for a fixed square-free integer D D , the count of Salem numbers with minimal polynomial f f satisfying f ⁡ ( 1 ) ​ f ​ ( − 1 ) = − D f(1)f(-1)=-D in ℚ × / ℚ × 2 \mathbb{Q}^{\times}/\mathbb{Q}^{\times 2} . To do this, we make use of results on the distribution of Salem numbers, as well as classical methods for counting Pythagorean triples and Gauss’ lattice-counting argument. To this end, we give a generalization of the count of Pythagorean triples and provide an elementary proof which may be of independent interest.

1.
Introduction

A
Salem number
is a real algebraic integer
\lambda>1
such that all of its Galois conjugates except
\lambda^{-1}
have absolute value equal to 1. The minimal polynomial
f(x)
of a Salem number is necessarily symmetric and of even degree. In addition to the roots
\lambda
and
\lambda^{-1}
, all other roots appear in complex pairs along the unit circle.
Salem numbers arise in many areas of mathematics, and their study is closely related to the elusive Lehmer’s problem on Mahler measure.

The distribution of Salem numbers was studied by Götze and Gusakova in
[
GG20
]
, where they obtained an asymptotic count of the Salem numbers of fixed degree with bounded Mahler measure. In this article, we restrict this to count the Salem numbers of fixed degree with bounded Mahler measure whose minimal polynomials satisfy a certain geometrically-motivated condition.

Theorem A
.

Let
m
be a positive integer and
D
a square-free positive integer. The number of Salem polynomials
f
of degree
2m
for which
f(1)f(-1)\equiv-D
in
\mathbb{Q}^{\times}/\mathbb{Q}^{\times 2}
with Mahler measure
\leq Q
is bounded above by

\frac{\kappa(m,D)}{\pi\sqrt{D}}Q^{m-1}\log Q+O(Q^{m-1})

where
\kappa(m,D)
is a constant depending only on
D
and
m
.

The condition
f(1)f(-1)\equiv-D
has applications to the study of arithmetic hyperbolic orbifolds. Indeed, Bayer-Fluckiger
[
BF15
]
stated this condition as a necessary condition for the existence of an isometry in a quadratic space with characteristic polynomial
f
.
It is known that Salem numbers are closely related to the lengths of geodesics in arithmetic hyperbolic orbifolds.
Recent work of Emery, Ratcliffe, and Tschantz made this relationship more explicit.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
ERT19
, Theorem 1.1]

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
be a classical arithmetic lattice of the first type defined over a totally real number field

be a hyperbolic element of

, and

The theorem above implies that a Salem number

if

. We consider the following question.

Background

2.1.
Quadratic forms

Let
K
be a field. A
quadratic form
is a homogeneous polynomial in
n
variables of degree 2 with coefficients in
K
. It can be written as

q(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i}^{n}a_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}=x^{t}S_{q}x

where
S_{q}
is the symmetric matrix with diagonal entries
(S_{q})_{ii}=a_{ii}
and off-diagonal entries
(S_{q})_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}a_{ij}
. The
rank
of
q
is the number of variables
n
. The
determinant
of
q
is
\det(q)=\det(S_{q})
. We will assume that
q
is nondegenerate, that is,
S_{q}
is invertible.

We say that a quadratic form
q
represents
a\in K
if there is a vector
v\in K^{n}
satisfying
q(v)=a
. A quadratic form is
isotropic
if it represents 0 by a nonzero vector and it is
anisotropic
otherwise.

Two quadratic forms
q
and
q^{\prime}
over
K
are said to be
K
-equivalent if there exists a matrix
M\in\mathrm{GL}(n,K)
such that
M^{t}S_{q}M=S_{q^{\prime}}
. Any quadratic form over
K
is
K
-equivalent to a diagonal quadratic form
q^{\prime}
, which means
q^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x_{i}^{2}
. We write
q^{\prime}=\langle a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\rangle
.

Let
V=K^{n}
. Then the
quadratic space

(V,q)
has isometry group given by the orthogonal group

\mathrm{O}(q)=\mathrm{O}(q,K)=\{A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,K)\>|\>A^{t}S_{q}A=S_{q}\}.

We will focus on quadratic forms
q
defined over
K=\mathbb{Q}
.
The signature of
q
is the pair
(n_{p},n_{n})
where
n_{p}
is the number of positive eigenvalues of
S_{q}
and
n_{n}
is the number of negative eigenvalues.
Since
q
is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, we may scale
q
by a positive integer so that the coefficients and the determinant lie in
\mathbb{Z}
. Note that scaling does not change the rank, the signature, nor the isometry group.
Considering
(-1)
as a “prime” for our purposes, let
\det(q)=p_{1}^{\epsilon_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{\epsilon_{k}}
be the prime decomposition for
\det(q)
. The
reduced determinant
of
q
is the square-free integer
\det_{r}(q)=p_{1}^{\epsilon^{\prime}_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{\epsilon^{\prime}_{k}}
with
\epsilon^{\prime}_{i}=0
when
\epsilon_{i}
is even and
\epsilon^{\prime}_{i}=1
when
\epsilon_{i}
is odd.

2.2.
Hyperbolic space

The hyperbolic
n
-space
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is the simply connected Riemannian
n
-manifold in which the sectional curvature is constant
-1
. We will consider the hyperboloid model of hyperbolic space defined as follows.

Let
n\geq 2
and
q=\langle 1,\dots,1,-1\rangle
be the standard quadratic form with signature
(n,1)
. The bilinear form
B
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
associated with the quadratic form
q
is given by

{\displaystyle B(x,y)=\frac{q(x+y)-q(x)-q(y)}{2}}.

The set

\mathcal{S}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1};q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})=-1\}

forms a two-sheeted hyperboloid. We denote the positive sheet by

\mathcal{S}^{+}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})\in\mathcal{S};x_{n+1}>0\}.

The
hyperboloid model
of hyperbolic
n
-space is obtained by identifying
\mathbb{H}^{n}
with the
n
-manifold
\mathcal{S}^{+}
together with the Riemannian metric given by
d(x,y)=\arccos\left(B(x,y)\right)
.

The orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}(q,\mathbb{R})
preserves the hyperboloid
\mathcal{S}
. We identify the isometry group

\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{n})=\mathrm{PO}(q,\mathbb{R})=\mathrm{PO}(n,1)\cong\mathrm{SO}(q,\mathbb{R})=\mathrm{SO}(n,1)

with the isometry group of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
in this model. It has two connected components. The identity component consists of orientation-preserving isometries and the other component consists of orientation-reversing isometries.
We remark that one may construct isometric hyperboloid models for
\mathbb{H}^{n}
from any quadratic form
q
with signature
(n,1)
defined over a real field.

An element
\gamma\in\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
is called
hyperbolic
if there is a unique geodesic
L\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
, called the
axis
of
\gamma
, on which
\gamma
acts as a translation by a positive distance
\ell(\gamma)
, called the
translation length
of
\gamma
. In this case,
\gamma
has exactly
n-1
eigenvalues of norm one and two real eigenvalues not on the unit circle,
\lambda=e^{\ell(\gamma)}>1
and
\lambda^{-1}=e^{-\ell(\gamma)}<1
(see
[
CG74
]
).

A
lattice
is a discrete subgroup
\Gamma<\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
such that the quotient
\mathbb{H}^{n}/\Gamma
has finite volume. If
\mathbb{H}^{n}/\Gamma
is compact, we say that
\Gamma
is
cocompact
. Whenever
\Gamma
is a lattice, the quotient
\mathbb{H}^{n}/\Gamma
is a complete finite-volume hyperbolic orbifold. In this case, most elements of
\Gamma
are hyperbolic and the axes of hyperbolic elements map to closed geodesics in the quotient. The quotient
\mathbb{H}^{n}/\Gamma
will be a manifold whenever
\Gamma
is torsion free.

Two lattices
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
in
\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
are
commensurable
if there exists
g\in\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
such that
\Gamma_{1}\cap g\Gamma_{2}g^{-1}
has finite index in both
\Gamma_{1}
and
g\Gamma_{2}g^{-1}
.

2.3.
Arithmetic lattices

Let
k
be a totally real number field with ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{k}
. Consider a quadratic form
q:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with signature
(n,1)
defined over
k
with respect to the identity embedding
k\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We say that such a form
q
is
admissible
if
q^{\sigma}
has signature
(n,0)
with respect to any other non-identity embedding
\sigma:k\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}
(in other words,
q^{\sigma}
is positive definite). This condition on the Galois conjugates of
q
guarantees that the group

\mathrm{SO}(q,\mathcal{O}_{K})=\{A\in\mathrm{GL}(n+1,\mathcal{O}_{k});A^{t}S_{q}A=S_{q}\}

is a discrete subgroup of
\mathrm{SO}(q,\mathbb{R})
. Furthermore,
\mathrm{SO}(q,\mathcal{O}_{k})
is a lattice in
\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
in the sense that it is a discrete subgroup of finite co-volume; that is, the quotient
\mathbb{H}^{n}/\mathrm{SO}(q,\mathcal{O}_{k})
has finite volume with respect to the hyperbolic metric.
Any lattice
\Gamma<\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
commensurable with some
\mathrm{SO}(q,\mathcal{O}_{k})
for an admissible
q
defined over a totally real number field
k
is called
arithmetic of the first type
(also known as
arithmetic of simplest type
). The quotient orbifold
\mathbb{H}^{n}/\Gamma
is also called
arithmetic of the first type
.

An arithmetic lattice of the first type
\Gamma
is called
classical
if it is contained in
\mathrm{SO}(q,k)
for an admissible
q
defined over a totally real number field
k
. It turns out that every arithmetic lattice in
\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
for
n
even is classical, and necessarily of the first type (see, e.g.,
[
ERT19
, Lemma 4.2]
). When
n
is odd, there are arithmetic lattices which are not of the first type; however, if
\Gamma
is arithmetic of the first type, then
\Gamma^{(2)}
, the subgroup generated by the squares of the elements in
\Gamma
, is classical (see
[
ERT19
, Lemma 4.5]
).

We focus on arithmetic lattices of the first type defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. All non-cocompact arithmetic lattices are of the first type and defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. The quotient hyperbolic orbifold
\mathbb{H}^{n}/\mathrm{SO}(q,\mathcal{O}_{k})
is compact whenever
q
is anisotropic, and cusped whenever
q
is isotropic. In dimension
2
, there is a unique commensurability class of non-cocompact arithmetic lattices which contains, via the isomorphism
\mathrm{Isom}^{+}(\mathbb{H}^{2})\cong\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
, the modular group
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. All other arithmetic lattices of the first type defined over
\mathbb{Q}
in dimension
2
are cocompact.
In dimension
3
, there are infinitely many commensurability classes of non-cocompact arithmetic lattices each of which contains, via the isomorphism
\mathrm{Isom}^{+}(\mathbb{H}^{3})\cong\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
, a Bianchi group
\mathrm{PSL}(2,R_{D})
where
R_{D}
is the ring of integers in
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-D})
. There are also infinitely many commensurability classes of cocompact arithmetic lattices in dimension
3
which are of the first type and defined over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Whenever
n\geq 4
, it follows from Meyer’s theorem that the quadratic forms over
\mathbb{Q}
with signature
(n,1)
are always isotropic. Thus in dimensions at least
4
, arithmetic lattices of the first type defined over
\mathbb{Q}
are exactly those which are non-cocompact.

2.4.
Salem numbers and lengths of geodesics

For any non-constant polynomial

p(x)=a_{d}x^{d}+a_{d-1}x^{d-1}+\dots+a_{1}x+a_{0}=a_{d}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\alpha_{i}),

we define the
Mahler measure of
p(x)
as

M(p)=|a_{d}|\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathrm{max}(|\alpha_{i}|,1).

One can show via Jensen’s formula that this is equivalent to

M(p)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{1}\log|p(e^{2\pi it})|\;dt\right),

which can be viewed as the geometric mean of
|p(z)|
over all values of
z
lying on the unit circle. One can immediately observe that
M(p)=1
precisely when
p(x)=x^{k}\displaystyle\prod_{i}\Phi_{n_{i}}(x)
. There is a lower bound on the Mahler measure in terms of the coefficients (see
[
Mah62
, §2]
)

(1)

|a_{k}|\leq\binom{d}{k}M(p).

In 1933, Lehmer asked whether the Mahler measure reaches a minimal value when considered over all minimal polynomials of algebraic integers that are not roots of unity. This became known as Lehmer’s conjecture, which can be stated as follows:

Conjecture 2.1

(Lehmer’s conjecture)
.

There exists a real number
m>1
such that
M(p)\geq m
for all non-cyclotomic polynomials
p
.

The smallest Mahler measure (greater than
1
) that is currently known is attained by Lehmer’s polynomial,

L(x)=x^{10}+x^{9}-x^{7}-x^{6}-x^{5}-x^{4}-x^{3}+x+1,

which has Mahler measure
M(L)\approx 1.176280818
. By plotting the roots of Lehmer’s polynomial, one can observe that the real roots are
\lambda=1.176280818....
and
\lambda^{-1}=0.850137...
. There are also eight complex roots of
L(x)
that lie along the unit circle. This motivates a definition:

Definition 2.2
.

A
Salem number
is a real algebraic integer
\lambda>1
such that all of its Galois conjugates except
\lambda^{-1}
have norm
1
. A polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
is a
Salem polynomial
if it is the minimal polynomial of a Salem number.

In 2019, Emery, Ratcliffe and Tschantz established a more direct relationship between Salem numbers and lengths of geodesics in hyperbolic orbifolds which are arithmetic of the first type. Whenever
\gamma
is a hyperbolic element in a classical arithmetic lattice in
\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
,
\lambda=e^{\ell(\gamma)}
is a Salem number (see
Theorem

1.1
). Conversely, we have:

Theorem 2.3
.

[
ERT19
, Theorem 6.3]

Let
\lambda
be a Salem number, let K be a subfield of
\mathbb{Q}(\lambda+\lambda^{-1})
, and let
n\geq 2
such that
\deg_{K}(\lambda)\leq n+1
. Then there exist a classical arithmetic lattice
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
of the first type over
K
and an orientation preserving hyperbolic element
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that
\lambda=e^{\ell(\gamma)}
.

Due to this connection, it is possible to reformulate Lehmer’s conjecture in terms of geodesic lengths.

Conjecture 2.4

(Short Geodesic Conjecture)
.

There exists a positive universal lower bound on the length of geodesics in an arithmetic hyperbolic orbifold of the first type.

We say that a Salem number
\lambda
is
realized
in
\Gamma<\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
if there exists a hyperbolic element
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that
\lambda=e^{\ell(\gamma)}
, alternatively if
\lambda
is the real eigenvalue of
\gamma
satisfying
\lambda>1
.

Counting Diophantine solutions

Let
m
be a positive integer and
D
a square-free positive integer. We are interested in counting the number of Salem polynomials
f
of degree
2m
for which
f(1)f(-1)\equiv-D
in
\mathbb{Q}^{\times}/\mathbb{Q}^{\times 2}
with Mahler measure
\leq Q
. Any such

produces a solution
(A,B,C)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
of the degree
2
Diophantine equation

(2)

A^{2}+DB^{2}=C^{2}

for the following reasons. Since
D
is square-free, we can assume that
f(1)f(-1)=-Dk^{2}
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Now, if
f(x)=\sum^{2m}_{k=0}c_{k}x^{k}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]\text{ with }c_{k}=c_{2m-k}\text{ and }c_{0}=c_{2m}=1
take

A=\sum_{k\\
\text{ even }}c_{k},\hskip 28.45274ptC=\sum_{k\text{ odd}}c_{k},\hskip 28.45274ptB=k.

The goal of this section is to prove the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
D
be a square-free positive integer and
t
be the number of distinct prime factors of
D
. Denote the number of integer solutions to
A^{2}+DB^{2}=C^{2}
with
|C|\leq X
by

P_{D}(X)=\#\{(A,B,C)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\mid A^{2}+DB^{2}=C^{2},\mbox{ and }|C|\leq X\}.

Then

P_{D}(X)=\begin{cases}2^{t}\frac{8X}{\pi\sqrt{D}}+O(\sqrt{X}\log X)&\text{ if $D$ is odd},\\
2^{t}\frac{6X}{\pi\sqrt{D}}+O(\sqrt{X}\log X)&\text{ if $D$ is even}.\end{cases}

We will begin by stating some useful lemmas about the number of lattice points in convex sets.

Let
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded, convex, centrally-symmetric region.
As a bounded set, there exists an
R\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that any point
(x,y)\in\mathcal{C}
satisfies
-R\leq x\leq R
and
-R\leq y\leq R
.

Define
N(\alpha,\mathcal{C})=\#\{\alpha\mathcal{C}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}
and
M(\alpha,\mathcal{C})=\#\{\alpha\mathcal{C}\cap(2\mathbb{Z}+1)^{2}\}
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For all
\alpha
, we have

\displaystyle N(\alpha,\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{C})+O(\alpha),

\displaystyle M(\alpha,\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\alpha^{2}\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{C})+O(\alpha).\

Proof.

The key is to adapt Gauss’ classical lattice point counting argument, which says that we can express

\#\{\mathrm{Lattice\ points\ in\ a\ circle\}=\mathrm{Area}+O(\mathrm{Perimeter}).}

In our case, we relate the lattice point count of
\alpha\mathcal{C}
to the volume of the bounded region
\mathcal{C}
and the size of its boundary. Since
\alpha\mathcal{C}
is a dilation of
\mathcal{C}
by a factor
\alpha
then the boundary of
\alpha\mathcal{C}
will be a linear function of
\alpha
. The volume of
\alpha\mathcal{C}
is
\alpha^{2}\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{C})
. Thus, we obtain

N(\alpha,\mathcal{C})=\alpha^{2}\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{C})+O(\alpha).

When computing
M(\alpha,\mathcal{C})
, we only consider odd lattice points, which shrinks the count by a factor of
4
since both coordinates in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
must be odd.
∎

We are interested in restricting the counts of
N(\alpha,\mathcal{C})
and
M(\alpha,\mathcal{C})
to only the primitive points, that is, points
(A,B)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
for which
\gcd(A,B)=1
. Thus, let
N^{\prime}(\alpha,\mathcal{C})
and
M^{\prime}(\alpha,\mathcal{C})
denote the corresponding counts for the primitive points. Then we can derive the following result from
Lemma

3.2
.

Lemma 3.3
.

With
N^{\prime}
and
M^{\prime}
defined as above, we have

\displaystyle N^{\prime}(\alpha,\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle=\frac{6\alpha^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{C})+O(\alpha\log\alpha),

\displaystyle M^{\prime}(\alpha,\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{C})+O(\alpha\log\alpha).

Proof.

The proof follows from
[
HW08
, Theorem 259]
with our
\alpha
playing the role of
1/\rho
. We also have our
N(\alpha,\mathcal{C})
and
N^{\prime}(\alpha,\mathcal{C})
playing the roles of
g(\rho)+1
and
f(\rho)+1
respectively. We will bound the difference between
N^{\prime}(\alpha,\mathcal{C})
and
\frac{6\alpha^{2}}{\pi^{2}}
, showing that it is
O(\alpha\log\alpha)
. According to
[
HW08
, 24.10.8]
,

(3)

N^{\prime}(\alpha,\mathcal{C})-1-\frac{\alpha^{2}\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{C})}{\zeta(2)}=\alpha^{2}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(m)}{m^{2}}\left(\frac{m^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\left(N({\alpha/m},\mathcal{C})-1\right)-\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{C})\right).

Note that if
\frac{\alpha}{m}<\frac{1}{R}
, then
N(\alpha/m,\mathcal{C})=1
since this will only count the origin. Thus, the right-hand side of the above expression becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor\alpha R\rfloor}\mu(m)\left(N\left(\frac{\alpha}{m},\mathcal{C}\right)-1\right)-\alpha^{2}\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{C})\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(m)}{m^{2}}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{C})\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor\alpha R\rfloor}\frac{\mu(m)}{m^{2}}+O\left(\alpha\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor\alpha R\rfloor}\frac{\mu(m)}{m}\right)-\alpha^{2}\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{C})\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(m)}{m^{2}}

\displaystyle=O\left(\alpha\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor\alpha R\rfloor}\frac{\mu(m)}{m}\right)-\alpha^{2}\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{C})\sum_{m=\lceil\alpha R\rceil}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(m)}{m^{2}},

where the second line follows from applying
Lemma

3.2
to the first term.

For the sum inside the Big O, one can use the classical result (which follows from partial summation) that

\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor\alpha R\rfloor}\frac{\mu(m)}{m}\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor\alpha R\rfloor}\frac{1}{m}\leq\log\alpha R+\gamma+O\left(\frac{1}{\alpha R}\right),

where
\gamma
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.

Therefore, the expression in (
3
) is bounded by

\alpha\log(\alpha R)+\alpha\gamma+O\left(\frac{1}{R}\right)-\alpha^{2}\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{C})\sum_{m=\lceil\alpha R\rceil}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(m)}{m^{2}}

where the last sum can be bounded above by
\frac{1}{\alpha R-1},
so the final subtracted term has magnitude
O(\alpha)
. Since
R
is a fixed constant, depending only on
\mathcal{C}
, we can absorb it into the Big O constant, yielding the claimed error term of
O(\alpha\log\alpha).

To obtain the count for
M^{\prime}(\alpha,\mathcal{C})
from the count for
N^{\prime}(\alpha,\mathcal{C})
, we use the fact that primitive points cannot have two even coordinates.
If we write our lattice points as
(A,B)
then the probability that at least one of
A
or
B
is not divisible by
p
is given by
1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}
. So, looking for the number of points where neither
A
nor
B
is divisible by
p=2
simply amounts to removing
p=2
from the Euler product and considering that
\frac{1}{4}
of all lattice points will have the combination with
A
and
B
both odd. Thus, we have

M^{\prime}(\alpha,\mathcal{C})=\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{2}}\right)}N^{\prime}(\alpha,\mathcal{C})=\frac{1}{3}N^{\prime}(\alpha,\mathcal{C}).

∎

Note that a sharp form of the estimate
N^{\prime}(\alpha,\mathcal{C})
appears in
[
HN96
]
but we will not need anything that strong for our purposes.

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

3.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Let
D
be positive and square-free. We will count integer solutions to the Diophantine equation
A^{2}+DB^{2}=C^{2}
where
\gcd(A,C)=1
and
C\leq X
. We begin with the classical method of drawing an ellipse
A^{2}+DB^{2}=1
and drawing a reference point along the ellipse (for example,
(1,0)
), and then drawing a line from this reference point to a rational point
r
along the
y
-axis. The point where the line intersects the ellipse will also be a rational solution to
A^{2}+DB^{2}=1
. A line through both
(1,0)
and
r
is given by the equation
B=(1-A)r
. By substituting
B=(1-A)r
into the Diophantine equation and solving for
A
and
B
, we obtain
A=\frac{-1+Dr^{2}}{1+Dr^{2}}
and
B=\frac{2r}{1+Dr^{2}}
. Setting
r=V/U
yields the parametrization

(4)

A=S\frac{-U^{2}+DV^{2}}{T},\quad B=S\frac{2UV}{T},\text{ and }C=S\frac{U^{2}+DV^{2}}{T},

where
S,U,V
are integers satisfying
\gcd(U,V)=1
, and
T
is the greatest common divisor of the numerators.

Suppose
(A,B,C)
is a positive primitive solution written as in (
4
). We may then assume that
S=1
,
U,V\geq 0
, and
U^{2}\leq DV^{2}
. Let
D_{1}=\gcd\{U^{2}+DV^{2},UV\}
. Since
\gcd(U,V)=1
, it must be that
D_{1}
divides both
D
and
U
. There is some
D_{2}>0
and
u\geq 0
such that
D=D_{1}D_{2}
and
U=D_{1}u
. It follows that any positive primitive solution can be written as

(5)

(A,B,C)=\frac{1}{\tau}(-D_{1}u^{2}+D_{2}v^{2},2uv,D_{1}u^{2}+D_{2}v^{2})

where
D_{1}>0
,
D_{2}>0
,
D=D_{1}D_{2}
,
u\geq 0
,
v\geq 0
,
\gcd(u,v)=1
,
D_{1}u^{2}\leq D_{2}v^{2}
, and
\tau=1
when
B
is even or otherwise
\tau=2
and
u,v
are odd when
B
is odd.

We will now count the number of positive primitive solutions. We will consider four cases, depending on the parity of
D
and on the relationship between
A
and
C
. Notice that, if
\gcd(A,C)=1
, then
\gcd((C-A),(C+A))=1
or
2
.

Case I:

D
is even and
A\equiv C\bmod 2
.

Then
B
must be even.
By (
5
), there are unique integers
D_{1}>0,D_{2}>0
,
u\geq 0
, and
v\geq 0
with
\gcd(u,v)=1
for which

A=D_{2}v^{2}-D_{1}u^{2},\hskip 14.45377ptB=2uv,\hskip 14.45377ptC=D_{1}u^{2}+D_{2}v^{2}.

We will show that every choice of such
D_{1},D_{2},u,v
produces a positive primitive solution.

The congruence condition on
A
and
C
implies that
\gcd(C-A,C+A)=2
. Therefore, there is no odd prime that divides both
A
and
C
. If
A
and
C
are both even then
D_{1}u^{2}
and
D_{2}v^{2}
are both odd, which contradicts the fact that
D=D_{1}D_{2}
is even. As a result, for each choice of
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, we will count the elements in the set

\{(u,v)\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})^{2}:\gcd(u,v)=1,D_{1}u^{2}+D_{2}v^{2}\leq X,D_{1}u^{2}\leq D_{2}v^{2}\}.

This count is precisely
N^{\prime}(\sqrt{X},\mathcal{C})
where

\mathcal{C}=\{(x,y)\in(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})^{2}:D_{1}x^{2}+D_{2}y^{2}\leq 1,D_{1}x^{2}\leq D_{2}y^{2}\}.

Observe that

\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{C})=\frac{\pi}{8\sqrt{D_{1}D_{2}}}=\frac{\pi}{8\sqrt{D}}.

From our formula for
N^{\prime}(X,\mathcal{C})
, we obtain

N^{\prime}(\sqrt{X},\mathcal{C})\sim\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\times\frac{\pi}{8\sqrt{D}}\times(\sqrt{X})^{2}\sim\frac{3X}{4\pi\sqrt{D}}.

As we run over all choices of
D_{1}
, our count becomes

2^{t}\frac{3X}{4\pi\sqrt{D}}+O(\sqrt{X}\log X),

where the error term comes from the formula for
N^{\prime}(\sqrt{X},\mathcal{C})
.

Case II:

D
is even and
A\not\equiv C\bmod 2
.

This case does not occur, since it would contradict
C^{2}-A^{2}=DB^{2}
.

Case III:

D
is odd and
A\equiv C\bmod 2
.

Since
A\equiv C\bmod 2
, then
B
must be even. By (
5
), there are unique integers
D_{1}>0,D_{2}>0
,
u\geq 0
, and
v\geq 0
with
\gcd(u,v)=1
for which

A=D_{2}v^{2}-D_{1}u^{2},\hskip 14.45377ptB=2uv,\hskip 14.45377ptC=D_{1}u^{2}+D_{2}v^{2}.

Each choice of such
D_{1},D_{2},u,v
produces a solution to
A^{2}+DB^{2}=C^{2}
. However, not all choices produce a primitive solution. The greatest common divisor may be
2
, which occurs if both
u
and
v
are odd.

Thus, for each choice of
D_{1},D_{2}
, we count the elements in the set

\{(u,v)\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})^{2}\mid\gcd(u,v)=1,D_{1}u^{2}+D_{2}v^{2}\leq X,D_{1}u^{2}\leq D_{2}v^{2}\}

and subtract off the number of elements in the set

\{(u,v)\in(2\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}+1)^{2}\mid\gcd(u,v)=1,D_{1}u^{2}+D_{2}v^{2}\leq X,D_{1}u^{2}\leq D_{2}v^{2}\}.

This is precisely given by

N^{\prime}(\sqrt{X},\mathcal{C})-M^{\prime}(\sqrt{X},\mathcal{C}),

where
\mathcal{C}
is the same as in
Case I
. Using our formulae for
N^{\prime}
and
M^{\prime}
, we obtain

N^{\prime}(\sqrt{X},\mathcal{C})\sim\frac{3X}{4\pi\sqrt{D}},

as in
Case I
, minus

M^{\prime}(\sqrt{X},\mathcal{C})\sim\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\times\frac{\pi}{8\sqrt{D}}\times(\sqrt{X})^{2}\sim\frac{X}{4\pi\sqrt{D}}.

This difference is asymptotically
\frac{X}{2\pi\sqrt{D}}
. As we run over all choices of
D_{1}
, we obtain a contribution of
2^{t}\frac{X}{2\pi\sqrt{D}}+O(\sqrt{X}\log X).

Case IV:

D
is odd and
A\not\equiv C\bmod 2
.

Then
B
must be odd and
\gcd(C+A,C-A)=1
. By (
5
), there are unique integers
D_{1}>0,D_{2}>0
,
u\geq 0
, and
v\geq 0
with
\gcd(u,v)=1
and both
u,v
odd for which

A=\frac{1}{2}(D_{2}v^{2}-D_{1}u^{2}),\hskip 14.45377ptB=uv,\hskip 14.45377ptC=\frac{1}{2}(D_{1}u^{2}+D_{2}v^{2}).

Every choice of such
D_{1},D_{2},u,v
produces a positive primitive solution to
A^{2}+DB^{2}=C^{2}
. Indeed, if an odd prime
p
is a common divisor of
A
and
C
, then
p
also divides
A\pm C
, which is not possible since
\gcd(C+A,C-A)=1
.

We also have

2C=D_{1}u^{2}+D_{2}v^{2}\leq 2X.

Then, for each choice of
D_{1},D_{2}
, we count the elements in the set

\{(u,v)\in(2\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}+1)^{2}:\gcd(u,v)=1,D_{1}u^{2}+D_{2}v^{2}\leq 2X,D_{1}u^{2}\leq D_{2}v^{2}\}.

This is precisely

M^{\prime}(\sqrt{2X},\mathcal{C})\sim\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\times\frac{\pi}{8\sqrt{D}}\times(\sqrt{2X})^{2}\sim\frac{X}{2\pi\sqrt{D}}.

As we run over all choices of
D_{1}
, we obtain a total count of
2^{t}\frac{X}{2\pi\sqrt{D}}+O(\sqrt{X}\log X).

Collecting the information from all four cases, we see that the contribution from even values of
D
yields
2^{t}\frac{3X}{4\pi\sqrt{D}}+O(\sqrt{X}\log X)
solutions to the Diophantine equation
A^{2}+DB^{2}=C^{2}
. On the other hand, the contribution from odd values of
D
yields
2^{t}\frac{X}{2\pi\sqrt{D}}+O(\sqrt{X}\log X)
solutions. Observe that the above arguments only count positive solutions. Allowing for all
2^{3}
combinations of
\pm
solutions yields the result in
Theorem

3.1
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The above argument produces our main term. However, we can replace the error term above with a much sharper version due to Huxley
[
Hux93
]
, which allows us to improve the error term to
O(X^{\frac{131}{416}})
.

Remark 3.5
.

We remark that when
D=1
and
t=0
our
Theorem

3.1
coincides with the result for Pythagorean triples given by Fricker in
[
Fri77
]
.

4.
Counting Salem numbers

Let
D>0
be a square-free positive integer. Denote by
\mathcal{F}_{m,D}(Q)
the set of polynomials of degree
2m
of the form

(6)

f(x)=\sum^{2m}_{k=0}c_{k}x^{k}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]\text{ with }c_{k}=c_{2m-k}\text{ and }c_{0}=c_{2m}=1

which satisfy the conditions:

(i)

f
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
,

(ii)

f(1)f(-1)=-Dk^{2}
where
k\in\mathbb{Q}
, and

(iii)

f
has distinct roots
\lambda,\lambda^{-1},\mu_{1},\mu_{1}^{-1},\dots,\mu_{m-1},\mu_{m-1}^{-1}
where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
with
1<\lambda\leq Q
and each
\mu_{i}\notin\mathbb{R}
with
|\mu_{i}|=1
.

Since
D
is square-free, condition (ii) is equivalent to having
f(1)f(-1)=-Dk^{2}
where
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Denote by
\mathcal{S}_{m}(Q)
the number of polynomials of degree
2m
with form as in (
6
) which only satisfy conditions (i) and (iii). Götze and Gusakova showed the following.

Theorem 4.1
.

[
GG20
, Theorem 1.1]

With
\mathcal{S}_{m}(Q)
defined as above, we have

\#\mathcal{S}_{m}(Q)=\omega_{m}Q^{m}+O(Q^{m-1})

where

\omega_{m}=\frac{2^{m(m-1)}}{m}\prod^{m-2}_{k=0}\frac{k!^{2}}{(2k+1)!}.

Condition (ii) can be rewritten as
A^{2}+DB^{2}=C^{2}
by setting
B=k
and noting that
f(1)f(-1)=A^{2}-C^{2}
where
A
is the sum of the coefficients with even powers of
x
and
C
is the sum of the coefficients with odd powers of
x
:

(7)

A=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq k\leq 2m\\
\text{even}\end{subarray}}c_{k}\text{ and }C=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq 2m-1\\
\text{odd}\end{subarray}}c_{k}.

Lemma 4.2
.

With
A,C,Q
defined as above, we have
\#C\leq 2^{2m-1}Q
and

\#A\leq(2^{2m-1}-2)Q+2
.

Proof.

It follows from the Binomial Theorem that
\sum_{k\text{ odd}}\binom{2m}{k}=2^{2m-1}
. Then using (
1
) together with Condition (iii), which implies
M(p)\leq Q
, we obtain

C=\sum_{k\text{ odd}}c_{k}\leq\sum_{k\text{ odd}}|c_{k}|\leq\sum_{k\text{ odd}}\binom{2m}{k}Q=2^{2m-1}Q.

The second assertion follows a similar argument after setting
c_{0}=c_{2m}=1
.
∎

The following well-known result is stated as in
[
Nat00
]
(see also
[
Net58
,
PS70
]
).

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\alpha=\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}\}
be a set of
n
relatively prime positive integers. Let the partition function
p_{\alpha}(N)
denote the number of non-negative integer solutions to the linear equation
a_{1}x_{1}+a_{2}x_{2}+\cdots+a_{n}x_{n}=N
. Then

p_{\alpha}(N)=\frac{N^{n-1}}{(a_{1}\cdot a_{2}\cdots a_{n})(n-1)!}+O(N^{n-2}).

We will use
Lemma

4.3
to obtain the following result:

Lemma 4.4
.

Given
A
and
C
satisfying
Lemma

4.2
, the number of integer solutions for
\{c_{0},\dots,c_{2m}\}
satisfying
Equations

1
,
6
and
7
is bounded above by

\kappa_{0}(m)Q^{m-2}+O(Q^{m-3}),

where

\kappa_{0}(m)=\begin{cases}\frac{2^{(m-1)(m-2)}(2^{2m}-4)^{\frac{m}{2}-1}}{\left((\frac{m}{2}-1)!\right)^{2}}&\text{ if $m$ is even}\\
\frac{2^{(2m-1)(\frac{m-1}{2})}(2^{2m-1}-2)^{\frac{m-3}{2}}}{\left((\frac{m-1}{2})!\right)^{2}}&\text{ if $m$ is odd}.\end{cases}

Proof.

For fixed
A
and
C
, we count the number of integral solutions
\{c_{0},\dots,c_{2m}\}
satisfying
c_{k}\geq-\binom{2m}{k}\lfloor{Q}\rfloor
(see (
1
)) by applying
Lemma

4.3
to count the number of non-negative solutions after the substitutions
c^{\prime}_{k}=c_{k}+\binom{2m}{k}\lfloor{Q}\rfloor
. Note that we ignore the upper bound
c_{k}\leq\binom{2m}{k}\lfloor{Q}\rfloor
.

Suppose first that
m
is even. Then
C=2(c_{1}+c_{3}+\dots+c_{m-1})
, so
C
is even as well. For a fixed value of
C
, the number of integral solutions to this linear equation which has
\frac{m}{2}
variables is

\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}C+2^{2m-2}\lfloor{Q}\rfloor\right)^{\frac{m}{2}-1}}{(\frac{m}{2}-1)!}+O\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}C+2^{2m-2}\lfloor{Q}\rfloor\right)^{\frac{m}{2}-2}\right).

We also have that
A-2=2c_{2}+2c_{4}+\cdots+2c_{m-2}+c_{m}
. For a fixed value of
A
, the number of integral solutions to this linear equation which has
\frac{m}{2}
variables is

\frac{\left(A-2+(2^{2m-1}-2)\lfloor{Q}\rfloor\right)^{\frac{m}{2}-1}}{2^{\frac{m}{2}-1}(\frac{m}{2}-1)!}+O\left(\left(A-2+(2^{2m-1}-2)\lfloor{Q}\rfloor\right)^{\frac{m}{2}-2}\right).

Combining this with the bounds in
Lemma

4.2
, we obtain for
m
even that the number of solutions is bounded above by

\frac{2^{(m-1)(m-2)}(2^{2m}-4)^{\frac{m}{2}-1}}{\left((\frac{m}{2}-1)!\right)^{2}}Q^{m-2}+O(Q^{m-3}).

Suppose now that
m
is odd. Then
C=2c_{1}+2c_{3}+\dots+2c_{m-2}+c_{m}
. For a fixed value of
C
, the number of integral solutions to this linear equation with
\frac{m+1}{2}
variables is

\frac{\left(C+2^{2m-1}\lfloor{Q}\rfloor\right)^{\frac{m-1}{2}}}{2^{\frac{m-1}{2}}(\frac{m-1}{2})!}+O\left(\left(C+2^{2m-1}\lfloor{Q}\rfloor\right)^{\frac{m-3}{2}}\right).

We also have that
A-2=2(c_{2}+c_{4}+\cdots+c_{m-1})
, so
A
is necessarily even. For a fixed value of
A
, the number of integral solutions to this linear equation which has
\frac{m-1}{2}
variables is

\frac{\left(\frac{A-2}{2}+(2^{2m-2}-1)\lfloor{Q}\rfloor\right)^{\frac{m-3}{2}}}{(\frac{m-1}{2})!}+O\left(\left(\frac{A-2}{2}+(2^{2m-2}-1)\lfloor{Q}\rfloor\right)^{\frac{m-5}{2}}\right).

As before, we conclude for
m
odd that the number of solutions is bounded above by

\frac{2^{(2m-1)(\frac{m-1}{2})}(2^{2m-1}-2)^{\frac{m-3}{2}}}{\left((\frac{m-1}{2})!\right)^{2}}Q^{m-2}+O(Q^{m-3}).

∎

We are now able to prove
A
with explicit constants.

Theorem 4.5
.

With the notation as above, we have

\#\mathcal{F}_{m,D}(Q)\leq\frac{\kappa(m,D)}{\pi\sqrt{D}}Q^{m-1}\log Q+O(Q^{m-1})

where

\kappa(m,D)=\begin{cases}2^{t+2m}\cdot\kappa_{0}(m)&\text{ if $D$ is odd}\\
3\cdot 2^{t+2m-2}\cdot\kappa_{0}(m)&\text{ if $D$ is even}\end{cases}

and
\kappa_{0}(m)
is as in
Lemma

4.4
.

Proof of
A
.

Theorem

3.1
allows us to count the number of integer solutions for the triple
(A,B,C)
satisfying
A^{2}+DB^{2}=C^{2}
with
|C|\leq 2^{2m-1}Q
. However, we are interested in counting the integer solutions only for the tuple
(A,C)
, so we ignore the sign of
B
by dividing by
2
. Moreover, we only want to count the subset of solutions where
C
is even whenever
m
is even and
A
is even whenever
m
is odd (see the proof of
Lemma

4.4
), so we again divide by
2
. This results in the following count for admissible integral solutions
(A,C)
:

(8)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{2^{t+2m}}{\pi\sqrt{D}}Q+O(\sqrt{Q}\log Q)&\text{if $D$ is odd}\\
\frac{3\cdot 2^{t+2m-2}}{\pi\sqrt{D}}Q+O(\sqrt{Q}\log Q)&\text{if $D$ is even.}\end{array}

Let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{m,D}(Q)
denote the set of polynomials of the form in (
6
) satisfying conditions (ii) and (iii) (but not necessarily (i)). The size of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{m,D}(Q)
is bounded by the product of (
8
) with the bound in

; that is,

\#\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{m,D}(Q)\leq\frac{\kappa(m,D)}{\pi\sqrt{D}}Q^{m-1}\log Q+O(Q^{m-1}),

with
\kappa(m,D)
as in the statement of the theorem.

Now if some polynomial
f\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{m,D}(Q)
is not irreducible, it can be written as a product
f=g\cdot h
with
g,h\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
. Since
f
satisfies condition (iii), we may assume that
g
contains the root
\lambda
. It must then be the case that
g\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{m_{1},D}(Q)
is a polynomial of degree
2m_{1}
and
h
is a product of cyclotomic polynomials of degree
2m_{2}
, where
m_{1}+m_{2}=m
and
m_{1},m_{2}>0
. The number of such polynomials
h
is bounded above by some constant
\iota_{0}(m_{2})
. It follows that the subset
\mathcal{F}^{red}_{m,D}(Q)\subset\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{m,D}(Q)
of reducible polynomials satisfies

\#\mathcal{F}^{red}_{m,D}(Q)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\iota_{0}(i)\cdot\#\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{m-i}(Q)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m-1,D}\iota_{0}(i)\cdot\frac{\kappa(i,D)}{\pi\sqrt{D}}Q^{m-i-1}\log Q\leq\iota(m,D)Q^{m-2}\log Q

for some constant
\iota(m,D)
depending only on
m
and
D
. Since
\#\mathcal{F}_{m,D}(Q)=\#\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{m,D}(Q)-\#\mathcal{F}^{red}_{m,D}(Q)
, the theorem now follows by absorbing
\#\mathcal{F}^{red}_{m,D}(Q)
into the error term.
∎

5.
Counting lengths of closed geodesics in commensurability classes

Let
q
be an admissible quadratic form with signature
(n,1)
over
\mathbb{Q}
,
n
odd. The goal of this section is to count the Salem numbers realized in the commensurability class of
\mathrm{SO}(q,\mathbb{Z})
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
T
be an isometry in
\mathrm{SO}(q,\mathbb{Q})
where
q
is an admissible quadratic form over
\mathbb{Q}
. Suppose also that its characteristic polynomial
p_{T}(x)
has integer coefficients. Then a conjugate of
T
is contained in a classical arithmetic lattice commensurable with
\mathrm{SO}(q,\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

Let
r
denote the rank of
q
, which is also the degree of
p_{T}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
. The matrix powers
T,T^{2},\dots,T^{n-1}
all have entries in
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
\ell
denote the least common multiple of the denominators appearing in the entries of these
n-1
rational matrices.
By the Cayley-Hamilton Theorem,
T
satisfies its characteristic polynomial, so for any
k\geq 0
, we have

T^{k+r}=-\left(T^{k}+c_{1}T^{k+1}+\cdots+c_{r-1}T^{k+r-1}\right).

The subgroup of
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
generated by the
T^{k}\mathbb{Z}^{r}
for all
k\geq 0
lies between the subgroups
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
and
(\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbb{Z})^{r}
and is thus itself a free abelian group of rank
r
. We can therefore choose a change of basis matrix
g\in\mathrm{GL}(n+1,\mathbb{Q})
such that
g^{-1}Tg
has entries in
\mathbb{Z}
.

If
S_{q}
is the symmetric matrix associated to the quadratic form
q
, let
q^{\prime}
be the quadratic form associated to the symmetric matrix
S_{q^{\prime}}=g^{t}S_{q}g
. The result follows from checking that
g^{-1}Tg\in\mathrm{SO}(q^{\prime},\mathbb{Z})
and the fact that the automorphism groups of
\mathbb{Q}
-equivalent rational quadratic forms are commensurable, so
\mathrm{SO}(q,\mathbb{Z})
and
\mathrm{SO}(q^{\prime},\mathbb{Z})
are commensurable.
∎

Proof of
B
.

The Salem numbers which are realizable in the commensurability class of
\mathrm{SO}(q,\mathbb{Z})
have even degree
2m\leq n+1
. Let
\mathcal{R}_{q}(Q)
denote the set of realizable Salem numbers with
\lambda<Q
. By
[
MA13
]
, the reduced determinant of a rational quadratic form of even rank is a commensurability invariant. By
[
BF15
, Corollary 9.3]
, a necessary condition for the existence of an isometry in
\mathrm{O}(q,\mathbb{Q})
with minimal polynomial
f
is that
\det(q)\equiv f(1)f(-1)
in
\mathbb{Q}^{\times}/\mathbb{Q}^{\times 2}
. Since
f(0)=1
, such an isometry will be in
\mathrm{SO}(q,\mathbb{Q})
, and hence by
Lemma

5.1
, it will be in the commensurability class of
\mathrm{SO}(q,\mathbb{Z})
. Therefore we have

\mathcal{R}_{q}(Q)\subseteq\left(\sqcup_{i=1}^{m-1}\mathcal{S}_{i}(Q)\right)\sqcup\mathcal{F}_{m,D}(Q),

with
\mathcal{F}_{m,D}(Q)
as in
Section

4
. By
Theorem

4.1
and
Theorem

4.5
,

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{R}_{q}(Q)

\displaystyle\leq\#\mathcal{F}_{m,D}(Q)+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\#\mathcal{S}_{i}(Q)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa(m,D)}{\pi\sqrt{D}}Q^{m-1}\log Q+O(Q^{m-1}),

since for
i<m
,
\#\mathcal{S}_{i}(Q)
is absorbed into
O(Q^{m-1})
.
∎

We can now deduce
C
.

Proof of
C
.

Recall that the length of a hyperbolic element
\gamma\in\Gamma
satisfies
\ell(\gamma)=\log\lambda
where
\lambda
is the unique real eigenvalue of
\gamma
such that
\lambda>1
. Then
\ell(\gamma)\leq L
if and only
\lambda\leq e^{L}
.
The result follows from
B
by replacing
Q
with
e^{L}
.
∎

6.
Bounds on multiplicity of the length spectrum

Let
X=\mathbb{H}^{n}/\Gamma
be a hyperbolic orbifold, and let
L(X)=\{\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\cdots\}
be its primitive length spectrum without multiplicities. For a given
\ell_{k}\in L(X)
, the multiplicity
g(\ell_{k})
is defined as the number of homotopy classes of primitive closed geodesics in
X
with length
\ell_{k}
.

It is known that the length spectrum counting multiplicities contains important geometric and analytic information about
X
. For example, for
X
a compact hyperbolic manifold, Kelmer shows in
[
Kel14
]
that the length spectrum with multiplicities determines the Laplace spectrum of
X
.

Determining the numbers
\ell_{k},g(\ell_{k})
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
has been shown to be a difficult problem about which very little is known. Numerical calculations have been performed for the octagonal Fuchsian group
[
ABS91
]
, which already shows large fluctuations in the multiplicities. For modular and Bianchi orbifolds, the multiplicity relates to the class number of indefinite binary quadratic forms (see
[
Sar82
,
Sar83
]
). The determination of such class numbers is a wide open problem in algebraic number theory.

For arithmetic hyperbolic orbifolds, a lower bound for the average of the multiplicities can be obtained by counting Salem numbers. This study was initiated in
[
BLMT22
]
for even
n
, and in
[
Gre24
]
for odd
n
. Both of these papers used results about the so-called
square-rootable
Salem numbers. Let

N(\ell)=\sum_{\ell_{k}\leq\ell}g(\ell_{k}),\hskip 28.45274pt\widehat{N}(\ell)=\sum_{\ell_{k}\leq\ell}1,

and

G(\ell)=\frac{N(\ell)}{\widehat{N}(\ell)}.

Then
N(\ell)
counts the number of primitive closed geodesics in
X
with length
\leq\ell
,
\widehat{N}(\ell)
counts the lengths in
L(X)
up to
\ell
, and
G(\ell)
counts the average of the multiplicities
g(\ell_{k})
with
\ell_{k}\leq\ell
. Our techniques allow us to give a lower bound on
G(\ell)
for an arithmetic hyperbolic orbifold of odd
n>4
, when
\ell\to\infty
.

Proof of
E
.

By the Primitive Geodesic Theorem for non-compact lattices proved by Gangolli and Warner
[
GW80
, Prop. 5.4]
,
N(\ell)\sim e^{(n-1)\ell}/(n-1)\ell
. So, for
\ell
sufficiently large,

N(\ell)\geq\frac{1}{2(n-1)\ell}e^{(n-1)\ell}.

Since
X
is non-compact, it is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. Since
X
is classical, each length
\leq\ell
of a closed geodesic corresponds to a Salem number
\leq e^{\ell}
of degree
\leq n+1
(see
Theorem

1.1
). By
C
there is a constant
r>0
, depending only on
n
and on the commensurability class of
X
, such that

\widehat{N}(\ell)\leq r\cdot\ell e^{(n-1)\ell/2},

and then

G(\ell)\geq\frac{1}{2r(n-1)\ell^{2}}e^{\frac{(n-1)\ell}{2}},\hskip 28.45274pt\ell\to\infty.

∎

It is worth mentioning that our definition of ‘multiplicity average’ bounded in
E
coincides with the definition of
mean multiplicity
in
[
BLMT22
]
and
[
Gre24
]
. However, in this article, we call it the multiplicity average in order to distinguish our definition from the
mean multiplicity

\langle g_{p}(\ell)\rangle
studied by Bolte
[
Bol93
]
and Marklof
[
Mar96
]
.
Unfortunately, we are unable to compute the mean multiplicity in the sense of Bolte and Marklof, since the asymptotics of
\widehat{N}(\ell)
are not available to us at the present time.
