‘Cutting Through the Roots’: A Critical Appraisal of Transverse and Vertical Sectioning Techniques of Scalp Biopsy for Primary Alopecia
Authors: Seetu Palo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alopecia, a prevalent and distressing condition with diverse aetiologies, encompasses both scarring and non-scarring types and necessitates accurate diagnostic approaches for effective management. Apart from optimal biopsy site selection based on clinical suspicion, such as active borders for scarring alopecias and lesion borders for non-scarring alopecias, choice of appropriate method for grossing of alopecic scalp biopsies also forms a key step. Historically, debates on ‘One (biopsy) Vs Two (biopsies)’ and ‘Vertical/Longitudinal Vs Horizontal/Transverse’ sections persist, each approach carrying its advantages and disadvantages. Traditional methods, such as those proposed by Headington and Whiting, describe the landmarks for slicing a biopsy for transverse section. Newer innovative strategies, including the HoVert technique and the Tyler technique, aim to obtain both vertical and horizontal sections from a single biopsy. This article meticulously presents all the methodologies employed in the grossing of alopecic scalp biopsies in a simplified manner, presents a comprehensive overview of their strengths and limitations, and offers evidence-based recommendation for choosing optimal grossing techniques based on clinical scenarios.

Introduction

Alopecia is a common and often distressing condition, resulting from a wide range of underlying causes. Primary alopecia can be broadly classified into ‘scarring’ (or cicatricial) and ‘non-scarring’ (or non-cicatricial) types [Table 1].[12345] In addition to the clinical and trichoscopic evaluation, accurate characterisation of type of alopecia often requires a thorough examination of scalp biopsies by skilled pathologists. Scalp biopsy histopathological study is also important for monitoring disease progression or response to therapy.[6] Needless to say, grossing, the initial step in tissue processing, forms a critical aspect of scalp biopsy evaluation as it directly influences the diagnostic outcomes.[7] Hence, it is imperative to choose an apt grossing method in order to maximise the diagnostic yield so that more targeted and personalised alopecia treatment can be offered to the patients. Traditionally, if two biopsies are submitted, one is vertically (or longitudinally) sectioned and the other is grossed transversely (or horizontally). If a single biopsy is submitted, then it has to be decided whether to section it in vertical manner or transverse manner. Though a few investigators have tried certain innovative approaches to obtain both vertical and transverse sections from a single biopsy, the long-standing debate of ‘One (biopsy) Vs Two (biopsies)’ and ‘Vertical/Longitudinal Vs Horizontal/Transverse’ still remains a quandary. Both vertical and horizontal sections have their own advantages and disadvantages. In this review, all the grossing techniques of alopecic scalp biopsies described in the literature are revisited and presented in a simplified manner for easy understanding. The strengths and limitations of different grossing approaches shall be critically evaluated in order to identify best practices that maximise diagnostic yield in scalp biopsy assessment.

Methodologies of Grossing Alopecic Scalp Biopsies

Pictorial representation of the grossing techniques described till date and their pros and cons are presented under Table 2.[8910111213] Headington was one of the seminal authors to describe how to gross a scalp biopsy in a detailed manner. He stated that the best way to gross a scalp biopsy transversely is to cut it approximately 1 mm above the dermis-subcutis junction which roughly to corresponds to the plane where sebaceous ducts enter a follicle in a pilosebaceous unit.[8] A similar technique was later adopted by Whiting, wherein the cut is made 1 mm below the dermo-epidermal junction in specimens with a thin dermis or 1.5 mm below the dermo-epidermal junction in specimens with a thick dermis.[9] The two transverse halves thus achieved are embedded with their cutting surface down, preferably in a single paraffin block. Serial or deeper sectioning of the block will lead to achieving higher sections towards the epidermis from the upper half of the biopsy as well as lower sections towards the subcutis from the lower half of the biopsy. In the author’s experience, slicing the scalp biopsies 1–1.5 mm below the epidermal surface after overnight processing yields optimal transverse sections.[2] In the method advocated by Frishberg et al.,[10] the biopsy cylinder is bread-loafed into three to five discs depending upon the length of the biopsy. All the discs are embedded with cutting surface down, in a single block. Serial or deeper sectioning of the block will lead to achieving progressively superficial planes towards epidermis. In all these methods described, an additional scalp biopsy is required for achieving vertical sections. This second biopsy is simply bisected longitudinally, and both halves are embedded with cutting surface down, in a single block.[8910]

Elston et al.[11] were the first to conceptualise the idea of ‘best of both the worlds’ by including both transverse and vertical sections together in a single block, thereby allowing the pathologist to visualise both the sections side by side for easy interpretation. It also reduces labour and lab costs. But this would still require two biopsies. Notably, they also proposed to triage one of the ‘vertical half’ for direct immunofluorescence (DIF) studies, if clinically indicated, instead of going for a third biopsy. So, in this method, one of the unfixed fresh biopsy is vertically sliced; one vertical half goes for routine tissue processing after formalin fixation and the other half is triaged for DIF studies, if needed. The second biopsy is transversely sliced, approximately 1 mm above the dermo-subcutaneous junction, generating two horizontal halves. Stefanato[1] further modified and improvised this concept and framed ‘The St John’s multiteam clinicopathological protocol’, where the clinical findings is one of the key factors that dictates the grossing approach. In cases of scarring alopecia, two punch biopsies are procured from an active area or edge of the alopecic patch, one of which is transversely sectioned and the other is sliced vertically. One of the vertical halves is submitted for DIF studies, and the other half is submitted for routine processing. Transverse and vertical sections are embedded in separate paraffin blocks, in contrast to that proposed by Elston et al.,[11] where all the sections are processed in a single block. In cases of non-scarring alopecias, both the biopsies are transversely sectioned and the vertical sections are skipped altogether.[1]

In the HoVert technique, both vertical and horizontal (transverse) sections are ingeniously obtained from a single punch biopsy [‘Ho’ signify horizontal and ‘Vert’ means Vertical].[12] To begin with, the tissue cylinder is transversely sectioned about 1 mm below the epidermal surface, which is then longitudinally bisected into two halves to obtain vertical sections of the epidermis and dermo-epidermal junction. Parallel transverse cuts are made in the lower portion of the tissue cylinder to obtain two to three horizontal sections. All the vertical and horizontal sections can be embedded together in the same cassette or in two separate cassettes. ‘The Tyler technique’ is another such novel method of obtaining ‘two from one’, wherein the biopsy is bisected vertically first.[13] One ‘vertical half’ is then transversely sectioned. One vertical tissue piece and two transverse ‘D-shaped’ tissue pieces, thus obtained, are embedded in a single cassette with cut surface down.

Recommendations

The established best practice for a taking a scalp biopsy specimen involves utilising a 4-mm punch, inserted parallelly to the direction of hair growth and taking a deep biopsy that includes the subcutaneous fat to guarantee the sampling of the entire follicular unit and anagen follicles. It is essential to identify the appropriate location for a scalp biopsy, and this approach varies between different types of alopecia. In cases of suspected scarring alopecias, the biopsy should be taken from the active border of hair loss patch, whereas in cases of non-scarring alopecias such as alopecia areata, telogen effluvium, and trichotillomania, the biopsy can be obtained from lesion border or from an area of a positive pull test.[14] In suspected cases of androgenic alopecia, it is advisable to obtain paired punch biopsies, one from the clinically involved area (usually, frontal or vertex area) and another from the uninvolved site (usually, occipital area) to serve as a positive control for hair morphometrical characteristics.[114] Once the biopsy is taken, an appropriate grossing method has to be undertaken, chiefly taking the clinical diagnosis into consideration, and an algorithm is suggested under Figure 1 for the same. For scarring alopecia, if only a single biopsy is available, it has to be submitted in normal saline and the choice between the HoVert and Tyler techniques depends on whether direct immunofluorescence (DIF) studies are required: The HoVert technique is preferred when DIF studies are needed, while the Tyler technique is preferred when they are not. If two biopsies are available, the first is transversely sectioned using Frishberg’s technique in cases of non-scarring alopecia and Headington’s or Whiting’s technique for scarring alopecia. The second biopsy is vertically sectioned parallel to the direction of hair growth. When DIF studies are necessary, the fresh biopsy must be bisected, placing one half in Michel’s media and the other in 10% neutral buffered formalin. In non-scarring alopecia with a single biopsy, the choice among Frishberg’s, HoVert, or Tyler techniques depends on the pathologist’s preference; however, if HoVert or Tyler techniques are used, vertical and horizontal sections must be embedded in separate paraffin blocks to avoid critical tissue loss. This structured approach ensures optimal tissue handling and diagnostic yield based on clinical and procedural considerations.

A consensus meeting convened by the North American Hair Research Society (NAHRS) in 2001 suggested obtaining two scalp biopsies and using a combination of both vertical and horizontal sectioning methods for diagnosing different types of alopecia. However, it specifically recommended horizontal sectioning when only a single biopsy is submitted.[15] After this, many investigators evaluated the utility of horizontal sections and found it to be diagnostically sufficient or superior, especially in cases of alopecia areata.[6161718]

A few recent studies have shown encouraging results of non-invasive diagnostic modalities for characterisation of alopecia. Thakur et al.[19] found good concordance between the trichoscopic features and histopathological features in a small cohort of patients with primary cicatricial alopecias. Similarly, trichoscopy is helpful in non-scarring cases as well. Presence of yellow dots, short vellus hairs, and tapered hairs with dark thick broken tips are pointers of alopecia areata, whereas variable hair diameter, peripilar sign, and focal atrichia are suggestive of androgenetic alopecia.[1920] In vivo reflectance confocal microscopy is another useful tool for alopecia diagnosis.[21] Other such non-invasive diagnostic tools include videodermoscopy, multiphoton microscopy, and high-frequency ultrasonography. However, more studies are needed to validate the diagnostic accuracy of these techniques and histopathological analysis is still the gold standard.

Conclusion

This article provides the pathologists, dermatologists, and other healthcare professionals involved in the management of alopecia with a comprehensive, evidence-based, up-to-date reference that can aid in the selection of appropriate grossing techniques for scalp biopsies. It is to reiterate that effective communication between dermatologists and pathologists is pivotal to ensure the choice of optimal grossing method for each case of alopecia. The selection of the grossing method is influenced by several factors such as the suspected clinical diagnosis, number of biopsies provided, the pathologist’s preferences, and histotechnician’s expertise.
