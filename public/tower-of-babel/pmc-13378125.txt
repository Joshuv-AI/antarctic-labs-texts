Prevalence of ESBL producing uropathogens and associated risk factors in catheter associated urinary tract infections a cross-sectional study from Syria
Authors: Walid Khaddam, Bushra Durgham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCatheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTIs) are major healthcare-associated infections driven by indwelling devices that facilitate microbial adhesion. This study investigated the distribution of uropathogens in paired urine and catheter samples and evaluated the associations between extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) production and clinical factors.MethodsA total of 100 paired urine and catheter samples (n = 100 per sample type) were analyzed using a convenience sampling approach. Introduction

Catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTIs) represent a significant burden in clinical settings, particularly among hospitalized patients. These infections are frequently associated with the use of indwelling urinary devices, which provide surfaces for microbial adhesion and colonization. The ability of uropathogens to persist in such environments is largely attributed to their capacity to form biofilms—complex microbial communities embedded in a self-produced matrix [1, 2].

Biofilm formation on urinary catheters plays a role in the pathogenesis of CAUTIs by facilitating bacterial persistence and reducing susceptibility to antimicrobial agents [3]. As previously described by [4], the biofilm environment promotes resistance mechanisms, including limited antibiotic penetration and enhanced horizontal gene transfer, contributing to the dissemination of multidrug-resistant organisms [5–7].

Among the most clinically significant resistance mechanisms is the production of Extended-Spectrum Beta-Lactamases (ESBLs). ESBL-producing organisms, particularly Gram-negative bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Klebsiella spp., are increasingly reported in hospital-acquired infections, leading to limited therapeutic options and prolonged hospital stays [8, 9]. The relationship between ESBL production and biofilm formation is of particular concern, as the biofilm environment may facilitate genetic exchange and protect resistant phenotypes.

Beyond microbial factors, several clinical variables may influence bacterial colonization, including the duration of catheterization, patient demographics, and underlying comorbidities. Understanding the interplay between these factors is essential for improving infection control and antimicrobial stewardship [10].

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the prevalence of ESBL-producing uropathogens in paired catheter and urine isolates. Furthermore, it evaluates the observed associations between ESBL production and selected clinical factors. By comparing these two sample types, we aim to provide a clearer understanding of antimicrobial resistance dynamics in CAUTIs.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

A cross-sectional study was conducted among hospitalized patients catheterized for at least 48 h. This study followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines to ensure rigorous reporting. Due to the exploratory nature of the study and the limited number of eligible patients during the study period, a convenience sampling approach was employed, resulting in a total of 100 paired specimens. No formal a priori power calculation was performed.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the ethics committees of Al-Zahraa and Al-Ahli Hospitals in Homs, Syria. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians.

Diagnostic criteria for CAUTI

Patients were included if they met the clinical and microbiological criteria for Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infection (CAUTI). In accordance with the CDC/NHSN surveillance definitions, CAUTI was diagnosed when a patient had an indwelling urinary catheter in place for > 48 h and exhibited at least one of the following clinical signs: fever (> 38.0°C), suprapubic tenderness, costovertebral angle pain or tenderness, or acute hematuria. Microbiological inclusion required a urine culture with no more than two species of organisms, at least one of which was a bacterium at 10^5 CFU/mL.

Sample collection and processing

Urine specimens were obtained aseptically from the sampling port of the catheter tubing using sterile syringes. Simultaneously, 2–3 cm catheter tip segments were collected from the distal ends upon removal. To isolate biofilm-associated bacteria, catheter segments were processed by disinfecting the external surface with 70% ethanol to eliminate planktonic contaminants, followed by rinsing with sterile water to remove loosely attached microorganisms. Significant bacterial growth for catheter cultures was defined as 10^3 CFU/segment (Fig. 1).

Isolation and identification of bacterial isolates

All specimens were cultured on CLED agar, MacConkey agar, and blood agar, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. For catheter-derived samples, tip segments were rinsed with sterile distilled water and disinfected with 70% ethanol to target biofilm-associated bacteria. Segments were then aseptically sectioned and incubated in nutrient broth for 24 h for enrichment before subculturing. Bacterial identification was performed using standard microbiological procedures, including Gram staining and biochemical tests (catalase, oxidase, and TSI reactions).

Biofilm considerations

The role of biofilms in catheter-associated bacterial persistence and resistance in this clinical setting has been previously established in our earlier work [4]. Therefore, the current study focuses on the prevalence of ESBL-producing uropathogens and associated clinical factors.

Detection of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) production

Phenotypic detection of ESBL production was performed for all Gram-negative isolates according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI 2021) guidelines. The double-disc synergy test (DDST) was employed as previously described [4]. Briefly, a 0.5 McFarland bacterial suspension was inoculated onto Mueller–Hinton agar. An amoxicillin–clavulanic acid disc (20/10 µg) was placed centrally, with cefotaxime (30 µg) and ceftazidime (30 µg) discs positioned 20–30 mm apart. Following aerobic incubation at 37 °C for 18–24 h, any enhancement of the inhibition zone (synergy/keyhole effect) toward the clavulanate disc was interpreted as ESBL-positive.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 25.0). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between groups were performed using Pearson’s Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. For continuous data, the Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA was utilized. To account for multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted where necessary. All reported correlations are unadjusted for confounding factors as a multivariate model was not constructed. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The study population consisted of 100 patients (54% males and 46% females). The majority of participants (n = 70/100; 70%) were aged > 65 years, followed by those aged 50–65 years (n = 20/100; 20%) and those < 50 years (n = 10/100; 10%). All patients were recruited from the same clinical setting and met the clinical criteria for CAUTI.

Distribution of bacteria in urine samples

A total of 100 urine samples were analyzed, of which 56% (n = 56/100) showed bacterial growth. The most frequently isolated pathogen was Escherichia coli (n = 20/100; 20%). This was followed by Klebsiella spp (n = 7/100; 7%) and coagulase-negative Staphylococci (n = 7/100; 7%). Coagulase-positive Staphylococci and Enterobacter spp were identified in 6% (n = 6/100) of samples each, while Acinetobacter spp and Candida spp were detected in 5% (n = 5/100) of cases. Notably, 44% (n = 44/100) of urine samples showed no bacterial growth (Fig. 2).

Distribution of bacteria in catheter samples

Catheter-derived samples (n = 100) demonstrated a higher overall yield, with only 12% (n = 12/100) showing no growth. Klebsiella spp emerged as the predominant organism (n = 22/100; 22%), followed by E. coli (n = 20/100; 20%) and Enterobacter spp (n = 15/100; 15%). Pseudomonas spp (n = 12/100; 12%) was exclusively identified in catheter samples, showing a notable association with the catheter surface. Other isolates included coagulase-positive Staphylococci (n = 9/100; 9%) and Acinetobacter spp (n = 7/100; 7%) (Fig. 3).

Impact of demographic and clinical factors on bacterial growth

Gender

The association between patient gender and bacterial growth was evaluated for both sample types. In catheter samples, bacterial growth was detected in 92.6% (n = 50/54) of males and 82.6% (n = 38/46) of females. In urine samples, the rates were comparable between males (55.6%, n = 30/54) and females (56.5%, n = 26/46). No statistically significant association was found between gender and bacterial growth in either catheter (p = 0.132) or urine samples (p = 0.927).

Chronic diseases

A notable association was observed between the presence of chronic diseases and bacterial growth. In catheter samples, growth was detected in 98.5% (n = 64/65) of patients with chronic conditions compared to 68.6% (n = 24/35) in those without. Similarly, in urine samples, growth was observed in 69.2% (n = 45/65) of patients with chronic diseases versus 31.4% (n = 11/35) in those without comorbidities. These differences were statistically significant (p < 0.001); however, it should be noted that these associations are unadjusted for other clinical variables.

Duration of catheterization

Bacterial growth was significantly more frequent among patients with prolonged catheterization. For catheter samples, growth was detected in 94.3% (n = 66/70) of patients catheterized for > 14 days compared to 73.3% (n = 22/30) for those catheterized for \le 14 days (\chi^2 = 8.25, p = 0.004). A similar trend was observed in urine samples, with growth rates of 64.3% (n = 45/70) in the prolonged group versus 36.7% (n = 11/30) in the shorter duration group (p = 0.011).

Age

Age appeared to be associated with a higher likelihood of bacterial recovery. In catheter samples, the mean age of patients with bacterial growth was 69.7 \pm 13.6 years, compared to 52.1 \pm 15.1 years in those without growth. In urine samples, patients with bacterial growth also had a higher mean age (73.1 \pm 8.8 years) compared to those without growth (61.7 \pm 15.8 years) (p < 0.05). While age showed a significant univariate association, its role as an independent risk factor was not evaluated through multivariate modeling.

Discussion

The findings of this study underscore the complex physiological and microbiological dynamics of Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infections (CAUTIs). The observed disparities between urine and catheter-derived cultures highlight the fundamental role of the catheter surface as a distinct ecological niche. While Escherichia coli predominated in urine—reflecting its classical role as a primary uropathogen—the broader diversity of opportunistic organisms recovered from catheter specimens underscores the impact of device-associated colonization. Indwelling urinary catheters provide an ideal abiotic surface for initial microbial adhesion, a process that eventually leads to the development of mature biofilms [1, 2]. These structured communities facilitate bacterial persistence by shielding pathogens from host immune responses and diminishing the efficacy of systemic antimicrobial therapy.

The predominance of Klebsiella spp. and Pseudomonas spp. in catheter samples is particularly noteworthy. As emphasized by Ramadan [11], these organisms possess specific virulence factors, such as capsule-mediated adherence and enhanced siderophore production, which allow them to thrive in the nutrient-limited environment of a catheterized urinary tract. Furthermore, the variability in culture yields between the two specimen types suggests that catheters act as stable microbial reservoirs even when parallel urine cultures appear negative [12]. This phenomenon is partially explained by the presence of bacteria in "viable but non-culturable" (VBNC) states [13], or by the fact that antimicrobial exposure often clears planktonic bacteria from the urine while leaving protected biofilm communities intact on the catheter surface [14]. As explored in our previous work [4], this biofilm environment represents a critical microenvironment for persistence; recent evidence [15] further suggests that certain pathogens even enhance their biofilm-forming capacity when exposed to sub-inhibitory concentrations of antibiotics as a survival mechanism.

The high prevalence of ESBL-producing isolates in catheter samples (47.72%) compared to urine (25.49%) is a finding of significant clinical concern. This suggests that the catheter environment actively promotes the selection and dissemination of multidrug-resistant (MDR) traits. The proximity of diverse bacterial species within a dense biofilm matrix facilitates horizontal gene transfer via plasmid exchange, potentially leading to the rapid spread of ESBL-encoding genes [16, 17]. Our results showed a significant association (OR = 2.66) between catheterization and ESBL positivity, aligning with global reports of rising resistance in healthcare settings [16, 18]. This escalating resistance necessitates a shift toward exploring alternative or adjuvant antimicrobial strategies to manage persistent MDR clinical isolates [19], especially since the traditional β-lactam repertoire is increasingly compromised.

Regarding host-related factors, the lack of significant association between gender and bacterial growth (p > 0.05) indicated that the presence of the device itself may override the anatomical advantages typically associated with male gender in preventing UTIs [20]. In contrast, the strong associations observed with advanced age and chronic comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus pointed toward the role of immunosenescence and metabolic factors in facilitating colonization. However, given the inherent challenges in eradicating established biofilms, the medical community must prioritize the development of novel antibiofilm agents and advanced surface interventions [21]. Innovative approaches, including the development of non-traditional antibiotic alternatives [22] and the potential integration of nanoparticle-based antimicrobial systems into catheter materials [23], represent promising frontiers in reducing the burden of CAUTIs.

Conclusion

Catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTIs) remain a significant healthcare challenge, driven by a complex interplay between host-related factors, microbial characteristics, and device-associated conditions. In the present study, urinary catheters were associated with significantly higher rates of bacterial recovery and ESBL-producing isolates compared to urine samples, highlighting the role of indwelling devices as reservoirs for multidrug-resistant organisms.

Prolonged catheterization, advanced age, and underlying comorbidities showed notable associations with increased bacterial growth and ESBL positivity. These findings emphasize the clinical necessity of minimizing unnecessary catheterization, maintaining strict aseptic practices, and strengthening antimicrobial stewardship strategies. Future multicenter studies incorporating molecular characterization of resistance mechanisms and biofilm-associated factors are recommended to further improve our understanding of CAUTI epidemiology and refine prevention strategies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment. First, its cross-sectional design and convenience sampling within a single clinical setting may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study relied on phenotypic methods (DDST) for ESBL detection; due to resource constraints, molecular confirmation of resistance genes was not performed, which could have provided a more comprehensive understanding of resistance diversity.

Furthermore, multivariable logistic regression analysis was not conducted due to the sample size and distribution of events; therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted cautiously as potential correlates rather than independent predictors. Finally, information regarding the duration of hospitalization, specific ward types, and prior antibiotic exposure was not systematically available, which may have influenced the observed culture negativity and resistance patterns.
