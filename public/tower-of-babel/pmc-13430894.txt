Molecular evolution and mechanisms underlying extremely high-level carbapenem resistance in the emerging CRPA clone ST1976
Authors: Yue Gong, Yanjiang Shao, Yanlei Shen, Peiyu Li, Xiaoyu Zhao, Xin Tian, Ruichao Li, Shangshang Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA) represents a critical threat to clinical therapy, yet the genetic basis underlying extremely high-level carbapenem resistance remains poorly understood. An emerging CRPA clone, ST1976, has recently been identified in China with unusually high meropenem MICs.MethodsST1976 CRPA isolates collected between 2018 and 2022 were subjected to antimicrobial susceptibility testing, whole-genome and Nanopore sequencing, and bioinformatic analyses. Cloning assays and sodium mercaptoacetic acid inhibition tests were performed to investigate the molecular basis of carbapenem resistance.ResultsDuring the study period from 2018 to 2022, ST1976 was one of the predominant CRPA clones in Henan Province, exhibiting a significantly increasing trend and carrying significantly more resistance and virulence genes than other major regional clones. Phylogenomic analyses suggested that ST1976 emerged approximately two decades ago and may serve as a regional reservoir of blaIMP-10. In this study, the blaIMP-10 gene in all ST1976 CRPA isolates was located on the same plasmid type, and carriage of this plasmid did not impose a significant fitness cost on the bacterial host. Notably, all blaIMP-10-positive ST1976 isolates displayed extremely high-level meropenem resistance (MICs ≥ 1280 µg/mL), primarily mediated by IMP-10, with additional contributions from chromosomal oprD mutations.ConclusionsST1976 represents an emerging high-risk CRPA clone characterized by convergent plasmid- and chromosome-mediated mechanisms driving extremely high-level carbapenem resistance, highlighting the need for enhanced surveillance and targeted interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05174-6.

Introduction

Carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA) has emerged as a critical global public health concern due to its prominent role in nosocomial infections worldwide [1, 2]. Clinically, CRPA is a leading cause of severe invasive infections, including ventilator-associated pneumonia, bloodstream infections, and urinary tract infections, which are associated with high morbidity and mortality rates [3]. Immunocompromised individuals, such as patients with cystic fibrosis, malignancies, and those hospitalized in intensive care units (ICUs), are at increased risk of these infections. Global epidemiological surveillance indicated that CRPA is widely distributed across multiple continents, with particularly high prevalence reported in Asia, Europe, and the Americas [4]. In recognition of its critical clinical and public health impact, the World Health Organization (WHO) classified CRPA as a priority antimicrobial-resistant bacteria in 2024 [5].

Carbapenem resistance in P. aeruginosa is mediated by multiple strategies, including production of carbapenemases, inactivation of the outer membrane porin OprD, and overexpression of efflux pumps. Among these, carbapenemase acquisition plays a critical role in shaping the carbapenem-resistant phenotype of P. aeruginosa. The carbapenemases present in P. aeruginosa include Ambler class A (e.g. KPC, GES), class B metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs, such as IMP, VIM, and NDM), and class D (e.g. OXA-48-like, OXA-198) enzymes [6, 7]. However, previous studies have shown that carbapenemase production is detected in a relatively low proportion of CRPA isolates globally compared to non-enzymatic mechanisms [8, 9]. Critically, multiple resistance mechanisms are frequently present within a single clinical isolate, conferring carbapenem resistance with minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) typically ranging from 8 to 128 µg/mL and resulting in unpredictable antimicrobial susceptibility phenotypes [10]. However, the molecular basis underlying extremely high-level carbapenem resistance remains poorly understood, especially in emerging CRPA lineages exhibiting MICs far exceeding this conventional range.

The distribution of CRPA sequence types (STs) exhibits marked geographical variation. Internationally recognized high-risk clones, such as ST235 and ST244, play a significant role in the global dissemination of CRPA [4, 11]. Other STs, including ST463, ST111, and ST1457, have also been identified as prevalent and concerning clones in specific regions [4, 10, 12]. However, accumulating evidence indicates that the population structure of carbapenem-resistant strains has shifted over time, particularly in the post-COVID-19 period, with the emergence of multiple novel STs and the replacement of previously dominant clones [13]. These dynamic changes underscore the importance of continuous monitoring of ST distribution to inform effective surveillance and infection control strategies.

In this study, we report the emergence of ST1976 CRPA clinical isolates harboring blaIMP-10 in Henan Province of China, which exhibit extremely high-level carbapenem resistance. We comprehensively characterized their space–time evolution, and further investigated the underlying molecular mechanisms responsible for the high-level carbapenem-resistant phenotype.

Methods

Characterization of ST1976 CRPA isolates in clinical settings

We previously performed whole-genome sequencing on CRPA isolates collected between 2018 and 2022 and analyzed the genomic changes of CRPA populations before and after the COVID-19 pandemic [13]. During this analysis, a previously unrecognized ST, ST1976, was identified and found to be an increasingly prevalent clone during the 2018–2022 study period. Notably, this clone not only exhibited a clear increasing prevalence trend during the study period, but also commonly displayed extremely high-level resistance to meropenem. Based on these observations, ST1976 CRPA isolates were selected for further in-depth genomic and phenotypic analyses (Table S1).

Screening ST1976 Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates in retrospective data

In addition to the isolates characterized in this study, publicly available P. aeruginosa genome sequences were retrieved from public databases to identify additional ST1976 isolates. All P. aeruginosa genome sequences available at the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) genome database as of 2 February 2024 were retrieved. To reduce the influence of low-quality assemblies, genomes were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: genome size > 7 Mb, contig number > 1,000, N50 < 10,000 bp, missing collection year or country information, or ambiguous species annotation. Then, all quality-qualified ST1976 P. aeruginosa isolates were selected for further analysis (n = 7). Basic information of ST1976 P. aeruginosa isolates was collected from NCBI by using a python script. The country, year of isolation, and accession number of the isolates were listed in an Excel spreadsheet (Table S2).

Bioinformatics analysis

ARGs and plasmid replicons were detected using the online tools ResFinder v4.6.0 and PlasmidFinder v2.0.1, with default parameters [14, 15]. Virulence genes were determined using the virulence factor database (VFDB; accessed April 2025) [16]. The mlst v.2.30.0 tool was used to detect the MLST of isolates. The serotypes of all CRPA isolates were identified using pasty v.1.0.3 software with default parameters [17]. The network graph describing co-occurrence of ARGs and insertion sequences was constructed using Gephi v.0.10.1 [18].

Bayesian divergence estimates

To determine the evolutionary history of ST1976 lineage, a time-scaled phylogeny was inferred using a Bayesian framework. A core genome alignment of all ST1976 genomes (n = 31) was created using Roary v.3.13.0 and recombinant regions were identified and removed using Gubbins v.3.4. The resulting recombination-free core-genome alignment was used as the input for BEAST v.1.10.4 [19]. The GTR nucleotide substitution model with four gamma, an uncorrelated relaxed clock model, and a constant-size coalescent tree prior as the most appropriate demographic model. BEAST was run for 500 million iterations, logging every 1,000 steps, with the first 10% of samples discarded as burn‐in [20]. The maximum clade credibility (MCC) tree was calculated using TreeAnnotator v1.10.4, which was plotted in FigTree v1.4.4 (https://github.com/rambaut/figtree) and visualized in iTOL v.7.5 [21].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

MICs of ST1976 CRPA isolates against seven antibiotics (polymyxin B, meropenem, amikacin, levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, ceftazidime, and aztreonam) were determined by the broth microdilution method and interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100 guidelines. Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was served as the quality control strain. All experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates to ensure reproducibility.

Localization of the blaIMP-10 gene

To further investigate the genetic environment surrounding blaIMP-10, representative strain was selected for third-generation Nanopore sequencing based on the second-generation sequencing results. Representative isolate was selected for Nanopore sequencing based on three criteria: (i) carriage of blaIMP-10 and extremely high meropenem MICs, (ii) different positions within the ST1976 phylogenetic tree or different isolation years to cover the observed clonal diversity, and (iii) high-quality short-read sequencing data indicating the presence of blaIMP-10-carrying plasmid contigs. Complete plasmid sequences were assembled using Unicycler v.0.4.8 software. The complete plasmid sequence served as a reference plasmid for comparative analysis with second-generation sequences of ST1976 isolates from this study. Additionally, we searched the database for plasmids similar to the blaIMP-10-positive plasmids identified in this study to assess their distribution, and the results were visualized using BRIG v.0.95 [22].

Construction of blaIMP-10-positive plasmids carrying strains

In order to investigate the contribution of blaIMP-10-positive plasmids to carbapenem resistance, electroporation experiments were performed. Electrocompetent cells of P. aeruginosa PAO1 and PA19-3298 were prepared, and blaIMP-10-positive plasmids derived from a representative ST1976 strain (PA22-125) were introduced by electroporation using the following conditions: 200 Ω, 2,000 V, and 25 μF. The transformants were selected on LB agar plates supplemented with meropenem at concentrations of 5 µg/mL for PAO1 and 50 µg/mL for PA19-3298, respectively. Successful transformants were confirmed by colony PCR and DNA sequencing.

Construction of pUCP24-blaIMP-10 recombinant plasmid

The DNA fragments of blaIMP-10 and pUCP24 were amplified by PCR from PA22-125 plasmid DNA and pUCP24 plasmid using 2 × Phanta Max Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), respectively. The sequences of all primers used in this study are provided in Table S4. The resulting PCR products were subsequently ligated using a BM seamless cloning kit (CL116, Biomed, China) and then introduced into electrocompetent cells of PAO1 (a standard laboratory strain) and PA19-3298 (a clinical blaIMP-10-negative ST1976 isolate) via electroporation. Positive transformants were screened on LB agar plates containing meropenem at concentrations of 5 µg/mL for PAO1 and 50 µg/mL for PA19-3298, respectively. Successful transformants were confirmed by colony PCR and DNA sequencing.

Sodium mercaptoacetic acid (SMA) inhibition test

Previous studies have demonstrated that SMA can inhibit the activity of metalloenzyme IMP-10 [23]. To further confirm the contribution of IMP-10 to the extremely high-level carbapenem resistance in ST1976 CRPA isolates, we performed SMA inhibition test. Bacterial strains were grown overnight and diluted 100-fold in fresh Mueller–Hinton (MH) broth for another 4 h. Subsequently, bacterial cultures were adjusted to approximately 5 × 105 CFU/mL and then cultured with twofold serial dilutions of meropenem in combination with SMA at concentrations of 0.25, 0.5, or 1 mM. MICs were determined after incubation at 37 ℃ for 18 h. All experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates​ to ensure reproducibility.

Analysis of OprD Mutations

The inactivation of OprD is the most common mechanism of imipenem resistance in P. aeruginosa, typically resulting from insertion-deletions or nonsense mutations. To analyze the mutations in OprD among the ST1976 isolates in this study, we extracted the OprD sequences from all ST1976 strains and aligned with the reference oprD sequence of P. aeruginosa PAO1 (accession number: NC_002516).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and plotting were performed using R v4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Spearman correlation analysis was used to determine the correlation among ARGs, insertion sequences, VFs, and plasmid replicons. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distance was performed to evaluate the composition of the VFs and ARGs in the CRPA isolated in different countries. Clinical data were collated into Microsoft Excel 2013. X2 test statistics were performed using the standard library within the R statistical package.

Results

Overview of CRPA isolates

We previously conducted a molecular epidemiological analysis on 628 non-duplicate CRPA isolates collected from hospitals in Henan Province, China [13]. The 628 CRPA isolates could be classified into 153 known STs. ST235 was the dominant ST of CRPAs (21.5%), followed by ST244 (6.21%), ST274 (4.46%), ST357 (3.98%) and ST1976 (3.82%). Further analysis revealed that carbapenemase-producing ST1976 CRPA isolates were primarily distributed among male patients over 45 years of age (Fig. 1B). Violin plot results showed that the number of ARGs carried by ST1976 CRPA isolates was significantly higher than that of ST274 and ST357 isolates (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1C). Additionally, ST1976 CRPA isolates carried significantly more VFs compared to ST235 (21.5%), ST274 (4.46%), and ST357 (3.98%) isolates (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1D). Based on these findings, we proceeded with further investigation into the characteristics of ST1976 CRPA.

Evolutionary trajectory of ST1976 CRPA isolates

Serotyping analysis revealed that all 24 ST1976 CRPA isolates detected in this study were of the O11 serotype (Table S1). These isolates, collected between 2018 and 2022 in China, were recovered from a diverse range of clinical specimens, including sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, bronchial lavage fluid, tracheal secretions, urine, and cerebrospinal fluid. To explore the evolutionary relationship between the ST1976 CRPA isolates from this study and ST1976 P. aeruginosa from the NCBI database (n = 7), we constructed a Bayesian evolutionary tree (Fig. 2, Table S2). Pan-genome analysis showed the core genes were 5,525. Notably, all currently available ST1976 P. aeruginosa isolates, including those from our collection and the NCBI database, were found to originate from Henan Province, China. The estimated time to the most recent common ancestor of ST1976 P. aeruginosa for the whole genomic dataset was approximately 20 years ago. Elucidation of the resistance mechanisms in ST1976 CRPA

To investigate the antimicrobial resistance profiles of the ST1976 isolates, we conducted antimicrobial susceptibility testing and found that all ST1976 isolates exhibited high-level resistance to carbapenems, fluoroquinolones, and cephems, with the majority also showing resistance to monobactams. However, these ST1976 isolates remained susceptible to lipopeptides and aminoglycosides (Table S3). Notably, all ST1976 isolates carrying the blaIMP-10 gene displayed extremely high-level resistance to meropenem (MICs ≥ 1280 µg/mL), whereas a blaIMP-10-negative isolate (PA19-3298) showed a markedly lower MIC of 10 µg/mL. This marked difference suggests that blaIMP-10 plays a dominant role in conferring the extremely high-level meropenem resistance phenotype in ST1976 CRPA. Previous studies have reported that the meropenem MICs for blaIMP-10-carrying E. coli typically reach 64 µg/mL, and other non-ST1976 P. aeruginosa strains generally exhibit MICs of 128 µg/mL [24]. In contrast, the ST1976 strain in our study showed meropenem MICs of ≥ 1280 µg/mL (Table S3), which is at least tenfold higher than that observed in other genetic backgrounds. This significant discrepancy suggests that the genomic environment within ST1976 may synergistically enhance the meropenem resistance phenotype. To further explore the genetic environment of the blaIMP-10 gene, we selected isolate PA22-125 based on second-generation sequencing results and performed third-generation Nanopore sequencing. Plasmid backbone analysis revealed that blaIMP-10 was consistently located on IncP60512-IMP-type plasmids in all sequenced isolates (Fig. 4). To further confirm the contribution of other potential factors on the native plasmid to carbapenem resistance, we constructed pUCP24-blaIMP-10 recombinant plasmid, and transformed into PAO1 and PA19-3298. The resulting transformants also exhibited a high-level meropenem MIC of 640 µg/mL, which provides direct evidence that blaIMP-10 is the key genetic determinant responsible for the carbapenem resistance. This conclusion was further confirmed through a SMA inhibition assay. SMA is a well-known inhibitor of metalloenzyme IMP-10 [23]. The addition of SMA induced a dose-dependent reduction in the meropenem MIC of the constructed strains, confirming that the activity of IMP-10 is directly responsible for the carbapenem resistance.

Characterization of OprD Variant in ST1976

We observed that while blaIMP-10 plays a crucial role in mediating high-level resistance to meropenem in the strains, some isolates exhibited MIC values ≥ 1280 for meropenem. Furthermore, the meropenem MIC of 10 µg/mL observed in the blaIMP-10-negative isolate PA19-3298, which carries the oprD variant, is higher than previously reported MIC of 4 µg/mL for the PAO1 reference strain with a singular oprD mutation [25], indicating the involvement of additional resistance mechanisms within the ST1976 genetic background. Comparative sequence analysis revealed that ST1976 isolates harbored altered chromosomal oprD variants. Amino acid alignment showed that the N-terminal region of OprD was highly conserved between ST1976 isolates and the PAO1 reference strain (NC_002516), whereas the C-terminal region was markedly divergent (Fig. S1). Nucleotide alignment suggested that short indel events within the 3′ region of oprD may account for the altered C-terminal amino acid sequence. These C-terminal alterations are predicted to impair normal OprD porin function; however, complete functional loss could not be experimentally confirmed because OprD expression and porin activity were not directly measured in this study.

Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively characterized an emerging CRPA clone, ST1976, in Henan Province, China, which exhibits an unusual combination of sustained clonal expansion and extremely high-level carbapenem resistance. Unlike most circulating CRPA lineages, ST1976 is marked by a high prevalence of the metallo-β-lactamase gene blaIMP-10 and meropenem MICs far exceeding conventional resistance ranges. Through integrated genomic, phylogenetic, and functional analyses, we show that this resistance phenotype is driven by the combination of plasmid-borne carbapenemase production and chromosomal resistance mechanisms, underscoring the clinical and epidemiological significance of this clone.

A pivotal finding of this work is the near-universal carriage of blaIMP-10 among the ST1976 isolates, which contrasts with the global epidemiology of CRPA where carbapenemase production is not the predominant resistance mechanism [8, 9]. Phylogenomic analysis indicates that ST1976 likely emerged approximately two decades ago, implying sustained circulation and expansion rather than a recent outbreak, and positioning this clone as a long-term reservoir for blaIMP-10 within clinical settings. While numerous IMP variants have been identified globally, most are associated with moderate-to-high resistance levels [26, 27]. Our findings indicate that the extreme meropenem MICs observed in ST1976 are synergistically driven by potent hydrolysis of IMP-10 and chromosomal oprD mutations. While blaIMP-10 has been documented in E. coli and other non-ST1976 P. aeruginosa clones, associated meropenem MICs are generally in the range of 64–128 µg/mL [24]. The consistent observation of MICs ≥ 1280 µg/mL in ST1976 isolates suggests that additional lineage-specific factors potentiate its phenotypic impact. Together, the convergence of these mechanisms drives a breakthrough beyond the conventional resistance ceiling, rendering conventional carbapenem therapies futile. Together, these features suggest that ST1976 is not merely an emerging clone, but rather represents an evolutionarily optimized lineage in which resistance determinants have become stably integrated without compromising bacterial fitness.

From a public health perspective, the emergence of ST1976 as a stable carrier of blaIMP-10 is particularly concerning. The localization of blaIMP-10 on Incp60512-IMP-type plasmids without an apparent fitness cost suggests a high potential for long-term maintenance and clonal expansion in clinical environments (Fig. S2). Moreover, the identification of blaIMP-10-harboring CRPA across diverse regions, including Switzerland, Japan, South Korea, and China, indicates that this resistance determinant is spreading silently under selective pressure [24, 28–30]. Of particular concern is the potential future threat this clone poses to next-generation β-lactamase inhibitors. Recent evidence suggests that specific IMP variants, including IMP-6, IMP-10, IMP-14, and IMP-26 variants, showed an alarming capacity to resist emerging inhibitors like xeruborbactam [26]. While blaIMP-10 has been reported in various countries, its association with the ST1976 clone appears to be particularly prominent in Henan Province, China. Consequently, close surveillance and early intervention strategies are urgently needed to prevent the dissemination of high-risk clones such as ST1976.

This study has several limitations. First, the isolates analyzed were derived from a single province in China, which may limit the generalizability of our epidemiological findings. Multicenter and nationwide surveillance studies are warranted to better understand the epidemiology and clinical significance of blaIMP-10-harboring ST1976 CRPA. Additionally, although our in vitro experiments clearly demonstrate the contribution of blaIMP-10 and associated mechanisms to high-level carbapenem resistance, in vivo studies are required to further elucidate their roles in clinical outcomes. Finally, we did not directly quantify the expression of major efflux pump systems or assess their contribution using efflux pump inhibitors. Therefore, additional non-carbapenemase mechanisms may partly explain the residual meropenem resistance observed in the blaIMP-10-negative ST1976 isolate.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study systematically characterizes the emerging CRPA clone ST1976 in Henan Province, China, and elucidates the mechanisms underlying its extremely high-level carbapenem resistance. We demonstrate that this phenotype is synergistically driven by the convergence of plasmid-borne blaIMP-10 and chromosomal oprD mutations. The lack of an apparent fitness cost associated with the blaIMP-10-carrying plasmid may contribute to the persistence and expansion of this lineage. These findings highlight ST1976 as a high-risk CRPA clone and emphasize the need for continuous molecular surveillance and targeted infection control strategies.
