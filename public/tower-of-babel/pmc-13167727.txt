Traveling-wave transcranial alternating current stimulation (twtACS) causally links neural timing to cognitive function
Authors: Sangjun Lee, Jimin Park, Ivan Alekseichuk, Taylor A. Berger, Ana M. G. Manea, Harry Tran, Gabriela Delgado Salazar, Seth D. König, Alexander B. Herman, David P. Darrow, Jan Zimmermann, Alexander Opitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceCortical traveling waves (TWs) have been observed across species and cognitive states, yet their causal role in brain function has remained unclear. A major challenge has been the lack of tools to selectively impose TW-like spatiotemporal dynamics in a direction-specific manner. Here, we introduce traveling-wave transcranial alternating current stimulation (twtACS), a noninvasive approach that enables direction-specific shaping of neural activity. By combining intracranial validation, nonhuman primate neural spiking measurements, and human behavioral experiments, we demonstrate that directional twtACS aligns spike timing into a TW-like spatiotemporal pattern, accompanied by direction-dependent changes in cognitive performance. These findings establish TWs as a causal control parameter of brain function and provide a framework for direction-specific neuromodulation.

Cognitive dysfunction is a growing concern in both aging populations and individuals with psychiatric disorders, negatively impacting everyday functioning and quality of life (1, 2). Both neurodegenerative diseases [e.g., Parkinson’s disease (3), dementia (4)] and psychiatric disorders [e.g., major depressive disorder (5), schizophrenia (6)] have been linked to deficits in cognitive function, which are often not directly addressed by current treatments. To better understand these impairments, numerous studies have been conducted to explore the neural dynamics of brain oscillations underlying cognition. Brain oscillations play a fundamental role in coordinating neural activity across brain regions (7) and are crucial for various cognitive processes, including attention and memory (8, 9). Traditionally, they have been studied as markers of synchrony or functional connectivity between regions (10, 11). However, recent evidence suggests that neural activity can also manifest as spatially continuous patterns across the cortex, shifting the focus toward their role in large-scale neural coordination in various cortical areas (12–14).

Cortical traveling waves (TWs) are one potential mechanism for large-scale neural coordination during cognition (15, 16). Unlike stationary oscillations, TWs propagate across the cortex, carrying neural information through a gradual phase shift across cortical regions (17). These patterns have been observed across species, from small animals (12) to nonhuman primates (NHPs) (18, 19), and in humans (20), where distinctive TWs in the low-frequency range were observed during cognitive processes. In human studies, electrocorticography (ECoG) recordings have provided direct evidence of TWs, revealing alpha and theta TWs propagating in the anterior–posterior direction during memory-related tasks (13). In addition, electroencephalography (EEG) can record alpha TWs at the scalp level (21), showing their large-scale propagation. Notably, the direction of TWs is crucial, as it reflects the sequence of communication between cortical regions and plays a key role in shaping cognitive processes (22). Indeed, studies show that TW direction adapts to task demands and cognitive context. For instance, during memory tasks, TWs propagate forward (posterior-to-anterior) during encoding, whereas they travel backward (anterior-to-posterior) during recall (13). Similarly, in visual attention tasks, forward alpha TWs are strong in the contralateral hemisphere while backward TWs dominate in the ipsilateral hemisphere (23).

Transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS) has been widely used to enhance human cognition by entraining endogenous brain rhythms or modulating network synchronization (24, 25). For instance, tACS-induced synchronization has been shown to improve working memory in older adults (26, 27). However, conventional tACS is limited in its ability to generate dynamic patterns of neural activity, such as TWs, as it produces a spatially stationary tACS-induced electric field in the cortex, where it has the same phase across locations, so that the maximum electric field occurs at the same spatial site throughout the stimulation.

Traveling-wave tACS (twtACS) overcomes this limitation by injecting sinusoidal currents with systematically controlled phase differences through multiple electrodes (28, 29). The difference in phase causes the location of the maximum twtACS-induced electric field to shift across cortical sites over time, creating a spatial phase gradient, such as a traveling wave, in contrast to conventional tACS. Combined with phasor optimization, the phase of currents can be determined to produce a desired phase gradient across brain regions. This approach is conceptually similar to multichannel tACS optimization (30), except that the primary variables being optimized are the current phases rather than their amplitudes. This technique allows twtACS to generate spatiotemporal dynamics that closely mimic neural activity observed during cognitive tasks, enabling direct examination of how manipulating these patterns influences behavioral performance.

Here, we investigate the causal effects of directional TWs on cognition using noninvasive brain stimulation. First, we validate that twtACS can generate a directed TW-like electric field using intracranial recordings and computational modeling (Fig. 1 A and B). We then investigate whether neural population activity is modulated by twtACS, leveraging large-scale invasive recordings in a nonhuman primate (NHP). We found that large-scale shifts in multiunit activity (MUA) spike timing were spatially aligned with the direction of applied twtACS across the cortex. No spatial preference in spike timing alignment was observed during baseline and regular tACS conditions (Fig. 1C). Finally, we conduct behavioral experiments, stimulating regions across the right frontal and parietal cortices where TWs have been observed during cognitive tasks (13, 23). We apply either forward or backward twtACS to investigate its effects on behavioral performance in healthy participants. Participants performed two tasks—a visual attention task and an episodic memory task—both of which are known to elicit distinct directional TWs (Fig. 1D). We found evidence that twtACS can influence cognitive performance, with TW direction playing a critical role in task-specific outcomes. Notably, the direction of twtACS led to a dissociation in behavioral outcomes across tasks. This dissociation between tasks was further supported by electrophysiological changes in pre- and poststimulation resting-state EEG.

Results

Experiment 1: Physics of twtACS in Human ECoG.

We first recorded ECoG signals in two individuals to investigate whether twtACS produces TW-like electric fields on the cortical surface. To achieve precise phase control, we applied a phase optimization technique designed to generate the desired phase gradient across the cortex (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). In Experiment 1, twtACS was used to generate TW-like electric fields with two different spatial phase gradients: 45° and 90° anterior-lead (backward) conditions. Here, 45° and 90° refer to the total phase offset across the stimulation electrodes, with the phase gradient of the twtACS-induced electric field defined along the frontal–parietal (anterior–posterior) direction based on the electrode configuration. While the 45° condition produced a specific phase gradient of the electric field between frontal and parietal regions, the 90° condition was designed to produce a phase gradient that was twice as steep. These configurations allowed us to evaluate the precision of twtACS in imposing and modulating the magnitude of spatial phase gradients across the cortex.

Fig. 2 illustrates TW-like electric fields generated by twtACS with a cortical phase gradient, measured across ECoG electrodes. Using a realistic head model of Participant 1 with ECoG strip electrodes (Fig. 2A), we optimized the twtACS electrode condition to generate either 45° or 90° anterior-lead (backward) TW-like electric fields, with a gradual phase gradient over the cortical surface (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The amplitudes and phases of alternating currents for the active electrodes are listed in Table 1. As shown in Fig. 2B, the optimized phase closely matches the target angles for the optimization, with an average absolute error (mean ± SD) of 1.73° ± 1.52° for the optimized 45° condition and 3.71° ± 2.03° for the optimized 90° condition across eight strip electrodes.

We then applied two different stimulation conditions to the participant and calculated the electric field from ECoG recordings (Fig. 2C). The optimized 45° and 90° conditions successfully generated TW-like electric fields with a consistent phase gradient across electrodes in the desired backward direction. We confirmed that the desired phase gradient was generated by twtACS (see SI Appendix, Fig. S2B and Movie S1). Circular correlation analysis revealed a significant relationship between the simulated and measured phase gradients for the optimized 45° condition (r = 0.98, P = 0.03) and the optimized 90° condition (r = 0.99, P = 0.02) (Fig. 2D), confirming that twtACS accurately induced the phase pattern predicted by the simulations.

We also optimized the twtACS electrode condition in Participant 2 (Fig. 2E). The optimized phase angles align well with the target angles, with an average absolute error (mean ± SD) of 2.31° ± 1.70° for the optimized 45° condition and 4.84° ± 1.26° for the optimized 90° condition across four ROI points (Fig. 2F and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Fig. 2G demonstrates electric fields at four representative electrodes from the ECoG grid for two twtACS conditions. The optimized 45° and 90° conditions successfully produced TW-like electric fields across the electrodes in the desired backward direction (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B and Movie S2). Due to the grid electrodes representing a smaller cortical area compared to the ROI points used in the optimization, the resulting gradient observed in the grid is expected to be less than the desired phase gradient. Circular correlation analysis demonstrated that the measured phase gradients across grid electrodes were highly consistent with the simulated ones, showing significant correlations for the optimized 45° condition (r = 0.92, P = 0.003) and the optimized 90° condition (r = 0.95, P = 0.02) (Fig. 2G).

Experiment 2: Electrophysiological Effects of twtACS in NHP Recordings.

We next investigated how twtACS influences neural population activity in NHP recordings using intracortical tungsten microelectrodes. Directional twtACS (either forward or backward) was applied by imposing fixed phase offsets between active electrodes at a frequency of 10 Hz, thereby defining a TW-like electric field with a controlled spatial phase gradient along the anterior–posterior axis. In the forward condition, posterior sites led anterior sites, whereas the backward condition reversed this progression. We then evaluated whether the timing of neural population activity, represented by MUA, shifted across the cortex in alignment with stimulation direction (Fig. 3A). Specifically, we assessed whether the preferred phase of MUA with respect to the applied stimulation frequency systematically shifted along the anterior–posterior direction across the cortex, consistent with the direction of twtACS. We also evaluated MUA during baseline and standard tACS conditions (Fig. 3B).

Fig. 3C shows the preferred phase of MUA spike timing with respect to the applied alternating current waveform across electrodes. Under standard tACS, the MUA phase distribution did not exhibit a significant phase gradient across the cortex. This is consistent with previous work showing that standard tACS does not induce phase shifts across the cortex (31). In contrast, both forward and backward twtACS induced clear phase gradients: The preferred phase increased along the anterior–posterior direction during forward twtACS and decreased during backward twtACS. Rayleigh tests confirmed that MUA was significantly phase-locked to the external stimulation in all stimulation conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B), suggesting that the stimulation guided phase shifts by entraining neural population activity. These effects were further supported by significant linear–circular correlations between electrode location and MUA phase for both forward (R = 0.55, P < 0.01) and backward (R = 0.61, P < 0.01) twtACS conditions. In contrast, neither standard tACS (R = 0.08, P = 0.58) nor the baseline condition (R = 0.01, P = 0.98; see Fig. 3B) showed a significant phase gradient across the cortex. In addition, we compared the phase gradient of MUA to that of the electric field induced by twtACS (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). The spatial phase gradient of MUA showed a slope of 1.17 deg/mm in the forward condition and −1.24 deg/mm in the backward condition. Similarly, the slope of the electric field phase was 1.03 deg/mm and −1.19 deg/mm for the forward and backward conditions, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). This close match suggests that neural population activity is aligned with the propagating twtACS-induced electric fields.

Experiment 3: Causal Role of Directional Traveling Waves in Human Cognition.

Based on the NHP findings, we next examined whether similar direction-dependent effects were reflected in human behavior. For behavioral experiments, we applied optimized 10 Hz twtACS to generate either 90° posterior-lead forward or anterior-lead backward twtACS-induced electric fields across the frontoparietal regions. The stimulation conditions are listed in Table 1. Computational simulations confirmed that the optimized conditions successfully generated the desired phase gradient across targeted regions for both forward and backward conditions (Fig. 4A), achieving focal stimulation of these regions (Fig. 4B). During twtACS, participants first engaged in a visual attention task, followed by an episodic memory task (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). In the visual attention task, participants covertly attended to either the left or right flickering disk while maintaining central fixation, with the attended side indicated by a central arrow. Brief target and distractor stimuli were presented within the attended or unattended disk, and participants reported the presence of a target at the end of each trial. Left- and right-attention conditions were included because TW direction during visual attention differs between ipsilateral and contralateral cortical regions (23), enabling assessment of hemifield-specific interactions with directional twtACS. In the memory task, participants encoded 60 images during twtACS, and after a 10-min rest period following stimulation, memory was evaluated using an old–new recognition test featuring both previously seen and novel images.

Fig. 4 C–I depict the behavioral outcomes. A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant main effects of stimulation condition [F(1,21) = 5.90; P = 0.024] during the visual attention task, indicating that task performance differs between forward and backward twtACS. Importantly, there was a significant interaction between stimulation condition and attention [F(1,21) = 11.69; P = 0.003], suggesting that the effects of the stimulation condition on task performance depend on whether participants attended to the right or left visual field. Post hoc comparisons showed significantly higher accuracy for backward twtACS (83.71% ± 1.95%, mean ± SE) than forward twtACS (78.90% ± 2.69%) when attending to the right (P = 0.013). In contrast, no significant difference was found for left attention, where the accuracy was 79.53% ± 2.65% for the backward twtACS and 78.05% ± 3.04% for the forward twtACS (Fig. 4C). No significant effects were observed for reaction time (Fig. 4D). For the memory task (Fig. 4E), the task accuracy was significantly higher with the forward condition (85.57% ± 1.59%) than the backward condition (82.90% ± 1.96%; t(24) = 3.11, P = 0.005, paired Cohen’s d = 0.62, 95% CI [0.21, 1.03]), while there were no significant effects for reaction time (Fig. 4F). Interestingly, only the accuracy difference between forward and backward conditions in the memory task, not the visual attention task, correlated with memory-related questionnaire scores, including the PRMQ (r = 0.44; P = 0.037) and the everyday 13-item questionnaire (r = 0.47; P = 0.037), suggesting that effects of directional modulation were stronger in participants with self-reported poor memory performance (Fig. 4G and SI Appendix, Fig. S8).

Fig. 4H shows sham-corrected accuracy for the visual attention task. No significant interaction was observed between attention direction and stimulation direction. The sham-corrected accuracy (mean ± SE) was higher in the backward condition (3.97% ± 1.66%) than in the forward condition (-−0.97% ± 1.90%) for right visual attention, corresponding to a Cohen’s d of 0.76 (95% CI [−0.06, 1.59]). Bootstrapping indicated that the sham-corrected accuracy difference between the forward and backward conditions under right visual attention had a 95% CI of [−9.34, −1.43], which excludes zero. In contrast, under left visual attention, the corresponding bootstrap CI included zero (95% CI [−7.76, 1.16]). For the memory task (Fig. 4I), the sham-corrected accuracy was higher in the forward condition (1.58% ± 2.02%) than in the backward condition (−1.08% ± 1.27%), with a Cohen’s d of 0.7 (95% CI [−0.11, 1.51]). Bootstrapping indicated a mean sham-corrected accuracy difference between forward and backward stimulations of 2.67%, with a 95% CI of [0.42, 5.08]. These contrasts indicate that one stimulation direction tended to improve performance relative to sham, whereas the opposite direction tended to reduce it.

Resting state EEG recordings revealed a significant increase in forward (FW) wave power after forward twtACS in the right hemisphere (t = 2.03, P = 0.04), which was our targeted region (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S9A), based on 2D FFT analysis (23). In contrast, backward twtACS did not significantly affect wave power in either direction. Because FW and backward (BW) wave powers are decompositions of alpha power across limited space, we analyzed alpha power, not restricted to TW, across the entire scalp to uncover a more general effect of the stimulation. The alpha power analysis displayed a more systematic change in the alpha activity. Cluster permutation displayed a global increase in alpha power (t-sum = 45.52, P < 0.001), while no cluster with significant alpha power modulation was found for the backward twtACS. However, alpha lateralization of the parietal and frontal sites was only modulated by backward twtACS, showing significant and trend-level lateralization toward the contralateral hemisphere to the stimulation (left hemisphere), respectively (parietal: t = 2.32, P = 0.03; frontal: t = 2.01, P = 0.055). Forward twtACS did not modulate the alpha lateralization (parietal: t = 1.28, P = 0.21; frontal: t = 0.36, P = 0.72). The changes for alpha power and lateralization for forward and backward twtACS are depicted in Fig. 5 B, C and 5 D-E, respectively.

Discussion

Here we present evidence to establish a direction-specific causal effect of traveling waves on cognition through experimentally validated twtACS optimization, directional modulation of neural activity, and dissociable behavioral outcomes. Guided by computational simulations, twtACS successfully generated precise TW-like electric fields on the cortical surface, as confirmed by intracranial ECoG recordings. In NHP experiments, we further confirmed that directional twtACS induced systematic shifts in neural population activity, with MUA spike timing aligned to the direction of wave propagation. These neural effects translated into human behavior, where forward and backward twtACS selectively enhanced performance in two different cognitive tasks. This revealed a clear dissociation in outcomes depending on the direction of twtACS. These behavioral effects were associated with direction-specific modulation of alpha-band activity within the frontoparietal regions, as measured by resting-state EEG. Together, these results provide converging evidence for a causal link between TWs and their direction-specific effects on cognitive outcomes. Our brain stimulation protocol enables precise targeting of traveling waves and provides a strong foundation for investigating their functional role in human cognition.

In NHP recordings, neural population activity was directionally entrained by the twtACS-induced electric field, resulting in a clear spatial phase gradient of MUA compared to baseline and standard tACS. This neural entrainment leverages the biophysical principles of ephaptic coupling, defined as the coordinating influence of local electric field gradients on neural spike timing (32, 33). While ephaptic coupling traditionally refers to endogenous field interactions, twtACS allows causal engagement of this principle by imposing external electric fields, thereby inducing targeted spatial phase shifts in neural activity across large-scale cortical regions and providing direct evidence that twtACS can organize spatiotemporal neural activity. Importantly, these externally imposed electric fields differ fundamentally from endogenous electric fields, which arise from synaptic activity and reflect the underlying microcircuit architecture. Accordingly, the effects observed here should be interpreted specifically in the context of twtACS, rather than as a general property of endogenous field interactions. The observed effects arise from the interaction between alternating current stimulation and spike timing (34, 35). Under standard tACS, spikes tend to phase-lock to a specific phase of the electric field [e.g., the peak phase (36)], resulting in spatially uniform spike timing across electrodes and no observable phase gradient, consistent with our data. In contrast, under twtACS, the peak of the electric field propagates across the cortex due to the imposed phase gradient. As spikes remain locked to the phase of the local electric field, this causes the timing of neural population activity to shift across space, forming a spatial phase gradient. These findings establish direct evidence that externally imposed phase gradients can induce spatial patterns in neural activity.

Compared to traveling waves observed during the human resting state (17), we did not observe a spatial phase gradient in MUA under baseline conditions. This dissociation likely reflects the distinct physiological basis of local field potentials (LFPs) and MUA. LFPs integrate spatially broad, volume-conducted synaptic activity, whereas MUA reflects the output of a more spatially local neural population (37), such that pinpointing the temporal interaction between distal populations during rest is much more difficult barring a common input. Indeed, recent work has demonstrated that smooth TWs in LFP can emerge from sequential activation of local neural activity, even when spiking activity lacks continuous wave-like propagation (38). Accordingly, during twtACS, spike timing can be selectively modulated even in the absence of detectable changes in ongoing LFPs (39). This suggests that spiking-level modulation can occur independently of measurable LFP changes (SI Appendix, Figs. S11A and S14). During tasks, traveling waves are transiently recruited in a task-dependent direction. Directional twtACS facilitates this process by aligning spike timing along the same spatiotemporal direction in the cortex, thereby supporting task-relevant propagation of neural activity. Our interpretation does not assume the entrainment of endogenous oscillations; rather, we propose that the externally current-driven alignment of spike timing by twtACS across a neural population creates a temporally structured population response that becomes detectable at LFPs when referenced to the stimulation cycle.

Notably, although both tACS and twtACS conditions exhibited pronounced periodic spiking (SI Appendix, Fig. S11A), a robust spatial phase gradient in MUA emerged only during directional twtACS. Standard tACS produced stronger stimulation and greater spike phase preference (due to the absence of return current through the reference electrode; see SI Appendix, Figs. S10 and S14A). However, it did not elicit spatial phase organization of spiking activity. This suggests that stimulation strength alone is insufficient to generate spatial phase gradients in neural activity (SI Appendix, Fig. S11D). This interpretation is further supported by additional analyses. Permutation and bootstrapping analyses confirmed that the spatial phase gradients along the anterior–posterior direction were observed only during twtACS (SI Appendix, Figs. S11 B and C), were direction-specific, and were unlikely to arise by chance, arguing against interpretations based on stimulation artifacts or the emergence of additional oscillatory activity. Moreover, no spatial phase organization of MUA was observed after stimulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S11E), indicating that the emergence of spatial phase gradients in spiking activity was transient rather than reflecting lasting changes in endogenous oscillations. Consistent with this, although a prominent spectral peak was observed at the stimulation frequency during electrical stimulation, this peak did not persist after stimulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S14).

Our findings further demonstrate the effects of directional alpha twtACS over the right frontoparietal regions on cognitive function. In the visual attention task, backward twtACS selectively improved performance for rightward attention. Previous studies have shown that visual attention is accompanied by strong backward alpha TWs in the ipsilateral hemisphere and weaker forward TWs in the contralateral hemisphere (23, 40). Furthermore, backward TWs also increase when participants successfully hit the target and decrease during misses (23). These findings can be explained by BW waves that support top–down control, which is known to drive alpha lateralization during spatial attention (41, 42). Consistent with this, our resting-state EEG results showed that backward twtACS over the right frontoparietal regions increased both alpha power and BW wave power in the left hemisphere (Fig. 5E and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 B and C). This pattern suggests a lateralized reinforcement of top–down dynamics that support rightward attention. This interpretation aligns with evidence that top–down alpha oscillations mediate inhibitory control and filter out irrelevant stimuli during selective attention (43, 44). In contrast, forward twtACS, potentially involved in sensory processing (45, 46), may disrupt endogenous backward TWs essential for attention, as reflected in lower task accuracy, not only compared to backward twtACS but also to the sham condition.

In contrast to the visual attention task, forward twtACS significantly enhanced memory performance. This aligns with recent evidence that alpha oscillations travel forward during successful memory encoding, but bidirectionally during unsuccessful trials (13). Episodic memory formation involves neural activity flowing from posterior to frontal regions, as previously reported (47), and forward twtACS appears to reinforce this endogenous pattern to support effective memory transfer. Forward TWs have also been associated with improved task performance and are known to emerge even before stimulus onset during encoding (13), potentially priming relevant cortical regions (48, 49). Combined with our EEG results, forward twtACS increased FW wave power, suggesting engagement of a feedforward loop from parietal regions that transfers visual information and supports bottom–up preparatory processing during memory encoding. On the other hand, backward twtACS may disrupt this feedforward flow of information by amplifying the bidirectional TW pattern (50), leading to poorer encoding outcomes. These effects are unlikely due to stimulation amplitude or cutaneous sensations. Voltage amplitudes were similar between forward and backward conditions (SI Appendix, Figs. S5 F and G and S7), and simulations confirmed that the twtACS-induced electric field strength was matched across conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E). In addition, participants reported no significant differences in cutaneous sensations (e.g., itching, burning, and pain) between stimulation conditions (SI Appendix, Table S3). Furthermore, neural activity showed directional propagation that aligned with the phase gradient of the twtACS-induced electric fields, supporting the interpretation that cognitive modulation was driven by the directionality of twtACS. Sham-corrected behavioral performance also showed a directional contrast, with forward and backward twtACS producing opposite trends relative to the sham condition. While these effects were modest and based on a limited sample size, the primary aim of the present study was to isolate the effect of twtACS direction rather than to demonstrate absolute improvements over sham conditions. Overall, our results suggest that the impact of TW direction may depend on task context, with different stimulation directions exerting distinct influences on cognitive performance.

Previous EEG studies have shown that TWs occur during cognitive tasks, such as forward or backward propagation in working memory (51) or interhemispheric power changes in visual attention (23). While these results are broadly consistent with our resting-state EEG findings, caution is warranted when interpreting whether twtACS directly modulates endogenous brain waves, as resting-state measurements do not include task-related neural dynamics. Nevertheless, previous studies have shown that resting-state EEG can capture increases in oscillatory power at stimulation frequency (52, 53), implying that tACS can alter ongoing brain activity in ways that influence behavior. Indeed, in our additional analyses, theta (4 to 7 Hz) and beta (14 to 30 Hz) activity did not show stimulation effects comparable to those observed in the alpha band (SI Appendix, Figs. S12 and S13). Importantly, both cognitive tasks were performed under the same stimulation condition, with directional twtACS applied during task execution, placing them within a common stimulation-induced brain state. This design is consistent with our pre- and poststimulation resting-state EEG measurements used to characterize global, task-independent electrophysiological changes induced by twtACS. Accordingly, the EEG results indicate that twtACS altered the underlying brain state, whereas task-specific behavioral outcomes reflect how this altered state interacts with the distinct demands of each task. Task order was therefore fixed across participants to preserve this consistency and the structured demands of the memory task (e.g., the encoding, retention, and recall sequence), thereby allowing task-dependent effects of twtACS to be interpreted without additional variability introduced by task counterbalancing. Although we employed a 20-channel EEG system sampled at 500 Hz, the temporal resolution was suitable for evaluating alpha oscillations. While dense EEG could provide finer spatial precision, the consistency of phase and amplitude among head models indicates that our approach was sufficient to robustly capture stimulation effects on resting-state brain activity (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Finally, 2D FFT–based TW analyses rely on an assumption of uniform spatial sampling that may be violated by the irregular spacing of EEG electrodes. As discussed in a recent study (51), this limitation can introduce artifacts and should be considered when interpreting EEG-based wave estimates.

Our work extends previous NHP findings (28, 29) by showing that twtACS can reliably generate TW-like electric fields across the human cortical surface with a precise phase gradient through optimization. In the present study, we demonstrated twtACS, integrated with computational simulations, can produce TW-like electric fields with a precise phase gradient across the cortical surface. These findings represent a crucial advance toward creating externally applied electric fields that mimic brain oscillations propagating across the cortical surface to transfer neural information (15, 19, 20). Our results further demonstrate the adaptability of twtACS in generating TW-like electric fields across a wide frequency range, spanning from the theta to beta bands (4 Hz and 23 Hz in human ECoG and 10 Hz in the NHP). This frequency generality indicates that the ability of twtACS to impose directional phase gradients is not restricted to a specific oscillatory band, as demonstrated in Experiment 1, allowing subsequent experiments to focus on 10 Hz stimulation due to its established relevance for cognitive processes. Combined with simulation-guided targeting, twtACS offers a potential tool to shape neural activity via TW-like electric fields in a frequency- and region-specific manner, such as the alpha band (8 to 13 Hz) in the visual cortex (21, 23), the beta band (15 to 30 Hz) in the motor cortex (54), and the theta band (4 to 7 Hz) in the prefrontal cortex (19).

Unlike standard tACS, which primarily modulates local excitability through fields aligned with the pyramidal cell axis (55), twtACS delivers TW-like electric fields with a phase gradient along the anterior–posterior (AP) direction across the cortex, targeting coordinated neural population activity across brain regions. Such surface-oriented fields can also shift neural timing (36) and enhance coordinated population activity (56), supporting the idea that directional stimulation is capable of modulating both neural populations and large-scale dynamics. Based on this rationale, we focused our analyses on MUA, which captures population-level synchrony more directly than single-unit activity (SUA). While SUA can provide valuable insights into the cellular mechanisms of electrical stimulation (35), we considered MUA to be the more appropriate measure for addressing spatiotemporal dynamics targeted here. Nevertheless, future work incorporating SUA analyses will be important for developing a more detailed mechanistic understanding of twtACS.

We found that twtACS-induced electric field amplitudes measured in NHPs were comparable to those estimated in human head models (SI Appendix, Table S4). NHPs are widely used in brain stimulation research because their anatomical and physiological similarity to humans makes observed stimulation effects directly translatable (28, 35, 57), thereby providing a bridge between invasive animal recordings and human behavioral outcomes, including those demonstrated in our study. An important caveat is that NHP recordings were obtained during rest, whereas human experiments were performed during cognitive tasks. As task engagement is known to amplify TWs (13), it is plausible that persistent twtACS entrainment during tasks enhanced endogenous rhythms and contributed to the observed behavioral outcomes. Notably, TWs can be entrained even without precise phase locking, as shown in other tACS studies (25, 26, 58), yet synchronizing twtACS with task stimuli may produce stronger effects (59).

TWs in the frontoparietal regions play a central role in cognitive functions such as attention and memory (13), and abnormalities in their dynamics have been linked to various neurological and psychiatric disorders (60–63). Our findings provide causal evidence that twtACS can influence cognitive performance, with the direction of wave propagation playing a critical role in determining task-specific outcomes. This evidence highlights the promise of twtACS as a therapeutic approach for treating cognitive dysfunction, potentially in clinical populations. Notably, individuals with lower self-reported memory function exhibited stronger direction-specific effects, suggesting that twtACS may be particularly beneficial for those with reduced cognitive capacity. This may reflect the greater potential for improvement in these individuals, as those with higher baseline performance often show smaller gains due to limited room for enhancement (64). In addition, twtACS allows for precise control of phase dynamics as supported by a consistent phase gradient across individuals (SI Appendix, Fig. S6) and focal stimulation of distinct cortical regions. However, larger phase gradients were accompanied by increasing error, as shown in Experiment 1, reflecting a trade-off between amplitude and phase during optimization that may be alleviated by using more stimulation electrodes. Our study highlights the promise of twtACS as a neuromodulation technique. Future studies aimed at tuning stimulation parameters such as stimulation frequency and phase alignment to individual neural activity may further enhance its efficacy. Such advancements could contribute to the development of personalized and noninvasive brain stimulation protocols for improving cognitive function in aging and clinical populations.

Materials and Methods

We developed an optimization framework for twtACS to control spatiotemporal electric field patterns based on our previous studies (25, 29, 65). The optimized twtACS was validated using intracranial recordings in human participants, where local twtACS-induced electric field properties were directly measured, extending our previously established approaches for electric field estimation during electrical stimulation (28, 31). The effects of directional twtACS were then assessed in a nonhuman primate using laminar neural recordings to quantify spatiotemporal bias in neural spike timing. Finally, the optimized twtACS was applied in healthy human participants to assess directional effects on two cognitive tasks known to involve TW dynamics (13, 23). Animal procedures in nonhuman primates were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Minnesota and conducted in accordance with the Public Health Service Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All human participant procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Minnesota, and all participants provided written informed consent. MR data used for head modeling were obtained from previously approved datasets (66). Full details of the optimization procedure, experimental protocols, recordings, and analyses are provided in the SI Appendix.
