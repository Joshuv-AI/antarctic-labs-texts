Can superficial parotid sparing alone impact xerostomia in head and neck cancer? A retrospective study correlating the type of tobacco use, chronic xerostomia, and parotid sparing
Authors: Nikunj Patil, Sachi Chauhan, Sidharth Satish Menon, Umesh Velu, Anshul Singh, Krishna Sharan, Shirley Lewis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHead and neck cancer is one of the most common cancers in India, with tobacco chewing being the predominant form of tobacco consumption. We aimed to assess the impact of superficial gland sparing on xerostomia.Materials and methodsPatients with histopathological diagnosis of head and neck cancer treated with curative intent radiotherapy with intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) to a dose of 60–70 Gy in 30–35 fractions with or without chemotherapy from June 2017 to March 2020 were included in the study. The superficial and deep lobes of the parotid were contoured retrospectively. The physician-reported Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) xerostomia toxicity grades at two years were retrieved from records.ResultsOne hundred seventy-four patients were included in the study. Tobacco chewing was the most common form of use, followed by smoking. Tobacco chewers had significantly smaller mean parotid (53cc vs. 60cc, p = 0.02) and mean submandibular gland volumes (6 cc vs. 14 cc, p < 0.001) as compared to smokers. Bilateral or contralateral parotid sparing (mean dose < 26 Gy) was achieved in 62.7%, bilateral or contralateral superficial lobe in 27.6% and no sparing in 9.8% of patients. The xerostomia was similar in smokers and chewers (p = 0.95). Patients with bilateral or contralateral superficial lobe sparing had lower grade II/III xerostomia rates than the no-sparing group (p = 0.038).ConclusionsTobacco chewers have smaller volumes of salivary glands. Contralateral or bilateral superficial parotid sparing translated into better xerostomia scores at two years.

Introduction

Head and neck cancer has an annual burden of over 666,000 new cases and 325,000 deaths [1]. It is the most common cancer in India, with over 225,419 new cases annually. The tobacco consumption patterns seen in India are unique. Tobacco smoking is the most common form practiced across the world; however, in India, tobacco chewing is more evident [2, 3]. The consumption of ‘paan’, which is a mixture of tobacco and lime wrapped in betel leaves, is prevalent across India [4–6]. This has led to rising head and neck cancer incidence rates, especially those arising from the oral cavity.

Most cases present in a locally advanced stage (stage III or IV). If they are deemed operable, they receive adjuvant radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy depending on the risk factors [7, 8]. Radical chemoradiation is the treatment of choice for tumours arising from the nasopharynx, oropharynx, and hypopharynx [9–11]. With the advent of parotid-sparing intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), the incidence of chronic xerostomia has declined [12]. However, 40% of patients experience some degree of xerostomia post-radiation. This has a poor impact on swallowing, speech, and taste and impairs the patient’s quality of life. Due to the heavy burden of disease, it is challenging to spare the parotid glands.

Chronic xerostomia occurs due to damage to the salivary gland stem cells (SCS) and a lack of subsequent proper cell renewal [13]. The SCS are located around the major ducts within the superficial lobe of the parotid [14]. The active component in tobacco is nicotine, which acts on certain cholinergic receptors within the brain and leads to altered salivary secretion. Long-term tobacco users (smokers or chewers) have lower salivary flow rates and a more acidic pH of saliva [15]. Lime in tobacco chewers can react with the bicarbonate buffering system, causing a loss of bicarbonate and making saliva more acidic [16, 17]. This may impact the size of parotids and thereby the sparing achieved with radiotherapy.

Few studies have reported on improved xerostomia rates with superficial parotid gland sparing [18]. There is scarce data on superficial lobe sparing among patients with locally advanced head and neck cancer with advanced nodal burden and its correlation with the type of tobacco use. We aimed at correlating the incidence of chronic xerostomia with the kind of tobacco use (chewers vs. smokers) and superficial parotid gland sparing. The secondary aim was to look at the correlation between the type of tobacco use and the volume of the parotid gland.

Materials and methods

This institutional ethics committee-approved retrospective study was conducted between June 2017 and March 2020. 314 patients with head and neck cancer were treated with a radical intent at our institute. We included all patients with histologically proven squamous cell carcinoma of the oral cavity, nasopharynx, oropharynx, hypopharynx, larynx, or cervical lymph node metastases of unknown primary cancer, who received conventionally fractionated curative-intent radiotherapy (bilateral neck) to a dose of 60–70 Gy. Patients with a previous history of radiotherapy, Stage I carcinoma of the larynx treated with small bilateral portals, not including the whole neck, patients treated with unilateral neck irradiation, and patients with connective tissue disorder affecting the glands were excluded from the study. Figure 1 shows the consort diagram.

Medical records were retrieved for all patients who fit the inclusion criteria and were included in the study. The demographic variables collected were age, sex, stage, treatment details and xerostomia rates. The delineation of the superficial and deep lobes of the parotid was done by one author (NP) retrospectively. The superficial and deep lobes were contoured based on guidelines given by Zhang et al. [19]. The lobes are divided into two based on the facial nerve process, the retromandibular vein, and anatomy. All patients were treated with IMRT using the MONACO treatment planning system. The patients were treated with radiotherapy plans optimised for whole parotid sparing. There was no delineation of superficial parotid, nor was plan optimisation based on superficial parotids, as delineation was done retrospectively. The dose-volume parameters were extracted from the treatment plans. For the purpose of this study, the superficial, deep or whole glands were considered as spared if the mean dose of any of them was less than 26 Gy. The radiotherapy plans were superimposed on the contoured structures to look at the dose received by the ipsilateral and contralateral superficial and deep lobes of the parotid gland. The patients were divided into five groups based on the doses received by the whole or superficial parotid gland. Group 1 includes patients with bilateral parotid sparing, Group 2 consists of those with contralateral parotid sparing, Group 3 consists of contralateral superficial parotid sparing, Group 4 consists of bilateral superficial lobe sparing, while in Group 5, no parotid gland sparing was achieved. The medical records and prospectively maintained database were used to obtain the physician-reported Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) xerostomia toxicity grades for all patients at two years.

Data was entered in MS Excel and analysed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 21. Descriptive statistics were used for demographic variables. The data was assessed for normality. The Spearman correlation coefficient was used to correlate doses received by superficial parotids with xerostomia grade as assessed by the RTOG scale [20]. A chi-square test compared the groups (superficial parotid sparing vs. whole parotid sparing vs no sparing) and type of tobacco use with xerostomia grade. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

A total of 174 patients were included in the study. Table 1 shows the patient characteristics. The oropharynx was the most common subsite (30.5%), with 83% of patients in stage III/IV. The most predominant pattern of tobacco consumption was chewing 39.4% (n = 69), followed by smoking 35.6% (n = 62). Around 5.7% (n = 10) of patients were both chewers and smokers. Addiction patterns of 8% (n = 14) patients were unknown, and 10.9% (n = 19) patients had no addictions.

We divided the patients into five groups depending on their parotid sparing. Group 1 consisted of patients with bilateral parotid sparing 37.4% (n = 65), group 2 with contralateral parotid sparing 25.3% (n = 44), group 3 with contralateral superficial lobe sparing 12.1% (n = 21), group with bilateral superficial lobe sparing 15.5% (n = 27) and, lastly, group 5 with no parotid sparing 9.8% (n = 17). The incidence of chronic xerostomia at the end of two years was as follows: 4.6% (n = 8) patients had grade 0, 47.1% (n = 82) with grade I, 9.2% (n = 16) with grade II, 0.6% with grade III and none of the patients reported grade IV xerostomia.

The mean bilateral parotid volume was 59 cc (20–89 cc), and the superficial parotid lobe volume was 26.2 cc (9–61 cc). We observed that tobacco chewers had significantly smaller mean parotid (53 cc vs. 60 cc, p = 0.02) and submandibular gland volumes (6 cc vs. 14 cc, p < 0.001) as compared to smokers or non-tobacco chewers. Figure 2 shows the difference in volume of a tobacco chewers’ parotid gland versus a that of smokers. We correlated the type of tobacco use with chronic xerostomia grade and found that the incidence was similar in smokers and chewers (p = 0.95) (Supplementary File — Tab. S1).

We correlated the ipsilateral and contralateral superficial mean parotid dose with the grade of chronic xerostomia. We found that both the ipsilateral (26.5 Gy vs. 37.8 Gy, p < 0.001) and contralateral (23.3 Gy vs. 34.1 Gy, p < 0.001) superficial parotid mean dose in patients with grade 0/I and ≥ grade II xerostomia was significantly different. Patients with bilateral or contralateral superficial lobe sparing had lower grade II/III xerostomia rates than the no-sparing group (p = 0.038) (Tab. 2).

Discussion

This retrospective analysis found that bilateral or contralateral superficial lobe sparing had lower grade II/III xerostomia rates than the no-sparing. Although the parotid volumes were smaller in chewers, there was no difference in xerostomia rates between chewers and smokers.

We postulated that smokeless tobacco (tobacco chewing) would be associated with a smaller volume of salivary glands. Our study demonstrated that the volume of salivary glands in tobacco chewers was significantly smaller than in tobacco smokers. To our knowledge, this is the first study to look at tobacco use and the volume of salivary glands. This is particularly relevant in our setting as tobacco chewing is the predominant form of tobacco use, and patients present with advanced stages. In these patients, sparing the parotids is a challenge. Superficial parotid sparing, irrespective of the type of tobacco use, resulted in lower xerostomia rates.

Saliva is a complex body fluid that has a significant impact on oral health due to specific and non-specific physical and chemical properties [21, 22]. Any alteration in the quantity or quality of saliva will not only impact speech and swallow function but will also disturb oral health. It is well established that tobacco has a multifold impact on saliva’s physical and chemical properties. Tobacco alters salivary flow rate, buffer capacity, and salivary ph. It is also associated with dental caries, halitosis, gingivitis, and calculus [15, 23, 24]. Long-term tobacco users, particularly chewers, have lower salivary flow rates and a more acidic pH of saliva [15].

Preclinical studies on rats, as demonstrated by Konings et al., showed regional radiosensitivity between the cranial and caudal parts of the parotid gland [25]. This can be correlated with findings of Pringle et al., who suggested that salivary gland stem or progenitor cells are close to the main excretory ducts [26]. An analogy can be made between rats and humans. The superficial lobe contains Stenson’s duct along with a large volume of glands. If the progenitor cells are close to the main duct, as in the rat parotid gland, then sparing both superficial lobes and parotid gland stem cells close to the main ducts may result in less stem cell damage and faster salivation recovery.

To investigate the location of progenitor cells, Luijk et al. looked at the distribution of cells expressing c-Kit, a known marker of progenitor cells [13]. They were exclusively found in the ducts of the parotid gland, and their intensity was higher in larger ducts than in smaller ones. This pattern was found in rat and mouse parotid glands. They irradiated various segments of the rat parotid with a high-precision proton beam. They concluded that the most radiosensitive region was located at the junction of the ventral, dorsal, and lateral lobes (located next to the dorsal edge of the mandible), supporting the presupposition that progenitor cells reside around the ducts.

The PARSPORT trial aimed at contralateral parotid-sparing IMRT (CLPS), whereas the PARSPORT II trial aimed at bilateral superficial lobe parotid-sparing IMRT (BSLPS) [12, 27]. The BSLPS technique was designed with the hypothesis that radiotherapy can be delivered to the parapharyngeal space without compromising salivary recovery. Both these trials looked at salivary recovery at one year. The PARSPORT trial was a phase III trial demonstrating that CLPS was associated with superior salivary recovery without impacting oncological outcomes. Grade 2 or higher chronic xerostomia was seen in 38% of patients. Miah et al. conducted a phase II trial in central head and neck tumours, such as the base of tongue and nasopharynx, which tend to metastasize to bilateral neck nodes. They assessed the impact of BSLPS on salivary recovery and 2-year PFS. Grade 2 or higher chronic xerostomia was significantly lower (about 21%) in patients with BSLPS. In node-positive neck, aiming to spare the whole parotid results in higher recurrence, as shown by Chen et al. [28]. Hence, sparing superficial parotids may be more reasonable in patients with the bilateral node-positive neck.

Data on superficial lobe sparing in the literature are scarce. The field is currently investigated in multiple studies supporting the hypothesis of superior salivary recovery with bilateral superficial lobe sparing compared to bilateral lobe sparing. Miah et al. compared CLPS with BSLPS-IMRT in head and neck tumours from data of 2 prospective studies, PARPORT I and II. The mean dose to the ipsilateral parotid, contralateral parotid and superficial lobes was significantly lower in the BSLPS group. Parotid salivary flow recovery incidence at 1 year after radiotherapy was 67.1% with BSLPS-IMRT vs. 52.8% with CLPS-IMRT (p 0.12 [p = 0.12???]). They also postulated that D50 (the mean dose at which 50% of patients experience toxicity) indicates salivary recovery better than whole gland mean dose.

Steenbakkers et al. compared salivary recovery at one year with parotid stem cell region (SCR) sparing radiation with standard parotid sparing radiation [29]. The dose received by SCR in ipsilateral was 16 Gy and 26 Gy (p = 0.001), and contralateral was 11 Gy and 16 Gy (p = 0.004) in the SCR sparing and standard arm, respectively. Salivary recovery trended better towards SCR sparing radiation. Nevens et al. reported on superficial parotid sparing and compared it with whole gland sparing. They found xerostomia rates to be higher in those with superficial compared to whole gland, but lower than those without parotid sparing [18]. A recent phase II randomised study was reported by Huang et al. comparing superficial parotid lobe sparing (SPLS) IMRT with conventional IMRT (C-IMRT) in carcinoma nasopharynx [30]. At one year, the rates of xerostomia were significantly lower in SPLS IMRT (83% vs. 95%, p = 0.007) and lower grade 3 xerostomia (0% vs. 12.5%, p < 0.001). The three-year local control, disease-free survival and overall survival were similar in both groups.

Similarly, our study found that the superficial parotid mean dose was lower in patients with grade 0/1 xerostomia than in those with grade II or higher xerostomia. Hence, patients with bilateral or contralateral superficial lobe sparing had lower rates of grade II or higher rates of xerostomia than the no-sparing group (p < 0.001). Large phase III randomised studies comparing BSPLS IMRT with conventional IMRT are needed, particularly in our setting with locally advanced tumours.

The strengths of the present study are uniform delineation by one observer and the correlation of tobacco use with parotid volumes and xerostomia rates. This paper presents introductory evidence correlating smaller parotid volumes in patients consuming smokeless tobacco. This needs further prospective evaluation in a larger number of patients. The limitations of our study are small patient numbers, retrospective data collection, which might have impacted rates of xerostomia, and the lack of use of patient-reported xerostomia inventory scores, which would have given better insight into salivary recovery. Given the relatively small number of patients each group had, we could not further subgroup these patients to account for dose to other related organs.

Conclusions

Tobacco chewers have smaller parotids, and the volume of parotid correlates with the sparing achieved with IMRT in head and neck cancer. Delineating superficial parotid lobes and their sparing, wherever contralateral gland sparing is not possible, can limit chronic xerostomia.
