Products, crossed products, and Zappa–Szép products for k k -graphs Thanks: This research was supported by the US National Science Foundation (grant DMS-1800749 to E.G.).
Adlin Abell-Ball, Elizabeth Gillaspy, George Glidden-Handgis, S. Joseph Lippert

Abstract
. We use the lens of Zappa–Szép decomposition to examine the relationship between directed graph products and k k -graph products. There are many examples of higher-rank graphs, or k k -graphs, whose underlying directed graph may be factored as a product, but the k k -graph itself is not a product. In such examples, we establish that the Zappa–Szép structure of the k k -graph gives rise to “actions” of the underlying directed factors on each other. Although these “actions” are in general poorly behaved, if one of them is trivial (or trivial up to isomorphism), we obtain a crossed-product-like structure on the k k -graph. We provide examples where this crossed-product structure is visible in the associated C ∗ C^{*} -algebra, and we characterize those k k -graphs whose Zappa–Szép induced actions are trivial up to isomorphism.

2.1.
Higher-rank graphs

Definition 2.1
.

[
12
, Def 1.1]
Let
\Lambda

d:\Lambda\to\mathbb{N}^{k}
a functor. If

factorization property
—that is, for every morphism
\lambda\in\Lambda

such that
d(\mu)=m
,

, and

is a
k
-graph
(or higher-rank graph of rank
k
). We will frequently abuse notation and refer to
\Lambda
as a
k
-graph, without mentioning the functor

.

For a
k
-graph
\Lambda
and
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
we define

\Lambda^{A}:=\{\lambda\in\Lambda:d(\lambda)\in A\}.

We suppress the set-builder notation for singleton sets. For example,
\Lambda^{e_{1}}:=\Lambda^{\{e_{1}\}}
.
By the factorization rule, for each
\lambda\in\Lambda
there exist unique
v,w\in\Lambda^{0}
so that
\lambda=v\lambda w
; we write
v=:r(\lambda)
and
w=:s(\lambda)
.
Combining this with the notation above, we obtain the sets

v\Lambda^{n}=\{\lambda\in\Lambda:r(\lambda)=v,d(\lambda)=n\},\quad v\Lambda w=\{\lambda\in\Lambda:r(\lambda)=v,s(\lambda)=w\}

and so on.

We say that
\Lambda
is

if
v\Lambda^{n}
is finite for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
.

Definition 2.2
.

For a higher-rank graph
\Lambda
, we define its
one-skeleton
as an edge-colored directed graph

G(\Lambda):=(\Lambda^{0},\Lambda^{E},s,r,d),

where
s,r:\Lambda^{E}\to\Lambda^{0}
are defined as above,
and we view

as the color of the edge

We will say that

is

if the underlying undirected graph of

is connected; that is, for any
v,w\in\Lambda^{0},
there is a (not necessarily composable) string of edges
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in\Lambda^{E}
linking

. Precisely, we require that for all
i
,
\{s(g_{i}),r(g_{i})\}\cap\{s(g_{i+1}),r(g_{i+1})\}\not=\emptyset

Conversely, given a directed graph
G=(G^{0},G^{1},r,s)
, its path category

is

G^{*}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{g_{1}\cdots g_{n}\in(G^{1})^{n}:s(g_{i})=r(g_{i+1})\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq n\}.

If

, then the factorization rule implies that each

For example, if

Consequently,
\Lambda\not=G(\Lambda)^{*},
but we can view

as a quotient of the path category
G(\Lambda)^{*}

However, not every equivalence relation on the path category

. Our next goal is to describe those equivalence relations that will. (For consistency with the

-graph setting, we view an edge-coloring on a directed graph as a map

Definition 2.3
.

The

By the factorization property, if

Theorem 2.4
.

[

Let

2.2.
Zappa–Szép products

In group theory, there is a generalization of the semi-direct product known as the Zappa–Szép product, where the two factor groups each act on the other in a compatible way. In recent years, this structural theory has been extended to groupoids and small categories
[
4
,
6
,
15
]
, and in particular to
k
-graphs
[
15
]
, where these two-sided actions are called
matched pairs
.

Recall that, if
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
are categories with the same object space
(\mathcal{C}^{0}=\mathcal{D}^{0}
), then (using the arrows-only picture of category theory) we denote

\mathcal{C}*\mathcal{D}=\{(c,d)\in\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D}:s(c)=r(d)\in\mathcal{C}^{0}=\mathcal{D}^{0}\}.

Following
[
15
]
, a
left action
of a category
\mathcal{C}
on another category
\mathcal{D}
with
\mathcal{C}^{0}=\mathcal{D}^{0}
is a map
\mathcal{C}*\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{D},
denoted
c\vartriangleright d,
such that
(c_{1}c_{2})\vartriangleright d=c_{1}\vartriangleright(c_{2}\vartriangleright d)
whenever
s(c_{1})=r(c_{2}).

Similarly, a
right action
of
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{C}

is a map
\mathcal{C}*\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C},
denoted
c\vartriangleleft d,
such that
c\vartriangleleft(d_{1}d_{2})=(c\vartriangleleft d_{1})\vartriangleleft d_{2}
whenever
s(d_{1})=r(d_{2}).

Definition 2.8
.

[
15
, Def. 3.1]
A

is a quadruple
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D},\vartriangleright,\vartriangleleft)
consisting of small categories

and
\mathcal{D}
with
\mathcal{C}^{0}=\mathcal{D}^{0}
, a left action

\vartriangleright:\mathcal{C}*\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{D}
, and a right action

\vartriangleleft:\mathcal{C}*\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}
such that for all composable quadruples
(c_{1},c_{2},d_{1},d_{2})\in\mathcal{C}^{2}*\mathcal{D}^{2}
,

(MP1)

s(c_{2}\vartriangleright d_{1})=r(c_{2}\vartriangleleft d_{1})
,

(MP2)

c_{2}\vartriangleright(d_{1}d_{2})=(c_{2}\vartriangleright d_{1})((c_{2}\vartriangleleft d_{1})\vartriangleright d_{2})
, and

(MP3)

(c_{1}c_{2})\vartriangleleft d_{1}=(c_{1}\vartriangleleft(c_{2}\vartriangleright d_{1}))(c_{2}\vartriangleleft d_{1})
.

The factorization rule of

A quasi-product (see Definition
3.1
below) is a
(k_{1}+k_{2})
-graph which at the one-skeleton level resembles a product graph. In this section, we
study the properties of the Zappa–Szép actions
\vartriangleright,\vartriangleleft
on quasi-products at the graph level. While these “actions” need not be particularly well-behaved (cf. Examples
3.6
and
3.13
below), if one of them is trivial, we obtain an elegant structural decomposition of the quasi-product
\Omega
(Proposition
3.11
and Theorem
3.12
).

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a
(k_{1}+k_{2})
-graph. If there exist
k_{1}
- and
k_{2}
-graphs,
\Lambda
and
\Gamma
, such that

(1)

G(\Omega)\cong G(\Lambda\times\Gamma)
and

(2)

there exist injective functors
\psi_{1}:\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Omega
and
\psi_{2}:\Gamma\hookrightarrow\Omega

(3)

with
\pi_{i}:\mathbb{N}^{k_{1}+k_{2}}\to\mathbb{N}^{k_{i}}
the canonical projection,
d_{\Lambda}=\pi_{1}d_{\Omega}\psi_{1}
and
d_{\Gamma}=\pi_{2}d_{\Omega}\psi_{2}
,

then we call
\Omega
a
quasi-product
with
quasi-factors

\Lambda
and
\Gamma
.

Remark 3.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a
(k_{1}+k_{2})
-graph and consider its matched-pair decomposition
(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2},\vartriangleright,\vartriangleleft)
from Lemma
2.9
. Then
\Omega
is not a quasi-product with quasi-factors
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, because

\Omega_{1}^{0}=\Omega_{2}^{0}=\Omega^{0}.

Therefore,

(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})^{0}=\Omega^{0}\times\Omega^{0}\not=\Omega^{0},

so we do not have
G(\Omega)\cong G(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})
.

When
\Omega
is a quasi-product with quasi-factors
\Lambda
and
\Gamma
, we will often
use the notation for edges and paths in
G(\Lambda\times\Gamma)
(see Example
2.5
) to describe edges in
G(\Omega)
. This emphasizes the fact that the only difference between
\Lambda\times\Gamma
and
\Omega
is their factorization rules,
\sim_{\times}
and
\sim_{\Omega}
, respectively.

Proposition 3.3
.

If
\Omega
is a quasi-product
(k_{1}+k_{2})
-graph with associated matched pair
(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2},\vartriangleright,\vartriangleleft)
as in Lemma
2.9
, then
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
both decompose as disjoint unions:

\Omega_{1}=\bigsqcup_{w\in\Gamma^{0}}\Omega_{1}^{w}\times\{w\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\Omega_{2}=\bigsqcup_{x\in\Lambda^{0}}\{x\}\times\Omega_{2}^{x}.

Proof.

Note that
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}
have one-skeletons
G(\Omega_{1})
and
G(\Omega_{2})
. To investigate the structure of these one-skeletons, we split the standard basis
E:=\{e_{1},\cdots,e_{k_{1}+k_{2}}\}
of
\mathbb{N}^{k_{1}+k_{2}}
into
E_{1}:=\{e_{1},\cdots,e_{k_{1}}\}
and
E_{2}=E\setminus E_{1}
.
Then

G(\Omega_{1})=(\Omega^{0},\Omega^{E_{1}},s,r)\quad\text{ and }\quad G(\Omega_{2})=(\Omega^{0},\Omega^{E_{2}},s,r).

As
G(\Omega)\cong G(\Lambda\times\Gamma)
, every edge in
G(\Omega)
is of the form
(e,v)
or
(x,f)
for some
e\in G(\Lambda),f\in G(\Gamma),x\in\Lambda^{0},v\in\Gamma^{0}.

Since
s
and
r
evaluate coordinate-wise, if
v\neq w\in\Gamma^{0}
, then
G(\Lambda)\times\{v\}
is disconnected from
G(\Lambda)\times\{w\}
in
G(\Omega_{1})
. A similar argument applies to
G(\Omega_{2})
giving the simplification

G(\Omega_{1})=\bigsqcup_{w\in\Gamma^{0}}G(\Lambda)\times\{w\}\quad\text{and}\quad G(\Omega_{2})=\bigsqcup_{x\in\Lambda^{0}}\{x\}\times G(\Gamma).

The factorization rule of
\Omega
restricts to connected components; that is, for any
w\in\Gamma^{0},x\in\Lambda^{0}
we have
k_{i}
-graphs

(G(\Lambda)\times\{w\})/\sim_{\Omega}=:\Omega_{1}^{w}\times\{w\},\qquad(\{x\}\times G(\Gamma))/\sim_{\Omega}=:\{x\}\times\Omega_{2}^{x}

which yields the desired decomposition of
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}
.
∎

In any
k
-graph
\Omega
with associated matched pair
(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2},\vartriangleright,\vartriangleleft)
, the fact that (Lemma
2.9
)
\omega_{1}\omega_{2}=(\omega_{1}\vartriangleright\omega_{2})(\omega_{1}\vartriangleleft\omega_{2})
whenever
(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in\Omega_{1}*\Omega_{2}
yields

(2)

s(\omega_{1}\vartriangleleft\omega_{2})=s(\omega_{2})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad r(\omega_{1}\vartriangleright\omega_{2})=r(\omega_{1}).

If
\Omega
is a quasi-product, then for any
p\in G(\Lambda)^{*},q\in G(\Gamma)^{*}
, if we set

\omega_{1}=([p],r(q))\in\Omega_{1}^{r(q)}\times\{r(q)\},\qquad\omega_{2}=(s(p),[q])\in\{s(p)\}\times\Omega_{2}^{s(p)},

then
(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in\Omega_{1}*\Omega_{2}
is a composable pair. Since
d(\omega_{1}\vartriangleleft\omega_{2})=d(\omega_{1})
and
d(\omega_{1}\vartriangleright\omega_{2})=d(\omega_{2}),
we therefore have
\omega_{1}\vartriangleleft\omega_{2}\in\Omega_{1}^{s(q)}\times\{s(q)\}
and
\omega_{1}\vartriangleright\omega_{2}\in\{r(p)\}\times\Omega_{2}^{r(p)}
.

As
G(\Omega_{1}^{w})=G(\Lambda)
for all
w\in\Gamma^{0}
and
G(\Omega_{2}^{x})=G(\Gamma)
for all
x\in\Lambda^{0}
, we can obtain, from the Zappa–Szép structure and the decomposition above, an
action of paths in
G(\Lambda)
on paths in
G(\Gamma)
and vice versa.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\Omega
be a quasi-product with quasi-factors
\Lambda,\Gamma
. For any
p\in G(\Lambda)^{*},q\in G(\Gamma)^{*}
, we define color order preserving functions
p_{\vartriangleright}:G(\Gamma)^{*}\to G(\Gamma)^{*}
and
q_{\vartriangleleft}:G(\Lambda)^{*}\to G(\Lambda)^{*}
, via

(3)

\displaystyle(r(p),[p_{\vartriangleright}(q)])

\displaystyle:=([p],r(q))\vartriangleright(s(p),[q])

(4)

\displaystyle([q_{\vartriangleleft}(p)],s(q))

\displaystyle:=([p],r(q))\vartriangleleft(s(p),[q]).

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\Omega
be a quasi-product with quasi-factors
\Lambda
and
\Gamma
. For any
p\in G(\Lambda)^{*},q\in G(\Gamma)^{*}
,

r(q_{\vartriangleleft}(p))=r(p),\ s(q_{\vartriangleleft}(p))=s(p)\quad\text{ and }\quad r(p_{\vartriangleright}(q))=r(q),\ s(p_{\vartriangleright}(q))=s(q).

Proof.

Equation (
2
) implies that
r(r(p),[p_{\vartriangleright}(q)])=r([p],r(q))=(r(p),r(q))
and similarly that
s([q_{\vartriangleleft}(p)],s(q))=s(s(p),[q])=(s(p),s(q)).
Conseqently,

r(p_{\vartriangleright}(q))=r(q)\in\Gamma^{0}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad s(q_{\vartriangleleft}(p))=s(p)\in\Lambda^{0}.

For the remaining assertions, recall that, thanks to (MP1),

((r(p),[p_{\vartriangleright}(q)]),([q_{\vartriangleleft}(p)],s(q)))\in\Omega_{2}*\Omega_{1}

is a composable pair. In particular,
s(r(p),[p_{\vartriangleright}(q)])=(r(p),s(p_{\vartriangleright}(q)))
must equal
r([q_{\vartriangleleft}(p)],s(q))=(r(q_{\vartriangleleft}(p)),s(q))
. The conclusion follows.

∎

In general,
p_{\vartriangleright}
and
q_{\vartriangleleft}
need not be particularly well behaved. Although they are degree-preserving by Lemma
2.9
and source- and range-preserving by Proposition
3.5
, one or both may fail to be injective or surjective (see Example
3.13
), or
may not respect composition.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\Omega
be a quasi-product with quasi-factors
\Lambda
and
\Gamma
. If
p=p^{1}p^{2}\in G(\Lambda)^{*},q=q^{1}q^{2}\in G(\Gamma)^{*}
, then

p_{\vartriangleright}(q^{1}q^{2})=(p_{\vartriangleright}(q^{1}))(q^{1}_{\vartriangleleft}(p))_{\vartriangleright}(q_{2})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad q_{\vartriangleleft}(p^{1}p^{2})=(p^{2}_{\vartriangleright}(q))_{\vartriangleleft}(p^{1})(q_{\vartriangleleft}(p^{2})).

Proof.

By (MP2) and the fact that
r(q^{1}_{\vartriangleleft}(p))=r(p),

\displaystyle([p],r(q))\vartriangleright(s(p),[q^{1}q^{2}])

\displaystyle=([p],r(q))\vartriangleright\left(s(p),[q^{1}])(s(p),[q^{2}])\right)

\displaystyle=(r(p),[p_{\vartriangleright}(q^{1})])\left(([q^{1}_{\vartriangleleft}(p)],s(q^{1}))\vartriangleright(s(p),[q^{2}])\right)

\displaystyle=(r(p),[p_{\vartriangleright}(q^{1})])(r(p),[(q^{1}_{\vartriangleleft}(p))_{\vartriangleright}(q^{2})]).

A symmetric argument, using (MP3), establishes the second claim.
∎

If
\Omega=\Lambda\times\Gamma
is a product
(k_{1}+k_{2})
-graph, then
p_{\vartriangleright},q_{\vartriangleleft}
are much better behaved. In that case,
for any
\lambda\in\Lambda,\gamma\in\Gamma
,

(\lambda,\gamma)=(\lambda,r(\gamma))(s(\lambda),\gamma)=(r(\lambda),\gamma)(\lambda,s(\gamma)).

In other words, we have
p_{\vartriangleright}=\operatorname{id}
and
q_{\vartriangleleft}=\operatorname{id}
for all
p\in G(\Lambda)^{*},q\in G(\Gamma)^{*}.
In what follows, we show that assuming one of the equalities above to hold in a quasi-product
k
-graph leads to a crossed-product-like structure.

Definition 3.8
.

For a quasi-product
\Omega
with quasi-factors
\Lambda
and
\Gamma
we say that
\Gamma
is a
stable
quasi-factor if
p_{\vartriangleright}=\operatorname{id}
for all
p\in G(\Lambda)^{*}
.

Remark 3.9
.

As an alternative to Definition
3.8
, if
q_{\vartriangleleft}=\operatorname{id}
for all
p\in G(\Lambda)^{*}
, we could say that
\Lambda
is a stable quasi-factor. In this article, for simplicity and coherence of notation, we will phrase all results using
\Gamma
as the stable quasi-factor, leaving the straightforward translation to left-stability to the reader.

The following Proposition shows that one can determine stability by checking the way
\vartriangleright
behaves on edges.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\Omega
be a quasi-product with quasi-factors
\Lambda
and
\Gamma
. The quasi-factor
\Gamma
is stable iff
e_{\vartriangleright}(g)=g
for all edges
e\in G(\Lambda),g\in G(\Gamma).

Proof.

Necessity is evident, so we prove sufficiency. Observe first that as
\vartriangleright
is a left action, for any paths
p=p^{1}p^{2}\in G(\Lambda)^{*},q\in G(\Gamma)^{*}
we have

\displaystyle([p^{1}p^{2}],r(q))

\displaystyle\vartriangleright(s(p),[q])=\left(([p^{1}],r(q))([p^{2}],r(q))\right)\vartriangleright(s(p),[q])

\displaystyle=([p^{1}],r(q))\vartriangleright\left(([p^{2}],r(q))\vartriangleright(s(p),[q])\right).

If
q,p^{1},p^{2}
are edges and we assume
e_{\vartriangleright}(g)=g
for all edges
e\in G(\Lambda)^{1},g\in G(\Gamma)^{1}
, the above becomes

([p^{1}p^{2}],r(q))\vartriangleright(s(p),q)=(p^{1},r(q))\vartriangleright(r(p^{2}),q)=(r(p^{1}),q);

that is,
(p^{1}p^{2})_{\vartriangleright}(q)=q.

By induction, it follows that
p_{\vartriangleright}(q)=q
for all paths
p\in G(\Lambda)^{*}
and all edges
q\in G(\Gamma)^{1}.

We now show that if
q=q^{1}q^{2}
is a path of length 2 in
G(\Gamma)^{*}
, then
p_{\vartriangleright}(q)=q
for any
p\in G(\Lambda)^{*}
. By the previous paragraph,
q^{1}_{\vartriangleleft}(p)=:\tilde{p}\in G(\Lambda)^{*}
also satisfies
\tilde{p}_{\vartriangleright}(g)=g
for all edges
g\in G(\Gamma)^{*}.
Thus,
Proposition
3.7
implies that

\displaystyle p_{\vartriangleright}(q)

\displaystyle=(p_{\vartriangleright}(q^{1}))(q^{1}_{\vartriangleleft}(p))_{\vartriangleright}(q^{2})

\displaystyle=q^{1}(\tilde{p}_{\vartriangleright}(q^{2}))=q^{1}q^{2}.

By induction, we conclude that if
e_{\vartriangleright}(g)=g
for all edges
e\in G(\Lambda)^{1},g\in G(\Gamma)^{1}
, then for any paths
p\in G(\Lambda)^{*},q\in G(\Gamma)^{*}
, we have
p_{\vartriangleright}(q)=q
; that is,
\Gamma
is a stable quasi-factor.
∎

Definition
3.8
imposes a substantial (perhaps a surprising) amount of structure on a stable quasi-product
k
-graph; the next several results detail this structure.
To begin, Definition
3.8
is stated in terms of paths in
G(\Lambda)^{*},G(\Gamma)^{*};
recall from Definition
3.4
that we will interpret
p_{\vartriangleright}(q)\in G(\Gamma)^{*}
as representing
([p],s(q))\vartriangleright(s(p),[q])\in\Omega_{2}^{r(p)},
whereas we compute
[q]
with respect to the equivalence relation in
\Omega_{2}^{s(p)}.

However, the fact that for any
\omega\in\Omega_{1},\omega\vartriangleright\cdot
is a well-defined map on the
k_{2}
-graph
\Omega_{2}
means that the factorization rules on
G(\Gamma)^{*}
yielding
\Omega_{2}^{s(p)}
and
\Omega_{2}^{r(p)}
agree in a stable quasi-product, as we now show. That is, despite its graphical definition, stability is a
k
-graph concept.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
\Omega
be a quasi-product with quasi-factor
\Lambda
and stable quasi-factor
\Gamma
. For any
p\in G(\Lambda)^{*}
and
q\in G(\Gamma)^{*}
we have
[q]_{s(p)}=[q]_{r(p)}
.
In particular, if
\Lambda
is connected, then for every
v,w\in\Lambda^{0}
the factorization rules in
\Omega_{2}^{v}
and
\Omega_{2}^{w}
agree.

Proof.

Fix a path
p\in G(\Lambda)
and suppose
q,q^{\prime}\in G(\Gamma)^{*}
satisfy
q\sim q^{\prime}
in
\Omega_{2}^{s(p)}
.
Since
\vartriangleright
is defined at the level of the category, i.e. the equivalence class,

(r(p),[p_{\vartriangleright}(q)]_{r(p)})=([p],r(q))\vartriangleright(s(p),[q]_{s(p)})=([p],r(q^{\prime}))\vartriangleright(s(p),[q^{\prime}]_{s(p)})

must equal
(r(p),[p_{\vartriangleright}(q^{\prime})]_{r(p)}).

Thus, if
\Gamma
is stable, we must have
q\sim q^{\prime}
in
\Omega_{2}^{r(p)}
.

By definition,
p_{\vartriangleright}(q^{\prime})
has the same color order as
q^{\prime}
.
As
q^{\prime}\in[q]_{s(p)}
was arbitrary, and
[q]_{r(p)}
(like
[q]_{s(p)})
contains exactly one path of each color order, this implies that the factorization rule in
\Omega_{2}^{r(p)}
agrees with that in
\Omega_{2}^{s(p)}
. From Definition
3.1
, we know there is
v\in\Lambda^{0}
with
\Omega_{2}^{v}\cong\Gamma
as
k_{2}
-graphs; the conclusion follows.
∎

In particular, if
\Gamma
is a stable quasi-factor and
\Lambda
is connected, then for each
p\in G(\Lambda)^{*},p_{\vartriangleright}

is not just a range-, source-, and degree-preserving map, but yields an isomorphism of
k_{2}
-graphs
\Omega_{2}^{s(p)}\to\Omega_{2}^{r(p)}
, which we will denote
\widehat{p}_{\vartriangleright}
. Theorem
3.12
below shows that the stability of
\Gamma
implies that
q_{\vartriangleleft}
also implements an isomorphism
\widehat{q}_{\vartriangleleft}:\Omega_{1}^{r(q)}\to\Omega_{1}^{s(q)}
of
k_{1}
-graphs for all
q\in G(\Gamma)^{*}
.
In certain cases, this reveals a crossed-product structure to
C^{*}(\Omega)
, as we discuss in Section
4
.

Theorem 3.12
.

If
\Omega
is a row-finite quasi-product with quasi-factor
\Lambda
and stable quasi-factor
\Gamma
, then for any
q\in G(\Gamma)^{*}
,
q_{\vartriangleleft}
induces a
k_{1}
-graph isomorphism
\widehat{q}_{\vartriangleleft}:\Omega_{1}^{r(q)}\to\Omega_{1}^{s(q)}
.
If
\Gamma
is connected, then for all
v,w\in\Gamma^{0}
we have
\Omega_{1}^{v}\cong\Omega_{1}^{w}\cong\Lambda.

Proof.

We will check that the map
\widehat{q}_{\vartriangleleft}:\Omega_{1}^{r(q)}\to\Omega_{1}^{s(q)}
given by

\widehat{q}_{\vartriangleleft}([p]_{r(q)})=[q_{\vartriangleleft}(p)]_{s(p)}

is injective, surjective, and respects composition.

For injectivity, suppose that
[q_{\vartriangleleft}(p)]=[q_{\vartriangleleft}(p^{\prime})]
. Then
r(p)=r(p^{\prime})
and
s(p)=s(p^{\prime})
, since
r(p)=r(q_{\vartriangleleft}(p))=r(q_{\vartriangleleft}(p^{\prime}))=r(p^{\prime})
by Proposition
3.5
.
Stability implies that

p_{\vartriangleright}(q)=q=p^{\prime}_{\vartriangleright}(q)
, so
Lemma
2.9
yields

\displaystyle([p],r(q))(s(p),[q])

\displaystyle=\left(([p],r(q))\vartriangleright(s(p),[q])\right)\left(([p],r(q))\vartriangleleft(s(p),[q])\right)

\displaystyle=(r(p),[p_{\vartriangleright}(q)])([q_{\vartriangleleft}(p)],s(q))=(r(p),[p^{\prime}_{\vartriangleright}(q)])([q_{\vartriangleleft}(p^{\prime})],s(q))

\displaystyle=([p^{\prime}],r(q))(s(p^{\prime}),[q]).

Since
q_{\vartriangleleft}(\cdot)
preserves color order, our hypothesis that
[q_{\vartriangleleft}(p)]=[q_{\vartriangleleft}(p^{\prime})]
implies that
d(p^{\prime})=d(p)
.
The factorization property thus implies that
([p^{\prime}],r(q))=([p],r(q))
, proving injectivity.

The stability of
\Gamma
, together with (MP3), implies that
\widehat{q}_{\vartriangleleft}
preserves composition:

\displaystyle([q_{\vartriangleleft}(p^{1}p^{2})],r(q))

\displaystyle=([p^{1}p^{2}],r(q))\vartriangleleft(s(p^{2}),[q])

\displaystyle=(([p^{1}],r(q))\vartriangleleft(s(p^{1}),[p^{2}_{\vartriangleright}(q)]))([q_{\vartriangleleft}(p^{2})],r(q))

\displaystyle=([p^{1}],r(q))\vartriangleleft(s(p^{1}),[q])([q_{\vartriangleleft}(p^{2})],r(q))

\displaystyle=([q_{\vartriangleleft}(p^{1})q_{\vartriangleleft}(p^{2})],r(q))

since
s(p^{1})=r(p^{2}).

To see that
\widehat{q}_{\vartriangleleft}
is onto, recall that
\widehat{q}_{\vartriangleleft}
is well defined and injective, and
G(\Omega_{1}^{r(q)})\cong G(\Omega_{1}^{s(q)}).

Moreover, since
\Omega
is row-finite, there are only finitely many elements of
(v,r(q))\Omega_{1}^{r(q)}(w,r(q))
of a given degree, for any
v,w\in\Lambda^{0}
.
Since
\widehat{q}_{\vartriangleleft}
preserves source, range, and degree, the injectivity of
\widehat{q}_{\vartriangleleft}
forces it to be an isomorphism.
∎

The existence of the
k_{1}
-graph isomorphism
\widehat{q}_{\vartriangleleft}
is dependent on the stability of
\Gamma
as a quasi-factor. Perhaps surprisingly, it is not enough to assume that
\widehat{p}_{\vartriangleright}
is a
k_{2}
-graph isomorphism in order to conclude that
\widehat{q}_{\vartriangleleft}
is a
k_{1}
-graph isomorphism.

\displaystyle(f,y)(u,b)

\displaystyle=(v,c)(g,x);

\displaystyle(g,y)(u,c)

\displaystyle=(v,c)(f,x);

\displaystyle(f,y)(u,c)

\displaystyle=(v,b)(f,x);

and in all other situations we use the product graph relations. (For instance,
(v,a)(f,y)=(f,y)(u,a)
.)
Then

(b_{\vartriangleleft}(g),y)=(g,x)=(b_{\vartriangleleft}(f),y)
, so
\widehat{b}_{\vartriangleleft}
is not an isomorphism.

However, we claim that
\widehat{p}_{\vartriangleright}
is an isomorphism for all paths
p\in G(\Lambda)^{*}.

Indeed, if
p
contains
f
, then
p_{\vartriangleright}(q)
swaps
b
and
c
if either of them occurs in
q
; otherwise,
p_{\vartriangleright}=id
.

To see this, we first consider a path
p
of the form
e^{n}fh^{m}
.
Observe that

(e,x)^{n}(f,x)(h,x)^{m}(u,d)^{k}=(e,x)^{n}(v,d)^{k}(f,x)(h,x)^{m}=(v,d)^{k}(e,x)^{n}(f,x)(h,x)^{k},

and similarly
(e,y)^{n}(f,y)(h,y)^{m}(u,a)^{k}=(v,a)^{k}(e,y)^{n}(f,y)(h,y)^{m}.
That is, on both
a^{k}
and
d^{k}
,
(e^{n}fh^{m})_{\vartriangleright}
acts as the identity.
It remains to compute the action of
(e^{n}fh^{m})_{\vartriangleright}
on paths of the form
a^{j}bd^{k}.
Observe that

\displaystyle(e,y)^{n}(f,y)(h,y)^{m}(u,a)^{j}(u,b)(u,d)^{k}

\displaystyle=(e,y)^{n}(f,y)(u,a)^{j}(h,y)^{m}(u,b)(u,d)^{k}

\displaystyle=(e,y)^{n}(v,a)^{j}(f,y)(u,b)(h,x)^{m}(u,d)^{k}

\displaystyle=(v,a)^{j}(e,y)^{n}(v,c)(g,x)(u,d)^{k}(h,x)^{m}

\displaystyle=(v,a)^{j}(v,c)(e,x)^{n}(v,d)^{k}(g,x)(h,x)^{m}

\displaystyle=(v,a)^{j}(v,c)(v,d)^{k}(e,x)^{n}(g,x)(h,x)^{m}.

Consequently,
(e^{n}fh^{m})_{\vartriangleright}(a^{j}bd^{k})=a^{j}cd^{k}.

Similarly,
(e^{n}fh^{m})_{\vartriangleright}(a^{j}cd^{k})=a^{j}bd^{k}
.

Similar computations to the above reveal that
(e^{n}gh^{m})_{\vartriangleright}=id
.
In particular, for each path
p
in
G(\Lambda)^{*}
and
q
in
G(\Gamma)^{*}
,
p_{\vartriangleright}(q)
contains the same number of edges with a given source and range as
q
does, and if
p
contains
f
then
p_{\vartriangleright}
interchanges
b
and
c
if either of them occurs in
q
.

We conclude that
p_{\vartriangleright}
is a bijection (and, being range- and source-preserving, is thus an isomorphism) for all paths
p
in
G(\Lambda)^{*}
.

Given the structural constraints imposed by stability, we conclude this section with the following method of guaranteeing that a

-graph is a stable quasi-factor.

.

.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\Omega
be a quasi-product with quasi-factors
\Lambda
and
C_{n,k_{2}}
. If
\Lambda^{0}
is finite then

C^{*}(\Omega)\cong\left(\bigoplus_{i=0}^{n-1}C^{*}(\Omega_{1}^{w_{i}})\right)\rtimes_{\rho}\mathbb{Z}^{k_{2}}\cong\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}C^{*}(\Lambda)\right)\rtimes_{\rho}\mathbb{Z}^{k_{2}}

with
\rho_{x}=\rho_{1}^{x_{1}}\cdots\rho_{k_{2}}^{x_{k_{2}}}
.

Proof.

Firstly, we will demonstrate that

\{U_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq k_{2}}

are a commuting family of unitaries, that is, a unitary representation of
\mathbb{Z}^{k_{2}}
. For this claim, we rely on the Cuntz-Krieger relations.
To see that
U_{j}U_{\ell}=U_{\ell}U_{j},
recall that
S^{*}_{(v,f_{m,j})}S^{*}_{(u,f_{i,\ell})}=\delta_{v,u}\delta_{m,i-1}S^{*}_{(v,f_{i,j}f_{i-1,\ell})}
. Since
f_{i,j}f_{i-1,\ell}=f_{i,\ell}f_{i-1,j}\in C_{n,k_{2}}
,

\displaystyle U_{j}U_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{v\in\Lambda^{0}}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}S_{(v,f_{m,j})}^{*}\right)\left(\sum_{u\in\Lambda^{0}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}S^{*}_{(u,f_{i,\ell})}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\Lambda}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}S^{*}_{(v,f_{i,j}f_{i-1,\ell})}=\sum_{v\in\Lambda}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}S^{*}_{(v,f_{i,\ell}f_{i-1,j})}

\displaystyle=U_{\ell}U_{j}.

To see that
U_{j}
is a unitary, we observe that
S_{(v,f_{i-1,j})}S_{(v,f_{m-1,j})}^{*}=\delta_{i,m}S_{(v,w_{i})}=S_{(v,f_{i,j})}^{*}S_{(v,f_{m,j})}
.
Furthermore,
|\Lambda^{0}|<\infty\implies|\Omega^{0}|<\infty
; the universality of
C^{*}(\Omega)
therefore implies that
1=\sum_{v\in\Lambda^{0}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}S_{v,w_{i}}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle U_{j}U_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{v\in\Lambda^{0}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}S^{*}_{(v,f_{i,j})}\right)\left(\sum_{u\in\Lambda^{0}}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}S_{(u,f_{m,j})}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\Lambda}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}S^{*}_{(v,f_{i,j})}S_{(v,f_{i,j})}=\sum_{v\in\Lambda}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}S_{v,w_{i}}

\displaystyle=1=\left(\sum_{v\in\Lambda^{0}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}S_{(v,f_{i,j})}\right)\left(\sum_{u\in\Lambda^{0}}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}S_{(u,f_{m,j})}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=U_{j}^{*}U_{j}.

We conclude that
\{U_{j}\}
is a unitary representation of
\mathbb{Z}^{k_{2}}
contained in
C^{*}(\Omega)
.

To see that conjugation by
U_{j}
will induce the action of
\rho_{j}
, fix
\lambda\in\Omega_{1}^{w_{m}}(\cong\Lambda).
By Lemma
2.9
,

(\lambda,w_{m})(s(\lambda),f_{m-1,j})=(r(\lambda),f_{m-1,j})((\widehat{f}_{m-1,j})_{\vartriangleleft}(\lambda),w_{m-1})

since
C_{n,k_{2}}
is a stable quasi-factor. (CK2) now implies that

\displaystyle U_{j}S_{(\lambda,w_{m})}U_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{v\in\Lambda^{0}}S_{(v,f_{i,j})}^{*}\right)S_{(\lambda,w_{m})}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\sum_{u\in\Lambda^{0}}S_{(u,f_{\ell,j})}\right)

\displaystyle=S_{(r(\lambda),f_{m-1,j})}^{*}S_{(\lambda,w_{m})}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\sum_{u\in\Lambda^{0}}S_{(u,f_{\ell,j})}\right)

\displaystyle=S_{(r(\lambda),f_{m-1,j})}^{*}S_{(\lambda,w_{m})}S_{(s(\lambda),f_{m-1,j})}

\displaystyle=S_{(r(\lambda),f_{m-1,j})}^{*}S_{(r(\lambda),f_{m-1,j})}S_{((\widehat{f}_{m-1,j})_{\vartriangleleft}(\lambda),w_{m})}

\displaystyle=S_{((\widehat{f}_{m-1,j})_{\vartriangleleft}(\lambda),w_{m})}=\rho_{m-1,j}(S_{(\lambda,w_{m})}).

As each
\rho_{i,j}
is a
*
-homomorphism, and
C^{*}(\Omega_{1}^{w_{m}})
is generated by
\{S_{(\lambda,w_{m})}\}_{\lambda}
, we conclude that the automorphism
\rho_{j}
of
\bigoplus_{i=0}^{n-1}C^{*}(\Omega_{1}^{w_{i}})
is indeed given by conjugation by
U_{j}
.

This demonstrates that
\{U_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k_{2}}
yields a unitary representation of
\mathbb{Z}^{k_{2}}
which induces
\rho
on
C^{*}(\Omega_{1})\cong\bigoplus_{i=0}^{n-1}C^{*}(\Omega_{1}^{w_{i}})
via conjugation. The universal property of the crossed product now gives a
*
-homomorphism

\Phi:\left(\bigoplus_{i=0}^{n-1}C^{*}(\Omega_{1}^{w_{i}})\right)\rtimes_{\rho}\mathbb{Z}^{k_{2}}\to C^{*}(\Omega)

with image
\langle C^{*}(\Omega_{1}),U_{j}\rangle
.
It remains to show that
\Phi
is bijective.

We demonstrate surjectivity
by observing first that each vertex projection
S_{(v,w_{i})}
lies in

C^{*}(\Omega_{1})
and therefore
S_{(v,w_{i+1})}U_{j}^{*}=S_{(v,f_{i,j})}\in\text{Im}\,\Phi
for all

. In particular, every generator of
C^{*}(\Omega_{2})
lies in
\text{Im}\,\Phi
,
making the image all of
C^{*}(\Omega)
.

To demonstrate injectivity, we
construct a Cuntz–Krieger
\Omega
-family inside of
\left(\bigoplus_{i}C^{*}(\Omega_{1}^{w_{i}})\right)\rtimes_{\rho}\mathbb{Z}^{k_{2}}
, such that the associated
*
-homomorphism

\Psi:C^{*}(\Omega)\to\left(\bigoplus_{i}C^{*}(\Omega_{1}^{w_{i}})\right)\rtimes_{\rho}\mathbb{Z}^{k_{2}}
satisfies
\Psi\Phi=\operatorname{id}
.
To that end, write
V_{j}
for the canonical generators of
\mathbb{Z}^{k_{2}}
in the crossed product, and for each

and each vertex

define

We now show that
\{T_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Omega^{E}\cup\Omega^{0}\}
is a Cuntz–Krieger
\Omega
-family within
\left(\bigoplus_{i}C^{*}(\Omega_{1}^{w_{i}})\right)\rtimes_{\rho}\mathbb{Z}^{k_{2}}
. To that end,
observe first that
(CK1) is ensured since
the vertex projections
S_{(v,w_{i})}
in
\bigoplus_{i}C^{*}(\Omega_{1}^{w_{i}})
are orthogonal.

Before checking (CK2), we observe that for any vertex projection,

V_{j}S_{(v,w_{i})}V_{j}^{*}=\rho_{j}(S_{(v,w_{i})})=\rho_{i-1,j}(S_{(v,w_{i})})=S_{(v,w_{i-1})}.

Moreover, the unitaries

commute, since they represent the Abelian group

\displaystyle T_{(v,f_{i,j})}T_{(v,f_{i-1,\ell})}

\displaystyle=V_{j}^{*}S_{(v,w_{i})}V_{\ell}^{*}S_{(v,w_{i-1})}=S_{(v,w_{i+1})}V_{j}^{*}V_{\ell}^{*}S_{(v,w_{i-1})}

\displaystyle=S_{(v,w_{i+1})}V_{\ell}^{*}V_{j}^{*}S_{(v,w_{i-1})}=V_{\ell}^{*}S_{(v,w_{i})}V_{j}^{*}S_{(v,w_{i-1})}

\displaystyle=T_{(v,f_{i,\ell})}T_{(v,f_{i-1,j})}.

That is, (CK2) holds for the generators
T_{(v,f_{i,j})}
associated to
C_{n,k_{2}}.

We know that (CK2) holds among the generators
T_{(e,w_{i})}
since (CK2) holds for
\bigoplus_{i}C^{*}(\Omega_{1}^{w_{i}})\cong\bigoplus_{i}C^{*}(\Lambda)
by construction. To see that
T_{(e,w_{i+1})}T_{(s(e),f_{i,j})}=T_{(r(e),f_{i,j})}T_{((\widehat{f}_{i,j})_{\vartriangleleft}(e),w_{i})}
, observe that

\displaystyle T_{(r(e),f_{i,j})}T_{((\widehat{f}_{i,j})_{\vartriangleleft}(e),w_{i})}

\displaystyle=V_{j}^{*}S_{(r(e),w_{i})}S_{((\widehat{f}_{i,j})_{\vartriangleleft}(e),w_{i})}=V_{j}^{*}S_{((\widehat{f}_{i,j})_{\vartriangleleft}(e),w_{i})}

\displaystyle\text{ while }\quad T_{(e,w_{i+1})}T_{(s(e),f_{i,j})}

\displaystyle=S_{(e,w_{i+1})}V_{j}^{*}S_{(s(e),w_{i})}

\displaystyle=S_{(e,w_{i+1})}S_{(s(e),w_{i+1})}V_{j}^{*}=S_{(e,w_{i+1})}V_{j}^{*}.

As the unitaries
V_{j}
implement the action
\rho_{i,j}
which is induced by
(\widehat{f}_{i,j})_{\vartriangleleft},
we have
V_{j}S_{(e,w_{i+1})}V_{j}^{*}=S_{((\widehat{f}_{i,j})_{\vartriangleleft}(e),w_{i})}
; that is, (CK2) holds.

Observe that (CK3) and (CK4) are satisfied for
\{T_{(e,w_{i})}\}
since they hold in

.
Checking (CK3) for

For (CK4), recall that for each

there is only one edge in

with range
(v,w_{i+1})
and degree

so

\sum_{e\in(v,w_{i+1})\Omega^{e_{j+k_{1}}}}T_{e}T_{e}^{*}=T_{(v,f_{i,j})}T_{(v,f_{i,j})}^{*}=V_{j}^{*}T_{(v,w_{i})}V_{j}^{*}=T_{(v,w_{i+1})}.

We conclude that
\{T_{e}:e\in G(\Omega)^{1}\}
is a Cuntz–Krieger
\Omega
-family. Thus, the universal property of

yields a
*
-homomorphism
\Psi:C^{*}(\Omega)\to\left(\bigoplus C^{*}(\Omega_{1}^{w_{i}})\right)\rtimes_{\rho}\mathbb{Z}^{k_{2}}
satisfying

. In particular,

it can be easily checked that

on the generators of the crossed product. Hence,

is an isomorphism as claimed.

5.2.
Relaxed stability and products

The key difference between Example

\Theta
is a homomorphism when restricted to
\Omega_{1}
or
\Omega_{2}.

To see that
\Theta
is a
k
-graph homomorphism, we recall the product structure in
\Omega:

\displaystyle([p],[q])

\displaystyle=([p],r(q))(s(p),[q])=\left(([p],r(q))\vartriangleright(s(p),[q])\right)\left(([p],r(q))\vartriangleleft(s(p),[q])\right)

\displaystyle=(r(p),[p_{\vartriangleright}(q)])([q_{\vartriangleleft}(p)],s(q)).

Thus,
to see that
\Theta
is a
k
-graph homomorphism it suffices to check that

\Theta(r(p),[p_{\vartriangleright}(q)])=(r(p),[(\xi^{r(p)})_{\vartriangleright}^{-1}(p_{\vartriangleright}(q))])

equals

\displaystyle\Theta([p],r(q))\widetilde{\vartriangleright}\Theta(s(p),[q]))

\displaystyle=([p],r(q))\widetilde{\vartriangleright}(s(p),[(\xi^{s(p)})_{\vartriangleright}^{-1}(q)])=(r(p),[(\xi^{s(p)})_{\vartriangleright}^{-1}(q)])

and that
\Theta([q_{\vartriangleleft}(p)],s(q))=([q_{\vartriangleleft}(p)],s(q))
equals

\displaystyle\Theta([p],r(q))\widetilde{\vartriangleleft}\Theta(s(p),[q]))

\displaystyle=([p],r(q))\widetilde{\vartriangleleft}(s(p),[(\xi^{s(p)})_{\vartriangleright}^{-1}(q)])

\displaystyle=([\left(\xi^{s(p)}_{\vartriangleright}(\xi^{s(p)})_{\vartriangleright}^{-1}(q)\right)_{\vartriangleleft}(p)],s(q))=([q_{\vartriangleleft}(p)],s(q)).

That is, the second equality holds because
\vartriangleright
is a left action.

The first equality holds thanks to our hypothesis that the function
\widehat{\ell}_{\vartriangleright}
is bijective and completely determined by
s(\ell),r(\ell)
. Because of this,
(\xi^{r(p)})^{-1}_{\vartriangleright}\circ p_{\vartriangleright}=(\xi^{s(p)})^{-1}_{\vartriangleright}
, which yields the first equality. That is,
\Theta
is a bijective
k
-graph homomorphism. As
\widetilde{\Omega}
is stable, this concludes the proof.

∎

Remark 5.6
.

We compute the isomorphism

As we establish in the following Theorem, relaxed stability yields a complete characterization of when a quasi-product is isomorphic to a product graph (and hence has a tensor-product
C^{*}
-algebra).

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
\Omega
be a quasi-product with connected quasi-factors
\Lambda
and
\Gamma
. We have
\Omega\cong\Lambda\times\Gamma
iff
\Lambda,\Gamma
are both relaxed stable quasi-factors of
\Omega
.

Proof.

If
\Lambda,\Gamma
are both relaxed stable quasi-factors of
\Omega
, then applying Theorem
5.4
twice (once for each factor) yields the desired isomorphism. On the other hand, if
\theta:\Omega\to\Lambda\times\Gamma
is an isomorphism, we need to show that for any
p\in\widehat{G}(\Lambda)^{*},q\in\widehat{G}(\Gamma)^{*},\ \widehat{p}_{\vartriangleright},\widehat{q}_{\vartriangleleft}
are bijective and only depend on
r(p),s(p)
[
r(q),s(q)
respectively].

We will first show that, thanks to our hypothesis that
\Lambda,\Gamma
are connected,
\theta
restricts to isomorphisms
\theta_{x}:\Omega_{2}^{x}\to\Gamma,\ \theta^{w}:\Omega_{1}^{w}\to\Lambda
for each
x\in\Lambda^{0},w\in\Gamma^{0}
. As
\theta:\Omega\to\Lambda\times\Gamma
is an isomorphism, it suffices to show that
\theta
sends all elements of
\Omega_{2}^{x}
to
\{y\}\times\Gamma
for a unique
y\in\Lambda^{0}
, and similarly that
\text{Im}(\theta(\Omega_{1}^{w}))\subseteq\Lambda\times\{z\}
for a unique
z\in\Gamma^{0}.

Fix
q,q^{\prime}\in G(\Gamma)^{*}
and
x\in\Lambda^{0}
, and suppose
\theta(x,[q])\in\{y\}\times\Gamma
and
\theta(x,[q^{\prime}])\in\{u\}\times\Gamma
. We will show
u=y
.
Since
\Gamma
is connected, there exists a path
e^{1}_{\pm}\cdots e^{n}_{\pm}\in\widehat{G}(\Gamma)^{*}
with
r(e^{1}_{\pm})=s(q)
and
s(e^{n}_{\pm})=s(q^{\prime}).
As
\theta
is a degree-preserving isomorphism, for each
i
,

\theta(x,e^{i})\in\{x_{i}\}\times G(\Gamma)^{1}
for some
x_{i}\in\Lambda^{0}
. We therefore have

s(\theta(x,e^{i})),r(\theta(x,e^{i}))\in\{x_{i}\}\times\Gamma^{0}\text{ for all }i.

Since
\theta
commutes with the source and range maps, the fact that
s(e^{i}_{\pm})=r(e^{i+1}_{\pm})
implies that for all
i<n,

we have

\theta(s(x,e^{i}_{\pm}))\in\{s(\theta(x,e^{i+1})),r(\theta(x,e^{i+1}))\}\subseteq\{x_{i+1}\}\times\Gamma^{0}.

Similarly,
\{x_{n}\}\times\Gamma^{0}\ni\theta(s(x,e^{n}_{\pm}))=s(\theta(x,[q^{\prime}]))\in\{u\}\times\Gamma^{0}

and
\{x_{1}\}\times\Gamma^{0}\ni\theta(r(x,e^{1}_{\pm}))=s(\theta(x,[q]))\in\{y\}\times\Gamma^{0}.
It follows that

y=x_{1}=\cdots=x_{n}=u

as desired. The same argument will show that, for each
w\in\Gamma^{0},

\text{Im}(\theta(\Omega_{1}^{w}))\subseteq\Lambda\times\{z\}
for a single
z\in\Gamma^{0}
.

Our next goal is to prove that for any
\xi\in\widehat{G}(\Lambda)^{*}
,
\xi_{\vartriangleright}
only depends on
r(\xi),s(\xi)
. Define
\tau_{v,w}:\{w\}\times\Gamma\to\{v\}\times\Gamma
such that
(w,\gamma)\mapsto(v,\gamma)
;
if
\text{Im}\theta_{x}=\{y\}\times\Gamma
, we write
\tilde{x}:=y\in\Lambda^{0}
.
It is enough to show that for any
\xi\in\widehat{G}(\Lambda)^{*}
with
s(\xi)=w
and
r(\xi)=v
, we have
\theta_{v}\widehat{\xi}_{\vartriangleright}=\tau_{\tilde{v},\tilde{w}}\theta_{w}
.
To prove this, we set
\xi=p^{1}_{\pm}\cdots p^{n}_{\pm}
with
p^{i}\in G(\Lambda)^{*}
and induct on
n
.

For the case
n=1
, we first assume
\xi=p^{1}
. Fix
q\in G(\Gamma)^{*}
, and write
(\tilde{w},\gamma):=\theta(w,[q]).
Observe that

\displaystyle\theta((v,[p^{1}_{\vartriangleright}(q)])([q_{\vartriangleleft}(p^{1})],s(q)))

\displaystyle=\theta(([p^{1}],r(q))(w,[q]))

\displaystyle=\theta([p^{1}],r(q))(\tilde{w},\gamma)=:(\lambda,y)(\tilde{w},\gamma)

\displaystyle=(r(\lambda),\gamma)(\lambda,s(\gamma)),

thanks to the factorization rule in
\Lambda\times\Gamma
. Moreover, the factorization property implies that

\tilde{v}\times\Gamma\ni\theta(v,[p^{1}_{\vartriangleright}(q)])=(r(\lambda),\gamma);

that is,
r(\lambda)=\tilde{v}
and
\theta_{v}p^{1}_{\vartriangleright}(w,[q])=(\tilde{v},\gamma)=\tau_{\tilde{v},\tilde{w}}\theta_{w}(w,[q])
, as desired. In particular, as
\tau_{\tilde{v},\tilde{w}},\theta_{w},\theta_{v}
are
k
-graph isomorphisms, we can write

\widehat{p}_{\vartriangleright}^{1}=\theta_{v}^{-1}\tau_{\tilde{v},\tilde{w}}\theta_{w}.

We pause briefly to observe that for any
p\in G(\Gamma)^{*}
the equality above ensures bijectivity of
\widehat{p}_{\vartriangleright}
.
In particular,
(\widehat{p}_{\vartriangleright})^{-1}
is well defined and also depends only on
s(p^{-1}),r(p^{-1}).

Having established the base case, suppose now that for any path
\eta=m^{1}_{\pm}\cdots m^{j}_{\pm}\in\widehat{G}(\Lambda)^{*}
with
j\leq n-1
, we have
\theta_{r(\eta)}\eta_{\vartriangleright}=\tau_{\widetilde{r(\eta)},\widetilde{s(\eta)}}\theta_{s(\eta)}
. Given
\xi=p^{1}_{\pm}\cdots p^{n}_{\pm}\in\widehat{G}(\Lambda)^{*}
, write
\eta=p^{2}_{\pm}\cdots p^{n}_{\pm}
and
u=r(\eta)=s(p^{1}_{\pm})
. The inductive hypothesis yields
(\widehat{p^{1}_{\pm}})_{\vartriangleright}=\theta_{v}^{-1}\tau_{\tilde{v},\tilde{u}}\theta_{u}
and
\widehat{\eta}_{\vartriangleright}=\theta_{u}^{-1}\tau_{\tilde{u},\tilde{w}}\theta_{w}
. Since
\widehat{\xi}_{\vartriangleright}=(\widehat{p}^{1}_{\pm})_{\vartriangleright}\circ\widehat{\eta}_{\vartriangleright}
,

\widehat{\xi}_{\vartriangleright}=(\widehat{p}^{1}_{\pm})_{\vartriangleright}\circ\widehat{\eta}_{\vartriangleright}=\theta_{v}^{-1}\tau_{\tilde{v},\tilde{u}}\theta_{u}\theta_{u}^{-1}\tau_{\tilde{u},\tilde{w}}\theta_{w}=\theta_{v}^{-1}\tau_{\tilde{v},\tilde{w}}\theta_{w}.

A symmetric argument
will show that, if we write
\tau^{x,y}:\Lambda\times\{y\}\to\Lambda\times\{x\}
for the map sending
(\lambda,y)
to
(\lambda,x)
, then for any
\zeta\in\widehat{G}(\Gamma)^{*}
,

\theta^{{s(\zeta)}}\widehat{\zeta}_{\vartriangleleft}=\tau^{\widetilde{s(\zeta)},\widetilde{r(\zeta)}}\theta^{r(\zeta)}.

In particular,
\widehat{\zeta}_{\vartriangleleft}
is bijective and only depends on
s(\zeta),r(\zeta).
∎

5.3.
Applications: polytrees and
k
-trees

In some situations, the structure of one of the quasi-factors
\Gamma
can force any quasi-product with
\Gamma
as a quasi-factor to be a product
k
-graph. For example, this occurs if
\Gamma
is a polytree or a product of polytrees (see Theorem
5.10
below).

Definition 5.8
.

[
5
,
\lx@sectionsign 9.6
]

A
polytree
is a directed graph
E
such that whenever
\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\widehat{E}
satisfy
s(\xi)=s(\xi^{\prime}),r(\xi)=r(\xi^{\prime})
, then
\xi=\xi^{\prime}
.

Remark 5.9
.

The obvious generalization of Definition
5.8
to
k
-graphs is problematic, because any commuting square
gh\sim h^{\prime}g^{\prime}
in
\Lambda
yields a cycle
\xi:=h^{\prime}g^{\prime}h^{-1}g^{-1}
in
\widehat{G}(\Lambda)
with
r(\xi)=r(g)=s(\xi)
but
\xi\not=s(g)
.

Theorem 5.10
.

If
\Omega
is a quasi-product with quasi-factors
\Lambda
a
k
-graph and
E
a polytree, then
\Omega\cong\Lambda\times E
.

Proof.

We begin by demonstrating that
E
is a stable quasi-factor. To this end, we appeal to Lemma
3.15
.
Let
\sigma

be a vertex fixing graph automorphism of
E
. Then
s(\sigma(e))=s(e)
and
r(\sigma(e))=r(e)
for all
e\in E^{1}
, and so
e=\sigma(e)
and thus
\sigma=\operatorname{id}
. Thus, Lemma
3.15
forces
E
to be a stable quasi-factor.

We now demonstrate that
\Lambda
is relaxed stable. To this end, let
\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\widehat{E}
such that
s(\xi)=s(\xi^{\prime})
and
r(\xi)=r(\xi^{\prime})
.
Then
\xi=\xi^{\prime}
and in particular
\xi_{\vartriangleleft}=\xi^{\prime}_{\vartriangleleft}
.
Furthermore, Theorem
3.12
guarantees that
\widehat{q}_{\vartriangleleft}
is a bijection for all
q\in\widehat{E}
.
We conclude that
\Lambda
is relaxed stable.

Using Theorem
5.4
, we obtain an isomorphism
\Theta:\Omega\to\widetilde{\Omega}
such that
\Lambda
and
E
are stable quasi-factors. By Theorem
5.7
,
\widetilde{\Omega}\cong\Lambda\times E
, and hence
\Omega\cong\Lambda\times E
.
∎

We may appeal to the associativity of graph products to generalize polytrees to the higher-rank graph setting in a more appropriate way.

Definition 5.11
.

Let
\Gamma
be a
k
-graph such that
\Gamma\cong\prod E_{i}
. If each
E_{i}
is a polytree, then we call
\Gamma
a
k
-tree.

One notable
k
-tree is the lattice
\Omega_{k}=\{(m,n)\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\times\mathbb{N}^{k}:m\leq n\}
with
d(m,n)=n-m
,
s(m,n)=n
, and
r(m,n)=m
from
[
12
, Example 1.7.ii]
, as we detail in the next example.

Example 5.12
.

Let
E
be the digraph with vertices
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and edges
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with
s(e_{i})=w_{i+1}
and
r(e_{i})=w_{i}
. Observe that paths in
E^{*}
are in bijection with pairs of integers
(j,\ell)
with
j\leq\ell
; the source of the associated path
\omega_{(j,\ell)}:=e_{j}\cdots e_{\ell-1}
in
E^{*}
is
w_{\ell}
and the range is
w_{j}
. Fix some rank
k
, and define
\Omega:=\prod_{i=1}^{k}E^{*}
. An element
m=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
corresponds to the vertex
w_{m}:=(w_{m_{1}},\cdots,w_{m_{k}})\in\Omega^{0}
.
w_{n}\in\Omega^{0}
.

If
n\geq m
,
define
\omega_{(m,n)}:=(\omega_{(m_{1},n_{1})},\cdots,\omega_{(m_{k},n_{k})})\in\Omega
. The functor

(m,n)\mapsto\omega_{(m,n)}
yields an isomorphism
\Omega_{k}\cong\Omega
.

Corollary 5.13
.

If
\Omega
is a quasi-product with quasi-factors
\Lambda
and
\Gamma
, with

\Gamma
a
k_{2}
-tree, then
\Omega\cong\Lambda\times\Gamma
.

Proof.

We prove this by induction on
k_{2}
. The base case is assured by Theorem
5.10
. We now suppose the corollary holds for
(k_{2}-1)
-trees.

Let
\Gamma
be a
k_{2}
-tree. We write
\Gamma\cong\Gamma^{\prime}\times E
for some
(k_{2}-1)
-tree
\Gamma^{\prime}
and some polytree
E
. Next, we leverage associativity of Cartesian products to observe that
\Omega
may be viewed as a quasi-product with quasi-factors
\Omega^{\prime}
and
E
, where
\Omega^{\prime}
is a quasi-product with quasi-factors
\Lambda
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
. Theorem
5.10
assures us that
\Omega\cong\Omega^{\prime}\times E
.

The injections
\psi_{1}:\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Omega
and
\psi_{2}:\Gamma\hookrightarrow\Omega
may be restricted to injections
\psi_{1}:\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Omega^{\prime}
and
\psi_{2}:\Gamma^{\prime}\hookrightarrow\Omega^{\prime}
. That is, the quasi-factor
\Omega^{\prime}
is itself a quasi-product with quasi-factors
\Lambda
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
. We then use the inductive hypothesis to conclude that
\Omega^{\prime}\cong\Lambda\times\Gamma^{\prime}
giving us
\Omega\cong(\Lambda\times\Gamma^{\prime})\times E=\Lambda\times(\Gamma^{\prime}\times E)=\Lambda\times\Gamma
.
∎
