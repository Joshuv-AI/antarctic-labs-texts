Dual Use of Electronic and Combustible Cigarettes and Stroke Risk: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Ah-Ram Sul, Tae-Hyeok Kim, Heejin Kimm, Yu-Jin Paek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesDual use of electronic and combustible cigarettes is increasing, but its association with cardiovascular outcomes, including stroke, remains unclear. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated associations of dual use with stroke and other cardiovascular outcomes compared with study-specific reference groups.MethodsOvid-MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, KoreaMed, and the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform were searched for studies published from 2003 through September 2025. Comparative studies of adults reporting adjusted effect estimates for cardiovascular or cerebrovascular outcomes were eligible. When ≥5 methodologically comparable studies were available, random-effects meta-analysis with restricted maximum likelihood estimation was performed. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran’s Q test and Higgins I2 statistic. Leave-one-out analyses assessed the robustness of the pooled estimates.ResultsTen observational studies were included: 4 prospective cohort studies and 6 cross-sectional studies. Seven studies reported stroke outcomes. In pooled analyses of cross-sectional studies, dual use was associated with higher odds of stroke than never smoking (adjusted odds ratio, 1.90; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.50 to 2.40), with substantial heterogeneity (I2=91.3%). Leave-one-out analyses yielded consistent results. Composite cardiovascular outcomes suggested elevated risk but were not pooled because outcome definitions varied substantially. One prospective cohort study reported increased all-cause mortality risk (adjusted hazard ratio, 2.44; 95% CI, 1.90 to 3.13).ConclusionsDual use of electronic and combustible cigarettes was associated with higher odds of stroke than never smoking; however, evidence was predominantly cross-sectional and heterogeneous. Prospective studies using standardized exposure definitions and validated cardiovascular outcomes may clarify long-term cardiovascular risk.Keywords: Electronic nicotine delivery systems, Tobacco use, Smoking, Stroke, Cardiovascular diseases

INTRODUCTION

Electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS) comprise a range of non-combustible nicotine products, including liquid-based electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes [ECs]) and heated tobacco products (HTPs) [1,2]. ECs aerosolize nicotine-containing liquids, whereas HTPs heat processed tobacco without combustion [1]. Over the past decade, the global use of ENDS has increased substantially. However, most epidemiological studies have focused on ECs rather than HTPs; consequently, the present review primarily reflects evidence on EC exposure. Although ECs are frequently discussed in public health and regulatory contexts as potentially less harmful alternatives to combustible cigarettes (CCs) [3,4], real-world patterns of use are more complex. Epidemiological and mechanistic evidence indicates that EC exposure may have clinically relevant cardiovascular effects despite the absence of combustion, particularly when ECs are used concurrently with CCs [5,6].

The expanding availability of ENDS has facilitated dual and poly-use, particularly concurrent use with CCs [7]. Poly-use, defined as the concurrent use of 2 or more tobacco or nicotine products, has become increasingly prevalent in many populations [7]. In practice, many EC users do not completely replace CCs with ECs but instead continue to use both products. Population-based data indicate that a substantial proportion of EC users concurrently smoke cigarettes; a prior analysis found that 85.3% of EC users in Korea between 2013 and 2017 reported dual use [3]. These patterns raise public health concerns because continued exposure to both products may sustain or amplify vascular risk.

Nicotine exposure and aerosol constituents from ECs can affect several biological pathways relevant to cardiovascular disease. Experimental and clinical studies, together with recent scientific statements and consensus reports, indicate that nicotine-containing ENDS can increase heart rate and blood pressure through sympathetic activation and are associated with oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and platelet activation [1,2,8,9]. Chronic exposure has also been associated with impaired endothelial function and altered vascular reactivity [2,10]. Recent systematic evidence further indicates that acute ENDS exposure may impair vascular function and autonomic balance, supporting the biological plausibility of an association with cardiovascular risk [5]. These pathophysiological mechanisms are implicated in atherosclerosis and thromboembolic cardiovascular events, including stroke [2].

Although complete switching from CCs may reduce exposure to combustion-related toxicants, dual use may sustain or potentially exacerbate vascular injury. Evidence syntheses have yielded heterogeneous findings regarding cardiovascular outcomes associated with EC use. One meta-analysis reported increased odds of cardiovascular disease among dual users, whereas associations among exclusive EC users were weaker or not statistically significant [11]. Similarly, a scoping review concluded that adverse cardiovascular effects remain detectable among EC users despite potential reductions in toxicant exposure among individuals who switch completely from CCs [4]. Longitudinal analyses have further emphasized the importance of distinguishing exclusive EC use from dual use when evaluating cardiovascular risk [12]. Recent reviews also describe persistent uncertainty regarding the long-term cardiovascular consequences of vaping products and emphasize the need to distinguish complete substitution from concurrent tobacco product use [6,13].

Evidence regarding stroke specifically remains inconsistent. One meta-analysis reported increased odds of stroke among current ENDS users compared with non-users [14], whereas another systematic review concluded that the association remains inconclusive because of confounding by CC smoking and methodological heterogeneity among studies [15]. Additional pooled analyses have reported elevated odds of cardiovascular disease among dual users while also identifying substantial between-study heterogeneity and a predominance of cross-sectional data [11,16]. Overall, most available evidence is derived from cross-sectional studies, and longitudinal data on incident cardiovascular and cerebrovascular outcomes remain limited [11,14–17]. Evidence specifically addressing dual use is also limited.

Stroke is a clinically important outcome when evaluating ENDS and dual tobacco product use. Mechanistically, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, sympathetic activation, and platelet activation associated with ENDS exposure provide biological plausibility for an association with cerebrovascular risk [1,2,10]. At the population level, dual use has also been associated with elevated cardiovascular risk in some studies, raising the possibility of additive or synergistic vascular effects [3,11].

Interpretation of the epidemiological evidence is further complicated by demographic differences among user groups. Exclusive EC users tend to be substantially younger than CC smokers, whereas cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases typically develop over decades of cumulative exposure to vascular risk factors [18]. This age difference may attenuate associations observed in cross-sectional datasets and complicate causal interpretation [3,11]. Restricting analyses to adults may therefore improve interpretability and reduce potential confounding from transient or experimental nicotine use patterns, which are more common among adolescents [11].

Given the increasing prevalence of dual tobacco product use worldwide, clarifying the potential cardiovascular and cerebrovascular consequences of this pattern has implications for tobacco control policy and risk communication. Accordingly, this systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated cardiovascular disease and stroke outcomes associated with dual use of ECs and CCs compared with never smoking among adults. Adjusted effect estimates were prioritized, quantitative synthesis was restricted to outcomes with sufficient methodological comparability, and between-study heterogeneity and robustness were examined through sensitivity analyses.

METHODS

Study Design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [19] and the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) guidelines [20]. The study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration No. CRD420251057424).

Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in Ovid-MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, KoreaMed, and the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform. Because the modern form of ECs was patented in 2003 and entered commercial markets in the mid-2000s, the search was restricted to studies published from 2003 onward [21]. Citation tracking of relevant articles was also performed to identify additional eligible studies.

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms (e.g., Medical Subject Headings [MeSH] and Emtree) with free-text keywords related to ENDS and cardiovascular outcomes. Separate searches were conducted for ECs and HTPs. The final search was performed on September 10, 2025. Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN) methodology filters were applied to identify comparative epidemiological studies. Detailed search strategies are provided in Supplemental Material 1.

Eligibility Criteria and Study Selection

The review question followed the Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcome (PECO) framework: adults aged ≥18 years (Population), dual use of ECs and CCs (Exposure), non-smoking or never-use reference groups as defined in the original studies (Comparator), and cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease (including stroke), or mortality (Outcome). Because exposure and comparator definitions varied among studies, the study-specific definitions were extracted and are reported in Table 1.

Outcome definitions followed those used in the original studies.

Studies were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) included adults aged ≥18 years; (2) examined dual use or other forms of multiple tobacco product use in relation to cardiovascular or cerebrovascular outcomes; and (3) reported adjusted effect estimates (e.g., adjusted odds ratios [aORs] or adjusted hazard ratios [aHRs]) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Although broader multiple-product use was eligible, all included studies specifically evaluated dual use of ECs and CCs. Randomized and non-randomized comparative designs were eligible; however, all included studies were observational.

For the primary analysis, studies using non-smoking or never-use reference groups were included. Outcomes of interest were cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease (including stroke), and mortality. Because no study reported cardiovascular-specific mortality, mortality analyses were limited to all-cause mortality.

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts and subsequently assessed the full texts of potentially eligible studies. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Data Extraction and Risk-of-bias Assessment

Data were extracted using a standardized data collection form. One reviewer performed the initial extraction, and a second reviewer verified the extracted data for accuracy. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

Extracted variables included study characteristics, participant demographics, exposure and comparator definitions, outcome definitions, adjusted effect estimates, and covariate adjustments. Exposure definitions followed those used in the original studies. In most included studies, dual use was defined as current use of both ECs and CCs, although the classification of tobacco use patterns varied among studies. Accordingly, the study-specific exposure definitions are reported in Table 1.

Risk of bias was independently assessed by 2 reviewers using the Revised Risk of Bias Assessment Tool for Non-randomized Studies (RoBANS 2.0).

Data Analysis

Findings were first summarized narratively. When 5 or more studies reported methodologically comparable adjusted effect estimates for the same outcome, a random-effects meta-analysis was performed [3].

Because composite cardiovascular outcomes were defined heterogeneously among studies, quantitative synthesis was restricted to stroke outcomes, for which 7 studies provided adjusted estimates that were considered comparable. Estimates from prospective cohort studies were summarized descriptively and were not pooled with cross-sectional estimates because of differences in study design and effect estimation.

Effect estimates were log-transformed, and standard errors were derived from the reported 95% CIs. Pooled estimates were calculated using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood estimation to account for anticipated between-study heterogeneity [22,23].

Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q test and Higgins I2 statistic [24]. I2 values of approximately 25%, 50%, and 75% were interpreted as indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [25].

Sensitivity analyses included leave-one-out analyses in which the meta-analysis was repeated after the sequential omission of each study to assess the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimate. Because fewer than 10 studies were included in the pooled analysis, publication bias was not formally assessed using funnel plots or statistical tests [26].

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/MP version 17.0 (StataCorp., College Station, TX, USA). A 2-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the substantial heterogeneity among studies, pooled estimates were interpreted cautiously, with greater emphasis on the direction and consistency of associations than on the magnitude of the pooled effect [24].

Ethics Statement

Because this study analyzed previously published data, institutional review board approval was not required.

RESULTS

Search Results

The search for liquid-based ECs identified 2999 records. After 245 duplicates were removed, 2754 records underwent title and abstract screening, and 134 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility (Figure 1). Six studies were identified through database searches, and 4 additional studies were identified through citation tracking. Overall, 10 observational studies were included in the primary evidence synthesis.

A separate search for HTPs identified no studies meeting the predefined inclusion criteria (Supplemental Material 2).

Study Characteristics

The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1 [12,27–35]. Four studies were prospective cohorts, and 6 were cross-sectional. Sample sizes ranged from 4022 to 480 317 participants. Most studies used nationally representative United States datasets, including the Population Assessment of Tobacco and Health, National Health Interview Survey, National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System, and All of Us.

Stroke was the most frequently reported outcome (7 studies), followed by composite cardiovascular outcomes (3 studies) and all-cause mortality (1 study). Most stroke outcomes were based on self-reported physician diagnoses obtained through cross-sectional surveys.

Risk-of-bias assessments are presented in Supplemental Material 3. Most studies were judged to have a low risk of bias in the selection and confounding domains. However, the risk of bias in exposure assessment was frequently rated as unclear because tobacco use was self-reported.

Individual study estimates are presented in Supplemental Material 4. Adjusted effect estimates for stroke ranged from approximately 1.12 to 2.91, with most point estimates indicating a higher risk among dual users than among never smokers. Composite cardiovascular outcomes also showed consistently positive associations, although outcome definitions differed among studies. One prospective cohort study found no statistically significant association between dual use and stroke, although the CI was wide.

Composite Cardiovascular Events

Three studies [27,29,30] evaluated composite cardiovascular outcomes. Adjusted effect estimates consistently indicated a positive association between dual use and cardiovascular outcomes, with estimates ranging from approximately 1.54 to 2.18 (Table 2). Because the outcome definitions differed among studies, quantitative pooling was not performed.

Stroke

Seven studies [12,28,29,31–34] reported adjusted effect estimates for the association between dual use and stroke compared with never smoking. Six were cross-sectional studies reporting aORs, and 1 was a prospective cohort study reporting an aHR.

In the pooled analysis of the cross-sectional studies, dual use was associated with higher odds of stroke than never smoking (pooled aOR, 1.90; 95% CI, 1.50 to 2.40) (Figure 2). Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (I2=91.3%; p<0.001). Despite the high heterogeneity, all included cross-sectional studies reported effect estimates greater than 1.0, indicating a consistent direction of association.

The prospective cohort study found no statistically significant association between dual use and stroke (aHR, 1.12; 95% CI, 0.33 to 3.79).

Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses showed that the pooled estimate remained statistically significant and directionally consistent across all iterations (Supplemental Material 5). Pooled aORs ranged from 1.80 to 2.04, indicating that no single study materially influenced the overall estimate.

Mortality

One prospective cohort study [35] evaluated all-cause mortality. Dual use was associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality than never smoking (aHR, 2.44; 95% CI, 1.90 to 3.13) (Table 2). Because only 1 study reported a mortality outcome, no meta-analysis was performed.

DISCUSSION

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, dual use of ECs and CCs was associated with higher odds of stroke than never smoking. The pooled aOR was approximately 1.90, and the direction of association remained consistent across leave-one-out sensitivity analyses. However, between-study heterogeneity was substantial, and most estimates were derived from cross-sectional studies. These limitations warrant cautious interpretation of the pooled estimates.

The substantial heterogeneity may reflect methodological and clinical differences among studies. Definitions of dual use differed among studies with respect to recency, frequency, and exposure intensity. Comparator definitions also varied, ranging from strictly defined never smokers to broader non-use categories that may have included former users. In addition, most stroke outcomes were based on self-reported physician diagnoses obtained through population surveys. Although validation studies suggest that self-reported stroke diagnoses have high specificity, their sensitivity and positive predictive value may be moderate compared with registry-based adjudication [36]. Such outcome misclassification could attenuate the observed associations.

Because most included studies were cross-sectional, temporal relationships between exposure and outcome could not be established. The observed associations should therefore not be interpreted as causal. Reverse causality is also possible if individuals with underlying vascular risk factors were more likely to initiate EC use or adopt dual use. Although most studies adjusted for socio-demographic and cardiometabolic factors, residual confounding related to cumulative smoking exposure and duration may remain [15–17].

An additional consideration is that tobacco use behavior may change after a cardiovascular disease diagnosis. Individuals diagnosed with cardiovascular disease are often advised to quit smoking or modify their tobacco use, which may affect how they are classified as current or former users [12,35]. Consequently, associations observed among current dual users in cross-sectional studies may be attenuated if individuals at higher cardiovascular risk had already reduced or discontinued tobacco use. This possibility could lead to underestimation of the association between current dual use and stroke risk [12,27,35].

Despite these limitations, the observed association is biologically plausible. Experimental and clinical evidence indicates that ENDS exposure can increase sympathetic activation and blood pressure and is associated with oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and platelet activation—mechanisms involved in atherosclerosis and thromboembolic disease [1]. Chronic EC use has also been associated with impaired endothelial function and reduced nitric oxide bioavailability [10]. In dual users, continued exposure to CC smoke may further amplify these vascular effects and offset any potential reduction in toxicant exposure achieved through partial substitution with ECs. Recent experimental and clinical syntheses similarly report that vaping products can induce acute hemodynamic changes and vascular dysfunction that may contribute to cardiovascular risk [5,6,13].

Recent systematic reviews and network meta-analyses have also reported higher odds of composite cardiovascular outcomes associated with EC use and dual use, with more consistent associations for myocardial infarction and stroke among cigarette and dual users [16]. The consistency of these findings across evidence syntheses supports the plausibility of the association observed in the present review, although substantial uncertainty remains regarding causality and the magnitude of risk. These reviews similarly emphasize the predominance of cross-sectional evidence and substantial between-study heterogeneity.

An important question is whether dual use is associated with greater cardiovascular risk than exclusive use of either product. Current evidence suggests that dual use is generally associated with higher cardiovascular risk than exclusive EC use, likely because continued exposure to CC smoke remains a major contributor to cardiovascular harm [27,30,35]. However, comparisons between dual use and exclusive CC use are less consistent. Several studies reported comparable risks of cardiovascular disease, stroke, or mortality in dual users and exclusive cigarette smokers [12,27,35], whereas others reported higher risks or point estimates among dual users for selected outcomes, including stroke and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [29,30,32]. Overall, cardiovascular risk among dual users appears to be at least comparable to that among exclusive cigarette smokers, although whether dual use is consistently more harmful remains uncertain and may vary by outcome and study population.

Importantly, this review was not designed to directly compare dual use with exclusive CC smoking. It therefore cannot determine whether dual use is associated with additional cerebrovascular risk beyond that associated with CC smoking alone. Although dual use may represent a transitional stage toward smoking cessation for some individuals, prospective studies directly comparing dual use, exclusive CC use, and exclusive EC use are needed to clarify their relative cardiovascular risks.

Strengths of this review include the use of adjusted effect estimates, restriction to clearly defined comparator groups, explicit assessment of heterogeneity, and comprehensive sensitivity analyses.

Future research should prioritize prospective cohort studies using standardized exposure definitions and objectively verified cardiovascular outcomes, ideally through registry linkage or electronic health records. Determining whether dual use is associated with incremental cerebrovascular risk beyond exclusive combustible cigarette smoking remains an important research priority.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. As discussed above, the predominance of cross-sectional designs limited causal inference, self-reported stroke outcomes were subject to potential misclassification, and variation in exposure and comparator definitions may have contributed to between-study heterogeneity.

In addition, EC use and CC smoking were generally self-reported, introducing potential exposure misclassification. Because fewer than 10 studies were available for the primary meta-analysis, formal assessment of publication bias was not performed. Finally, evidence regarding mortality was limited to a single prospective cohort study, precluding quantitative synthesis and limiting conclusions regarding long-term mortality risk.

Policy and Practice Implications

The increasing prevalence of dual use of ECs and CCs presents challenges for tobacco control policy and cardiovascular disease prevention. Public health messaging has often emphasized the potential harm-reduction role of ECs when they completely replace CC smoking. However, the present findings suggest that concurrent use of both products may not constitute a clear harm-reduction pattern from a cerebrovascular perspective. Risk communication should therefore distinguish complete switching from dual use and emphasize that continued exposure to both products may sustain cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk.

From a policy perspective, surveillance systems and epidemiological studies should use more detailed classifications of tobacco product use, including exclusive use, dual use, and poly-use. Regulatory and tobacco control policies should also consider the population-level implications of dual use, particularly among adults who continue to smoke while adopting ECs. More precise exposure definitions and improved surveillance of emerging nicotine products may inform evidence-based strategies to reduce the cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease burden associated with changing tobacco use patterns.

CONCLUSION

Dual use of ECs and CCs was associated with higher odds of stroke than never smoking. Although this association remained consistent across sensitivity analyses, the available evidence was derived largely from cross-sectional studies and showed substantial heterogeneity, limiting causal interpretation.

These findings indicate that dual use should not be assumed to be free of cerebrovascular risk and may not provide the cardiovascular benefits sometimes attributed to complete switching from CCs. Prospective studies using standardized exposure definitions and validated cardiovascular outcomes are needed to clarify long-term risk and inform clinical practice and public health policy.
