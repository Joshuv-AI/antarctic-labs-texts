Waterscape dataset of the municipality of Girona
Authors: Fiona Corominas, David Cunillera-Montcusí, Luis Martín, Dani Boix, Jordi Bou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This dataset presents a detailed cartographic and classification of freshwater body types within the Girona’s municipality territory to support integrated urban planning and ecological conservation strategies. The data were collected through a systematic spatial analysis of water bodies, combining GIS mapping and field validation techniques to characterize the water bodies and cartography aquatic systems across the urban and peri-urban landscape. The resulting database, that was converted to a grid with 100 × 100 m cells, categorizes each water body according to four key typologies: type of aquatic system (F1: lentic or lotic), degree of urbanization (F2: urban or peri-urban), water permanence (F3: permanent or temporary), and geographical location (F4: central or peripheral). Each water body is also georeferenced and quantified by surface area (m²), enabling comparative analysis across categories. Griona’s waterscape is dominated by lotic water bodies and permanent hydroperiods. Lotic water bodies are more distributed on the peri-urban area contrasting with lentic ones, which are located more within the urban area. The distribution pattern of the waterscape over the geographical location shows a similar pattern to the degree of urbanization, with a small difference on lentic water bodies. This dataset provides a resource for understanding the structure and characteristics of urban and peri‑urban freshwater systems. Through integrating hydrological and spatial data into a cohesive framework, it can support decision-making in land-use planning, conservation prioritization, and sustainable urban development, while also serving as a valuable tool for further research and applied modelling.

Background

Freshwater aquatic ecosystems are an essential part of the natural landscape of the territory. They combine the two most relevant aspects for the conservation of global biodiversity: 1) they are some of the most threatened ecosystems and 2) they are those that contain the highest biodiversity [1,2]. The essential role that freshwater plays for human societies [3] has fostered the level of impacts in these habitats and the biodiversity they sustain, which is particularly vulnerable to human activities and environmental changes. Consequently, biodiversity loss is much greater in freshwater than in terrestrial ecosystems [4,5]. Inland waters contain great richness of flora and fauna species. For example, they account for approximately 40 % of the world's fish diversity and a quarter of the world's vertebrate diversity live in freshwater [6]. When amphibians, aquatic reptiles, and mammals are added together with fish diversity, the overall count reaches until one-third of all vertebrate species. However, surface water habitats contain only about 0.01 % of the world’s water and cover only about 0.8 % of the Earth’s surface [2]. Moreover, freshwater ecosystems are under a high human pressure that have led to widespread habitat degradation, pollution, flow regulation and water extraction, overfishing and the introduction of exotic species [2]. The combination of these factors defines a high rate of habitat and ecosystem degradation for freshwaters which is of great concern for biodiversity loss and conservation [1].

The status of aquatic biodiversity in urban contexts is of particular interest, given the increasing extension of urbanization worldwide. Urban ecosystems are the only ecosystem that significantly increases their surface area day by day and it is important to study their structure as well as the effect that they have on biodiversity. In this regard, cities and their surroundings offer a wide variety of aquatic habitats, such as rivers, streams and ponds, as well as artificial systems such as fountains, ponds and ornamental ponds, which contain a diversity of organisms adapted to the aquatic environment [7]. This interplay across different aquatic ecosystems highlights the need to better comprehend and map these increasingly widespread environments. Overall, advancing our knowledge on these ecosystems will shed light on their potential role in preserving regional biodiversity and its implications for the conservation of aquatic ecosystems, human well-being and urban sustainability.

Data Description

The resulting cartographic database is composed of a classification of water bodies into different typologies: type of aquatic system (F1, including two categories: lentic and lotic); urbanization degree (F2, including two categories: urban and peri‑urban; see section “Experimental design, material and methods” for more details), water permanence (F3, including two categories: permanent and temporary), and geographical location of the water body (F4, including two categories: central and peripheral; see section “Experimental design, material and methods” for more details). Regarding the first typology (F1), it distinguishes lentic and lotic water bodies. A larger surface area is observed for lotic water bodies (97 %) compared to lentic ones, which represent only 3 %. For the remaining typologies (F2, F3, and F4), the data has been analyzed and broken down according to whether the waters are lentic or lotic (F1).

In terms of urbanization degree (F2), lotic waters are more commonly represented by peri‑urban rivers and streams (61 %), than urban ones (39 %). The opposite occurs with lentic water bodies, where 57 % are urban ponds and only 43 % are peri‑urban.

In the case of water permanence (F3), most lotic water bodies (rivers and streams) are permanent (74 %), while 26 % are temporary. A similar pattern, though to a lesser extent, is found in lentic water bodies, where 62 % are permanent ponds and 38 % are temporary.

Finally, regarding geographical location (F4), 63 % of lotic water bodies are located in peripheral areas (i.e. areas surrounding the city center), compared to 37 % in central areas (i.e. more heavily urbanized areas). In contrast, the distribution of lentic water bodies is more balanced, with 54 % in peripheral areas and 46 % in central areas.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The study is focused on the municipality of Girona (Fig. 3). However, the study area has considered not only the area corresponding to the administrative limits, since the hydrographic system exceeds these limits, consequently a circular study area has been defined around the centroid of the municipality (considering a radius of 5 km).Fig. 3Map of the study area where the geographical location (central or peripheral) radius are represented in blue and the municipal district of Girona in red.Fig 3

In order to quantify the water masses, a waterscape cartography has been developed, by joining three previous cartographies of the region: CORINE habitats of the municipality of Girona (1:10.000) [8], CORINE Habitats Cartography of Catalonia (CHC25v3) [9,10] and the hydrographic system [11]. ArcGIS Pro 3.0.3 was used to manage the cartographic database, primarily using ArcToolbox > Spatial Analyst Tools [12].

To obtain information on the water bodies within the municipality, the digital cartography of CORINE habitats of the municipality of Girona [8] was used. It was created in 2023 at a 1:10.000 scale, based on infrared and color orthophotos at 1:5.000 scale from the ICGC (May 2022) and fieldwork [13]. The cartography includes two layers of information. One contains polygons representing all habitats that cover a significant area (typically >2000 m²), and the other contains points indicating the location of relevant habitats that occupy a smaller area (<2000 m²). The cartography uses the legend of the CORINE units from the Manual of the Habitats of Catalonia [14]. However, this cartography only covers the municipality, so it is not sufficient to cover the entire study area. To complete the remaining small areas, the CORINE Habitats and Habitats of Community Interest Cartography of Catalonia (CHC25v3), at a 1:25.000 scale [9,10], was used. This database was used to complete the mapping of the water bodies located within the study area but outside the municipality of Girona. The layer with points has been converted to polygons, using the surface field of each point, with the buffer function as has been done in other studies.

These habitat cartographies do not map narrow and linear water bodies such as small streams and creeks as they follow the CORINE habitat cartographies protocols. Consequently, small creeks are not well represented on these maps. Therefore, to complete the database the hydrographic system cartography was used. This has been exported from the topographic cartography from the Institut Cartogràfic i Geològic de Catalunya [11], that includes linear vector layers of the remaining streams and creeks. The lines have been transformed into polygons, given a different width according to the stream order. The 1st order stream corresponds to 0,25 m width, the 2nd order stream to 0,5 m, the 3rd order stream to 0,75 m, the 4th order stream to 1 m and the 5th order stream to 1,25 m. In addition, the cartography of lentic water bodies was expanded by consulting reference works on these environments in the region [15].

The combination of all these sources of information has allowed the creation of a new custom database of the waterscape of Girona [16]. The unification of the different sources has been made by GIS tools, merging layers. In case of overlayers, an only unique feature was produced with the accumulative geometries. The surface of each final feature has been calculated by GIS geometry calculation. Then, the database has been converted to a grid with 100 × 100 m cells, in which the water surface area from each type of habitat has been calculated for each cell.

Also, water bodies were characterized by their physical and chemical characteristics. In general, aquatic systems have specific characteristics to which species are adapted. These characteristics allow for the classification of aquatic systems into different types, based on certain features such as water renewal rate or its permanence [[17], [18], [19]]. However, there are also other types of characteristics, related to anthropogenic factors, through which water bodies can be distinguished. For this reason, the waterscape database specifies four typologies, called factors (F), which differentiate between various categories and informs about these ecosystems functioning. Thus, factors can be separated between physical characteristics (F1 and F3) and anthropogenic characteristics (F2 and F4).

The type of aquatic system (F1) allows for the classification of systems as lotic, or flowing water systems (with a high water renewal rate), such as rivers and streams, or lentic, or still/stagnant water systems (with a low renewal rate), such as ponds. Classifying water bodies into these categories allows us to distinguish these two types of habitats, which have different characteristics and often contain different species assemblages, therefore must be managed differently. All the sources used to elaborate this database have this information recorded for the water bodies.

The urbanization degree (F2) distinguishes urban systems, including urban centers or cities, from peri‑urban systems, covering the surrounding areas around the city. The water bodies have been assigned between these categories using the urban matrix of Girona municipality dataset [20]. This layer classifies all the landscape of the study area in these two categories, based on the CORINE habitats cartography that identify the urban units (CORINE habitat type 85 and 86). Urban landscape has been considered when the mosaics dominated by these units exhibit a continuum of buildings and artificial units while in the peri‑urban case defines a more fragmented distribution of these units.

Water permanence (F3) distinguishes between systems that contain water year-round (permanent) and those that dry out during part of the year (temporary). It is important to note that permanent or temporary marks two different ecosystems that will have different diversities and interests. Most of this information has been obtained from the CORINE habitats by Bou et al. (2025). If the data is not available on this source or the other complementary sources of the database, surveys have been performed for missing data.

Geographical location (F4) distinguishes central systems, located in the center of the study area (within a 2,5 km radius from the central point), from peripheral systems, located on the outer part (between the 2,5 and 5 km radius).

Limitations

Temporary water bodies can be difficult to detect due to its short hydroperiod, especially the more ephemeral ones. In mediterranean regions these water bodies are an important part of the waterscape. In the same sense mapping small water bodies can be difficult, due to the sampling effort needed to detect them. The database can show limitations on these two kinds of water bodies. However the study case has a local scale, and the sources are high resolution cartographies, which reduces the possibility of gaps on the database. Moreover, the high amount of sampling efforts made in the different sources prevent missing water bodies. The fine scale used on this local study helps to minimize that limitation.
