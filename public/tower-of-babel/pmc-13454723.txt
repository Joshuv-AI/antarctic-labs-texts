Age Modifies the Association Between Smoking and Carotid Artery Plaque: A Cross-Sectional Study in Non-Diabetic Adults
Authors: Shu-Mei Zhai, Han Zhang, Xiao-Yan Yang, Yu-Qiang Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine whether age modifies the cross-sectional association between current smoking and carotid artery plaque (CAP) in non-diabetic adults.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included 9088 non-diabetic adults undergoing health examinations at the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University. CAP was assessed via ultrasonography. Smoking was classified as current smoker versus non-smoker (including former smokers). Multivariable logistic regression evaluated the interaction between current smoking and age group (<45 vs ≥45 years). Sensitivity analyses included age-stratified models, a continuous age-by-smoking interaction model, additional adjustment for drinking status, and a male-only analysis. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis characterized the non-linear association between age and CAP.ResultsCAP was detected in 1086 (11.95%) participants. A significant age-group-by-smoking interaction was observed (interaction OR = 0.342, 95% CI: 0.223–0.522; P < 0.001), indicating that the relative smoking-CAP association was weaker among adults aged ≥45 years. Current smoking was associated with CAP among adults aged <45 years in both the interaction model (OR = 2.104, 95% CI: 1.465–3.022; P < 0.001) and the age-stratified model (OR = 1.754, 95% CI: 1.179–2.610; P = 0.006), whereas the association was attenuated among adults aged ≥45 years. Continuous age-by-smoking and male-only sensitivity analyses showed consistent age-dependent patterns. The continuous RCS model confirmed a significant non-linear relationship (P < 0.001), visually demonstrating that the risk curves for smokers and non-smokers diverged most widely in early adulthood (<45 years) and converged at older ages. Male-only analysis yielded a consistent pattern.ConclusionCurrent smoking was associated with prevalent CAP predominantly among adults aged <45 years, and the relative association weakened at older ages. These findings support age-aware interpretation of smoking-related carotid atherosclerosis but should be considered hypothesis-generating because of the cross-sectional design and lack of cumulative smoking exposure data.Keywords: carotid artery plaque, effect modification, restricted cubic spline, risk stratification, interaction

Introduction

Carotid artery plaque (CAP) is a highly prevalent manifestation of subclinical atherosclerosis and a well-established predictor of acute ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction.1,2 Consequently, the early identification and personalized risk stratification of individuals with CAP are pivotal for mitigating the burden of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.3 Recent large-scale cohort evidence underscores that optimizing modifiable environmental and lifestyle contexts can significantly reduce stroke risk, reinforcing the critical need to identify these vascular risk patterns before clinical events occur.4

Among modifiable exposures, smoking is strongly associated with cardiovascular disease morbidity, contributing significantly to endothelial dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and accelerated atherogenesis.5–7 While the deleterious impact of smoking on vascular health is well established, emerging evidence suggests that the magnitude of its association with vascular outcomes may not be uniform across population subgroups.8 Alongside central mechanisms like lipid metabolism and novel targets such as PCSK9,9 smoking remains a dominant and prevalent driver of atherosclerotic burden.

In this context, age is a fundamental determinant of cardiovascular disease burden. Younger arteries may exhibit distinct pathophysiological responses to injurious stimuli compared with older and more extensively remodeled vessels.10,11 Yet, the potential effect modification by age on the smoking-CAP association remains under-investigated, particularly in adults without diabetes. Existing epidemiological studies have largely focused on populations aged ≥45 years, in which competing risks are prevalent, or have reported average effects that may obscure meaningful age-related heterogeneity.12–14

This large-scale cross-sectional study was conducted in a non-diabetic adult population to bridge this knowledge gap. We hypothesized that the association between current smoking status and prevalent CAP would be more pronounced in younger (<45 years) than in middle-aged and older (≧45 years) adults. The 45-year threshold was selected a priori because it is commonly used to distinguish younger from middle-aged adults in cardiovascular risk assessment, and in this cohort, it also corresponded closely to the 65th percentile of age and to the point at which the age-CAP curve began to rise more steeply. Because smoking was available only as current smoking status, the analysis was designed to evaluate current smoking rather than lifetime tobacco burden.

Method

Participants and Study Design

The present retrospective cross-sectional study analyzed data from individuals undergoing annual health check-ups at the physical examination center of the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University between January and December 2025. All clinical and demographic data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical records system. The eligibility of participants was determined based on strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, as detailed below. Inclusion criteria: (1) adults aged ≥18 years without a history of diabetes; (2) completion of a standard health questionnaire, physical examination, and comprehensive laboratory testing; and (3) completion of a bilateral carotid artery ultrasound with sufficient image quality for diagnosis. Exclusion criteria: (1) diagnosis of diabetes, fasting blood glucose level of ≥7.0 mmol/L, or glycated hemoglobin level of ≥6.5% at the time of examination; (2) established cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, including a history of myocardial infarction, stroke, or coronary/carotid revascularization; (3) missing key data, such as smoking status records or carotid ultrasound findings; and (4) severe comorbidities that could confound systemic inflammation and atherosclerotic progression, including severe renal impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min/1.73 m2), known autoimmune disease, or active malignancy. The analysis was restricted to adults without diabetes because diabetes is a strong determinant of atherosclerosis and could obscure age-related heterogeneity in the association between smoking and CAP. The final analytic cohort comprised 9088 individuals (Figure 1). Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Approval No. 2023-R386). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Research Ethics Committee because this retrospective study used anonymized data extracted from electronic medical records. Permission to access and analyze the de-identified electronic medical record data was granted by the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University.

Data Collection and Clinical Measurements

Baseline demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, height, and weight, were extracted from the electronic medical records system. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m2). Lifestyle factors were self-reported; smoking status was classified as current smoker versus non-smoker (the latter encompassing both never and former smokers due to database constraints), and alcohol consumption was categorized as current drinker versus non-drinker.

Hemodynamic parameters, comprising pulse rate, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), were measured using standard clinical protocols. Hypertension (HTN) was defined as an SBP ≧140 mmHg, a DBP ≧90 mmHg, or the current use of antihypertensive medications. Fasting venous blood samples were analyzed for a standard lipid panel, including high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C).

Carotid Ultrasonography and CAP Assessment

Carotid ultrasonography was performed bilaterally by trained sonographers using a standardized protocol evaluating the common carotid artery, carotid bulb, internal carotid artery, and external carotid artery. CAP was defined in accordance with the consensus standards established by the American Society of Echocardiography.15 For the purposes of this study, the primary outcome was the dichotomous presence of plaque; detailed plaque morphology (eg, number, size, composition, or stability) was not analyzed due to inconsistent availability in the retrospective dataset.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Between-group differences were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. The primary age strata were defined a priori as younger adults (<45 years) and middle-aged adults (≧45 years). Missingness was assessed for age, sex, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, LDL-C, HDL-C, BMI, and CAP before model construction; no participant had missing values for these variables, and all 9088 participants were retained in the analytic dataset.

Prior to multivariable modeling, multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and no significant collinearity was detected (all VIFs < 5). Multivariable logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association between current smoking and prevalent CAP. Model A included smoking status, the prespecified age-group indicator (<45 vs ≥45 years), continuous age, sex, BMI, hypertension (HTN), LDL-C, and HDL-C. Continuous age was retained to account for residual age-related variation within age strata, whereas the age-group indicator was used to evaluate age-related heterogeneity. Model B additionally incorporated an age-group-by-smoking interaction term to formally evaluate effect modification. Non-smokers and participants aged <45 years were used as reference categories. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and the interaction term was evaluated using the Wald test.

An additional sensitivity model further adjusted for drinking status. Because current smoking was exclusively observed among men in this cohort, sex-specific interaction estimates were considered unreliable and omitted. However, to ensure the robustness of the smoking effect, a sensitivity analysis was conducted exclusively within the male subgroup.

Two further sensitivity analyses were performed to assess robustness. First, multivariable logistic regression models were fitted separately among participants aged <45 years and ≥45 years. Second, age was modeled as a continuous variable centered at 45 years and scaled per 10-year increment, with a continuous age-by-smoking interaction term included. Finally, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis with four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of age (25, 35, 45, and 59 years) was used to characterize the non-linear association between continuous age and CAP and to support the clinical interpretability of the 45-year threshold. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3). A two-sided P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics and CAP Prevalence

A total of 9088 participants were included, comprising 5827 adults aged <45 years (64.12%) and 3261 adults aged ≧45 years (35.88%). CAP was detected in 1086 participants, corresponding to an overall prevalence of 11.95%. CAP prevalence was 3.38% among participants aged <45 years (197/5827) and 27.26% among participants aged ≧45 years (889/3261).

Current smokers exhibited a higher crude CAP prevalence than non-smokers overall 22.86% (203/888) vs 10.77% (883/8200). Within the <45-year stratum, CAP prevalence was 9.89% in current smokers and 2.84% in non-smokers; within the ≧45-year stratum, CAP prevalence was 35.89% in current smokers and 25.90% in non-smokers. Baseline characteristics are detailed in Table 1. Compared with participants aged <45 years, those aged ≧45 years had a higher prevalence of HTN and current smoking, alongside less favorable lipid profiles (all P < 0.05).

In this interaction model, current smoking was associated with higher odds of CAP among adults aged <45 years (OR = 2.104, 95% CI: 1.465–3.022; P < 0.001). The inverse interaction term indicated attenuation of the relative smoking-CAP association among adults aged ≥45 years. This pattern should be interpreted cautiously because crude CAP prevalence remained higher among older smokers than older non-smokers, whereas the fully adjusted relative association was weakened after accounting for age and other covariates.

Age-Stratified and Sensitivity Analyses of the Smoking-CAP Association

Sensitivity analyses supported the age-dependent attenuation. In age-stratified multivariable models, current smoking remained positively associated with CAP among participants aged <45 years (OR = 1.754, 95% CI: 1.179–2.610; P = 0.006), whereas the association was attenuated and not statistically significant among participants aged ≥45 years (OR = 0.792, 95% CI: 0.619–1.014; P = 0.064). When age was modeled continuously, each 10-year increase in age was associated with a lower relative smoking-CAP association (interaction OR = 0.547, 95% CI: 0.455–0.658; P < 0.001) (Table 3 and Figure 2). These findings indicate that the observed effect modification was not solely dependent on the 45-year age threshold.

Further adjustment for drinking status did not materially alter the categorical interaction result (interaction OR = 0.341, 95% CI: 0.223–0.522; P < 0.001). Because all current smokers in the analytic dataset were men, sex-by-smoking interaction estimates were not considered reliable and were therefore not reported. However, a male-only sensitivity analysis yielded a consistent pattern: the age-group-by-smoking interaction remained significant (interaction OR = 0.371, 95% CI: 0.238–0.578; P < 0.001), with a positive smoking-CAP association among men aged <45 years (OR = 1.742, 95% CI: 1.169–2.595; P = 0.006) but not among men aged ≥45 years (OR = 0.816, 95% CI: 0.637–1.046; P = 0.108).

Non-Linear Relationship Between Age and CAP

RCS analysis was employed to characterize the non-linear association between continuous age and CAP. The analysis confirmed a significant overall and non-linear association (both P < 0.001). As shown in Figure 3, the predicted odds of CAP increased gradually at younger ages and rose more steeply after approximately 45 years of age, supporting the use of 45 years as a clinically interpretable threshold for age-stratified analyses.

Discussion

The observed attenuation of the relative association in older adults should not be misconstrued as evidence that smoking becomes harmless with age. Instead, it may reflect an epidemiological shift in multifactorial disease. In older individuals, vascular aging, higher baseline plaque burden, and the cumulative impact of metabolic comorbidities such as hypertension and dyslipidemia may account for a larger proportion of prevalent CAP. Consequently, the independent relative contribution of current smoking may become less distinct after multivariable adjustment.

Additionally, survival bias (where highly susceptible smokers experience clinical events and leave the screening pool early), smoking cessation dynamics in later life, and the potential misclassification caused by grouping former smokers into the non-smoker category may further explain the weakened relative estimates in the older stratum.

Several lines of evidence support the biological plausibility of a more prominent relative association between smoking and CAP in younger, less extensively remodeled vessels. Endothelial dysfunction represents a pivotal early pathway. Tobacco smoking is linked to impaired vascular biology and endothelial function in humans,16 partly through suppression of endothelial nitric oxide signaling,17 a process that may improve after smoking cessation.6 In younger adults, who generally have a lower burden of established age-related atherosclerotic plaques, smoking-induced endothelial injury may be more visible as a relative difference in early atherogenesis. This interpretation is consistent with recent paradigms on premature atherosclerotic disease and plaque erosion, which emphasize endothelial injury, localized inflammation, and thrombosis in early-stage vascular events.18,19

Oxidative stress and systemic inflammation provide complementary mechanistic explanations. Tobacco exposure is associated with increased oxidative stress,20 platelet activation,21 elevated endothelin-1 expression,22 and higher pro-inflammatory cytokine levels.23 This microenvironment can promote leukocyte recruitment, LDL oxidation, and macrophage foam-cell formation, thereby accelerating early-stage plaque development.24,25 Broader experimental work in cardiometabolic biology also highlights the roles of oxidative stress, lipid dysregulation, and inflammatory pathways in vascular injury.26,27 Although these models do not directly test the present smoking-age-CAP association, they provide supportive mechanistic context.

Recent carotid imaging evidence is also compatible with this interpretation, linking current smoking to irregular plaque surfaces and calcification in men and to early plaque progression.28,29 Clinically, these findings do not warrant population-wide ultrasound screening based solely on smoking status, but they support careful vascular risk assessment and targeted smoking-cessation counseling in younger adults.

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal or temporal inferences. Second, smoking exposure was characterized strictly by current status; the lack of granular data on pack-years, duration, and cessation timelines prevented formal dose-response evaluation and led to the inclusion of former smokers in the reference group. Third, while alcohol consumption was controlled for, data on physical activity, socioeconomic status, and specific medication classes were unavailable. Fourth, the ultrasound outcome was limited to plaque presence, leaving potential smoking-related variations in plaque volume, vulnerability, or stenosis unexamined. Finally, because current smoking was observed only among men, sex-specific interaction analyses were not reliable; however, the male-only sensitivity analysis yielded a pattern consistent with the primary analysis. Future prospective longitudinal cohort studies with detailed lifetime smoking metrics are needed to clarify temporality and map plaque evolution across age strata.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study of non-diabetic adults, current smoking was associated with prevalent CAP primarily among adults aged <45 years, and the relative association weakened with advancing age. Sensitivity analyses using age-stratified models and a continuous age-by-smoking interaction supported this age-dependent pattern, and additional adjustment for drinking status did not materially change the interaction. These findings suggest that age may be considered when interpreting the association between current smoking and carotid atherosclerosis, particularly for vascular risk assessment in younger adults. However, the results should be interpreted cautiously because the study could not establish temporality and lacked data on former smoking, pack-years, smoking duration, and cessation time. Prospective studies with detailed smoking exposure data and longitudinal plaque phenotyping are needed to confirm causality and clinical implications.
