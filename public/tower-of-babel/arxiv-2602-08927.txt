Online monotone density estimation and log-optimal calibration
Rohan Hore, Ruodu Wang, Aaditya Ramdas

Abstract
We study the problem of online monotone density estimation, where density estimators must be constructed in a predictable manner from sequentially observed data. We propose two online estimators: an online analogue of the classical Grenander estimator, and an expert aggregation estimator inspired by exponential weighting methods from the online learning literature. In the well-specified stochastic setting, where the underlying density is monotone, we show that the expected cumulative log-likelihood gap between the online estimators and the true density admits an O ⁡ ( n 1 / 3 ) O(n^{1/3}) bound. We further establish a n ​ log ⁡ n \sqrt{n\log{n}} pathwise regret bound for the expert aggregation estimator relative to the best offline monotone estimator chosen in hindsight, under minimal regularity assumptions on the observed sequence. As an application of independent interest, we show that the problem of constructing log-optimal p-to-e calibrators for sequential hypothesis testing can be formulated as an online monotone density estimation problem. We adapt the proposed estimators to build empirically adaptive p-to-e calibrators and establish their optimality. Numerical experiments illustrate the theoretical results.



1
Introduction

In this work, we study the problem of online monotone density estimation. Let
{\mathcal{D}}
be the class of non-increasing densities supported on
[0,1]
, defined by

{\mathcal{D}}:=\biggl\{f:[0,1]\to[0,\infty):\int f(u)\,\mathrm{d}u=1,\;f(x)\leq f(y)\ \textnormal{for all}\ x\geq y\biggr\}.

(1.1)

Suppose
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots{\overset{iid}{\sim}\,}\mathbb{Q}
are observations drawn sequentially, where
\mathbb{Q}
admits a density
q=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\in{\mathcal{D}}
. Throughout this paper,
\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
. Our aim is to construct an
algorithm

\hat{f}
, by which we mean a predictable sequence of density estimators
\hat{f}=(\hat{f}_{t})_{t\geq 1}
. Formally, at each time
t\geq 2
, the algorithm outputs a density
\hat{f}_{t}
, a measurable function of the past observations
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t-1})
, that is,
\hat{f}_{t}\in\mathcal{F}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t-1})
, where
\mathcal{F}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t-1})
denotes the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t-1})
.

Restricting attention to densities supported on a compact interval is standard in monotone density estimation and is often essential for meaningful risk bounds; see, for example,
(
8
, Section 1)
. While in this paper, we assume densities are supported on
[0,1]
, any compact interval can be studied by suitable rescaling.

1.1
Risk and regret for online monotone density estimation

We measure the performance of an online algorithm
\hat{f}
through its sequential log-likelihood. In particular, for a predictable density
f\in{\mathcal{D}}
, we define
\ell_{t}(f):=-\log f(X_{t})
, the negative log-likelihood of the observation
X_{t}
, as the instantaneous loss at time
t
. Accordingly, the cumulative loss incurred by an online algorithm
\hat{f}
up to time
n
is then given by

\mathcal{L}(\hat{f},n)=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\ell_{t}(\hat{f}_{t})=-\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log\hat{f}_{t}(X_{t}).

For a static estimator
f
, we will write
\mathcal{L}(f,n)
to denote
-\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log f(X_{t})
. We study this loss under both stochastic and adversarial regimes, comparing the online algorithm with the population-level and hindsight-optimal benchmarks.

Stochastic well-specified setting.

When
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are iid observations from
\mathbb{Q}
, with density
q\in{\mathcal{D}}
, the true density
q
is Bayes-optimal under log-loss. This motivates the following notion of
excess KL-risk
,

\mathrm{Risk}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\hat{f};n)={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\bigl[\mathcal{L}(\hat{f},n)\bigr]-\min_{f\in{\mathcal{D}}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\bigl[\mathcal{L}(f,n)\bigr]={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\bigl[\mathcal{L}(\hat{f},n)\bigr]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\bigl[\mathcal{L}(q,n)\bigr].

(1.2)

For any online algorithm
\hat{f}
, since
\hat{f}_{s}
is
\mathcal{F}_{s-1}
-measurable, by tower property, we can express excess KL-risk equivalently as
\sum_{s=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\bigl[\mathrm{KL}(q\|\hat{f}_{s})\bigr]
.

Adversarial setting.

We also compare an online algorithm against the best monotone density that could be selected offline with full access to the data till a fixed time
n\geq 1
, in order to assess how well the online procedures track a hindsight-optimal benchmark. For

0\leq a\leq b\leq\infty
, let

{\mathcal{D}}_{a,b}:=\bigl\{f\in{\mathcal{D}}:a\leq f(u)\leq b\ \textnormal{for all }u\in[0,1]\bigr\}

denote the subclass of
{\mathcal{D}}
that are uniformly bounded below and above by
a
and
b
, respectively. A natural offline benchmark is given by the solution of the maximum likelihood problem constrained to
{\mathcal{D}}_{a,b}
,

\tilde{f}^{{\,\mathrm{MLE}}}_{n,a,b}\in\argmax_{f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{a,b}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f(X_{i}).

(1.3)

The unconstrained case
a=0
and
b=\infty
was originally introduced by
9
and is known as the Grenander density estimator in the literature; therefore, we refer to (
1.3
) as the
constrained Grenander estimator
.
Comparing an online algorithm
\hat{f}
to this benchmark leads to:

\mathrm{Regret}(\hat{f};n,a,b):=\mathcal{L}(\hat{f},n)-\min_{f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{a,b}}\mathcal{L}(f,n),

(1.4)

which provides a pathwise performance measure without imposing any distributional assumptions.

For theoretical analysis, we will often restrict attention to the bounded class
{\mathcal{D}}_{a,b}
. In practice, however, one may take
a=0
and
b=\infty
, recovering the classical Grenander estimator. Ideally, a well-designed online procedure should adapt automatically to the effective range of the density, without requiring prior knowledge of such bounds. This perspective applies equally to all online algorithms developed in this work.

1.2
The Grenander estimator and its properties

9
characterized the Grenander estimator as the left-continuous density corresponding to the least concave majorant of the empirical distribution function. Consequently, the Grenander estimator is simply a monotone histogram. Since this foundational work, the theoretical properties of the Grenander estimator have been studied extensively; see
6
for a comprehensive historical overview.

Early work by
18
and
4
characterized the asymptotic distribution of
\tilde{f}^{\mathrm{MLE}}_{n}(x)
in regions where the true density is locally strictly decreasing and locally flat, respectively. Later studies established sharp global convergence rates under various loss functions. In particular, convergence at rate
n^{-1/3}
in Hellinger distance was proved in
19
;
8
, while asymptotic normality of the
L_{1}
error was analyzed in
10
. Additional asymptotic and non-asymptotic properties of the Grenander estimator have been developed in
1
;
3
;
15
;
11
.

1.3
Our contributions

Online monotone density estimation.

Despite the extensive offline literature, monotone density estimation has not been systematically studied in the online setting. This work fills this gap.

•

We propose two online monotone density estimators: an online analogue of the classical Grenander estimator, and an expert aggregation estimator based on exponential weighting methods from online learning.

•

In the well-specified stochastic setting, where the true density
q
is monotone, we establish statistical guarantees for both estimators, showing that their excess KL-risk is of order
n^{1/3}
.

•

In the adversarial setting, we derive a pathwise regret bound of order
\sqrt{n\log n}
for the expert aggregation estimator, under mild regularity conditions on the observed sequence.

•

The pathwise regret bound suggests improved finite-sample adaptivity of the expert aggregation estimator relative to the online Grenander estimator in a mis-specified stochastic setting, a phenomenon that we confirm empirically through numerical simulations.

Application to sequential testing.

We develop a non-trivial application of online monotone density estimation to the
construction of sequential hypothesis tests.

•

While most classical tests are formulated in terms of p-values, e-values provide a flexible framework for sequential inference under optional stopping. We show that the problem of constructing optimal p-to-e calibrators reduces to online monotone density estimation.

•

We adapt the proposed online density estimators to develop empirically adaptive p-to-e calibrators, yielding valid sequential tests. If under the alternative, the p-values are iid with a monotone density, the resulting calibrators are asymptotically log-optimal.

Organization of the paper.

Section
2
introduces the proposed online monotone density estimators and establishes bounds on their excess KL-risk and regret. Section
3
presents an application to sequential testing, yielding valid procedures with provable optimality guarantees. Finally, Section
4
illustrates the
theoretical results through numerical experiments.

2
Online monotone density estimation

In this section, we introduce two online algorithms and then establish their theoretical properties.

Online Grenander algorithm.

Motivated by the classical Grenander estimator
\tilde{f}_{n}^{{\,\mathrm{MLE}}}
, a natural approach is to adapt the maximum likelihood formulation to the online setting. Specifically, for any
0\leq a\leq b\leq\infty
, the online Grenander (OG) algorithm
\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{OG}}_{a,b}=(\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{OG}}_{t,a,b})_{t\geq 1}
produces, at each time
t\geq 2
, the estimator

\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{OG}}_{t,a,b}\in\argmax_{f\in\mathcal{D}_{a,b}}\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\log f(X_{i}),\qquad\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{OG}}_{1,a,b}\equiv 1.

(2.1)

This algorithm is the direct online analogue of the constrained offline MLE
\tilde{f}_{n,a,b}^{{\,\mathrm{MLE}}}
. While statistically natural, it recomputes a full monotone MLE at each time step.

Expert aggregation algorithm.

To address these limitations, we consider an aggregation-based alternative, well studied in the online learning literature
(
5
;
14
)
. Fix any
0\leq a\leq b\leq\infty
and a finite collection of monotone density experts

\mathcal{E}_{m}=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{a,b}.

The expert aggregation (EA) algorithm
\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{EA}}_{a,b}=(\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{EA}}_{t,a,b})_{t\geq 1}
outputs, at each time
t\geq 1
, the estimator

\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{EA}}_{t,a,b}(u)=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{E}_{m}}w_{t}(f)\,f(u),\qquad w_{t}(f)=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{t-1}f(X_{i})}{\sum_{f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}_{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{t-1}f^{\prime}(X_{i})},\qquad u\in[0,1].

(2.2)

Thus, the EA algorithm produces a predictable sequence of estimators formed as convex combinations of experts, with weights favoring densities that assign higher likelihood to past observations.

2.1
Risk bounds for online monotone density estimation

We now bound the excess KL-risk, defined in (
1.2
), for both the OG algorithm (
2.1
) and the EA algorithm (
2.2
), which serves as a measure of the statistical accuracy of the proposed online procedures in estimating the true monotone density
q
. Throughout this section, we assume that

X_{1},X_{2},\ldots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{Q},\qquad q:=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\in{\mathcal{D}}.

To place our results in context, we first note down some relevant theoretical results of the Grenander estimator. If
q
is bounded above, then the Grenander estimator satisfies
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}(q,\tilde{f}^{{\,\mathrm{MLE}}}_{n})=O_{p}(n^{-1/3})

(
8
;
19
)
, where
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{H}}
denotes the Hellinger distance. When both
q
and the estimator are additionally bounded away from zero and infinity, this result implies a corresponding
n^{-2/3}
rate for the expected Kullback–Leibler divergence.
We state the precise implication for the KL risk formally below, for later use in our analysis.

Lemma 2.1
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose that there exist
0<a<b<\infty
such that
q\in\mathcal{D}_{a,b}
. Then, the constrained Grenander estimator
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{MLE}}_{n,a,b}\equiv\hat{f}^{\mathrm{MLE}}_{n,a,b}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
satisfies

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\bigl[\mathrm{KL}\bigl(q\|\hat{f}^{\mathrm{MLE}}_{n,a,b}\bigr)\bigr]\leq\kappa\,n^{-2/3}

for some constant
\kappa>0
depending only on
a
and
b
.

We are now ready to state the bounds on excess KL-risk for OG and the EA estimators.

Theorem 2.2

(Risk bounds for OG and EA algorithms)
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<a<b<\infty
. For any
q\in\mathcal{D}_{a,b}
and
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{Q}
, the OG algorithm satisfies

\mathrm{Risk}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{OG}}_{a,b};n)\leq\Gamma_{\mathrm{OG}}(a,b)\,n^{1/3},

and there exists a collection of monotone density experts
\mathcal{E}_{m_{n}}
with size
m_{n}<\infty
such that for all
q\in\mathcal{D}_{a,b}
, the corresponding EA algorithm satisfies

\mathrm{Risk}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{EA}}_{a,b};n)\leq\Gamma_{\mathrm{EA}}(a,b)\,n^{1/3},

where the constants
\Gamma_{\mathrm{OG}}(a,b)
and

\Gamma_{\mathrm{EA}}(a,b)
depend only on
a
and
b
.

Although both procedures attain the
n^{1/3}
rate for online monotone density estimation, their performance may be very different in finite samples. In particular, empirical results suggest that EA often achieves smaller regret in finite samples, reflecting better empirical adaptivity.

2.2
Bounding regret for the EA algorithm

In this subsection, we fix
0<a<b<\infty
and derive a pathwise regret bound for the EA algorithm under mild spacing and boundary conditions on the observed sequence
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
. The proof is based on a carefully chosen finite class of monotone histogram experts, that we define below.

We begin by introducing a binning of the unit interval. For
\beta>0
and
n\geq 1
, define the grid

\mathcal{B}_{n}:=\left\{0,\Delta_{b},2\Delta_{b},\ldots,\lfloor n^{(\beta+1)}\rfloor\Delta_{b}\right\},\qquad\Delta_{b}:=n^{-(\beta+1)}.

Building upon this grid, we construct a finite class of monotone histogram densities. For integers
k\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}
denote the collection of functions
g\in{\mathcal{D}}_{a,b}
of the form

g(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\theta_{j}\,{\mathbbm{1}}\left\{{t_{j-1}\leq x<t_{j}}\right\},

where the parameters satisfy the following:

(i)

the number of bins
r
satisfies
r\leq k
;

(ii)

the breakpoints
t_{1},\ldots,t_{r-1}
belong to the grid
\mathcal{B}_{n}
, and
t_{0}=0,t_{r}=1
;

(iii)

the heights are non-increasing,
\theta_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\theta_{r-1}
, with
\log\theta_{j}\in\Lambda_{n,a,b}
for
j=1,\ldots,r-1
, where
\Lambda_{n,a,b}:=\log(a)+\log(b/a)\,\mathcal{B}_{n}
;

(iv)

the final height
\theta_{r}
is defined as
\theta_{r}:=\frac{1-\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\theta_{j}(t_{j}-t_{j-1})}{1-t_{r-1}}
, and is required to satisfy that
\theta_{r}\in[a,b]
and
\theta_{r-1}\geq\theta_{r}
.

We show in the next section that, for fixed
(n,k,\beta)
, we derive a bound on the size of
\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}
.

Before stating the regret bound, we formalize the regularity conditions imposed on the data sequence. For
\beta,\gamma>0
and
n\geq 1
, we define the
good
set

\mathcal{S}_{n}(\beta,\gamma):=\Bigl\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}:\min_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|x_{i}-x_{j}|\geq n^{-\beta},\ \max_{1\leq i\leq n}x_{i}\leq 1-n^{-\gamma}\Bigr\}.

(2.3)

Elements of
\mathcal{S}_{n}(\beta,\gamma)
are well spaced, with minimum separation of order
n^{-\beta}
, and remain uniformly away from the boundary point
1
by a margin of order
n^{-\gamma}
. Now, we are ready to formally state our result.

Theorem 2.3
.

Fix
\beta>0
,
\gamma\in(0,\beta-\tfrac{1}{2})
. For
n\geq 1
, and for all
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n}(\beta,\gamma)
,
the EA algorithm
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{EA}}_{a,b}
based on the set of experts
\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}
with
k=\lfloor(n/\log{n})^{1/2}\rfloor
satisfies

\mathrm{Regret}(\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{EA}}_{a,b};n,a,b)\;\leq\;\Gamma(a,b,\beta)\,\sqrt{n\log n},

where
\Gamma(a,b,\beta)
is a constant depending only on
a
,
b
, and
\beta
.

Although the regularity conditions encoded in
\mathcal{S}_{n}(\beta,\gamma)
may appear restrictive, they are mild in the stochastic setting. In particular, when
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{Q}
with
q\in{\mathcal{D}}
, for any choices of
\beta>2
and any
\gamma>1
, the sample path
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
belongs to
\mathcal{S}_{n}(\beta,\gamma)
with high probability. We formalize these statements in Lemma
B.2
, given in Appendix
B
.
As an immediate consequence, we get the following high-probability regret bound under the iid alternative model.

Corollary 2.4
.

Suppose
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{Q}
with
q\in{\mathcal{D}}
. Consider the EA algorithm
\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{EA}}_{a,b}
based on the set of experts
\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}
with
k=\lfloor(n/\log{n})^{1/2}\rfloor
for any
\beta>2
. Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exists
N\equiv N(\delta,\beta)
such that for all
n>N
,

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\mathrm{Regret}(\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{EA}}_{a,b};n,a,b)\;\leq\;C\,\sqrt{n\log n}\right)\geq 1-\delta,

where
C
is a constant, only depending on
a,b,\beta
.

2.3
Proof sketch for the pathwise regret bound (Theorem
2.3
)

Here, we outline the main ideas underlying the proof of the pathwise regret bound, deferring the proofs of the technical lemmas to Appendix
A.2
. Throughout this section, we write
V:=\log(b/a)
. We also write
\mathrm{Hist}_{k,\downarrow}
to denote the class of monotone histograms with
k
bins, that is

\mathrm{Hist}_{k,\downarrow}:=\biggl\{h:x\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{k}\theta_{i}\,{\mathbbm{1}}\left\{{t_{i-1}\leq x<t_{i}}\right\}\mbox{ for some $t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{k}$ and $\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{k}$}\biggr\}\cap\mathcal{D}_{a,b}.

The proof proceeds by comparing the EA algorithm to a monotone histogram approximation of the constrained Grenander estimator. We summarize the key steps below.

Step 1: a monotone histogram approximation of
\tilde{f}^{{\,\mathrm{MLE}}}_{n,a,b}
with controlled height variation:

We begin by noting that, as in the unconstrained case, the constrained variant
\tilde{f}^{{\,\mathrm{MLE}}}_{n,a,b}
is also a monotone histogram. For the sake of completion, we state and prove this structural property in Lemma
B.1
.

However, there is no immediate control on the heights or breakpoints of
\tilde{f}^{{\,\mathrm{MLE}}}_{n,a,b}
. Therefore, next, we establish such a control. Specifically, we show that
\tilde{f}^{{\,\mathrm{MLE}}}_{n,a,b}
can be approximated by a monotone histogram with a
k
bins, while incurring only an additional cost of
\mathrm{O}(1/k)
. We also show that despite using a compressed representation with at most
k
bins, adjacent bin heights are well separated on the logarithmic scale and the induced distortion in
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,n)
is small. The following lemma formally states this.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\tilde{f}^{{\,\mathrm{MLE}}}_{n,a,b}
be the constrained Grenander estimator in
(
1.3
). For any integer
k\geq 1
, there exists a monotone histogram
h\in\bigcup_{r=1}^{k}\mathrm{Hist}_{r,\downarrow}
of the form

h(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\theta_{i}\,{\mathbbm{1}}\left\{{t_{i-1}\leq x<t_{i}}\right\},\qquad 0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{L}=1,\qquad L\leq k,

such that
t_{L-1}\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}
and, for every
j\in\{1,\ldots,L-1\}
,

\log\theta_{j}-\log\theta_{j+1}\geq\frac{V}{k}.

(2.4)

Moreover,
h
satisfies

\bigl|\mathcal{L}_{n}(\tilde{f}^{{\,\mathrm{MLE}}}_{n,a,b})-\mathcal{L}_{n}(h)\bigr|\leq 2n\,\frac{V}{k}.

(2.5)

We denote by
\tilde{f}^{{\,\mathrm{MLE}}}_{n,(k),a,b}
, the compressed histogram returned by Lemma
2.5
, with breakpoints
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{L}=1
and heights
\theta_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\theta_{L}
. This approximation serves as the offline benchmark against which the EA algorithm is compared in the remainder of the proof.

Step 2: Discretizing the compressed estimator into the expert class.

We now show that the compressed histogram
\tilde{f}^{{\,\mathrm{MLE}}}_{n,(k),a,b}
constructed in Step 1 can be closely approximated by an element of the expert class
\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}
, with only a small additional distortion in
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,n)
.

The key ingredient is the following lemma, which shows that any monotone histogram with well-separated log-heights can be rounded to the expert grid without significantly affecting
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,n)
. While this may seem to follow from a straightforward rounding of endpoints and heights, the proof requires careful arguments to preserve feasibility, monotonicity, and retained membership in
{\mathcal{D}}_{a,b}
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Fix integers
n\geq 1
and
k\geq 2
. Let
f\in\bigcup_{r=1}^{k}\mathrm{Hist}_{r,\downarrow}
be of the
form

f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\theta_{j}\,{\mathbbm{1}}\left\{{t_{j-1}\leq x<t_{j}}\right\},\qquad 0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{r}=1,\qquad r\leq k,

and suppose that
t_{r-1}\leq 1-n^{-\gamma}
and
\log\theta_{r-1}-\log\theta_{r}\geq V/k
. Then there exists a histogram
g\in\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}
whenever

\frac{V}{k}<1,\qquad V\Delta_{b}<1,\qquad a\frac{V}{k}\ \geq\ b\Delta_{b}V+b(\Delta_{b}V+2k\Delta_{b})n^{\gamma}.

(2.6)

Moreover, for any
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n}(\beta,\gamma)
, we have that
\bigl|\mathcal{L}(f,n)-\mathcal{L}(g,n)\bigr|\leq n\Delta_{b}V+(k-1)V
.

Here, we apply Lemma
2.6
with
f=\tilde{f}^{{\,\mathrm{MLE}}}_{n,(k),a,b}
. Note that by Lemma
2.5
and the assumption that
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{S}_{n}(\beta,\gamma)
, the final breakpoint satisfies

t_{L-1}\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq n}x_{i}\leq 1-n^{-\gamma}
,
and that the last two heights obey
\log\theta_{L-1}-\log\theta_{L}\geq\frac{V}{k}
.
Therefore, whenever (
2.6
) holds, there exists a histogram
g^{\star}_{n,k,\beta}\in\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}
and

\bigl|\mathcal{L}(\tilde{f}^{{\,\mathrm{MLE}}}_{n,(k),a,b},n)-\mathcal{L}(g^{\star}_{n,k,\beta},n)\bigr|\leq n\Delta_{b}V+(k-1)V.

Step 3: bounding the size of the expert class
\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}
.

We now derive an upper bound on the cardinality of the expert class

\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}
in terms of
n
,
k
, and
\beta
. By definition, the grid of admissible breakpoints satisfies

|\mathcal{B}_{n}|\leq n^{\beta+1}+2.

The discretization of the log-heights is defined as

\Lambda_{n,a,b}:=\log(a)+V\mathcal{B}_{n}
, so that
|\Lambda_{n,a,b}|=|\mathcal{B}_{n}|
.

We upper bound the size of
\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}
by ignoring the monotonicity constraint on the heights and the feasibility conditions imposed on the last height, since both can only reduce the number of admissible histograms. A histogram in
\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}
with at most
k
bins is specified by at most
k-1
internal breakpoints chosen from
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and at most
k-1
independent log-height values chosen from
\Lambda_{n,a,b}
. Hence,

|\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}|\leq|\mathcal{B}_{n}|^{k-1}\,|\Lambda_{n,a,b}|^{k-1}\;\lesssim\;\bigl(n^{\beta+1}\bigr)^{2k-2},

where the implicit constant is universal. Consequently,
\log|\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}|\;\lesssim\;k(\beta+1)\log n
.

Step 4: Aggregation over the finite expert class.

We now combine the previous steps and complete the proof of regret bound for EA algorithm
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{EA}}_{a,b}
defined over the finite class
\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}
. The key property we use is that exponential aggregation under log-loss performs nearly as well as the best expert in hindsight, up to a logarithmic penalty in the size of the class.

We state and prove this in Lemma
B.4
, by which we have that
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{EA}}_{a,b}
satisfies

\mathcal{L}(\hat{f}^{\mathrm{EA}}_{a,b},n)\;\leq\;\min_{g\in\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}}\mathcal{L}(g,n)\;+\;\log|\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}|.

(2.7)

From Step 3, we have
\log|\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}|\;\lesssim\;k(\beta+1)\log n.
Moreover, combining Steps 1 and 2, whenever the condition
(
2.6
) holds, there exists an expert

g^{\star}_{n,k,\beta}\in\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}
such that

\bigl|\mathcal{L}(\tilde{f}_{n,a,b}^{{\,\mathrm{MLE}}},n)-\mathcal{L}(g^{\star}_{n,k,\beta},n)\bigr|\;\leq\;2n\frac{V}{k}+nV\Delta_{b}+(k-1)V.

Now, we make the choice

,
and recall that

with

\sqrt{n\log n}
, and the size of

also satisfies

. Furthermore, the condition (

.
Substituting these bounds into (

uniformly over all sequences

This completes the proof of Theorem

3
Applying monotone density estimation to sequential testing

This section shows how online monotone density estimation can be used to construct sequential tests for a fixed null hypothesis
H_{0}
. We begin with a brief review
of sequential testing and the role of e-values as a core tool in developing these tests. We then introduce p-to-e calibration as a way to embed classical p-value based tests into the e-value framework, and show that learning
‘optimal’ calibrators naturally reduces to online monotone density estimation. Finally, we establish theoretical guarantees for the resulting calibrators.

3.1
Sequential testing: background

Sequential testing studies hypothesis testing with data observed sequentially, allowing decisions to be made at data-dependent times. Such problems arise naturally in modern machine learning and statistics, and have a long history in statistics
(
2
;
21
;
13
)
.

E-values have emerged as a convenient and flexible tool for constructing sequential tests in this setting
(
22
;
20
;
12
;
16
)
; see
17
for a comprehensive treatment. Formally, an e-value is a nonnegative random variable
E
satisfying
{\mathbb{E}}[E]\leq 1
under the null hypothesis. This simple expectation constraint underlies their use in tests that remain valid under data-dependent stopping rules.

In a sequential setting, suppose
(E_{t})_{t\geq 1}
is a sequence of e-values adapted to a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
, with
{\mathbb{E}}_{H_{0}}[E_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq 1
for all
t\geq 1
. An associated e-process
M=(M_{t})_{t\geq 1}
can be defined as

M_{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}E_{s},\qquad M_{0}=1.

(3.1)

The process
(M_{t})
is a nonnegative supermartingale under the null hypothesis. As a consequence, for any data-dependent stopping time
\tau
satisfying

\{\tau\leq t\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
, we have
{\mathbb{E}}_{H_{0}}[M_{\tau}]\leq 1
, and we also have
{\mathbb{P}}_{H_{0}}(\sup_{t\geq 1}M_{t}\geq\frac{1}{\alpha})\leq\alpha

for all
\alpha\in(0,1]
. Therefore, thresholding
(M_{t})
at
1/\alpha
yields a valid sequential test of
H_{0}
, regardless of the stopping rule. These properties form the basis for widespread use of e-values as building blocks for sequential inference.

On the other hand, most classical hypothesis testing procedures are formulated in terms of p-values and are designed for fixed-sample settings. While supported by a rich theory characterizing their validity and power in offline regimes, such tests generally fail to control Type I error under data-dependent stopping rules. To leverage this extensive body of work in sequential settings, it is therefore natural to seek principled ways to embed p-value based methods into the e-value framework.
p-to-e calibration
provides a simple and general mechanism for doing so.

3.2
Embedding classical tests via p-to-e calibration

Formally, a p-to-e calibrator is a function that maps p-values to e-values. That is, for any valid p-value
P
, the transformed quantity
h(P)
must be a valid e-value. Since this requirement must hold uniformly over all super-uniform p-values, it imposes a structural constraint on the calibrator: it must be dominated by a nonnegative and non-increasing function on
[0,1]
.

One defines a
p-to-e calibrator
as a non-increasing function
h:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
satisfying
\int_{0}^{1}h(p)\,\mathrm{d}p\leq 1
, equivalently, a monotone (sub-)probability Lebesgue-density function on
[0,1]
. By definition, if
P
is a valid p-value, then
h(P)
is a valid e-value.
20
further show that any admissible calibrator must attain the equality. Thus, we observe that

All admissible calibrators are decreasing densities supported on
[0,1]
.

We restrict our attention to the admissible calibrators, that is the class
{\mathcal{D}}
. This link between calibration and monotone densities has not been appreciated or exploited before. From this point onward, we refer to a p-to-e calibrator simply as a calibrator.

We now describe how calibrators integrate with the e-value framework when only p-values are observed. Suppose we observe a sequence
(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
of p-variables produced by classical hypothesis tests, satisfying under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
that

{\mathbb{P}}_{H_{0}}(P_{t}\leq\alpha\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\leq\alpha,\qquad\textnormal{for all }t\geq 1,\ \alpha\in(0,1).

Our goal is to convert these p-values into e-values in an online and adaptive
manner. At each time
t
, we select an
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-predictable calibrator

h_{t}\in{\mathcal{D}}
and transform
P_{t}
into an e-value
h_{t}(P_{t})
. The resulting
e-process is given by

M_{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}h_{s}(P_{s}),\qquad M_{0}=1.

(3.2)

By construction, this enables a valid sequential test for any such online calibrator.

3.3
Empirically adaptive p-to-e calibration

While any calibrator
h
yields a valid sequential test via (
3.2
), the power of the resulting procedure depends critically on how the calibrators are chosen. To formalize an optimal benchmark,
17
introduce the following oracle procedure.

Definition 1

(Log-optimal decreasing calibrator)
.

For a given alternative distribution
\mathbb{Q}
, the
\mathbb{Q}
-log-optimal calibrator

h^{\mathrm{opt}}=(h_{t}^{\mathrm{opt}})_{t\geq 1}
is defined by

h_{t}^{\mathrm{opt}}\in\argmax_{h\in{\mathcal{D}}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\log h(P_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right].

Since the alternative distribution
\mathbb{Q}
is unknown, the oracle calibrator
h_{t}^{\mathrm{opt}}
is not directly computable. This motivates the construction of empirically adaptive calibrators that approximate the oracle and achieve high power in practice.

Throughout the remainder of this section, we work under the alternative hypothesis, which posits that
P_{1},P_{2},\ldots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{Q}
with a monotone density
q\in{\mathcal{D}}
. In this case, the log-optimal calibrator is given by
h_{t}^{\mathrm{opt}}=q
for all
t\geq 1
. Indeed, for any
h\in{\mathcal{D}}
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\log h(P_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\log q(P_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]-\mathrm{KL}(q\|h)\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\log q(P_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right].

Consequently, we obtain the following interesting fact, which has not been previously recognized:

The log-optimal calibrator is the KL-projection of
q
onto monotone densities. Approximating the log-optimal calibrator from the observed
p
-values reduces to the problem of online monotone density estimation.

We therefore use the two online monotone density estimators introduced in Section
2
as empirically adaptive calibrators:

•

OG calibrator.

The estimator
\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{OG}}_{a,b}
yields a natural adaptive calibrator. Its online likelihood maximization can be interpreted as empirical log-optimal calibration, where the conditional distribution of the p-value
P_{t}
under
\mathbb{Q}
is replaced by the empirical distribution of past observations,
(t-1)^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\delta_{P_{s}}
.

•

EA calibrator.

Alternatively,
\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{EA}}_{a,b}
defines an adaptive calibrator by exponentially weighting a finite collection of expert calibrators, with
weights updated according to accumulated log-wealth from past
p
-values.

Both
\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{OG}}_{a,b}
and
\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{EA}}_{a,b}
yield valid
e
-processes via (
3.2
), and therefore induce valid sequential tests. In practice, a natural choice is to take
a>0
and
b=\infty
to ensure that calibrators never output zero while allowing unbounded values to enable strong evidence accumulation.

3.4
Optimality of empirically adaptive calibrators

We now study optimality properties of these empirically adaptive calibrators, establishing their effectiveness as practical procedures for sequential testing.
In particular, we show that both the OG and EA algorithms are asymptotically log-optimal calibrators.

Theorem 3.1

(Asymptotic log-optimality)
.

Suppose
P_{1},P_{2},\ldots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{Q}
, where
q\in{\mathcal{D}}_{a,b}
for some
0<a<b<\infty
. Then for any calibrator
\hat{h}\in\{\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{OG}}_{a,b},\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{EA}}_{a,b}\}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\Bigl(\log M_{n}(\hat{h})-\log M_{n}(h^{\mathrm{opt}})\Bigr)=0\qquad\mathbb{Q}\textnormal{-almost surely},

where
h^{\mathrm{opt}}
denotes the log-optimal calibrator from Definition
1
.

Here, by
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{EA}}_{a,b}
we refer to the EA calibrator based on the set of experts
\mathcal{E}_{k,n,\beta}
with
k=\lfloor(n/\log{n})^{1/2}\rfloor
. Theorem
3.1
shows that the proposed calibrators asymptotically achieve the same per-sample log-wealth as the optimal calibrator tailored to the true distribution
\mathbb{Q}
. As a consequence, for both procedures, the accumulated evidence diverges under any non-uniform monotone alternative.

Corollary 3.2

(Almost sure divergence of log-wealth)
.

Suppose
P_{1},P_{2},\ldots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{Q}
, where

q\in{\mathcal{D}}_{a,b}
for some
0<a<b<\infty
and
\int_{0}^{1}q(u)\log q(u)\,\mathrm{d}u>0
.
Then for any
\hat{h}\in\{\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{OG}}_{a,b},\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{EA}}_{a,b}\}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log M_{n}(\hat{h})=\int_{0}^{1}q(u)\log q(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\qquad\mathbb{Q}\textnormal{-almost surely}.

In particular,
\log M_{n}(\hat{h})\to\infty

\mathbb{Q}
-almost surely as
n\to\infty
.

We observe that the condition
\mathrm{KL}(q\|f_{U})=\int_{0}^{1}q(u)\log q(u)\,\mathrm{d}u>0
, with writing
f_{U}
to the density of
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
, simply requires
\mathbb{Q}\not\overset{d}{=}\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
. Under such alternatives, we do expect the log-wealth to diverge and the associated sequential test to have a finite detection time almost surely.

4
Simulations

Now, we study the empirical performance of the proposed online estimators through a series of numerical experiments.

Stochastic well-specified setting.

We first consider the well-specified stochastic regime, where
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{Q}
with a monotone density
q\in{\mathcal{D}}
. We examine three representative models: (1) a linear density
q(u)=5/4-u/2
, (2) a quadratic density
q(u)=3(1-u)^{2}
, and (3) a piecewise-constant density on four equal-width bins with heights
5/4,\ 13/12,\ 11/12,
and
3/4
.

For each model, we generate sample paths of length
n=1000
and track the cumulative log-likelihood
-\mathcal{L}(f,t)
. Figure
1
reports the average trajectories over
B=50
independent replications, comparing the OG and EA algorithms with two benchmarks: the true density
q
and the offline Grenander estimator
\tilde{f}_{n}^{{\,\mathrm{MLE}}}
computed in hindsight.

As expected, the offline Grenander estimator is optimistic and attains the largest cumulative likelihood. Both online methods closely track the true density, while the EA algorithm typically exhibits smaller regret relative to the offline Grenander than the OG algorithm, indicating better finite-sample adaptivity. This difference is most pronounced in the linear model, where the monotone structure is relatively mild; in the remaining settings, the two methods behave similarly.

Mis-specified setting.

We next consider a mis-specified scenario in which the data-generating density changes over time. Specifically,
n=1000
observations are drawn iid from a linear monotone density
q(u)=\delta_{1}-(\delta_{1}-\delta_{0})u
with
(\delta_{0},\delta_{1})=(0.5,1.5)
up to a change point
\tau=200
, after which the parameters switch to
(\delta_{0},\delta_{1})=(0.75,1.25)
. We compare cumulative log-likelihood trajectories of the OG and EA algorithms against the offline Grenander benchmark.

In this setting, the adaptive nature of EA becomes evident. By reweighting experts based on recent observations, EA adjusts more quickly to the new data-generating regime and incurs substantially smaller regret. In contrast, OG is more strongly influenced by pre-change observations and therefore adapts more slowly following the shift. This experiment illustrates the advantage of expert aggregation when the monotone or iid assumptions are only approximately satisfied.

Figure 1:
Cumulative log-likelihood trajectories in well-specified and mis-specified stochastic setting—both OG and EA closely track the trajectory of true density
q
, with EA exhibiting smaller finite-sample regret. In the mis-specified setting with a structural change (rightmost), EA adapts more rapidly to the evolving density, while OG lags due to its reliance on the full history.

5
Discussion

In this work, we introduced two online algorithms for monotone density estimation. In the well-specified stochastic setting, where the true density
q
is monotone, we derived bounds on the excess KL-risk of both procedures. We also obtained a pathwise regret bound for the EA algorithm, comparing its performance to that of the offline Grenander estimator computed in hindsight.

We further showed that the problem of p-to-e calibration in sequential testing can be naturally formulated as an instance of online monotone density estimation. This connection allowed us to adapt the proposed online algorithms as data-driven calibrators and to establish their optimality.

A promising direction for future work is to analyze the behavior of these empirically adaptive calibrators under non-iid alternatives, which would further enhance their applicability in practical sequential testing settings.

A.3.1
Proof of Theorem
3.1
: asymptotic log-optimality of calibrators

We start with recalling that, since
q\in{\mathcal{D}}
, the log-optimal decreasing calibrator satisfies

h_{t}^{\mathrm{opt}}=q\quad\text{for all}~~t\in\mathbb{N}.

Consequently, we have

\log M_{n}(h^{\mathrm{opt}})=\sum_{s=1}^{n}\log q(p_{s}),\qquad\log M_{n}(\hat{h})=\sum_{s=1}^{n}\log\hat{h}_{s}(p_{s}),

for any online calibrator
\hat{h}=(\hat{h}_{s})
.
Now, we write

\log M_{n}(\hat{h})-\log M_{n}(h^{\mathrm{opt}})=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log\frac{\hat{h}_{t}(P_{t})}{q(P_{t})}=:\sum_{t=1}^{n}\Delta_{t},

and define

a_{t}:={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\Delta_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\log\frac{\hat{h}_{t}(P_{t})}{q(P_{t})}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]=-\mathrm{KL}(q\|\hat{h}_{t}).

We decompose

\sum_{t=1}^{n}\Delta_{t}=S_{n}-A_{n},\qquad S_{n}:=\sum_{t=1}^{n}(\Delta_{t}-a_{t}),\qquad A_{n}:=\sum_{t=1}^{n}a_{t}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathrm{KL}(q\|\hat{h}_{t}).

By construction,
\{S_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is a martingale with respect to

\{{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\}
. Since
q,\hat{h}_{t}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{a,b}
by the theorem hypothesis, we have

|\Delta_{t}|\leq\log(b/a),\qquad|\Delta_{t}-a_{t}|\leq 2\log(b/a).

Therefore, by the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\bigl(|S_{n}|\geq n\varepsilon\bigr)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{8(\log(b/a))^{2}}\right).

Since
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-cn}<\infty
for any
c>0
, the first
Borel–Cantelli lemma implies

\frac{S_{n}}{n}\to 0\qquad\mathbb{Q}\text{-almost surely}.

It remains to show that
A_{n}/n\to 0
almost surely. By
Theorem
2.2
, for
\hat{h}\in\{\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{OG}}_{a,b},\hat{f}^{\,\mathrm{EA}}_{a,b}\}
, there exists

C=C(a,b)>0
such that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[A_{n}\right]\leq C\,n^{1/3}.

Consider the dyadic subsequence
n_{k}=2^{k}
. By Markov’s inequality, for any
\epsilon>0
,

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\frac{A_{n_{k}}}{n_{k}}\geq\epsilon\right)\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon}\,2^{-2k/3}.

Since
\sum_{k\geq 1}2^{-2k/3}<\infty
, the first Borel–Cantelli lemma yields

\frac{1}{2^{k}}A_{2^{k}}\to 0\qquad\mathbb{Q}\text{-almost surely}.

Finally, since
\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathrm{KL}(q\|\hat{h}_{t})
is non-decreasing in
n
,
for
2^{k}\leq n<2^{k+1}
,

\frac{A_{n}}{n}\leq 2\cdot\frac{A_{2^{k+1}}}{2^{k+1}}

which converges to zero almost surely. Combining the two parts proves

\frac{1}{n}\bigl(\log M_{n}(\hat{h})-\log M_{n}(h^{\mathrm{opt}})\bigr)\to 0\qquad\mathbb{Q}\text{-almost surely}.

\blacksquare

Lemma B.1

(Histogram form of the constrained Grenander)
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
0=x_{(0)}<x_{(1)}<\cdots<x_{(r_{n})}<x_{(r_{n}+1)}=1
denote the distinct order statistics of the sample path
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, where
r_{n}\leq n
. Further, fix
0<a\leq b<\infty
, and let
\tilde{f}^{\,\mathrm{MLE}}_{n,a,b}
denote the constrained Grenander estimator, defined in (
1.3
).

Then
\tilde{f}^{\,\mathrm{MLE}}_{n,a,b}
can be chosen to be a monotone histogram of the form

\tilde{f}^{\,\mathrm{MLE}}_{n,a,b}(u)=\theta_{j},\qquad u\in(x_{(j)},x_{(j+1)}],\quad j=0,\ldots,r_{n},

where

b\geq\theta_{0}\geq\theta_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\theta_{r_{n}}\geq a.

In particular, the knots of the MLE may be taken at the order statistics.

Proof.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{a,b}
be any candidate density, and define its
cumulative distribution function

F(x):=\int_{0}^{x}f(u)\,du.

Since
f
is non-increasing,
F
is concave and absolutely continuous on
[0,1]
, with

a\leq F^{\prime}(x)=f(x)\leq b\quad\text{a.e.}

Next, define a piecewise-linear function
\tilde{F}
by chord interpolation of
F
at the knots
\{x_{(j)}\}_{j=0}^{r_{n}+1}
:

\tilde{F}(u):=F(x_{(j)})+\theta_{j}\,(u-x_{(j)}),\qquad u\in[x_{(j)},x_{(j+1)}],

where we define

\theta_{j}:=\frac{F(x_{(j+1)})-F(x_{(j)})}{x_{(j+1)}-x_{(j)}}.

Since
F
is concave, the slopes must satisfy

\theta_{0}\geq\theta_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\theta_{r_{n}}.

Moreover, since

\theta_{j}=\frac{1}{x_{(j+1)}-x_{(j)}}\int_{x_{(j)}}^{x_{(j+1)}}f(u)\,du\in[a,b],

(B.1)

\tilde{F}
is a concave CDF whose derivative

\tilde{f}:=\tilde{F}^{\prime}
belongs to
{\mathcal{D}}_{a,b}
. At each distinct observation
x_{(j)}
, by (
B.1
),

\tilde{f}(x_{(j)})=\theta_{j-1}\geq f(x_{(j)}).

Hence, it follows that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f(x_{i})\;\leq\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\tilde{f}(x_{i}).

Applying this argument with

f=\tilde{f}^{\,\mathrm{MLE}}_{n,a,b}
shows that any maximizer of the likelihood
may be replaced by its chord-interpolated version without decreasing the likelihood. Therefore,
\tilde{f}^{\,\mathrm{MLE}}_{n,a,b}
can be chosen to be piecewise
constant with knots at the order statistics, as claimed.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}{\overset{iid}{\sim}\,}\mathbb{Q}
, where
\mathbb{Q}
is supported on
[0,1]
and has density
q\in{\mathcal{D}}_{a,b}
. Fix
\beta>2
and
\gamma>1
.
Then, there exists
N\equiv N(\delta,\beta,\gamma)
such that for all
n>N
,

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left(\min_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|X_{i}-X_{j}|\geq n^{-\beta},\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}\leq 1-n^{-\gamma}\right)\;\geq\;1-\delta.

Proof.

By a union bound,

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left(\min_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|X_{i}-X_{j}|\leq\tau\right)\leq\binom{n}{2}\,{\mathbb{P}}_{X^{\prime}_{1},X^{\prime}_{2}{\overset{iid}{\sim}\,}\mathbb{Q}}(|X^{\prime}_{1}-X^{\prime}_{2}|\leq\tau).

Since
\mathbb{Q}
has density bounded above by
b
,

{\mathbb{P}}_{X^{\prime}_{1},X^{\prime}_{2}{\overset{iid}{\sim}\,}\mathbb{Q}}(|X^{\prime}_{1}-X^{\prime}_{2}|\leq\tau\mid X_{1})\leq\frac{2bn^{2}}{2n^{\beta}}=\frac{b}{n^{\beta-2}}.

Next, we note that for any distribution
\mathbb{Q}
with density
q\in{\mathcal{D}}_{a,b}
,
Q
is stochastically dominated by
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
. Therefore,

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}\geq 1-n^{-\gamma}\right)\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]}\!\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}\geq 1-n^{-\gamma}\right)

Now, by a union bound,

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]}\!\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}\geq 1-n^{-\gamma}\right)\leq n^{-\gamma+1}

Combining both probability bounds by a union bound, the existence of a
N\equiv N(\delta,\beta,\gamma)
is immediate, such that for all
n>N
,

{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left(\min_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|X_{i}-X_{j}|\geq n^{-\beta},\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}\leq 1-n^{-\gamma}\right)\;\geq\;1-\delta.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma B.3
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{D}_{\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}}
, where
0<\gamma_{0}\leq\gamma_{1}<\infty
.
Then the following bounds hold:

1.

\mathrm{KL}(f\|g)\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}}\int_{0}^{1}(f(u)-g(u))^{2}\,du
.

2.

\mathrm{KL}(f\|g)\;\leq\;\frac{8\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{0}}\,\textnormal{d}_{\textnormal{H}}^{2}(f,g)

3.

\|\cdot\|_{2}
and
\textnormal{d}_{\textnormal{H}}
are equivalent on
\mathcal{D}_{\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}}
in the sense that

4\gamma_{0}\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(\sqrt{f(u)}-\sqrt{g(u)}\bigr)^{2}\,du\;\leq\;\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(f(u)-g(u)\bigr)^{2}\,du\;\leq\;4\gamma_{1}\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(\sqrt{f(u)}-\sqrt{g(u)}\bigr)^{2}\,du.

Proof.

We prove each part separately.

Proof of part 1.

We write

\mathrm{KL}(f\|g)=\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\log\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\log\Bigl(1+\frac{f(x)-g(x)}{g(x)}\Bigr)\,dx.

Since
f(x)\geq\gamma_{0}
, we have
\frac{f(x)-g(x)}{g(x)}>-1
, and hence
\log(1+u)\leq u
yields

\mathrm{KL}(f\|g)\leq\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\frac{f(x)-g(x)}{g(x)}\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(f(x)-g(x))^{2}}{g(x)}\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}(f(x)-g(x))\,dx.

The last integral is zero because
f
and
g
are in
\mathcal{D}
, so

\mathrm{KL}(f\|g)\leq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(f(x)-g(x))^{2}}{g(x)}\,dx\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}}\int_{0}^{1}(f(x)-g(x))^{2}\,dx,

which proves the first bound.

Proof of part 2.
For the second bound, note that

(f(x)-g(x))^{2}=(\sqrt{f(x)}-\sqrt{g(x)})^{2}(\sqrt{f(x)}+\sqrt{g(x)})^{2}.

Since
f(x),g(x)\leq\gamma_{1}
, we have
(\sqrt{f(x)}+\sqrt{g(x)})^{2}\leq 4\gamma_{1}
, and thus

\int_{0}^{1}(f(x)-g(x))^{2}\,dx\leq 4\gamma_{1}\int_{0}^{1}(\sqrt{f(x)}-\sqrt{g(x)})^{2}\,dx.

Combining this with the first bound and
\textnormal{d}_{\textnormal{H}}^{2}(f,g)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(\sqrt{f(x)}-\sqrt{g(x)})^{2}\,dx

gives

\mathrm{KL}(f\|g)\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}}\int_{0}^{1}(f(x)-g(x))^{2}\,dx\leq\frac{4\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{0}}\int_{0}^{1}(\sqrt{f(x)}-\sqrt{g(x)})^{2}\,dx=\frac{8\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{0}}\textnormal{d}_{\textnormal{H}}^{2}(f,g).

Proof of part 3.
Finally, using again
(f(x)-g(x))^{2}=(\sqrt{f(x)}-\sqrt{g(x)})^{2}(\sqrt{f(x)}+\sqrt{g(x)})^{2}
and the bounds

(\sqrt{f(x)}+\sqrt{g(x)})^{2}\leq 4\gamma_{1},\qquad(\sqrt{f(x)}+\sqrt{g(x)})^{2}\geq(\sqrt{f(x)})^{2}\geq\gamma_{0},

we obtain

\gamma_{0}\int_{0}^{1}(\sqrt{f(x)}-\sqrt{g(x)})^{2}\,dx\leq\int_{0}^{1}(f(x)-g(x))^{2}\,dx\leq 4\gamma_{1}\int_{0}^{1}(\sqrt{f(x)}-\sqrt{g(x)})^{2}\,dx,

which implies the displayed equivalence.
∎

Lemma B.4
.

\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{M}

\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log\hat{f}_{t}(x_{t})\;\geq\;\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log g_{j}(x_{t})\;+\;\log\pi_{j}.

\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log\hat{f}_{t}(x_{t})\;\geq\;\min_{1\leq j\leq M}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log g_{j}(x_{t})\;-\;\log M.
