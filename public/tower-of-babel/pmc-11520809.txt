MAKO robot-assisted total hip arthroplasty: a comprehensive meta-analysis of efficacy and safety outcomes
Authors: Rafael Llombart-Blanco, Gonzalo Mariscal, Carlos Barrios, Pablo Vera, Rafael Llombart-Ais
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntroduction: Robotic surgery in total hip arthroplasty (THA) has emerged as a promising approach for improving precision and reducing errors. This meta-analysis aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of robot-assisted MAKO total hip arthroplasty.MethodsStudies were searched using four databases. Meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager 5.4. Efficacy was assessed radiologically, and functional scores and complications were recorded.ResultsTwelve studies (1224 hips) were analyzed. The MAKO group achieved greater cup anteversion (MD 1.53, 95%CI 1.04–2.03) and a higher percentage of components within safe inclination and anteversion ranges (p > 0.05). Harris Hip Scores did not differ significantly (MD 0.61, 95%CI -0.22–1.45) but the forgotten joint scores favored MAKO (MD 5.99, 95% CI 4.10–7.88), although not exceeding the minimally clinically significant difference. No differences in intraoperative complications emerged (OR 0.96, 95%CI 0.51–1.79) but preoperative plans significantly mismatched the final cup placement after MAKO (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThe use of the MAKO robot in THA improves radiological outcomes by enhancing safe prosthesis placement. However, no significant differences were observed in terms of complications. Longer follow-up studies are required to assess the clinical impact of improved radiological results.Level of evidenceLevel IV metaanalysis of nonrandomized clinical trials.RegistrationCRD42023433733Keywords: MAKO, Robot-assisted, Total hip arthroplasty, THA, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a common surgical procedure used for various hip conditions. Achieving successful long-term results with THA relies on optimal implant positioning for procedural success [1]. The semi-active MAKO robot from Stryker Corporation in Kalamazoo, MI, USA utilizes computed tomography (CT) and 3D reconstruction to aid surgeons in achieving precise alignment and biomechanics [1]. Accurate hip prosthesis placement is crucial in preventing both short- and long-term complications. Surgeons often encounter difficulties in visualizing anatomy during surgery, potentially leading to errors in judgment [2].

Although the MAKO system shows promising short-term outcomes, these results have not been statistically analyzed through meta-analysis [3]. Studies have also presented conflicting findings when comparing robotic surgery with traditional methods and preoperative plans [4]. This discrepancy extends to functional assessments like the Harris Hip Score, which has displayed inconsistent reporting in the literature [5, 6]. Meta-analyzing complications, albeit infrequent, is crucial for enhancing sample size and determining potential discrepancies between the two techniques [5, 7]. Robotic surgery has shown improved accuracy and consistency in cup placement angles compared to conventional methods in some studies. This precision in cup positioning within safe zones reduces variability between surgeons. Common complications of traditional THA such as inaccurate component placement can lead to instability, while poor cup positioning outside the safe zones increases the risk of dislocation. Robotic systems can assist in achieving a safe cup orientation, potentially lowering dislocation rates. Furthermore, robotic assistance aids in more consistent leg length restoration, reducing discrepancies caused by uneven reaming or femoral component placement [8–10].

This study aimed to conduct a meta-analysis comparing the MAKO system with conventional techniques in THA regarding precision, functional outcomes, and complications. Analyzing one robotic system should enhance accuracy in evaluating its clinical impact. The choice to focus exclusively on the MAKO system was driven by its growing adoption and unique features, which allowed for a more directed analysis and reduced heterogeneity among studies, thereby precisely assessing its clinical impact on hip arthroplasty.

Materials and methods

Eligibility criteria

This meta-analysis was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023433733). This is because the data used in the study was previously collected by other researchers and made publicly available. This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines (Fig. 1) [11]. The research question was conducted following the PICOS strategy: P) patients were those undergoing THA using the MAKO robot; I) interventions were patients treated surgically with the MAKO robot1; C) comparisons were patients who underwent conventional surgery (including the freehand technique and fluoroscopic technique); O) outcomes were efficacy assessed by functional scores and radiological parameters, and safety evaluated by adverse events; and S) cohort studies and clinical series were included. Exclusion criteria were patients with severe systemic diseases affecting musculoskeletal function, robots other than the MAKO robot, significant clinical heterogeneity, duplicated or incomplete data, and nonshared variables.

PRISMA flowchart illustrating the process of study selection for the meta-analysis

Information sources

A systematic literature search was conducted using the PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and Cochrane Collaboration Library databases. No date limit was specified, and the language was not restricted. Studies of interest that appeared in the references of the studies included in the initial search were also evaluated.

Search methods for identification of studies

We used the following search terms to search all trial registers and databases: ((THA OR “total hip arthroplasty” OR “total hip replacement”) AND MAKO). Two reviewers independently agreed on the selection of eligible studies and reached a consensus on which studies to include.

Data extraction and data items

Two authors independently reviewed studies on data extraction. If a consensus was not reached, a third review author was consulted to complete the data extraction form. The baseline characteristics of each study were obtained, including study name, follow-up duration, study design, geographic region, control group, number of hips, number of women, age, percentage of osteoarthritis (OA), body mass index(BMI), and surgical approach. Radiological outcomes included cup inclination, cup anteversion, lower limb discrepancy (LLD), percentage of inclination, and anteversion in safe zones, according to Lewinnek et al. and Canallan et al. [12, 13] Clinical outcomes included blood loss, operative time, Harris Hip Score (HHS), Forgotten Joint Score (FJS), length of hospital stay, and adverse events assessed based on intraoperative complications. Additionally, discrepancies between the preoperative planning and postoperative cup inclination, cup anteversion, horizontal distance, and vertical distance were collected.

Assessment of Risk of Bias in included studies

The quality of the included studies was assessed independently by two authors using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) criteria (Table 1). It was established to assess the quality of the comparative and non-comparative studies. The maximum score was 24 for the comparative studies and 16 for the non-comparative studies. For non-comparative studies, scores of 0–4 corresponded to very low quality, 5–7 corresponded to low quality, 8–12 corresponded to fair quality, and ≥ 13 corresponded to high quality, respectively. For comparative studies, scores of 0–6 corresponded to very low quality, 7–10 corresponded to low quality,11–15 corresponded to fair quality, and ≥ 16 correspond to high quality [14].

Assessment of results

Meta-analysis was performed using the Review Manager 5.4 software package provided by the Cochrane Collaboration. For dichotomous variables, odds ratios (ORs) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated. The mean difference (MD) and 95% CI were calculated for the continuous variables. The standard mean difference (SMD) and 95% CI were calculated for continuous variables that did not share the same scale or unit. Heterogeneity was checked using both the chi2 and I2 tests. I2 varied from 0 to 100%, considering the values of 25, 50%, and 75% as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. A fixed-effects model was used in the absence of statistical heterogeneity, whereas a random-effects model was used when the I2 statistic was equal to or exceeded 50%, indicating significant heterogeneity. WebPlotDigitizer version 13.1.4 was used to obtain accurate information from the figures in the articles. Missing data were handled in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook guidelines [15].

Additional analyses

We assessed the possibility of publication bias by evaluating a funnel plot (ReviewManager5.4) of the studies to determine differences in asymmetry.

A sensitivity analysis was also carried out using Review Manager 5.4 eliminating the top-weight study from the comparisons of all outcomes.

Results

Study selection

The search across PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and Cochrane Collaboration Library yielded 57 citations. After reviewing the titles and abstracts, 30 studies were excluded because they did not meet the criteria. The full texts of the remaining 27 citations were evaluated in more detail, and it was determined that 19 studies did not meet the inclusion criteria. After reviewing the references of the eight included articles, four additional studies met the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 12 studies were included in this meta-analysis (Fig. 1) [4, 5, 7, 16–24].

Study characteristics

Table 2 presents the baseline characteristics of the included studies. Twelve studies and a pooled sample of 1224 hips were included. There were six retrospective cohort studies, one prospective cohort study, one case-control study, and four case series. Eight studies compared the MAKO technique with a control group, with the conventional technique being the most frequently used. The study included 634 women (51.8%). The mean age varied between 22.3 and 64.0 years. Most previous studies have focused on OA. The BMI ranged from 22.8 to 29.1. The most frequently used surgical approach was posterolateral, followed by the posterior approach. The quality of the studies assessed using the MINORS scale (Table 1) showed that the comparative studies were of high quality, while the series studies were of fair quality.

Outcomes

Regarding the comparative studies, cup inclination did not show significant differences (MD 0.78, 95% CI 0.11–1.45; participants = 606; studies = 5; I2 = 77%), (Fig. 2a). Cup anteversion was higher in the MAKO group (MD 1.53, 95% CI 1.04–2.03; participants = 532; studies = 5; I2 = 86%) (Fig. 2b). LLD did not show differences between the groups (MD -1.75, 95% CI -3.84–0.34; participants = 444; studies = 4; I2 = 96%) (Fig. 2c). The percentages of anteversion and inclination obtained in safe ranges according to Lewinnek and Callanan are shown in Table 3. On the other hand, clinical outcomes showed that blood loss did not show significant differences (MD 47.95, 95% CI -24.56–120.45; participants = 310; studies = 3; I2 = 78%). Surgery time was shorter in the control group (MD 14.63, 95% CI 3.62–25.65; participants = 606; studies = 5; I2 = 79%). Length of hospital stay did not show differences (SMD 0.27, 95% CI -0.74–1.28; participants, 182; studies, 2; I2 = 91%). Other clinical results related to quality of life and functionality, such as HHS, did not show significant differences (MD 0.61, 95% CI -0.22–1.45; participants = 310; studies = 3; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 3a). However, the FJS group showed significant differences in favor of the MAKO group (MD 5.99, 95% CI 4.10–7.88; participants = 198; studies = 2; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 3b), although not exceeding the minimally clinically significant difference. Finally, intraoperative complications did not show significant differences (OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.51–1.79; participants = 606; studies = 5; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 3c).

Discussion

This study study found no significant differences in cup inclination, length of hospital stay, blood loss, and complications between the MAKO and control groups. However, the MAKO group achieved higher cup anteversion. The functional outcomes showed controversy between the two analyzed scores. There was also a significant discrepancy between the planned and actual postoperative radiological outcomes in terms of cup inclination, anteversion, and vertical distance.

While the study found statistically significant differences in cup placement between MAKO robot-assisted THA and conventional techniques, the average clinical discrepancies were small at 0.78° for inclination and 1.53° for anteversion. Similarly, the average pre-to-postoperative differences with the MAKO robot were 0.71 °and 0.97 °for inclination and anteversion, respectively. However, it is unclear whether these small angular deviations have long-term clinical relevance. Although cup position in THA is correct, other factors that may influence results, such as the volume of surgeries performed, surgical approach, and obesity, should be considered [25, 26]. Some studies suggest that the MAKO system may have a greater impact on less experienced surgeons, although a definitive relationship has not yet been established [27]. Despite an increasing number of studies controlling the surgical approach, the number is still insufficient to recommend a specific approach that improves the results with MAKO [13]. One study showed positive results when MAKO was combined with the direct anterior approach [28], whereas another comparative study found no significant differences in approach [29]. It is also important to acknowledge the learning curve associated with robotic surgical systems. The results obtained in this meta-analysis may be due to a lack of experience in using MAKO, although it has been shown that learning with this robot is faster than with the traditional technique [30].

Most studies do not consider spinopelvic parameters despite their proven importance [31]. Robotic systems have the potential to improve the reproducibility of implant positioning in complex cases in which soft tissue balance or bone defects pose challenges. Planning and precision afforded by robotic assistance may allow for enhanced reconstruction of abnormal hip anatomies, substantially improving patient outcomes [10, 32]. In cases involving deformities, bone loss, or unusual pelvic orientation, robotic guidance could assist surgical decision-making by facilitating improved visualization of intraoperative conditions compared to reliance on manual techniques alone [32]. Although further research directly comparing different robotic platforms is still needed, computerized navigation shows promise for enhancing reliability and confidence when performing THA in more complex patients [30].

Although no differences were found in functional outcomes assessed by HHS, this is in line with other meta-analyses that conclude that long-term assessment is needed; these meta-analyses, however, included various types of robots [9, 10]. This study showed differences in favor of the MAKO group with respect to FJS, although these differences did not exceed the minimal clinically important difference [33]. These studies also reported differences in favor of robot-assisted prostheses in terms of intraoperative complications. In our study, we did not observe any differences when only the MAKO robot was analyzed. The radiological outcomes also improved. Among the radiological results, one of the most relevant is LLD, as it is an important factor to consider because of its legal implications [34]; however, we did not find significant differences.

It has been hypothesized that minimally invasive procedures using systems such as MAKO could potentially influence infection outcomes through factors such as longer operative duration or greater blood loss than open surgery. Specifically, surgery exceeding 180 min and blood loss of more than 800mL of blood have been associated with an increased risk of infection [35]. Among the studies included in this meta-analysis, no study exceeded either the time or blood limit. Similarly, the potential impact of additional staff in the operating room requires further investigation. However, it is expected that the intraoperative time will decrease as surgeons gain more experience with the system. Regarding radiation, which was not analyzed in this study, the use of CT would increase the risk, although it is compensated for by the lower use of intraoperative fluoroscopy. Few studies have analyzed the cost-effectiveness of using MAKO, although Maldonado et al. concluded that the use of robots in THA was cost-effective in most cases [36]. However, the MAKO hip arthroplasty system requires significant investments in infrastructure, equipment, and training. Several sources have supported this assertion. For instance, a study published on the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) website mentioned that the MAKO system is the most expensive among the compared robotic-assisted systems, with a retail price of approximately US$1 million [37]. It is understandable to consider the costs associated with the acquisition and implementation of a MAKO system. However, our literature review did not identify studies that specifically and quantitatively assessed the investment required to establish robotic surgical platforms.

One of the criticisms of the MAKO robot is its closed platform. Open-platform robotic systems (e.g., ROBODOC) [38] are compatible with different manufacturers and implant designs, giving surgeons the freedom to choose implants based on the patient’s body [39]. One advantage of robotic systems is comprehensive preoperative and intraoperative surgical planning [1]. This is interesting because not adhering to the preoperative plan could generate stress in the surgeon [40].

When comparing the MAKO robotic system with other previously studied alternatives, we observe that like ROBODOC, MAKO shows an increase in surgical time [9]. However, ROBODOC demonstrated a notable reduction in intraoperative complications with 0 events in 208 cases compared to 14 complications in 201 conventional arthroplasty interventions, most of which were randomized controlled trials [9]. Although the literature on it is scarce, an increase of almost 50 min in surgery duration compared to the conventional technique was noted with CASPAR system, with no significant differences in intraoperative complications [9]. According to Siebel et al., the functional outcomes of CASPAR are equal to or lower than those of conventional techniques [41]. A meta-analysis by Kunze et al., which included five studies on ROBODOC, found no significant differences in complications or surgical revision rates between robotic arthroplasty and conventional or navigated techniques, highlighting an increase in surgical time for ROBODOC compared to both methods [42]. In another meta-analysis that evaluated various types of robots, Han et al. observed that studies focused on ROBODOC also reported a lower frequency of thigh pain and better stem alignment, although there were no differences in leg length discrepancy [10]. Finally, a meta-analysis by Ruangsomboon et al., which primarily analyzed ROBODOC, concluded that there were no significant differences in the incidence of reoperations compared with conventional arthroplasty. They also noted improved stem alignment and less radiolucency without factors such as the type of stem used or the year of recruitment, significantly influencing the results [43].

Limitations

One limitation of this meta-analysis is that one study was treated as a series, as the comparison was not with traditional techniques. In addition, standard deviations were estimated in many cases, following the Cochrane recommendations. Some studies included a small sample size, and some effect sizes included very few studies, making a sensitivity analysis impossible. The overall result depended on the highest weighted study. Likewise, a deviation was noted between the exclusion criteria used in the manuscript and those initially registered in the protocol. Although additional exclusion criteria such as “significant clinical heterogeneity, duplicated or incomplete data, and non-shared variables” are standard practices in meta-analyses to ensure data quality and comparability, these were not explicitly specified in the registered protocol. These criteria were applied post hoc after the data extraction phase, allowing for the identification and exclusion of studies with non-comparable data or those presenting a high risk of bias. Moreover, since the comparison with most studies had only five or six studies, it was difficult to draw solid conclusions about what approach could be optimal as well as to assess how obesity or the etiology of hip pathology influenced the results. Nevertheless, visually, there seemed to be no difference. Additionally, some studies were published in Chinese, and text translators were used. However, we adhered to the Cochrane guidelines without language exclusion. Also, it would be valuable for future studies to report the minimal clinically important differences on functional outcome scales. The original studies included in this meta-analysis did not specify these minimal important thresholds for scales such as the Harris Hip Score or Forgotten Joint Score. However, one of the strengths of this study is that it was very specific to the system used, which further reduced heterogeneity in relation to the robot used.

Conclusions

In conclusion, although the postoperative results did not match the preparatory planning, the use of the MAKO robot in THA has been shown to improve radiological outcomes in terms of enhancing the safe placement of the prosthesis compared to conventional technique. However, no significant difference was found in terms of intraoperative or immediate postoperative complications compared to the conventional technique. It is important to consider that longer follow-up studies are needed to evaluate whether improved radiological results have a significant impact on patients. Future studies with longer follow-up periods will confirm whether better radiological results than conventional techniques have a long-term impact. In addition, a variety of factors, such as surgery volume, surgical approach, and obesity, must be considered when assessing the effectiveness of MAKO in THA.
