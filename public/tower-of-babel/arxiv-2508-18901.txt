Sparse minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance for feature selection
Peter Naylor, Benjamin Poignard, Héctor Climente-González, Makoto Yamada

Abstract
We propose a feature screening method that integrates both feature-feature and feature-target relationships. Inactive features are identified via a penalized minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance (mRMR) procedure, which is the continuous version of the classic mRMR penalized by a non-convex regularizer, and where the parameters estimated as zero coefficients represent the set of inactive features. We establish the conditions under which zero coefficients are correctly identified to guarantee accurate recovery of inactive features. We introduce a multi-stage procedure based on the knockoff filter enabling the penalized mRMR to discard inactive features while controlling the false discovery rate (FDR). Our method performs comparably to HSIC-LASSO but is more conservative in the number of selected features. It only requires setting an FDR threshold, rather than specifying the number of features to retain. The effectiveness of the method is illustrated through simulations and real-world datasets. The code to reproduce this work is available on the following GitHub: https://github.com/PeterJackNaylor/SmRMR . Keywords: FDR, Feature screening, mRMR, Redundancy, Sparsistency



1
Introduction

A much employed approach to solve high-dimensional problems is sparse modelling, which focuses on variable selection by discarding unimportant variables for prediction. It aims to recover the true signal when the underlying model admits a sparse representation by applying a penalization procedure to the objective function. A flourishing literature has been dedicated to the devise of suitable penalty functions that can best fit the observed patterns and that satisfy desirable properties: in particular, see
Fan and Li 2001
and
Fan and Peng 2004
for asymptotic results; see
Loh and Wainwright 2017
or
Poignard and Fermanian 2022
for a non-asymptotic analysis.

When the data is ultra high-dimensional, that is
p\gg n
with
p
the number of variables and
n
the sample size, sparsity-based methods may not perform well due to their computational cost, lack in both statistical accuracy and algorithmic stability. In light of these issues,
Fan and Lv 2008
developed the sure independent screening (SIS) approach. The latter is based on marginal Pearson correlation learning between the features and the target variable. However, the procedure relies on a linear model with Gaussian predictors and responses so that it may not be robust to model misspecification. This gave rise to a broad range of studies on model-free SIS methods: the Distance Covariance of
Székely et al. 2007
and
Székely and Rizzo 2009
; the Distance Correlation of
Li et al. 2012
; the
\sup
-HSIC of
Balasubramanian et al. 2013
; the Kolmogorov filter of
Mai and Zou 2013
and
Mai and Zou 2015
; the Projection Correlation of
Liu et al. 2022
; and the mixture Kendall’s Tau and Spearman Rho of
Poignard et al. 2022
, among others. As pointed out by
Mai and Zou 2015
, feature screening and sparsity-based selection methods differ in their objectives: on the one hand, variable selection attempts to recover the true sparse signal, which may require high-level conditions such as the incoherence assumption; whereas on the other hand, feature screening aims to discover a majority of inactive features and it is thus less ambitious and requires weaker assumptions.

Furthermore, the technique employed to discover the features truly associated with the target variable should result in a limited number of false discoveries, that is, the False Discovery Rate (FDR) should be low. The selected model should be as large as possible while controlling the FDR, that is the type S error introduced by
Gelman and Tuerlinckx 2000
. To satisfy this constraint in linear regression analysis,
Barber and Candès 2015
developed the knockoff filter, which creates copies of the original variables serving as control variables to guarantee the control of the FDR by the feature selection technique. In addition to upper bounding the directional FDR as well as the standard FDR, it allows to report the selected features for a given confidence level. This novel technique has been extended in
Barber and Candès 2019
to accommodate the
p\gg n
case and to deal with the model-free viewpoint, as in, e.g.,
Romano et al. 2020
. In particular, the pertinence of the method has been illustrated in the context of biological data:
Srinivasan et al. 2021
proposed a two-step compositional knockoff filter to control the FDR in microbiome data;
Tian et al. 2022
developed Grace-AKO for graph-constrained estimation and improved the feature selection stability of gene selection by repeating the knockoff procedure and aggregating information.

In this paper, we propose a two step approach for feature screening. After screening out the inactive features, the second step aims to upper bound the FDR at a pre-specified level. In contrast with the standard SIS procedure, which only considers the feature-target variable relationship, our approach refines the screening step by incorporating the feature-feature relationship. This procedure builds upon the minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR) of
Peng et al. 2005
and the HSIC-LASSO of
Yamada et al. 2014
to solve the redundancy issue. More precisely, we consider the following problem: given
n
observations of
p
features
X_{1},\ldots,X_{p}
, we screen out the features whose marginal utility with the target variable
Y
is not significant. To do so, we develop a novel screening procedure, called sparse mRMR (SmRMR hereafter), which extends the standard mRMR algorithm through the introduction of a non-convex penalization. Indeed, the SmRMR criterion can be recast as a penalized M-estimation problem, where the mRMR parameters and their zero entries correspond to the relaxation parameters of the original mRMR problem and to non-significant dependence, respectively. The optimal threshold selection for screening out the features with the SmRMR is performed via cross-validation, contrary to model-free screening procedures. In the second step, we apply the knockoff filter to the features selected in the first step, allowing us to report the selected features for a given confidence level. Our main contributions are as follows: we establish the existence of a consistent estimator for the SmRMR problem and show that the true zero elements of the mRMR criterion can be consistently estimated, ensuring the correct identification of the non-active features; conditional on the screening step, using the knockoff filter on the selected features, we verify that our SmRMR method controls the FDR for a given confidence level.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
details the framework and the SmRMR procedure. Section
3
is devoted to the theoretical properties of the SmRMR. Section
4
details the knockoff filter for SmRMR and FDR control. Section
5
illustrates the performances of the proposed method through simulations and real data experiments. All the proofs of the
main text and auxiliary results are relegated to the Appendix.

2
Framework

2.1
Feature screening

Standard feature screening methods consider a response variable
Y
and
p
features
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})
among which they aim to discover the inactive ones through a marginal utility
\text{D}(.,.)
between
Y
and
X_{k},1\leq k\leq p
. The viewpoint is the Sure Independence Screening (SIS). In the same vein as in
Li et al. 2012
, let
{\mathcal{S}}
be the set of active features and
{\mathcal{I}}
be the set of inactive features, respectively defined as

\begin{array}[]{llll}{\mathcal{S}}&:=&\big\{k:\,\text{F}(Y|X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})\;\text{functionally depends on}\;X_{k},1\leq k\leq d\big\},\\
{\mathcal{I}}&:=&\big\{k:\,\text{F}(Y|X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})\;\text{does not functionally depend on}\;X_{k},1\leq k\leq d\big\},\end{array}

where
\text{F}(Y|X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})
is the probability distribution of
Y|X_{1},\ldots,X_{p}
. The SIS approach aims to find a majority of
{\mathcal{I}}
, that is given the set of active features
\{X_{k},k\in{\mathcal{S}}\}
, it checks that the features
\{X_{k},k\in{\mathcal{I}}\}
are independent of
Y
. To estimate
{\mathcal{S}}
and screen out
\{X_{k},k\in{\mathcal{I}}\}
, SIS computes an estimator of the marginal utility
\text{D}(.,.)
, denoted by
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(.,.)
, which quantifies the dependence between
Y
and feature
X_{k}
. More formally, the estimator
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})
is deduced from the sample of observations
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
and
\mathbf{X}_{k}=(X_{1k},\ldots,X_{nk}),k=1,\ldots,p
, and the theoretical set
{\mathcal{S}}
is estimated by the set

\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\lambda_{n}}:=\big\{k:\;\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})\geq\lambda_{n},\;k=1,\ldots,p\big\},

(2.1)

where
\lambda_{n}\geq 0
is a threshold parameter that depends on the sample size and controls the number of selected active features. The calibration of
\lambda_{n}
is key to obtain the sure independence screening (SIS) property of the procedure, that is recovering with high probability the set
{\mathcal{S}}
. Here,
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(.,.)
is a V-statistic estimator, non-negative, of the marginal utility
\text{D}(.,.)
.

2.2
Minimal-redundancy Maximal-relevance (mRMR)

Aiming to the same objective of finding a set
S
of size
s
of active features, an alternative approach is the maximum relevance (MR) feature selection developed by
Peng et al. 2005
. Let
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})
be the V-statistic estimator of
\text{D}(.,.)
, then MR aims to maximize the relevance
(\text{card}(S))^{-1}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{S}}}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})
.
In their seminal work,
Peng et al. 2005
use the Mutual Information as the marginal utility. One drawback of the MR method is the tendency to select redundant features, that is the selected features can be highly correlated since it does not use the feature-feature relationship. This is a drawback also shared by the SIS approach. Redundant features refers to the situation where adding highly correlated variables does not improve information. Since no feature-feature relationship is considered in both the SIS and MR approaches, they are unable to discover which combination of features would result in the best performance: see
Peng et al. 2005
or
Guyon and Elisseeff 2003
for further details on the redundancy issue.

To overcome the redundancy problem,
Peng et al. 2005
devised the mRMR feature selection algorithm, which estimates the set of active features as

\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax\,}}_{{\mathcal{S}}}\;\frac{1}{s}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{S}}}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})\!-\!\frac{1}{s^{2}}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{S}}}\sum_{l\in{\mathcal{S}}}\!\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{X}_{l}).

(2.2)

The second term in (
2.2
) can be viewed as a “penalization” term that selects independent features: this is the redundancy term. Since
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{X}_{l})
takes non-negative values when
X_{k}
and
X_{l}
are non-independent, the second term takes large negative values if the selected features are not mutually independent. Thus, by selecting features through the maximization of equation (
2.2
), we can select features that are dependent on the output, and the selected features are mutually independent. This criterion selects the features with both minimum redundancy, approximated as the mean value of the association measure between each pair of variables in the subset, and maximum relevance, estimated as the mean value of the association measure between each feature and the target class.

2.3
Penalized mRMR

The original mRMR algorithm consists of a discrete optimization problem, and the optimization is in general difficult so that continuous optimization tends to be used to mitigate the problem. The mRMR algorithm can be recast as

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\underset{\mathbf{\beta}\in\{0,1\}^{p}}{\mathop{\mathrm{argmin\,}}}\;\;-\frac{1}{\mathbf{\beta}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{1}}_{p}}\sum_{k=1}^{p}\beta_{k}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})+\frac{1}{(\mathbf{\beta}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{1}}_{p})^{2}}\sum_{k,l=1}^{p}\beta_{k}\beta_{l}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{X}_{l}).

We then relax
\mathbf{\beta}
as
\mathbf{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots,\theta_{p})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
so that the problem becomes

\widetilde{\mathbf{\theta}}=\underset{\mathbf{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}_{+}}{\mathop{\mathrm{argmin\,}}}\;-\sum_{k=1}^{p}\theta_{k}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,l=1}^{p}\theta_{k}\theta_{l}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{X}_{l}).

The problem is equivalent to minimizing, up to some constant terms, the loss function

{\mathbb{L}}_{v,n}(\mathbf{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y})-\overset{p}{\underset{k=1}{\sum}}\theta_{k}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})+\frac{1}{2}\overset{p}{\underset{k,l=1}{\sum}}\theta_{k}\theta_{l}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{X}_{l}).

(2.3)

To perform feature screening while taking into account the feature-feature relationship, we apply a penalty term to
{\mathbb{L}}_{v,n}(\theta)
in the minimization problem so that the problem becomes

\widehat{\mathbf{\theta}}\in\underset{\mathbf{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}_{+}}{\mathop{\mathrm{argmin\,}}}\;n\,{\mathbb{L}}_{v,n}(\mathbf{\theta})+n\overset{p}{\underset{k=1}{\sum}}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},\theta_{k}),

(2.4)

when such a minimizer exists, where
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},x),x\geq 0
is a penalty function that fosters sparsity among
\mathbf{\theta}
with regularization parameter
\lambda_{n}\geq 0
. Problem (
2.4
) is our proposed SmRMR procedure. In this paper, we consider the LASSO penalty of
Tibshirani 1996
, defined as
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda,x)=\lambda x
for
x\geq 0
, and the non-convex penalty functions SCAD and MCP. The SCAD penalty of
Fan and Li 2001
is defined as: for every
x\geq 0
,

\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda,x)=\lambda x\,\mathbf{1}\big(x\leq\lambda\big)+\frac{2a_{\text{scad}}\lambda x-x^{2}-\lambda^{2}}{2(a_{\text{scad}}-1)}\,\mathbf{1}\big(\lambda<x\leq a_{\text{scad}}\lambda\big)+\frac{1}{2}(a_{\text{scad}}+1)\lambda^{2}\,\mathbf{1}\big(x>a_{\text{scad}}\lambda\big),

where
a_{\text{scad}}>2
and whose derivative is
\partial_{x}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda,x)=\lambda{\boldsymbol{1}}_{(x\leq\lambda)}+\frac{(a_{\text{scad}}\lambda-x)^{+}}{(a_{\text{scad}}-1)}
.
The MCP due to
Zhang 2010
is defined for
b_{\text{mcp}}>0
as: for every
x\geq 0
,

\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda,x)=\big(\lambda x-\frac{x^{2}}{2b_{\text{mcp}}}\big){\boldsymbol{1}}\big(x\leq b_{\text{mcp}}\lambda\big)+\lambda^{2}\frac{b_{\text{mcp}}}{2}{\boldsymbol{1}}\big(x>b_{\text{mcp}}\lambda\big),

and whose derivative is
\partial_{x}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda,x)=(\lambda-\frac{x}{b_{\text{mcp}}})^{+}
.
Note that when
a_{\text{scad}}\rightarrow\infty
and
b_{\text{mcp}}\rightarrow\infty
, then the SCAD and MCP become the LASSO. In light of (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), we observe the following properties: a strong dependence between
Y
and the
k
-th feature
X_{k}
implies a large positive value for
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})
, so that
\theta_{k}
should be estimated as a large positive value in (
2.4
). On the contrary, a weak relationship should translate into small values of
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})
, so that the corresponding parameter
\theta_{k}
should be shrunk to zero by the penalty function. Finally, when the
k
-th and
l
-th features
X_{k},X_{l}
are strongly correlated, then
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{X}_{l})
takes a large positive value, so that
\theta_{k}
or
\theta_{l}
should be shrunk to zero in (
2.4
) for minimization. Problem (
2.4
) is actually a modification of the standard feature screening method (
2.1
). Consider the first order condition (righ-derivative) of problem (
2.4
) with the LASSO penalty. We get

\forall 1\leq k\leq p,\;-n\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})+n\sum_{l=1}^{p}\widehat{\theta}_{l}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{X}_{l})+n\partial_{\theta_{k}}\big(\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},\theta_{k})\big)=0,

where
\partial_{\theta_{k}}\big(\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},|\theta_{k}|)\big)=\lambda_{n}\widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{k}
, with
\widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{k}
the subdifferential defined as

\widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{k}=\text{sgn}(\widehat{\theta}_{k})=1\;\;\text{when}\;\;\widehat{\theta}_{k}>0,\;\;\text{and}\;\;\widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{k}\in\big\{\widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{k}:\widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{k}\leq 1\big\}\;\;\text{when}\;\;\widehat{\theta}_{k}=0,

under the non-negative constraint. It can be noted that the subdifferential of the LASSO under the non-negative constraint is similar to the standard LASSO. Now consider the case
\widehat{\theta}_{k}>0,1\leq k\leq p
. The orthogonality condition becomes
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})=\sum_{l=1}^{p}\widehat{\theta}_{l}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{X}_{l})+\lambda_{n}
.
Under the non-negative constraint on the parameters and since the V-statistic estimator of
\text{D}(.,.)
is non-negative,
\sum_{l=1}^{p}\widehat{\theta}_{l}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{X}_{l})\geq 0
so that
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})\geq\lambda_{n}
by the orthogonality condition. Now, when
\widehat{\theta}_{k}=0,1\leq k\leq p
, then

\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})=\sum_{l\neq k}^{p}\widehat{\theta}_{l}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{X}_{l})+\lambda_{n}\widehat{\mathbf{z}}_{k},\;\text{so that}\;\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})\leq\sum_{l\neq k}^{p}\widehat{\theta}_{l}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{X}_{l})+\lambda_{n}.

Hence, the sparse estimation of
\mathbf{\theta}
under the non-negative constraint allows to perform feature screening in the same vein as in the standard procedure for feature screening. More precisely, a positive estimated parameter
\widehat{\theta}_{k}
implies
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})\geq\lambda_{n}
: from this inequality, the standard feature screening procedure identifies
k
as an important feature for
Y
in light of the set (
2.1
). Now, when the
k
-th component of
\mathbf{\theta}
is shrunk to zero by the penalty function, then
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})\leq\sum_{l\neq k}^{p}\widehat{\theta}_{l}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{X}_{l})+\lambda_{n}
. Standard feature screening screens out an unimportant feature when
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})<\lambda_{n}
. Thus, our proposed methodology refines the standard approach by incorporating the feature-feature relationship in the decision to select/exclude the feature.

In the SCAD case, when
0<\widehat{\theta}_{k}\leq\lambda_{n}
, the problem is similar to the LASSO shrinkage. If
\widehat{\theta}_{k}>\lambda_{n}\neq 0,1\leq k\leq p
(coefficient sufficiently large), the first order condition becomes
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})=\sum_{l=1}^{p}\widehat{\theta}_{l}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{X}_{l})+\frac{(a_{\text{scad}}\lambda_{n}-\widehat{\theta}_{k})^{+}}{(a_{\text{scad}}-1)}
.
When
\lambda_{n}<\widehat{\theta}_{k}<a_{\text{scad}}\lambda_{n}
, then the selection rule is
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})\geq\frac{a_{\text{scad}}\lambda_{n}-\widehat{\theta}_{k}}{(a_{\text{scad}}-1)}\geq\lambda_{n}-\frac{\widehat{\theta}_{k}}{a_{\text{scad}}-1}\cdot
When
a_{\text{scad}}\rightarrow\infty
, then the selection rule is the same as in the LASSO case, since the SCAD penalty tends to the LASSO penalty. In the same manner, when the penalty is the MCP, when
0<\widehat{\theta}_{k}<b_{\text{mcp}}\lambda_{n}
, then the selection rule is

2.4
Association measures

Solving problem (
2.4
) requires the specification of an association measure
\text{D}(.,.)
together with its estimator
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(.,.)
to characterize the dependence between two random variables, say
X
and
Y
. In this paper, we will consider the following two commonly used measures: the squared Projection Correlation (
\text{PC}^{2}
); the squared Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the cross-covariance operator (
HSIC
), which includes the Distance Covariance (
Dcov
) for a suitable kernel. These measures share common properties when measuring the dependence: both
HSIC
and
PC
are non-negative and equal zero if and only if the two arguments are independent. Hereafter, the two random variables
X,Y
take values on
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{B}}_{x})
and
({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{B}}_{y})
, respectively, where
{\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}}
are two separable metric spaces,
{\mathcal{B}}_{x},{\mathcal{B}}_{y}
are Borel
\sigma
-algebras. Then,
({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{B}}_{x}\times{\mathcal{B}}_{y})
is measurable, and the joint distribution is defined as
{\mathbb{P}}_{X\,Y}
, which assigns values to the product space
({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{B}}_{x}\times{\mathcal{B}}_{y})
.

2.4.1
Projection Correlation (
PC
)

The Projection Correlation of
X
and
Y
of
Zhu et al. 2017
, denoted by
\text{PC}(X,Y)
, is defined as the square root of
\text{PC}(X,Y)^{2}=\frac{\text{Pcov}(X,Y)^{2}}{\text{Pcov}(X,X)\text{Pcov}(Y,Y)}
,
where the squared Projection Covariance
\text{Pcov}(X,Y)^{2}
is:

\displaystyle\text{Pcov}(X,Y)^{2}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\big[\text{arccos}\big(\frac{(X_{1}-X_{3})^{\top}(X_{4}-X_{3})}{\|X_{1}-X_{3}\|\|X_{4}-X_{3}\|}\big)\text{arccos}\big(\frac{(Y_{1}-Y_{3})^{\top}(Y_{4}-Y_{3})}{\|Y_{1}-Y_{3}\|\|Y_{4}-Y_{3}\|}\big)\big]

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}\big[\text{arccos}\big(\frac{(X_{1}-X_{3})^{\top}(X_{4}-X_{3})}{\|X_{1}-X_{3}\|\|X_{4}-X_{3}\|}\big)\text{arccos}\big(\frac{(Y_{2}-Y_{3})^{\top}(Y_{5}-Y_{3})}{\|Y_{2}-Y_{3}\|\|Y_{5}-Y_{3}\|}\big)\big]

\displaystyle-2\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[\text{arccos}\big(\frac{(X_{1}-X_{3})^{\top}(X_{4}-X_{3})}{\|X_{1}-X_{3}\|\|X_{4}-X_{3}\|}\big)\text{arccos}\big(\frac{(Y_{2}-Y_{3})^{\top}(Y_{4}-Y_{3})}{\|Y_{2}-Y_{3}\|\|Y_{4}-Y_{3}\|}\big)\big]

\displaystyle=:S_{1}+S_{2}-2S_{3},

with
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{5},Y_{5})
independent copies of
(X,Y)
. By construction,
\text{Pcov}(X,Y)^{2}
does not require moment conditions on
X,Y
as it relies on vectors
(X_{k}-X_{l})/\|X_{k}-X_{l}\|
and
(Y_{k}-Y_{l})/\|Y_{k}-Y_{l}\|
. By proposition 1 of
Zhu et al. 2017
,
0\leq\text{PC}(X,Y)\leq 1
in general, and
\text{PC}(X,Y)=0
if and only if
X
and
Y
are independent. Equipped with the sample of observations
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
and
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
from the population
(X,Y)
,
\text{Pcov}(X,Y)^{2}
can be estimated by the V-statistic
\widehat{\text{Pcov}}_{v}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})^{2}
defined as
\widehat{\text{Pcov}}_{v}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})^{2}=\widehat{S}_{1,v}+\widehat{S}_{2,v}-2\widehat{S}_{3,v}
,
where

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{1,v}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\overset{n}{\underset{i,j,l=1}{\sum}}\text{arccos}\big(\frac{(X_{i}-X_{j})^{\top}(X_{l}-X_{j})}{\|X_{i}-X_{j}\|\|X_{l}-X_{j}\|}\big)\text{arccos}\big(\frac{(Y_{i}-Y_{j})^{\top}(Y_{l}-Y_{j})}{\|Y_{i}-Y_{j}\|\|Y_{l}-Y_{j}\|}\big),

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{2,v}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{5}}\overset{n}{\underset{i,j,l,m,r=1}{\sum}}\text{arccos}\big(\frac{(X_{i}-X_{l})^{\top}(X_{m}-X_{l})}{\|X_{i}-X_{l}\|\|X_{m}-X_{l}\|}\big)\text{arccos}\big(\frac{(Y_{j}-Y_{l})^{\top}(Y_{r}-Y_{l})}{\|Y_{j}-Y_{l}\|\|Y_{r}-Y_{l}\|}\big),

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{3,v}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{4}}\overset{n}{\underset{i,j,l,m=1}{\sum}}\text{arccos}\big(\frac{(X_{i}-X_{l})^{\top}(X_{m}-X_{l})}{\|X_{i}-X_{l}\|\|X_{m}-X_{l}\|}\big)\text{arccos}\big(\frac{(Y_{j}-Y_{l})^{\top}(Y_{m}-Y_{l})}{\|Y_{j}-Y_{l}\|\|Y_{m}-Y_{l}\|}\big).

By Theorem 1 of
Zhu et al. 2017
, the estimator
\widehat{\text{Pcov}}_{v}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})^{2}
of
\text{Pcov}(X,Y)^{2}
can be written as

\widehat{\text{Pcov}}_{v}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})^{2}=\frac{1}{n^{3}}\overset{n}{\underset{i,l,r=1}{\sum}}[{\mathcal{K}}_{X}]_{ilr}[{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}]_{ilr},

(2.5)

with
[{\mathcal{K}}_{X}]_{ilr}=K_{ilr}-\overline{K}_{i.r}-\overline{K}_{.lr}+\overline{K}_{..r}
,
[{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}]_{ilr}=L_{ilr}-\overline{L}_{i.r}-\overline{L}_{.lr}+\overline{L}_{..r}
, where

\displaystyle K_{ilr}

\displaystyle=\text{arccos}\big(\frac{(X_{i}-X_{r})^{\top}(X_{l}-X_{r})}{\|X_{i}-X_{r}\|\|X_{l}-X_{r}\|}\big),\overline{K}_{i.r}=\frac{1}{n}\overset{n}{\underset{l=1}{\sum}}K_{ilr},\overline{K}_{.lr}=\frac{1}{n}\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}K_{ilr},\overline{K}_{..r}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\overset{n}{\underset{i,l=1}{\sum}}K_{ilr},

\displaystyle L_{ilr}

\displaystyle=\text{arccos}\big(\frac{(Y_{i}-Y_{r})^{\top}(Y_{l}-Y_{r})}{\|Y_{i}-Y_{r}\|\|Y_{l}-Y_{r}\|}\big),\overline{L}_{i.r}=\frac{1}{n}\overset{n}{\underset{l=1}{\sum}}L_{ilr},\overline{L}_{.lr}=\frac{1}{n}\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}L_{ilr},\overline{L}_{..r}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\overset{n}{\underset{i,l=1}{\sum}}L_{ilr},

with
K_{ilr}=L_{ilr}=0
if
i=r
or
l=r
.
The V-statistic estimator of the squared projection correlation is
\widehat{\text{PC}}_{v}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})^{2}=\widehat{\text{Pcov}}_{v}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})^{2}/(\widehat{\text{Pcov}}_{v}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})\widehat{\text{Pcov}}_{v}(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y}))
.
In this paper, we will implement the estimator defined in (
2.5
) when using the (squared) projection correlation measure.

2.4.2
Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC)

HSIC
is a cross-covariance operator in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS). It has fostered a broad range of studies dedicated to feature selection: see
Gretton et al. 2005a
,
Song et al. 2012
for a theoretical analysis of
HSIC
. Let us first fix the setup to define this measure. Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a metric space and
{\mathcal{H}}
a Hilbert space of functions
f:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
.
{\mathcal{H}}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) induced by the inner product
\langle.,.\rangle
if there exists a function
k:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\forall\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in{\mathcal{X}},\;k(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},.)\in{\mathcal{H}}
, and
\forall f\in{\mathcal{H}},\forall\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in{\mathcal{X}},\langle f,k(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},.)\rangle=f(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
. For any probability measure
{\mathbb{P}}
defined on
{\mathcal{X}}
, the mean
\mu({\mathbb{P}})\in{\mathcal{H}}
is defined as
{\mathbb{E}}[f(X)]=\langle f,\mu({\mathbb{P}})\rangle
for any
f\in{\mathcal{H}}
, where the random variable
X
is sampled from
{\mathcal{X}}
. Likewise, consider
{\mathcal{G}}
another RKHS defined on a metric space
{\mathcal{Y}}
with kernel
l(\cdot)
. We then define the symmetric bounded kernels
k(\cdot,\cdot)
and
l(\cdot,\cdot)
on the spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
, respectively. Now given two separable RKHSs
{\mathcal{H}}
and
{\mathcal{G}}
and a joint measure
{\mathbb{P}}_{X\,Y}
defined over
({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{B}}_{x}\times{\mathcal{B}}_{y})
, by Lemma 1 of
Gretton et al. 2005a
,
HSIC
is defined as:

\displaystyle\text{HSIC}(X,Y)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{X_{1}X_{2}Y_{1}Y_{2}}\big[k(X_{1},X_{2})l(Y_{1},Y_{2})\big]

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{1}X_{2}}\big[k(X_{1},X_{2})\big]{\mathbb{E}}_{Y_{1}Y_{2}}\big[l(Y_{1},Y_{2})\big]-2{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{1}Y_{1}}\big[{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{2}}[k(X_{1},X_{2})]{\mathbb{E}}_{Y_{2}}[l(Y_{1},Y_{2})]\big],

with
(X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2})
independent copies of
(X,Y)
. When
X
and
Y
are independent,
\text{HSIC}(X,Y)=0
, and
\text{HSIC}(X,Y)>0
otherwise: see Section 5 in
Gretton et al. 2005b
or Subsection 2.3 in
Song et al. 2012
.
Equation (4) of
Gretton et al. 2007
provides the V-statistic estimator

\widehat{\text{HSIC}}_{v}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\overset{n}{\underset{i,j=1}{\sum}}K_{ij}L_{ij}+\frac{1}{n^{4}}\overset{n}{\underset{i,j,m,l=1}{\sum}}K_{ij}L_{ml}-\frac{2}{n^{3}}\overset{n}{\underset{i,j,m=1}{\sum}}K_{ij}L_{im},

(2.6)

with
K_{ij}=k(X_{i},X_{j})
,
L_{ij}=l(Y_{i},Y_{j})
. It can be alternatively written as

\widehat{\text{HSIC}}_{v}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}[{\mathcal{K}}_{X}]_{ij}[{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}]_{ij}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\text{tr}\left({\boldsymbol{K}}_{X}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{Y}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}\right),

(2.7)

where
{\mathcal{K}}_{X}={\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}{\boldsymbol{K}}_{X}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}={\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{Y}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}
,
[{\boldsymbol{K}}_{X}]_{ij}=k(X_{i},X_{j})
,
[{\boldsymbol{L}}_{Y}]_{ij}=l(Y_{i},Y_{j})
and
{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}={\boldsymbol{I}}_{n}-\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{1}}_{n}{\boldsymbol{1}}_{n}^{\top}
is the centering matrix with
{\boldsymbol{1}}_{n}
the
n
-dimensional vector containing
1
s only, so that

\displaystyle[{\mathcal{K}}_{X}]_{ij}

\displaystyle=K_{ij}-\overline{K}_{.j}-\overline{K}_{i.}-\overline{K},\overline{K}_{.j}=\frac{1}{n}\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}k(X_{i},X_{j}),\overline{K}_{i.}=\frac{1}{n}\overset{n}{\underset{j=1}{\sum}}k(X_{i},X_{j}),\overline{K}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\overset{n}{\underset{i,j=1}{\sum}}k(X_{i},X_{j}),

\displaystyle[{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}]_{ij}

\displaystyle=L_{ij}-\overline{L}_{.j}-\overline{L}_{i.}-\overline{L},\overline{L}_{.j}=\frac{1}{n}\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}l(Y_{i},Y_{j}),\overline{L}_{i.}=\frac{1}{n}\overset{n}{\underset{j=1}{\sum}}l(Y_{i},Y_{j}),\overline{L}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\overset{n}{\underset{i,j=1}{\sum}}l(Y_{i},Y_{j}).

This V-statistic estimator is biased with a bias of order
O(n^{-1})
according to Theorem 1 of
Gretton et al. 2005a
. The unbiased version of
HSIC
proposed by, e.g.,
Gretton et al. 2007
- see their equation (3) - is defined as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{\text{HSIC}}_{u}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\underset{(i,j)\in\mathbf{i}^{n}_{2}}{\sum}K_{ij}L_{ij}+\frac{1}{n(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)}\underset{(i,j,m,l)\in\mathbf{i}^{n}_{4}}{\sum}K_{ij}L_{ml}\\
-\frac{2}{n(n-1)(n-2)}\underset{(i,j,m)\in\mathbf{i}^{n}_{3}}{\sum}K_{ij}L_{im},\end{split}

(2.8)

with
\mathbf{i}^{n}_{m}
the index set denoting the set of all
m
-tuples drawn without replacement from
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Hereafter and to clarify our arguments, we consider non-negative symmetric and continuous kernels
k(\cdot,\cdot),l(\cdot,\cdot)
, bounded by
1
. This condition includes several commonly used kernels, such as the Gaussian kernel or the Laplacian kernel. We will consider the Gaussian kernel in our empirical studies. Both of these kernels are universal in the sense of
Gretton et al. 2005b
and
Fukumizu et al. 2009
.

In this paper, the theoretical and empirical results will be based on the normalized version of HSIC formulated in
Da Veiga 2015
as a sensitivity index, which will be denoted by
nr-HSIC
, and defined as:

\text{nr-HSIC}(X,Y)=\cfrac{\text{HSIC}(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\text{HSIC}(X,X)\text{HSIC}(Y,Y)}},

(2.9)

where
\text{HSIC}(X,X)>0,\text{HSIC}(Y,Y)>0
. Replacing
\text{HSIC}(X,Y),\text{HSIC}(X,X),\text{HSIC}(Y,Y)
by their estimators based on (
2.7
), the V-statistic estimator of the normalized HSIC is

\text{nr-}\widehat{\text{HSIC}}_{v}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\frac{n^{-2}\text{tr}\left({\boldsymbol{K}}_{X}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{Y}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}\right)}{\sqrt{n^{-2}\text{tr}\left({\boldsymbol{K}}_{X}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}{\boldsymbol{K}}_{X}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}\right)n^{-2}\text{tr}\left({\boldsymbol{L}}_{Y}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{Y}.{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}\right)}}\cdot

(2.10)

Note that, by the properties of the
\text{tr}(\cdot)
operator,
\sqrt{\text{tr}\left({\boldsymbol{K}}_{X}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}{\boldsymbol{K}}_{X}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}\right)}=\|{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}{\boldsymbol{K}}_{X}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}\|_{F}
.

Furthermore,
{\mathbb{L}}_{v,n}(\mathbf{\theta})
in (
2.3
) can be written as a least squares criterion in the same vein as in
Poignard and Yamada 2020
:

(i)

When
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(.,.)=\text{nr-}\widehat{\text{HSIC}}_{v}(.,.)
, the loss becomes

{\mathbb{L}}_{v,n}(\mathbf{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\;\frac{1}{n^{2}}\|\overline{{\mathcal{L}}}_{Y}-\sum_{k=1}^{p}\theta_{k}\overline{{\mathcal{K}}}_{X_{k}}\|^{2}_{F}=\frac{1}{2}\;\frac{1}{n^{2}}\overset{n}{\underset{i,j=1}{\sum}}\Big([\overline{{\mathcal{L}}}_{Y}]_{ij}-\sum_{k=1}^{p}\theta_{k}[\overline{{\mathcal{K}}}_{X_{k}}]_{ij}\Big)^{2},

where
[\overline{{\mathcal{L}}}_{Y}]_{ij}=[{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}]_{ij}/(n^{-1}\|{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}{\boldsymbol{L}}_{Y}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}\|_{F})
and
[\overline{{\mathcal{K}}}_{X_{k}}]_{ij}=[{\mathcal{K}}_{X_{k}}]_{ij}/(n^{-1}\|{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}{\boldsymbol{K}}_{X_{k}}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{n}\|_{F})
.

(ii)

When
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(.,.)=\widehat{\text{PC}}_{v}(.,.)^{2}
, the loss becomes

{\mathbb{L}}_{v,n}(\mathbf{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\;\frac{1}{n^{3}}\langle\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}_{Y}-\sum_{k=1}^{p}\theta_{k}\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}_{X_{k}},\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}_{Y}-\sum_{k=1}^{p}\theta_{k}\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}_{X_{k}}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\;\frac{1}{n^{3}}\overset{n}{\underset{i,j,m=1}{\sum}}\Big([\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}_{Y}]_{ijm}-\sum_{k=1}^{p}\theta_{k}[\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}_{X_{k}}]_{ijm}\Big)^{2},

where the expressions
[{\mathcal{D}}_{Y}]_{ijm}
and
[{\mathcal{D}}_{X_{k}}]_{ijm}
correspond to the quantities defined in (
2.5
),
[\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}_{Y}]_{ijm},[\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}_{X_{k}}]_{ijm}
are defined as
[\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}_{Y}]_{ijm}=[{\mathcal{D}}_{Y}]_{ijm}/\sqrt{(n^{-3}\sum^{n}_{l,q,r}[{\mathcal{D}}_{Y}]^{2}_{lqr})}
and
[\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}_{X_{k}}]_{ijm}=[{\mathcal{D}}_{X_{k}}]_{ijm}/\sqrt{(n^{-3}\sum^{n}_{l,q,r}[{\mathcal{D}}_{X_{k}}]^{2}_{lqr})}
, and
\langle{\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}}\rangle=\sum_{k,l,r=1}^{n}x_{klr}b_{klr}
denotes the inner product of two same-sized tensors
{\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n}
, so that
\widehat{\text{PC}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})^{2}=n^{-3}\langle\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}_{X_{k}},\overline{{\mathcal{D}}}_{Y}\rangle=n^{-3}\sum_{i,l,r=1}^{n}[{\mathcal{D}}_{X_{k}}]_{ilr}[{\mathcal{D}}_{Y}]_{ilr}/\sqrt{(n^{-3}\sum^{n}_{l,q,r}[{\mathcal{D}}_{X_{k}}]^{2}_{lqr})(n^{-3}\sum^{n}_{l,q,r}[{\mathcal{D}}_{Y}]^{2}_{lqr})}
.

Hereafter, we consider the SmRMR problem to perform feature screening with the Projection Correlation and HSIC measures. More precisely, our problem is the following one: the target is
Y\in{\mathbb{R}}
(which can be extended to
q
-dimensional responses) and the vector of features is
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
. Equipped with the sample
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
and
\mathbf{X}_{k}=(X_{1k},\ldots,X_{nk})
, we compute the V-statistic estimator of
\text{D}(X_{k},Y)
and solve problem (
2.4
), which provides the set of selected features. The results we derive in the next section hold for both
\widehat{\text{PC}}_{v}(.,.)^{2}
and
\text{nr-}\widehat{\text{HSIC}}_{v}(.,.)
; thus, unless stated otherwise,
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(.,.)
will be both measures. To clarify the terminology, SmRMR with PC (resp. HSIC) refers to criterion (
2.4
) with
\widehat{\text{PC}}_{v}(.,.)^{2}
(resp.
\text{nr-}\widehat{\text{HSIC}}_{v}(.,.)
).

3
Asymptotic properties

Our analysis is developed when the number of parameters
p
potentially diverges with the sample size. Therefore, the number of unknown parameters,
p_{n}
, varies with the sample size: the sequence
(p_{n})
could tend to the infinity with
n
, that is
p=p_{n}\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Hereafter, we denote by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n0}
the “pseudo-true coefficient”. Without loss of generality, we assume that the first
s_{n}
components of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n0}
are non-zero and the remaining components are zero. More formally, we assume
\theta_{n0,k}\neq 0
for
k\leq s_{n}
and
\theta_{n0,k}=0
for
k>s_{n}
with
s_{n}
the cardinality of the true sparse support, defined as
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}:=\{k=1,\ldots,p_{n}:\,\theta_{n0,k}\neq 0\}
: this is the sparsity assumption. For
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}\in\Theta_{n}
, we write
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n1}=(\theta_{n,1},\ldots,\theta_{n,s_{n}})^{\top}
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n2}=(\theta_{n,s_{n}+1},\dots,\theta_{n,p_{n}})^{\top}
. We define the loss
{\mathbb{L}}_{v,n}(\cdot)
as in (
2.3
) for a given association measure. The SmRMR problem we consider is

\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{n}\,\in\,\underset{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{n}}_{+}}{\mathop{\mathrm{argmin\,}}}\;n\,{\mathbb{L}}_{v,n}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n})+n\overset{p_{n}}{\underset{k=1}{\sum}}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},\theta_{n,k}),

(3.1)

when such a minimizer exists. We assume the following regularity conditions.

Assumption 3.1
.

For a measure
\text{D}(.,.)
, there exists a unique pseudo-true parameter value
\theta_{n0}
minimizing the function
{\mathbb{L}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}):=-\sum^{p_{n}}_{k=1}\theta_{n,k}\text{D}(X_{k},Y)+\frac{1}{2}\sum^{p_{n}}_{k,l=1}{\sum}\theta_{n,k}\theta_{n,l}\text{D}(X_{k},X_{l})
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{n}}_{+}
, and an open neighbourhood of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n0}
is contained in
{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{n}}_{+}
. Furthermore, for a given association measure
\text{D}(.,.)
, any pseudo-true parameter value
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n0}
satisfies the condition,
\forall k=1,\ldots,p_{n}
:
-\text{D}(X_{k},Y)+\sum^{p_{n}}_{l=1}\text{D}(X_{k},X_{l}){\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n0,l}=0
.

Assumption 3.2
.

Let
\text{D}_{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}}:=(\text{D}(X_{k},X_{l}))_{1\leq k,l\leq p_{n}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{n}\times p_{n}}
.
\exists\underline{\mu},\overline{\mu}
such that
0<\underline{\mu}<\lambda_{\min}\big(\text{D}_{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}}\big)\leq\lambda_{\max}(\text{D}_{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}})<\overline{\mu}<\infty
.

Assumption
3.1
concerns the orthogonality condition satisfied by
{\mathbb{L}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n0})
at the true pseudo-parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n0}
, that is
\partial_{\theta_{n,k}}{\mathbb{L}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n0})=0
, which is the derivative of the population level non-penalized loss. The parameter
\theta_{n0}
may belong to the boundary of the parameter set in our analysis: this is a problem discussed in
Fermanian and Poignard 2024
; see their Assumption 0 and the discussion relating to this point. Therefore, we define the partial derivatives w.r.t.
\theta_{n,k}
as in the latter work when the parameter is on the boundary. Assumption
3.2
bounds the eigenvalues of
\text{D}_{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}}
, a useful condition that will facilitate the proof for consistency. As for the penalty function
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},.)
, denoting by
\partial_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},x)
(resp.
\partial^{2}_{2,2}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},x)
) the first order (resp. second order) derivative of
x\mapsto\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},x)
, we assume the following conditions.

Assumption 3.3
.

Let

a_{n}:=\underset{1\leq k\leq p_{n}}{\max}\big\{\partial_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},\theta_{n0,k}),\theta_{n0,k}\neq 0\big\}\;\text{and}\;b_{n}:=\underset{1\leq k\leq p_{n}}{\max}\big\{\partial^{2}_{2,2}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},\theta_{n0,k}),

\theta_{n0,k}\neq 0\big\}
,
assume that
a_{n}=O(n^{-1/2})
,
b_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Moreover,
\exists K_{1},K_{2}
such that
|\partial^{2}_{2,2}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},\theta_{1})-\partial^{2}_{2,2}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},\theta_{2})|\leq K_{2}|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|
,
for any real numbers
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
such that
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}>K_{1}\lambda_{n}
.

Assumption 3.4
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n01}=(\theta_{n0,1},\ldots,\theta_{n0,s_{n}})^{\top}
be the sub-vector of non-zero coefficients and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n02}=(\theta_{n0,s_{n}+1},\ldots,\theta_{n0,p_{n}})^{\top}
be the sub-vector of zero entries, so that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n0}=({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}_{n01},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}_{n02})^{\top}
. Then
\underset{1\leq j\leq s_{n}}{\min}\theta_{n0,j}/\lambda_{n}\rightarrow\infty,\;\text{as}\;n\rightarrow\infty
.

Assumption
3.3
concerns the regularity of the penalty function satisfied by SCAD and MCP and are in the same vein as in
Fan and Peng 2004
. For the LASSO,
a_{n}=\lambda_{n},b_{n}=0
. Assumption
3.4
is a condition on the minimum true signal and is standard in the literature on sparse M-estimation. Our first result establishes the existence of a consistent penalized M-estimator for the SmRMR problem. The proof is detailed in
Appendix. B
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
-
3.3
are satisfied. Assume
p^{2}_{n}s_{n}\log(p_{n})=o(n)
. Then, there exists a sequence
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{n}
as defined in (
3.1
) that satisfies:
\|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n0}\|_{2}=O_{p}\Big(\sqrt{p_{n}s^{2}_{n}\log(p_{n})}\Big(n^{-1/2}+a_{n}\Big)\Big)
.

In the case of the LASSO penalty,
a_{n}=\lambda_{n}
. This consistency result includes the original HSIC-LASSO when the association measure is HSIC with Gaussian kernel.

The next result shows that the true zero coefficients can be recovered with probability one for the SCAD and MCP penalty functions, which ensures the correct identification of the non-relevant features.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},\cdot)
be the SCAD or MCP penalty. Under the conditions of Theorem
3.5
and Assumption
3.4
, assume that
\lim\inf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lim\inf_{x\rightarrow 0^{+}}\lambda^{-1}_{n}\partial_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},x)>0
. Moreover, assume
\lambda_{n}\rightarrow 0
,
\sqrt{n/(p_{n}s^{2}_{n}\log(p_{n}))}\lambda_{n}\rightarrow\infty
. Then with probability tending to one, the
\sqrt{n/(p_{n}s^{2}_{n}\log(p_{n}))}
-consistent local minimizer
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{n}
in Theorem
3.5
satisfies
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{n2}=0
.

The result stated in Theorem
3.6
relates to the sparsistency property as in
Lam and Fan 2009
: for a consistent (local) estimator, the true zero coefficients can be recovered with probability one for
n
large enough. This slightly differs from the support recovery property
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{n}={\mathcal{S}}_{n}
with probability tending to one: the latter holds in our framework when
p_{n}
is fixed, but it is not necessarily satisfied in the large-dimensional case. To obtain a support recovery result in the case of non-convex penalty functions ensuring that the estimator
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{n}=\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{n{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}
with probability one, with
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{n{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}
the oracle estimator obtained from minimizing
{\mathbb{L}}_{v,n}
over the true
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
, an alternative estimation framework would be required: see e.g.,
Loh and Wainwright 2017
which builds upon the restricted eigenvalue condition and the primal dual witness technique.

In the LASSO case, for the estimator to be
\sqrt{n/(p_{n}s^{2}_{n}\log(p_{n}))}
-consistent, the regularization parameter
\lambda_{n}
should satisfy
\lambda_{n}=O(\sqrt{s^{2}_{n}\log(p_{n})/n})
. But it cannot simultaneously satisfy
\sqrt{n/(p_{n}s^{2}_{n}\log(p_{n}))}\lambda_{n}\rightarrow\infty
. One way to fix this issue would be the adaptive LASSO, which requires the introduction of stochastic weights that depend on a first step consistent estimator. In light of the multi-stage procedure we propose hereafter in the context of ultra high-dimensional data, we prefer to avoid an additional layer of complexity that would be entailed by the adaptive LASSO.

4
FDR control

The issue we now investigate is the estimation of the number of false discoveries in the penalized estimator
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{n}
. To do so, we rely on the knockoff+ filtering method developed by
Barber and Candès 2015
. Since the seminal work of
Barber and Candès 2015
on the knockoff procedure and its applications for FDR control, a broad range of studies has been flourishing on the extensions and applications of the knockoff method:
Candès et al. 2018
devised the Model-X knockoff in the context of a random design matrix;
Barber and Candès 2019
addressed the issue of knockoff-based FDR control within the high-dimensional setting and emphasized how knockoffs can be applied to non-sparse signals;
Romano et al. 2020
devised a Model-X knockoff framework almost model-free with applications to unsupervised datasets; in the same vein as
Barber and Candès 2019
,
Fan et al. 2020
or
Liu et al. 2022
considered two-step approaches for FDR control;
Lu et al. 2018
applied the knockoff filtering to deep neural neural networks. The method can be broken down into the following three steps:

(1)

Construct the knockoff variables
\widetilde{X}_{k}
for all
k=1,\ldots,p_{n}
. To do so, two methods can be performed: the equicorrelated method or the semi-definite program method.

(2)

For each pair of original variable
X_{k}
and knockoff variable
\widetilde{X}_{k}
, we control the FDR using the importance score
\widehat{W}_{k}
defined as
\widehat{W}_{k}=\widehat{\theta}_{n,k}-\widetilde{\theta}_{n,k}
,
k=1,\ldots,p_{n}
, where
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{n}=(\widehat{\theta}_{n,k})_{1\leq k\leq p_{n}}
and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{n}=(\widetilde{\theta}_{n,k})_{1\leq k\leq p_{n}}
satisfying

(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}_{n},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}_{n})^{\top}\in\underset{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2p_{n}}_{+}}{\mathop{\mathrm{argmin\,}}}\;n{\mathbb{G}}_{v,n}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n})+n\overset{2p_{n}}{\underset{k=1}{\sum}}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},\theta_{n,k}),\;\text{with}

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathbb{G}}_{v,n}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n})=-\overset{p_{n}}{\underset{k=1}{\sum}}\theta_{n,k}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})-\overset{2p_{n}}{\underset{k=p_{n}+1}{\sum}}\theta_{n,k}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{k-p_{n}},\mathbf{Y})+\frac{1}{2}\overset{p_{n}}{\underset{k,l=1}{\sum}}\theta_{n,k}\theta_{n,l}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{X}_{l})\\
+\frac{1}{2}\overset{2p_{n}}{\underset{k,l=p_{n}+1}{\sum}}\theta_{n,k}\theta_{n,l}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{k-p_{n}},\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{l-p_{n}}).\end{split}

Here, a higher value of
\widehat{W}_{k}
gives evidence
X_{k}
is a true signal. Should
X_{k}
be inactive, then
|\widehat{W}_{k}|
is close to zero.

(3)

Define a selection rule to carry out feature selection. To do so, we specify a data-dependent threshold
T(\alpha)
similar to equation (13) of
Barber and Candès 2019
as

T(\alpha)=\min\big\{t\in\mathcal{W}_{n}:\;\frac{1+\text{card}(k:\,\widehat{W}_{k}\leq-t)}{\text{card}(k:\,\widehat{W}_{k}\geq t)\vee 1}\leq\alpha\big\},

(4.2)

and
T(\alpha)=+\infty
should this set be empty and where
\mathcal{W}_{n}=\big\{|\widehat{W}_{k}|:\;1\leq k\leq p_{n}\big\}\setminus\{0\}
is the set of unique nonzero values reached by the
|\widehat{W}_{k}|
’s. Then the active set is defined as

\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{n}(\alpha)=\big\{1\leq k\leq p_{n}:\;\widehat{W}_{k}\geq T(\alpha)\big\}.

As pointed out by
Barber and Candès 2015
, the properties of the
W_{k}
imply that

\text{card}\big(k\in{\mathcal{S}}^{c}_{n}:\;\widehat{W}_{k}\geq t\big)\;\overset{d}{=}\;\text{card}\big(k\in{\mathcal{S}}^{c}_{n}:\;\widehat{W}_{k}\leq-t\big)\leq\text{card}\big(k:\;\widehat{W}_{k}\leq-t\big),

where
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}:=\{k=1,\ldots,p_{n}:\,\theta_{n0,k}\neq 0\}
. At threshold
t
, the False Discovery Proportion (FDP) is estimated as the ratio

\widehat{\text{FDP}}(t)=\frac{\text{card}\big(k:\;\widehat{W}_{k}\leq-t\big)}{\text{card}\big(k:\;\widehat{W}_{k}\geq t\big)\vee 1}.

One key hurdle of step (1) when constructing the knockoff variables is the sample size requirement
2p_{n}<n
, which is often not satisfied in practice. To circumvent this issue and apply the knockoff, we consider a data splitting procedure that builds upon Section 4.1 of
Barber and Candès 2019
: on a first sub-sample, we perform a feature screening step to select a subset of variables, say
\widehat{S}_{0}\subset\{1,\ldots,p_{n}\}
; using the screened variables only, we employ the knockoff procedure on the remaining data. Such data splitting procedure was also employed by
Liu et al. 2022
or
Poignard et al. 2022
. Formally, the procedure can be summarized a follows:

(1)

Split the sample into two parts
n_{0}
and
n_{1}
such that
n_{0}+n_{1}=n
. Then define the vectors of observations
\mathbf{X}^{(0)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{0}\times{p_{n}}},\mathbf{Y}^{(0)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{0}}
and
\mathbf{X}^{(1)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{1}\times{p_{n}}},\mathbf{Y}^{(1)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{1}}
so that
\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbf{Y}^{(0)\top},\mathbf{Y}^{(1)\top})^{\top}
and
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{X}^{(0)\top},\mathbf{X}^{(1)\top})^{\top}
.

(2)

Perform a variable screening step on the sub-sample
n_{0}
according to the procedure detailed in Subsection
5.1.1
: this step can be performed by solving (
3.1
) for a given penalty, or by applying a feature screening technique such as
HSIC
or
\text{PC}^{2}
, to return a set of features likely to contain the active features and together with potentially many inactive features. This set
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n}
is selected such that its
s_{0,n}
variables satisfies
2s_{0,n}<n_{1}
.

(3)

Construct the knockoff variables from the subset
\mathbf{X}^{(1)}_{\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n}}
.
Then for each
k=1,\ldots,s_{0,n}
, with
s_{0,n}=\text{card}(\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n})
, compute the statistic for each pair of original variable and knockoff variable
\widehat{W}_{k}=\widehat{\theta}^{\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n}}_{n_{1},k}-\widetilde{\theta}^{\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n}}_{n_{1},k}
, where
(\widehat{\theta}^{\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n}}_{n_{1},k},\widetilde{\theta}^{\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n}}_{n_{1},k})
are the estimators deduced from (
4.1
) based on
(\mathbf{Y}^{(1)},(\mathbf{X}^{(1)}_{\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n}},\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{(1)}_{\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n}}))
. The number of parameters to be estimated is
s_{0,n}
and both estimators
\widehat{\theta}^{\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n}}_{n_{1},k}
and
\widetilde{\theta}^{\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n}}_{n_{1},k}
are non-negative. This stage corresponds to the selection step in
Barber and Candès 2019
. Then for a fixed
\alpha
, we use (
4.2
) to estimate the set of active features as

\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{n}(\alpha)=\big\{k:k\in\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n},\;\widehat{W}_{k}\geq T(\alpha)\big\}.

In step (2), the penalty employed for screening is the same as in step (3) and the resulting set
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n}
depends on the first portion of the data. A pre-screening procedure is employed in the case of ultra high-dimensional data: this aims to reduce the dimension prior to solving problem (
3.1
). In step (3), the specification of the importance score
\widehat{W}_{k}
determines the success of the FDR procedure. It should satisfy the sufficiency and anti-symmetry properties, so that the statistic has a symmetric distribution for an inactive variable and is positive or negative with the same probability. The statistic
\widehat{W}_{k}
satisfies these two properties. Indeed, the penalized problem is equivalent to the minimization of

-n\overset{p_{n}}{\underset{k=1}{\sum}}\theta_{n,k}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y})+\frac{n}{2}\overset{p_{n}}{\underset{k,l=1}{\sum}}\theta_{n,k}\theta_{n,l}\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{X}_{l})+n\overset{p_{n}}{\underset{k=1}{\sum}}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}(\lambda_{n},\theta_{n,k}).

Take
\widehat{\text{D}}_{v}(\cdot,\cdot)=\text{nr-}\widehat{\text{HSIC}}_{v}(\cdot,\cdot)
. Then, up to a factor of
n
, the non-penalized part of the previous problem can be expressed as follows

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\overset{n}{\underset{i,j=1}{\sum}}\sum_{k=1}^{p_{n}}\theta_{n,k}[\overline{{\mathcal{K}}}_{X_{k}}]_{ij}[\overline{{\mathcal{L}}}_{Y}]_{ij}-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\overset{n}{\underset{i,j=1}{\sum}}\sum_{k,l=1}^{p_{n}}\theta_{n,k}\theta_{n,l}[\overline{{\mathcal{K}}}_{X_{k}}]_{ij}[\overline{{\mathcal{K}}}_{X_{l}}]_{ij}.

Therefore, the problem depends on
(\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{p_{n}},\mathbf{Y})
through
[\overline{{\mathcal{K}}}_{X_{k}}]_{ij}[\overline{{\mathcal{L}}}_{Y}]_{ij},1\leq k\leq p_{n}
, and
[\overline{{\mathcal{K}}}_{X_{k}}]_{ij}[\overline{{\mathcal{K}}}_{X_{l}}]_{ij},1\leq k,l\leq p_{n}
, where
\overline{{\mathcal{K}}}_{X_{k}},\overline{{\mathcal{K}}}_{Y}
are continuous bounded functions of
X_{k},Y
, respectively.
\widehat{W}_{k}
can be expressed as
\widehat{W}_{k}=f((\mathbf{X},\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}),\mathbf{Y})_{k}
with
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{2p_{n}+1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{p_{n}}
.

To verify the validity of the selection step with data splitting, we assume that the screening procedure of step (2) has estimated the set
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n}
that includes all important features. In the case of ultra high-dimensional data, prior to estimating
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n}
, we employ a feature pre-screening as detailed in Section
2.1
: the marginal utility
\text{D}(\cdot,\cdot)
will be the same throughout the knockoff procedure. As emphasized in Subsection 4.2 in
Barber and Candès 2019
, we do not assume that
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n}
selects the true support of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n0}
. Instead, we only assume that the screening step includes the true relevant features with probability one. Then, we define the screening event
{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
as
{\mathcal{E}}_{n}=\big\{{\mathcal{S}}_{n}\subseteq\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n},\;2s_{0,n}<n_{1}\big\}
.
Here, the screening step performed on the sub-sample
n_{0}
does not necessarily need to recover the true support
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
exactly. Alternatively, it is only required to provide a set of features
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n}
likely to contain all the active features and some inactive features. Given the screening event, the following result shows that the feature-feature mRMR procedure can control the FDR of selected features for a given level
\alpha
. The proof can be found in
Appendix. B
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any level
0<\alpha<1
, let the data-dependent threshold
T(\alpha)=\min\big\{t\in\mathcal{W}_{n}:\;\frac{1+\text{card}(k:\,\widehat{W}_{k}\leq-t)}{\text{card}(k:\,\widehat{W}_{k}\geq t)\vee 1}\leq\alpha\big\}
,
with
\mathcal{W}_{n}=\big\{|\widehat{W}_{k}|:\;1\leq k\leq p_{n}\big\}\setminus\{0\}
and the set of active features
\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{n}(\alpha)=\big\{k:k\in\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{0,n},\;\widehat{W}_{k}\geq T(\alpha)\big\}
. Then

{\mathbb{E}}[\frac{\text{card}(k:\;k\in{\mathcal{S}}^{c}_{n}\;\;\text{and}\;\;k\in\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{n}(\alpha)}{\text{card}(k:\;k\in\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}}_{n}(\alpha))\vee 1}|{\mathcal{E}}_{n}]\leq\alpha.

Cross-validation and hyper-parameter optimization

We wish to minimize the non-penalized part in (
3.1
) on a left out validation set to find the best set of hyper parameters in an unbiased manner.
This procedure can be fine-tuned, in particular for solving problem (
3.1
) in step 2 of the screening step and problem (
4.1
) with the knockoff features.
However, we do not perform any fine-tuning in the screening step for two reasons: we retain only the top performing features to reduce the input space dimensionality as in LARS, modifying the penalization scalar only leads to shifting the feature acceptance threshold, which leads only to more or less features (their relative importance remains the same); to reduce the computational burden.
By default we take
\lambda_{n}=0.01
for this step.
In step 3 of the knockoff filter, we apply cross-validation for fine-tuning.
In particular, when appropriate we fine-tune with respect to
\lambda_{n}
via Bayes optimization methods, see
Dewancker et al. 2016
.

Constructing knockoff variables

We use the second-order model-X knockoff of
Candès et al. 2018
described in their Section 3.2: instead of requiring
(\mathbf{X},\widetilde{\mathbf{X}})_{\text{swap}({\mathcal{S}})}\overset{d}{=}(\mathbf{X},\widetilde{\mathbf{X}})
, where
(\mathbf{X},\widetilde{\mathbf{X}})_{\text{swap}({\mathcal{S}})}
is obtained from
(\mathbf{X},\widetilde{\mathbf{X}})
when swapping the entries
X_{k}
and
\widetilde{X}_{k}
for each
k\in{\mathcal{S}}
, the second-order method requires that
(\mathbf{X},\widetilde{\mathbf{X}})_{\text{swap}({\mathcal{S}})}
and
(\mathbf{X},\widetilde{\mathbf{X}})
to have the same mean and variance-covariance. To ensure the positive semidefinite construction of the latter variance-covariance, we employ the equicorrelated method.

Setting
\alpha

We consider that a good value for
\alpha
would be in the range of
(0.2,0.4)
depending on the data.
The model can be conservative and consider that the risk is too high and return an empty set of features.
This situation can be problematic when we fit the proposed procedure into a larger pipeline where the ultimate end-goal is to perform a classification or a regression.
In this situation only, i.e., when no features are returned for the set
\alpha
, we add a while loop in the last step of the knockoff filter to increase the value of
\alpha
by
0.05
. If however we reach
\alpha=1
and still no features are retained, we modify the algorithm to return the feature that maximises the knockoff statistic. This variant of SmRMR will be named SmRMR
2
. Unless, stated otherwise,
\alpha
is set to a default value of
0.3
.

Choice of the knockoff statistic

Several statistics are proposed in
Barber and Candès 2015
,
Barber and Candès 2019
and satisfy the sufficiency and anti-symmetry properties. Throughout our empirical applications, we employ the statistic

\forall k\in\widehat{{\mathcal{S}}},\ \widehat{W}_{k}=\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\tilde{\theta}_{k}
.

6
Conclusion

We introduced a novel framework for feature screening that leverages both feature-feature and feature-target relationships. By employing the penalized mRMR procedure, we have successfully identified inactive features and provided theoretical conditions for accurate zero parameter recovery.

The proposed multi-stage approach, incorporating the knockoff filter, based on our empirical evaluations on simulated and real-world datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method in terms of performance and in terms of feature selection. While being at similar levels of performance to HSIC-LASSO, our method returns fewer parameters, thus decreasing the FDR levels. In addition, it only requires a threshold level for FDR rather than a predetermined number of features in contrast to the other approaches. This advantage simplifies the practical application of the method.

In conclusion, the proposed framework offers a valuable theoretical tool for feature screening, providing a robust and efficient approach for identifying inactive features. Its potential applications extend to various domains where feature selection is a critical step in data analysis and modeling such as bio-informatics.

(B.3)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\leq
