On the hardness of the L 1 − L 2 L_{1}-L_{2} regularization problem
Yuyuan Ouyang, Kyle Yates

Abstract
The sparse linear reconstruction problem is a core problem in signal processing which aims to recover sparse solutions to linear systems. The original problem regularized by the total number of nonzero components (also known as L 0 L_{0} regularization) is well-known to be NP-hard. The relaxation of the L 0 L_{0} regularization by using the L 1 L_{1} norm offers a convex reformulation, but is only exact under certain conditions (e.g., restricted isometry property) which might be NP-hard to verify. To overcome the computational hardness of the L 0 L_{0} regularization problem while providing tighter results than the L 1 L_{1} relaxation, several alternate optimization problems have been proposed to find sparse solutions. One such problem is the L 1 − L 2 L_{1}-L_{2} minimization problem, which is to minimize the difference of the L 1 L_{1} and L 2 L_{2} norms subject to linear constraints. This paper proves that solving the L 1 − L 2 L_{1}-L_{2} minimization problem is NP-hard. Specifically, we prove that it is NP-hard to minimize the L 1 − L 2 L_{1}-L_{2} regularization function subject to linear constraints. Moreover, it is also NP-hard to solve the unconstrained formulation that minimizes the sum of a least squares term and the L 1 − L 2 L_{1}-L_{2} regularization function. Furthermore, restricting the feasible set to a smaller one by adding nonnegative constraints does not change the NP-hardness nature of the problems.



∎

Introduction

The sparse linear reconstruction problem, also known as the compressed sensing problem (CS), is a core problem in signal processing. The (CS) problem is the problem of finding a sparsest solution to a linear system. The original (CS) problem is the optimization problem

\begin{split}\min_{\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}&\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert_{0}\\
\text{subject to }&\textbf{Ax}=\textbf{b},\end{split}

(CS)

where
\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert_{0}
denotes the number of nonzero entries of
x
, also known as the
L_{0}
norm. Of particular interest for several optimization problems such as problem (
CS
) is their computational hardness. In computational complexity theory, a common characterization on the hardness of a problem is its NP-hardness. Specifically, a decision problem is in the nondeterministic polynomial-time (NP) class if it can be solved by a nondeterministic Turing machine in polynomial time, and it is NP-hard if every problem in NP can be reduced to it by a polynomial-time reduction. Informally, a problem is NP if it is easy to verify whether a proposed solution is correct, and a problem is NP-hard if solving it is as hard as solving any NP problem. For a detailed description of NP, NP-hardness, and other topics in computational complexity theory, see, e.g,
Garey and Johnson (1979)
. The common understanding is that a problem is considered difficult to solve if it can be proven to be NP-hard.

Unfortunately, problem (
CS
) is known to be NP-hard
Natarajan (1995)
(See also
Foucart and Rauhut (2013)
). Specifically, one can construct special instances of problem (
CS
) such that solving them would yield solutions to the exact 3-cover problem, which is already known to be NP-hard. Consequently, problem (
CS
) must also be NP-hard. Informally, here the key idea is to demonstrate that problem (
CS
) is at least as hard as a known NP-hard problem, hence establishing its NP-hardness. This analysis strategy, known as polynomial-time reduction, is a standard technique for proving NP-hardness in computational complexity theory.

Several alternate optimization problems have been proposed to approximate solutions to problem (
CS
) in order to overcome this computational hardness. This includes techniques such as minimizing the
L_{1}
norm subject to
\textbf{Ax}=\textbf{b}
for instance, which can lead to exact recovery of a sparsest solution under certain conditions
Donoho and Elad (2003)
;
Candès et al. (2006)
. However, verifying the exact recovery conditions of the
L_{1}
relaxation could itself be NP-hard; see, e.g.,
Bandeira et al. (2013)
;
Tillmann and Pfetsch (2013)
on the NP-hardness of certifying the restricted isometry property and the nullspace property. Several other models have been proposed in the hope of providing tighter relaxation than the
L_{1}
model, e.g.,
L_{p}

Frank and Friedman (1993)
, smoothly clipped absolute deviation (SCAD)
Fan and Li (2001)
, minimax concave penalty (MCP)
Zhang (2010)
, etc. Unfortunately, many such models have too been shown to be NP-hard (see, e.g.,
Chen et al. (2014)
;
Chen et al. (2019)
;
Ge et al. (2011)
;
Huo and Chen (2010)
;
Jiang and Hu (2020)
;
Nguyen et al. (2019)
and the references within). In spite of the unfavorable complexity results, it should be noted that several numerical methods have been developed that can efficiently solve these problems in practice (see, e.g.,
Bertrand et al. (2022)
;
Tibshirani and Taylor (2011)
).

Among the regularization strategies in sparse solution recovery, one important strategy is the
L_{1}-L_{2}
minimization problem
Esser et al. (2013)
. The idea is to add a penalty term that is the difference of the
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, yielding a non-convex optimization problem

\begin{split}\min_{\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}&\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert_{1}-\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}\\
\textup{subject to }&\textbf{Ax}=\textbf{b}.\\
\end{split}

(1)

For a fixed penalty parameter
\lambda>0
, the unconstrained version of problem (
1
) is the regularization model

\begin{split}\min_{\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}&\left\lVert\textbf{Ax}-\textbf{b}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda(\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert_{1}-\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}).\end{split}

(2)

We will refer to problems (
1
) and (
2
) as the constrained (CP) and unconstrained
L_{1}-L_{2}
minimization problems (UP), respectively. Various studies have been conducted on constrained and unconstrained
L_{1}-L_{2}
minimization (see, e.g.,
Bi and Tang (2022)
;
Bui et al. (2021)
;
Li et al. (2020)
;
Yin et al. (2015)
, and the references within), but most focus on the effectiveness of recovering sparse signals.

To the best of our knowledge, there has been no results developed in the literature concerning the computational complexity of problems (
1
) and (
2
). What makes the
L_{1}-L_{2}
model interesting from the complexity analysis point of view is that one could not derive any NP-hardness result based on previously developed hard instances of other non-convex regularization models. Indeed, for all the papers on the NP-hardness results of (CS)

Chen et al. (2014)
;
Chen et al. (2019)
;
Ge et al. (2011)
;
Huo and Chen (2010)
;
Jiang and Hu (2020)
;
Nguyen et al. (2019)
, none are applicable to problems (
1
) and (
2
). Among the NP-hardness proofs, the most general of such results is probably
Chen et al. (2019)
, in which they prove the strong NP-hardness for several unconstrained problems with a broad class of loss and penalty functions. This class consists of several sparse optimization approximators but does not include problem (
2
). In particular,
Chen et al. (2019)
shows that for given
\textbf{A}=(\textbf{a}_{1},\dots,\textbf{a}_{m})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and any parameter
\lambda>0
, the problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}&\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell(\textbf{a}_{i}^{\top}\textbf{x},b_{i})+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{n}p(|x_{j}|)\end{split}

is strongly NP-hard for some loss functions
\ell:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
and some penalty functions
p:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
, where
\mathbb{R}^{+}
denotes the nonnegative real numbers. This result does not apply to our objective function in problem (
2
) however, as the term
(\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert_{1}-\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert_{2})
can not be written in the penalty form required by
Chen et al. (2019)
. Thus, the computational hardness of problem (
2
) had therefore remained open.

It should be noted that extensive efforts on solving problems (
1
) and (
2
) have been reported in the literature, despite the unknown status of their computational hardness. Observing that problems (
1
) and (
2
) belong to the class of
difference of convex
(DC) programming problems,
in
Yin et al. (2015)
the authors
develop an iterative method based on a DC algorithm (DCA) to numerically solve the unconstrained problem (
2
). The findings in
Yin et al. (2015)
suggest that
L_{1}-L_{2}
minimization via DCA outperforms
L_{p}
solvers in recovering sparse signals for ill-conditioned matrices. The authors also reported that
L_{1}-L_{2}
minimization via DCA outperforms the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM)
Boyd et al. (2011)
for
L_{1}
regularization in every case. Subsequent studies (
Lou et al. (2015a)
;
Lou et al. (2015b)
) back up these findings for both the constrained and unconstrained versions of
L_{1}-L_{2}
minimization, suggesting that these models may offer a promising solution to finding sparse solutions in practice. Other follow-up studies also studies the methods for improving the performance of the ADMM method for
L_{1}-L_{2}
minimization problems (see, e.g.,
Lou and Yan (2018)
).

1.1
Our Contributions

In this paper, we prove the NP-hardness of both the constrained and unconstrained
L_{1}-L_{2}
minimization problems. We prove (
1
) is NP-hard by showing a polynomial-time reduction from the NP-complete partition problem to a variation of constrained
L_{1}-L_{2}
minimization with an additional fixed positive parameter, which holds for decision variables in both
\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\mathbb{R}
. We then prove that (
2
) is NP-hard for a range of
\lambda
, also by providing a polynomial-time reduction from the partition problem to the unconstrained
L_{1}-L_{2}
minimization problem. Much like the constrained version, our NP-hardness results again hold for decision variables in both
\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
\mathbb{R}
.

NP-Hardness of Constrained
L_{1}-L_{2}
Minimization

In this section, we prove that it is NP-hard to solve the constrained
L_{1}-L_{2}
minimization problem (
1
). In fact, we will prove a slightly more general statement that for any parameter

\tau\in(0,\sqrt{2})
, it is NP-hard to solve the following constrained minimization problem:

\begin{split}\min_{\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}&\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert_{1}-\tau\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}\\
\textup{subject to }&\textbf{Ax}=\textbf{b}.\\
\end{split}

(CP)

Throughout this paper, we call the above the constrained
L_{1}-\tau L_{2}
problem (
CP
). While (

) reduces to problem (
1
) when
\tau=1
, our NP-hardness result on more general

in an interval shows that the major computational hardness of solving the

problem is the nonconvex
(-\|\textbf{x}\|_{2})
term; such hardness could not be alleviated by setting a slightly smaller coefficient parameter
\tau
in front of the nonconvex
(-\|\textbf{x}\|_{2})
term. As a byproduct of our analysis, we will also show that the

problem does not become any easier when we only search its solution from a smaller feasible set by enforcing additional nonnegative constraints. Specifically, for any
\tau>0

\begin{split}\min_{\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}&\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert_{1}-\tau\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}\\
\textup{subject to }&\textbf{Ax}=\textbf{b}\text{ and }\textbf{x}\geq\textbf{0}.\end{split}

(NCP)

We call the above the nonnegative constrained

problem (
NCP
).

Our strategy of proving the NP-hardness of (

) and (

Given a multiset

Proposition 1

\text{subject to}&\begin{aligned} \quad u_{i}+v_{i}=&1\quad\text{ for }i=1,\dots,m\\
\textup{{a}}^{\top}(\textup{{u}}-\textup{{v}})=&0\\
\textup{{u}},\textup{{v}}\geq&\textup{{0}
}.\end{aligned}\end{split}

(3)

Here we denote
\textup{{a}}:=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})^{\top}

Proof

Consider a relaxation modification of problem (
3

\begin{split}\min_{\textbf{u},\textbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}&\sum_{i=1}^{m}-u_{i}^{2}-v_{i}^{2}\\

\textbf{u},\textbf{v}\geq&\textbf{0}.\end{aligned}\end{split}

The optimal value of

(5)

\|\textbf{w}^{(j)}\|_{2}\to\infty
, we have
g(\textbf{w}^{(j)})\to\infty
also. Hence
g
is a coercive function. Consequently,
g
is also continuous the problem
\min_{\textbf{w}}g(\textbf{w})
has at least one global optimizer.

Since the function
g
is differentiable, we may compute its minimizers by studying its stationary points that solve the system
\nabla g(\textbf{w})=0
. Here the
i
-th equation is

4w_{i}-\tau\cdot\frac{4w_{i}+8w_{i}^{3}}{2\sqrt{m+2\|\textbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}+2\|\textbf{w}\|_{4}^{4}}}=0,

which is equivalent to either
w_{i}=0
or

\frac{2}{\tau}\sqrt{m+2\|\textbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}+2\|\textbf{w}\|_{4}^{4}}-1-2w_{i}^{2}=0.

However, for any

\tau\in(0,\sqrt{2})
, the latter has no feasible solution since

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\tau}\sqrt{m+2\|\textbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}+2\|\textbf{w}\|_{4}^{4}}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{1+2w_{i}^{2}+2w_{i}^{4}}

\displaystyle>\sqrt{1+4w_{i}^{2}+4w_{i}^{4}}

\displaystyle=1+2w_{i}^{2}.

So the only stationary point is
\textbf{w}=0
which is the global optimizer. Our claim is now proved.
∎

With the help of Lemma
1
, we are now ready to prove that solving problem (
CP
) is NP-hard by following the same argument in the NP-hardness proof of (
NCP
).

Theorem 2.2

For any

\tau\in(0,\sqrt{2})
, it is NP-hard to solve the constrained
L_{1}-\tau L_{2}
problem (
CP
).

Proof

Since the set of optimal solutions to problem (
7
) is also
X^{*}
defined in (
5
), by the same argument as in the proof of Proposition
1
, we can prove that the problem of determining the solvability of the partition problem concerning multiset
S=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
is equivalent to determining whether the optimal objective value of problem (
6
) is
m-\tau\sqrt{m}
. Here the value
m-\tau\sqrt{m}
is the optimal objective value of problem (
7
) in the optimal solution set
X^{*}
. Moreover, note that problem (
6
) is an instance of (
CP
) with
n=2m
,
\textbf{x}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m},v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})^{\top}
, and

\textbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{I}_{m}&\textbf{I}_{m}\\
\textbf{a}^{\top}&-\textbf{a}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},\quad\quad\textbf{b}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{1}_{m}\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore, solving such (
CP
) instance is equivalent to determining the solvability of the partition problem, which is NP-hard.
∎

Our constructed hard instance (
6
) suggests that the constrained
L_{1}-\tau L_{2}
minimization captures the sparse solutions of the partition problem and hence reflects its NP-hardness. An immediate research question is whether we can reformulate the linear reconstruction problem to an unconstrained version instead and control the reconstruction sparsity by tuning a parameter in the regularization path. However, as we will show in the next section, the unconstrained
L_{1}-L_{2}
minimization problem will also capture the sparse solutions of the partition problem.

3
NP-Hardness of Unconstrained
L_{1}-L_{2}
Minimization

In this section, we prove that it is also NP-hard to solve the unconstrained
L_{1}-L_{2}
minimization problem (
2
). Recall the unconstrained
L_{1}-L_{2}
minimization problem (
2
) is the problem

\begin{split}\min_{\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}&\left\lVert\textbf{Ax}-\textbf{b}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda(\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert_{1}-\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert_{2})\end{split}

(UP)

for fixed penalty parameter
\lambda>0
. Throughout this section we will refer to the above as the unconstrained
L_{1}-L_{2}
problem (
UP
). We will also use (
NUP
) to denote the nonnegativity constrained version of problem (
UP
):

\begin{split}\min_{\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}&\left\lVert\textbf{Ax}-\textbf{b}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda(\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert_{1}-\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert_{2})\\
&\text{subject to }\textbf{x}\geq\textbf{0}.\end{split}

(NUP)

By utilizing the linear reconstruction instance (
6
), we will show in this section that both (
UP
) and (
NUP
) are NP-hard.
Similar to our analysis in the constrained case (
CP
) and (
NCP
), our analysis is based on showing that the
L_{1}-L_{2}
regularization captures the sparse solutions of the partition problem and hence reflects its NP-hardness.

Specifically, we will consider (
UP
) and (
NUP
) instances

\begin{split}\min_{\textbf{u},\textbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}f(\textbf{u},\textbf{v}):=&(\textbf{a}^{\top}(\textbf{u}-\textbf{v}))^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(u_{i}+v_{i}-1)^{2}\\
&+\lambda\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}|u_{i}|+|v_{i}|-\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2}}\right],\end{split}

(10)

\begin{split}\min_{\textbf{u},\textbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}f(\textbf{u},\textbf{v}):=&(\textbf{a}^{\top}(\textbf{u}-\textbf{v}))^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(u_{i}+v_{i}-1)^{2}\\
&+\lambda\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}+v_{i}-\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2}}\right]\\
\text{subject to }&\textbf{u},\textbf{v}\geq\textbf{0}.\end{split}

(11)

We will show that for any fixed regularization parameter
\lambda\in(0,2)
, solving either one of the instances (
10
) or (
11
) is NP-hard.

Before conducting our complexity analysis, we will first need to make sure that both problems (
10
) and (
11
) are solvable. The existence of their optimal solutions are shown in Proposition
2
.

Proposition 2

For any regularization parameter
\lambda>0
, there exists at least one optimal solution to
problem
(
10
).

Proof

We claim that if

\max\{|u_{j}|,|v_{j}|\}>1+\sqrt{m}+2m/\lambda
for any index
j
, then
f(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})>f(\textbf{0},\textbf{0})
. By our claim, we conclude that it suffices to study the minimization of
f(u,v)
within the compact set
\|(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})\|_{\infty}\leq 1+\sqrt{m}+2m/\lambda
. By Weierstrass theorem, the optimal solution of such problem must exist.

To prove the claim, note that if

\max\{|u_{j}|,|v_{j}|\}>1+\sqrt{m}+2m/\lambda
for some index
j
, then there are exactly four possible cases:

(1)

|u_{j}|>1+\sqrt{m}+2m/\lambda
and
|v_{j}|\leq 2m/\lambda
;

(2)

|v_{j}|>1+\sqrt{m}+2m/\lambda
and
|u_{j}|\leq 2m/\lambda
;

(3)

|u_{j}|>1+\sqrt{m}+2m/\lambda
and
|v_{j}|>2m/\lambda
;

(4)

|v_{j}|>1+\sqrt{m}+2m/\lambda
and
|u_{j}|>2m/\lambda
.

In the first case, we have

|u_{j}+v_{j}-1|\geq|u_{j}-1|-|v_{j}|\geq|u_{j}|-1-|v_{j}|>\sqrt{m}.

Noting that the
L_{1}-L_{2}
regularization function is nonnegative, we have
f(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})\geq(u_{j}+v_{j}-1)^{2}>m=f(\textbf{0},\textbf{0})
.
A similar result also holds for the second case.
In the third case, we have

|u_{j}|>1+\sqrt{m}+2m/\lambda>2m/\lambda
and
|v_{j}|>2m/\lambda
. Focusing on the regularization part we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}f(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})\geq&\lambda\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}|u_{i}|+|v_{i}|-\sqrt{\left(\sqrt{u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}}+\sum_{i=1,i\not=j}^{m}|u_{i}|+|v_{i}|\right)^{2}}\right]\\
=&\lambda(|u_{j}|+|v_{j}|-\sqrt{u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}}).\end{split}

Observing that

\displaystyle(|u_{j}|+|v_{j}|-\sqrt{u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}})\cdot\frac{|u_{j}|+|v_{j}|+\sqrt{u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}}}{|u_{j}|+|v_{j}|+\sqrt{u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}}}=\frac{(|u_{j}|+|v_{j}|)^{2}-u_{j}^{2}-v_{j}^{2}}{|u_{j}|+|v_{j}|+\sqrt{u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2|u_{j}||v_{j}|}{|u_{j}|+|v_{j}|+\sqrt{u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}}}

and

|u_{j}|+|v_{j}|+\sqrt{u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}}\leq 2(|u_{j}|+|v_{j}|),

we then have

\displaystyle f(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})\geq

\displaystyle\frac{2\lambda|u_{j}||v_{j}|}{|u_{j}|+|v_{j}|+\sqrt{u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}}}\geq\frac{\lambda|u_{j}||v_{j}|}{|u_{j}|+|v_{j}|}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda\min\{|u_{j}|,|v_{j}|\}\max\{|u_{j}|,|v_{j}|\}}{2\max\{|u_{j}|,|v_{j}|\}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}\min\{|u_{j}|,|v_{j}|\}>m=f(\textbf{0},\textbf{0}).

In the fourth case, we have
|u_{j}|>2m/\lambda
and
|v_{j}|>1+\sqrt{m}+2m/\lambda>2m/\lambda
, in which the argument is identical to the third case.

∎

We remark that the proof for existence of optimal solutions to problem (
10
) extends straightforwardly to the version of the problem with nonnegative variables. This is since our proof strategy is based on restricting the optimal solutions to a compact infinity-norm ball, which remains compact when intersected with nonnegativity constraints.
We formally state this result
as Corollary
1
.

Corollary 1

There exists at least one optimal solution to problem (
11
).

Proof

Immediate from the proof of Proposition
2
and restricting
\textbf{u},\textbf{v}\geq\textbf{0}
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the NP-hardness of the
(
UP
) and (
NUP
) instances in equations (
10
) and (
11
) (respectively) in the upcoming sections. We will start with the (
NUP
) instance (
11
) and derive a polynomial reduction from the partition problem.

3.1
NP-Hardness of the (
NUP
) instance when
\lambda\in(0,2)

In this section, we will show that the (
NUP
) instance (
11
) can be reduced from the partition problem for all
\lambda\in(0,2)
. Assuming that the partition problem with respect to multiset
S=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
has a solution, we will consider the following lower bound problem:

\begin{split}\min_{\textbf{u},\textbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}&g(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(u_{i}+v_{i}-1)^{2}+\lambda\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}+v_{i}-\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2}}\right]\\
&\text{subject to }u_{i},v_{i}\geq 0\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,m.\end{split}

(12)

Here
g(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})\leq f(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})
is a lower bound function with one less fitting term
(\textbf{a}^{\top}(\textbf{u}-\textbf{v}))^{2}
concerning the partition requirement. When
\lambda\in(0,2)
, the set of optimal solutions to this lower bound problem is stated in Lemma
2
.

Lemma 2

Suppose that
\lambda\in(0,2)
. Let
c(\lambda):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda/\sqrt{m}-\lambda+2\right)
and

g^{*}(\lambda):=\lambda\left((1-\frac{\lambda}{4})\cdot m+(\frac{\lambda}{2}-1)\cdot\sqrt{m}-\frac{\lambda}{4}\right).

The optimal objective value to problem (
12
) is
g^{*}(\lambda)
with optimal solution set

Y^{*}=\{(\textup{{u}}^{*},\textup{{v}}^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}|(u_{i}^{*},v_{i}^{*})=(c(\lambda),0)\textup{ or }(0,c(\lambda))\textup{ for all }i\in[m]\}.

Proof

For the objective function
g(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})
in problem (
12
), note that the nonnegativity of any feasible solution
(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})
yields

\displaystyle g(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m}(u_{i}+v_{i}-1)^{2}+\lambda\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}+v_{i}-\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(u_{i}+v_{i})^{2}}\right).

Here the equality holds if and only if
u_{i}v_{i}=0
for all
i
. Therefore, it suffices to study the optimization problem

\begin{split}\min_{\textbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\ h(\textbf{t}):=&\sum_{i=1}^{m}(t_{i}-1)^{2}+\lambda\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}-\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}^{2}}\right)\\
&\text{subject to }\textbf{t}\geq\textbf{0}.\end{split}

(13)

Note that for all
\textbf{t}=\textbf{u}+\textbf{v}
, we have
g(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})\geq h(\textbf{t})
. Moreover, for
any optimizer
\textbf{t}^{*}
of
problem (
13
), we can recover an optimizer
(\textbf{u}^{*},\textbf{v}^{*})
of problem (
12
) in which either
(u_{i}^{*},v_{i}^{*})=(0,t_{i}^{*})
or
(u_{i}^{*},v_{i}^{*})=(t_{i}^{*},0)
for all
i
. Furthermore, we claim that every optimal solution to (
12
) is of this form. Observe that for any feasible solution
(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})
to (
12
) with
u_{i}v_{i}>0
for some
i
, the solution
t
with
t_{i}=u_{i}+v_{i}
for all
i
is feasible to (
13
) and satisfies
h(\textbf{t})<g(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})
. We can then construct the solution
(\tilde{\textbf{u}},\tilde{\textbf{v}})=(\textbf{t},0)
to (
12
) which satisfies
g(\tilde{\textbf{u}},\tilde{\textbf{v}})=h(\textbf{t})<g(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})
. Hence, any feasible
(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})
to (
12
) with
u_{i}v_{i}>0
for some
i
is never optimal, and the set of optimal solutions to (
12
) is completely determined by the set of optimal solutions to (
13
).

By Corollary
1
, optimal solutions to problem (
12
) exist, so optimal solutions to problem (
13
) must exist too. Note that
\textbf{t}=\textbf{0}
is the only point of
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which is not differentiable. Except the
point
\textbf{t}=\textbf{0}
, with nonnegativity constraints the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) condition is necessary for optimality, i.e., if
t
is an optimal solution, then there exists multipliers
\mu_{i}
’s such that

\displaystyle\mu_{i}\geq 0,\ 2(t_{i}-1)+\lambda-\frac{\lambda t_{i}}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}t_{j}^{2}}}-\mu_{i}=0,\ (-t_{i})\mu_{i}

\displaystyle=0,\ \forall i\in[m].

(14)

We now show that there exists a unique KKT point with smaller objective function value than the non-differentiable point
\textbf{t}=\textbf{0}
. If there exists
i\in[m]
such that the multiplier
\mu_{i}>0
, then by (
14
) we have
t_{i}=0
and
-2+\lambda-\mu_{i}=0
, contradicting our assumption that
\lambda<2
. Therefore, any KKT point must have multipliers
\mu_{i}=0
for all
i\in[m]
. By (
14
), for all
i,k\in[m]
we have

\lambda t_{i}-2(t_{i}-1)\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}t_{j}^{2}}=\lambda\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}t_{j}^{2}}=\lambda t_{k}-2(t_{k}-1)\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}t_{j}^{2}},

(15)

The relation in (
15
) yields
t_{i}=t_{k}
since
\lambda t_{i}-2(t_{i}-1)=\lambda t_{k}-(2t_{k}-1)
, as
\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}t_{j}^{2}}\neq 0
. Thus we have

\lambda t_{i}-2(t_{i}-1)\sqrt{m}t_{i}=\lambda\sqrt{m}t_{i},

whose nonnegative solution is

t_{i}=c(\lambda)
. Thus, there is a unique KKT point
\textbf{t}^{*}
with
t_{i}^{*}=c(\lambda)
for all
i\in[m]
. Since
\lambda\in(0,2)
, it is then easy to verify that

\displaystyle h(\textbf{t}^{*})=g^{*}(\lambda)<\lambda(1-\lambda/4)m<m=h(\textbf{0}).

Therefore,
\textbf{t}^{*}
is the unique optimal solution to problem (
13
).
∎

The optimal set resulting from
Lemma
2
will be enough for us to solve the partition problem by solving a similarly constructed optimization problem.

Proposition 3

Suppose
\lambda\in(0,2)
. The problem of determining the solvability of the partition problem concerning multiset
S=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
is equivalent to determining whether the optimal objective value of the following optimization problem is
g^{*}(\lambda)
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\textup{{u}},\textup{{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\ (\textup{{a}}^{\top}(\textup{{u}}-\textup{{v}}))^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(u_{i}+v_{i}&-1)^{2}+\lambda\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}+v_{i}-\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2}}\right]\\
\text{subject to }&\ \textup{{u}},\textup{{v}}\geq\textup{{0}}.\end{split}

Here we denote
\textup{{a}}:=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})^{\top}
.

Proof

Let
c(\lambda),g^{*}(\lambda)
, and
Y^{*}
be as defined in Lemma
2
. It is clear that for any feasible
u,v
to problem (
11
) we have

(\textbf{a}^{\top}(\textbf{u}-\textbf{v}))^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(u_{i}+v_{i}-1)^{2}+\lambda\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}+v_{i}-\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2}}\right]\geq g^{*}(\lambda),

with equality holding if and only if
(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})\in Y^{*}
and
\textbf{a}^{\top}(\textbf{u}-\textbf{v})=\textbf{0}
.

Suppose that there exists a solution to the partition problem with respect to multiset
S=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
. Let us introduce vectors
\textbf{u},\textbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, in which
u_{i}=c(\lambda)
(or
v_{i}=c(\lambda)
respectively) if
a_{i}
is partitioned into the first subset (or second subset respectively), and
u_{i}=0
(or
v_{i}=0
respectively) otherwise. Clearly, we have
(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})\in Y^{*}
. Moreover, since the sum of elements in the two subsets are the same, we have
\textbf{a}^{\top}\textbf{u}=\textbf{a}^{\top}\textbf{v}
. Therefore, problem (
11
) achieves an optimal objective value of
g^{*}(\lambda)
.

Conversely, for a given partition problem on multiset
S=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
, suppose that
(\textbf{u}^{*},\textbf{v}^{*})
is an optimal solution to problem (
11
) with optimal value
g^{*}(\lambda)
. Then, we must have
\textbf{a}^{\top}(\textbf{u}^{*}-\textbf{v}^{*})=0
and
(\textbf{u}^{*},\textbf{v}^{*})
as an optimal solution to problem (
12
). Hence
(\textbf{u}^{*},\textbf{v}^{*})\in Y^{*}
. By partitioning element
a_{i}
to the first set if
u_{i}^{*}=c(\lambda)
and to the second set if
v_{i}^{*}=c(\lambda)
for all
i\in[m]
, we obtain a solution to the partition problem.
∎

We are now ready to prove that problem (
NUP
) is NP-hard. Similar to our proof of the constrained version, we can find a specific instance of problem (
NUP
) which is equivalent to problem (
11
), thus solving the partition problem. Theorem
3.1
formally states the result.

Theorem 3.1

For any
\lambda\in(0,2)
, it is NP-hard to solve the nonnegative unconstrained
L_{1}-L_{2}
problem (
NUP
).

Proof

Fixing any
\lambda\in(0,2)
, we will show that there is a polynomial-time reduction from the partition problem to problem (
NUP
). Specifically, supposing that we have an instance of the partition problem with multiset
S=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\}
, consider the optimization problem (
NUP
) in which
n=2m
,
\textbf{x}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m},v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})^{\top}
, and

\textbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{I}_{m}&\textbf{I}_{m}\\
\textbf{a}^{\top}&-\textbf{a}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},\quad\quad\textbf{b}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{1}_{m}\\
0\end{bmatrix},

where
\textbf{I}_{m}
is an
m\times m
identity matrix and
\textbf{1}_{m}
is a vector of
m
ones. By construction, it is clear that this instance of problem (
NUP
) is equivalent to problem (
11
). By Proposition
3
, solving such (
NUP
) instance is equivalent to determining the solvability of the partition problem, which is NP-hard.
∎

3.2
NP-Hardness of the (
UP
) instance when
\lambda\in(0,2)

Having finished proving the NP-hardness of problem (
NUP
), we are now ready to move onto problem (
UP
). Consider the optimization problem

\begin{split}\min_{\textbf{u},\textbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}g(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})&=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(u_{i}+v_{i}-1)^{2}+\lambda\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}|u_{i}|+|v_{i}|-\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2}}\right]\end{split}.

(16)

Our strategy will be to prove the optimal set of (
16
) is again
Y^{*}
. As we have already established the existence of optimal solutions to problem (
16
) in Proposition
2
, it suffices to use first order optimality conditions to find any possible optimal solutions, which is the strategy we use
in Lemma
3
.

Lemma 3

Suppose that
\lambda\in(0,2)
. Let
c(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda/\sqrt{m}-\lambda+2\right)
and

g^{*}(\lambda)=\lambda\left((1-\frac{\lambda}{4})\cdot m+(\frac{\lambda}{2}-1)\cdot\sqrt{m}-\frac{\lambda}{4}\right).

The optimal objective value to problem (
16
) is
g^{*}(\lambda)
with optimal solution set

Y^{*}=\{(\textup{{u}}^{*},\textup{{v}}^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}|(u_{i}^{*},v_{i}^{*})=(c(\lambda),0)\textup{ or }(0,c(\lambda))\textup{ for all }i\in[m]\}.

Proof

We make a few observations on points
(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})
that would not be optimal for the optimization problem (
16
). First, if
(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})
satisfies
u_{i}+v_{i}<0
for some
i
, then there exists
(\tilde{\textbf{u}},\tilde{\textbf{v}})
such that
g(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})>g(\tilde{\textbf{u}},\tilde{\textbf{v}})
. In fact, if

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{j}=\begin{cases}-u_{i}&j=i\\
u_{j}&j\not=i,\end{cases}\quad\tilde{v}_{j}=\begin{cases}-v_{i}&j=i\\
v_{j}&j\not=i,\end{cases}

then

\displaystyle g(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})-g(\tilde{\textbf{u}},\tilde{\textbf{v}})=(u_{i}+v_{i}-1)^{2}-(u_{i}+v_{i}+1)^{2}=(-2)\cdot(2u_{i}+2v_{i})>0.

Second, if
(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})
satisfies
u_{i}=v_{i}=0
for some
i
, then there exists
(\tilde{\textbf{u}},\tilde{\textbf{v}})
such that
g(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})>g(\tilde{\textbf{u}},\tilde{\textbf{v}})
. In fact, if

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{j}=\begin{cases}1-\frac{1}{2}\lambda&j=i\\
u_{j}&j\not=i,\end{cases}\tilde{v}_{j}=\begin{cases}0&j=i\\
v_{j}&j\not=i,\end{cases}

then letting
C:=\sum_{j=1,j\not=i}^{m}u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}
we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}g\textbf{(u},\textbf{v})-g(\tilde{\textbf{u}},\tilde{\textbf{v}})=&[1-\lambda\sqrt{C}]-\left[\frac{1}{4}\lambda^{2}+\lambda\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\lambda-\sqrt{(1-\frac{1}{2}\lambda)^{2}+C}\right)\right]\\
>&1-\frac{1}{4}\lambda^{2}-\lambda(1-\frac{1}{2}\lambda)\\
=&\frac{1}{4}(2-\lambda)^{2}\\
\geq&0.\end{split}

Third, if
(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})
satisfies
u_{i}>0
and
v_{i}>0
for some
i
, then there exists
(\tilde{\textbf{u}},\tilde{\textbf{v}})
such that
g(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})>g(\tilde{\textbf{u}},\tilde{\textbf{v}})
. In fact, if

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{j}=\begin{cases}u_{i}+v_{i}&j=i\\
u_{j}&j\not=i,\end{cases}\tilde{v}_{j}=\begin{cases}0&j=i\\
v_{j}&j\not=i,\end{cases}

then letting
C:=\sum_{j=1,j\not=i}^{m}u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}
we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}g(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})-g(\tilde{\textbf{u}},\tilde{\textbf{v}})=&\lambda\sqrt{(u_{i}+v_{i})^{2}+C}-\lambda\sqrt{u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2}+C}>0.\end{split}

By the above observations, for any optimal solution
(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})
to problem (
16
) we may partition its indices by the following:

\displaystyle\begin{split}I_{1}:=\{i|u_{i}>0,v_{i}=0\},I_{2}:=\{i|u_{i}>0,v_{i}<0\}.\end{split}

Since each index of our optimal solution
(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})
of consideration belongs now to either
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
, from first-order necessary optimality conditions we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}0=&\frac{\partial g}{\partial u_{i}}(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})=2(u_{i}-1)+\lambda-\frac{\lambda u_{i}}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}}},\ \forall i\in I_{1},\\
0=&\frac{\partial g}{\partial u_{i}}(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})=2(u_{i}+v_{i}-1)+\lambda-\frac{\lambda u_{i}}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}}},\ \forall i\in I_{2},\\
0=&\frac{\partial g}{\partial v_{i}}(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})=2(u_{i}+v_{i}-1)-\lambda-\frac{\lambda v_{i}}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}}},\ \forall i\in I_{2},\end{split}

which yield (after performing subtraction and addition on the last two relations)

\begin{split}&\left(2-\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}}}\right)u_{i}=2-\lambda,\ \forall i\in I_{1},\\
&\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}}}(u_{i}-v_{i})=2\lambda,\ \forall i\in I_{2},\\
&\left(4-\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}^{2}+v_{j}^{2}}}\right)(u_{i}+v_{i})=4,\ \forall i\in I_{2}.\end{split}

(17)

From the
relations in (
17
) we have
u_{j}=u_{k}
for all
j,k\in I_{1}
and
u_{j}=u_{k}
and
v_{j}=v_{k}
for all
j,k\in I_{2}
. Let us denote
u_{j}=t
for all
j\in I_{1}
and
u_{j}=r
,
v_{j}=s
for all
j\in I_{2}
. Applying our notations and noting that
\lambda>0
, from the second equation
in (
17
) we have

\sqrt{|I_{1}|t^{2}+|I_{2}|(r^{2}+s^{2})}=\frac{1}{2}(r-s).

(18)

Note that the
relation in (
18
) implies that
I_{2}=\emptyset
. To see this, assume by contrary that
|I_{2}|\geq 1
. We would then have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}(r-s)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}(r^{2}+s^{2})<|I_{2}|(r^{2}+s^{2})\leq|I_{1}|t^{2}+|I_{2}|(r^{2}+s^{2}),

yielding a contradiction. Hence we could only have
I_{2}=\emptyset
. Recalling that
u_{j}=t
and
v_{j}=0
for all
j\in I_{1}
, from the first equation of (
17
)
we can solve that

t=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda/\sqrt{m}-\lambda+2\right)
.
∎

The rest of our analysis is then similar to the previous section concerning the hard (
NUP
) instance: the optimal set
Y^{*}
is enough to find an optimization problem which solves the partition problem. We state the rest in Proposition
4
.

Proposition 4

Suppose that
\lambda\in(0,2)
. The problem of determining the solvability of the partition problem concerning multiset
S=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
is equivalent to determining whether the optimal objective value of the following optimization problem is
g^{*}(\lambda)
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\textup{{u}},\textup{{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}&(\textup{{a}}^{\top}(\textup{{u}}-\textup{{v}}))^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(u_{i}+v_{i}-1)^{2}\\
&+\lambda\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}|u_{i}|+|v_{i}|-\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2}}\right].\end{split}

(19)

Here we denote
\textup{{a}}:=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})^{\top}
.

Proof

Let
c(\lambda),g^{*}(\lambda)
, and
Y^{*}
be as defined in Lemma
3
. It is clear that for any
\textbf{u},\textbf{v}
we have

(\textbf{a}^{\top}(\textbf{u}-\textbf{v}))^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(u_{i}+v_{i}-1)^{2}+\lambda\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}|u_{i}|+|v_{i}|-\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2}}\right]\geq g^{*}(\lambda),

with equality holding if and only if
(\textbf{u},\textbf{v})\in Y^{*}
and
\textbf{a}^{\top}(\textbf{u}-\textbf{v})=0
. The remainder of the proof follows identically the proof of Proposition
3
for problem (
16
).
∎

We are now ready to prove our main result that solving (
UP
) is NP-hard.

Theorem 3.2

For any
\lambda\in(0,2)
, it is NP-hard to solve the unconstrained
L_{1}-L_{2}
problem (
UP
).

Proof

Fixing any
\lambda\in(0,2)
, we will show that there is a polynomial-time reduction from the partition problem to problem (
UP
). Specifically, supposing that we have an instance of the partition problem with multiset
S=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\}
, consider the optimization problem (
UP
) in which
n=2m
,
\textbf{x}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m},v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})^{\top}
, and

\textbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{I}_{m}&\textbf{I}_{m}\\
\textbf{a}^{\top}&-\textbf{a}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},\quad\quad\textbf{b}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{1}_{m}\\
0\end{bmatrix},

where
\textbf{I}_{m}
is an
m\times m
identity matrix and
\textbf{1}_{m}
is a vector of
m
ones. By construction, it is clear that this instance of problem (
UP
) is equivalent to problem
(
19
). By Proposition
4
, solving such (
UP
) instance is equivalent to determining the solvability of the partition problem, which is NP-hard.
∎

3.3
NP-hardness when
\lambda\geq 2

While our NP-hardness proofs in the previous two sections assume that
\lambda\in(0,2)
, we will show in this section that when
\lambda\geq 2
, problems (
UP
) and (
NUP
) are still NP-hard. Specifically, let us consider problem instances in which

\textbf{A}=\sqrt{\lambda}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{I}_{m}&\textbf{I}_{m}\\
\textbf{a}^{\top}&-\textbf{a}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},\quad\quad\textbf{b}=\sqrt{\lambda}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{1}_{m}\\
0\end{bmatrix},

and
\textbf{x}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m},v_{1},\dots,v_{m})
. Observe that the instance of (
UP
) with this choice of
A
and
b

is then the optimization problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\textbf{u},\textbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\ (\sqrt{\lambda}\textbf{a}^{\top}(\textbf{u}&-\textbf{v}))^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\sqrt{\lambda}u_{i}+\sqrt{\lambda}v_{i}-\sqrt{\lambda})^{2}\\
&+\lambda\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}+v_{i}-\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2}}\right].\end{split}

Clearly, we may factor
\lambda
and instead consider the optimization problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\textbf{u},\textbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\ (\textbf{a}^{\top}(\textbf{u}-\textbf{v}))^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(u_{i}+v_{i}&-1)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}+v_{i}-\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2}},\end{split}

which is NP-hard by the proof of Theorem
3.2
(with
\lambda=1
). The same argument may be made for problem (
NUP
) with the proof of Theorem
3.1
, meaning both (
UP
) and (
NUP
) are actually NP-hard for any finite
\lambda>0
.

There is still a slight distinction between the cases of
\lambda\in(0,2)
and
\lambda\geq 2
in our NP-hardness results. When
\lambda\geq 2
, the above choice of
A
and
b
must depend on the value of
\lambda
to prove NP-hardness with our proof techniques. On the contrary, When
\lambda\in(0,2)
, the choice of
A
and
b
stated in Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
does not depend on
\lambda
. Considering the fact that
\lambda
is usually selected as a tuning parameter while
A
and
b
are already determined, our results on the case with
\lambda\in(0,2)
are slightly stronger.

4
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we prove that it is NP-hard to solve both the constrained and unconstrained formulation of the linear reconstruction problem subject to the
L_{1}-L_{2}
minimization. We also show that restricting the feasible set to a smaller one by adding nonnegative constraints does not change the NP-hardness nature of the problems.

While our results are negative on the computational tractability of the
L_{1}-L_{2}
problem, our NP-hardness proof is based on a worse-case instance. It is interesting to study whether there are a smaller class of
L_{1}-L_{2}
problems whose exact solutions could be solved in polynomial time.

It should also be noted that our result focuses on the computational tractability issue of the exact optimal solution. While our result reveals that solving the exact solution is NP-hard, it remains an interesting problem on studying the computational tractability of an approximate solution. Recently, there has been new research results developed on the NP-hardness of solving approximate solutions to certain sparse optimization problems; see
Chen et al. (2019)
and the references within. While the
L_{1}-L_{2}
regularized problem does not belong to the problems covered in
Chen et al. (2019)
, it is interesting to see if the reduction techniques in
Chen et al. (2019)
could shed some light on the complexity analysis of approximation of the
L_{1}-L_{2}
regularized problem.
