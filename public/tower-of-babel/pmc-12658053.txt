Formation characteristics and decentralization analysis of post-urban agglomerations from the perspective of spatial reshaping: a case study of the great lakes urban agglomeration
Authors: Runlin Yang, Feng Zhen, Jue Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Over the past two decades, academic research on urban agglomerations has gradually declined, primarily due to the stabilization of their spatial configurations and the diminishing role of agglomeration effects in driving overall economic growth. Amid the ongoing transition from information technology to artificial intelligence (AI), this paper examines the spatial correlation between population growth and rising median individual income within the Great Lakes urban agglomeration, with a perspective of spatial reshaping. The findings reveal a shift toward decentralization in the spatial structure of the urban agglomeration following the U.S. financial crisis, a transformation that has contributed to economic recovery. This decentralization is attributed to AI-driven changes in work patterns, which have reduced residents’ dependence on spatial agglomeration. Accordingly, the paper introduces the concept of the “post-urban agglomeration,” aiming to uncover mechanisms that promote equitable development among cities in the emerging era of AI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26136-4.

Introduction

The formation of urban agglomerations in the 20th century reflects a context in which, under market economy conditions, central cities can no longer attain advantages solely through their own large-scale urbanization1. As a result, development potential has shifted to surrounding cities, creating a suitable agglomeration space through intercity division of labor and regional cooperation2. In the industrial era, the development model of urban agglomerations, driven predominantly by manufacturing, was centered on one or several central cities within the region to facilitate spatial cooperation and mobilize additional urban resources3. Within these agglomerations, central cities exerted their influence on surrounding non-central cities through the spillover effects of their demand for products and supply of services, thereby attracting population, capital, and technological resources to the region4. Simultaneously, these cities leveraged industrial chains to transfer and delegate their less competitive industries, enhancing the interconnectedness among cities within the agglomeration5. The spatial development of urban agglomerations during this period exhibited a centralized characteristic, aligning with the ideal state of urban hierarchy and spatial range division as described in central place theory6. Supported by the spatial form surrounding the central city7, agglomeration economies during this period fostered a positive relationship between city size and economic development level within the urban agglomeration8.

To further enhance the macroeconomic impact of urban agglomerations, governments worldwide have provided substantial support through regional development policies9, including improving transportation efficiency between cities within the agglomeration to reduce transportation costs10. When the resource aggregation capacity of central cities becomes saturated, the economic growth rate of the urban agglomeration slows, resulting in stagnation or even decline in the development levels of certain cities11. This situation results in a phenomenon where polarization and collapse coexist within the urban agglomeration12, causing economic imbalances and widening developmental disparities among cities13. Under conditions where urban agglomerations find it challenging to overcome spatial constraints for further resource agglomeration, the spatial form of urban agglomeration begins to optimize and upgrade its internal structure14 to unlock the potential for urban development. This shift occurs against the backdrop of a diminishing trend in monocentric urban agglomerations, with greater attention focused on the operations of each individual metropolitan area within the agglomeration15. A polycentric development trend has emerged, characterized by the formation of multiple central cities with relatively independent, multicenter structures16. This polycentric trend has redirected scholarly attention from analyzing the urban agglomeration as a whole to examining the development and theoretical frameworks of individual cities. Consequently, academic research on urban agglomerations has transitioned from examining macro spatial phenomena17 to analyzing the micro mechanisms of individual city development through data-driven approaches and other methodologies18.

Since the early 21 st century, traditional manufacturing industries in early-developed urban agglomerations have faced increasing competitive pressures—exacerbated by the 2008 U.S. financial crisis and the emergence of lower-cost, sectorally specialized urban regions in developing countries19,20, and the latecomer advantages afforded by the digital economy21. This situation has led to serious negative effects and even bankruptcy in some cities22,23, including Detroit in the Great Lakes urban agglomeration, Baltimore in the Atlantic urban agglomeration, and Birmingham and Nottingham in the Greater London urban agglomeration in the UK.

Despite the numerous challenges associated with both monocentric and polycentric spatial models in urban agglomerations since their inception, the academic community has rarely questioned the underlying rationale of the spatial agglomeration development mechanism. The primary reason for this is that, at the macro level, urban agglomerations have significantly enhanced regional development and the national economy—particularly in manufacturing-focused agglomerations, where the overall benefits appear to be increasing. Often influenced by the post hoc fallacy24, scholars tend to select favorable data to demonstrate the positive effects of spatial agglomeration, thereby overlooking the potential developmental constraints affecting some cities. Second, the spatial agglomeration model of urban agglomerations is both operable and replicable. To accelerate economic development, developing countries can quickly form urban agglomerations tailored to their national conditions. For example, the Yangtze River Delta urban agglomeration in China, with Shanghai as the central city, has rapidly gathered resources from surrounding cities through an extensive network of highways and high-speed rail, becoming a crucial driver of China’s rapid GDP growth. Third, cognitive oversight regarding the need for the spatial reshaping of urban agglomerations exists. Scholars who have grown with the development of urban agglomerations believe that informatization makes agglomeration more convenient or can expand the scope of agglomeration25. These scholars regard this as a new agglomeration model26 and primarily focus on optimizing the agglomeration mechanism through informatization, while giving limited attention to the potential emergence of new decentralized spatial forms from the perspective of spatial reshaping. Finally, when urban development issues arise, many scholars continue to attribute them to micro-level factors—such as industrial monoculture, ineffective governance, population migration, or immigration—without critically examining whether the operational mechanism of spatial configuration within the urban agglomeration negatively affects some cities or whether the underdevelopment of individual cities affects the urban agglomeration as a whole.

Developed countries currently face challenges into identifying new geographic spaces suitable for establishing urban agglomerations to foster agglomeration economies. As a result, the spatial reshaping of existing urban agglomerations27 is emerging as a critical economic and social issue in the exploration of future urban agglomeration development. Decentralization is one important approach to spatial restructuring. However, existing research offers relatively few explorations or practical cases of decentralizing urban agglomerations. This gap is not only due to the complexity of reshaping large-scale spatial systems, but also because it is difficult to predict how urban agglomerations might function once their core mechanism of spatial concentration is altered. In fact, if the spatial logic of agglomeration is dismantled, it becomes unclear whether such regions can still be considered coherent urban agglomerations at all. The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 forced many urban agglomerations into isolation, unintentionally initiating a decentralized mode of operation. During this period, residents adapted to decentralized patterns of living and working, with AI technologies playing a key enabling role. Notably, many of these decentralized practices have persisted beyond the pandemic. Studying the pre-pandemic spatial reshaping of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration can help us better understand how decentralization operates within urban regions, and what implications it might have for the future of urban development.

By analyzing the correlation between population and economic change, as well as the underlying mechanisms across 22 cities of different hierarchical levels within the Great Lakes urban agglomeration, we aim to assess the effectiveness and dynamics of decentralization in reshaping the spatial structure of urban agglomerations. Further examination of the spatial relationships both among central cities and between central and peripheral cities allows us to identify the processes and characteristics of decentralization across the region. This study seeks to address the current research gap on decentralized spatial operating mechanisms of urban agglomerations during the transition from the information era to the AI era, underscoring that an appropriate spatial operating mechanism is fundamental to the sustainable development of modern urban agglomerations. In addition, the paper introduces the concept of the “post-urban agglomeration,” offering both a theoretical framework and empirical grounding for its emergence in the AI era. This concept not only enables the academic community to distinguish decentralized, AI-era urban agglomerations from historically centralized, agglomeration-driven urban forms, but also repositions decentralization and urban agglomeration as central themes warranting renewed scholarly attention.

Literature review

A suitable spatial form can play a positive role in multiple aspects of urban agglomerations28, not only by providing favorable conditions for connections between the cities within the agglomeration29 but also by helping address various social issues related to the labor market, economic development, and spatial inequality. The theory of spatial reshaping originated in management30, aiming to explain changes in the nature and governance of public space. This theory has become a viable solution in fields including cultural geography, social sciences, and urban design when facing spatial dilemmas31. Spatial reshaping is an important process and operational mode of spatial evolution that reorganizes and reallocates spatial elements to alter social32, economic33, and interpersonal relationships. This phenomenon occurs not only at the micro level of individual spaces but also at the macro level within cities, regions, and even national spatial scales. The motivations for spatial reshaping include primarily promoting economic growth, enhancing social equity, improving environmental quality, and strengthening urban competitiveness.

Compared with concepts, for instance, spatial enhancement34 or spatial optimization35, spatial reshaping does not merely involve improving and optimizing the allocation of existing resources but rather focuses on reimagining and adjusting the entire spatial structure. Therefore, spatial reshaping requires a certain spatial scale and a level of developmental maturity27. In the case of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration, its existing cities have already reached a significant scale and maturity, optimizing the allocation and coordination of human resources, transportation, and industries to the fullest extent at the physical level. To meet the demands of the information and AI era and address increasingly complex challenges, a deeper reconsideration of urban agglomeration layout, functional zoning, and transportation networks is necessary to ensure the sustainable development of both the entire urban agglomeration and individual cities while enhancing their overall competitiveness at a higher level. The reason for the spatial reshaping of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration lies in the difficulty of maintaining the original agglomeration model. The spatial structure of the urban agglomeration needs to be readjusted and rebuilt to meet the demands of new economic and social environmental developments. Prior to 2008, the Great Lakes urban agglomeration operated and adjusted within a monocentric and polycentric spatial form. However, following the 2008 financial crisis, accompanied by a population decline and economic downturn, the spatial structure of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration began to shift toward decentralization.

Since the late twentieth century, the concept of artificial intelligence (AI) has gradually been advanced by information technology companies such as IBM, and subsequently explored and applied across a wide range of fields36. Among these developments, the progress of deep learning37, machine learning38, and neural networks39 has been particularly prominent. With the diffusion and penetration of information technologies, AI-based smart technologies began to be systematically embedded into urban governance frameworks after 201240. These technologies have been widely deployed in areas such as traffic management, environmental monitoring, and the optimization of public services, thereby enhancing the operational efficiency of cities while simultaneously providing residents with more targeted and personalized services41. From a societal perspective, the advent of generative AI—exemplified by ChatGPT—marked the first moment in which the public broadly recognized that AI had transcended its technical and professional origins, entering everyday life and serving a much wider population42.

With respect to the concepts of polycentricity and decentralization in urban agglomerations, the geography and planning fields have not strictly distinguished or defined these terms. Some scholars directly define decentralization as counter-agglomeration43. During the COVID-19 pandemic, AI technologies facilitated work from home, online education, and digital forms of social interaction, thereby helping individuals navigate the challenges of spatial isolation44. This process further deepened the integration of human–AI–space relations. Beyond offering practical solutions, it reshaped the organizational logic of urban space and catalyzed the emergence of decentralized spatial configurations distinct from traditional patterns of population agglomeration. Such dynamics not only provide new empirical cases but also offer a novel theoretical lens for understanding contemporary urban spatial transformations45.

Any field involving decentralization theories or structures responds to the control exerted by a system’s center46. From the perspective of urban agglomerations, decentralization is not about spatial dispersion or counter-agglomeration but rather focuses on a feedback mechanism addressing issues within the existing spatial composition, questioning and challenging the mechanism of resource agglomeration around central urban areas. Decentralization originated in the political realm and later found its application in the financial sector. The core principles of decentralization are the devolution of central authority and technological development47, both of which are equally applicable to the spatial reshaping needs of the urban agglomeration in a new era. The devolution of central authority in the urban agglomeration in a new era can be defined as a shift in urban economic development from being centered on the needs of a monocentric city to being centered on individual cities beginning to develop independently and diversify their industries. This transformation relies on leveraging network-enabled personal mobility and value and addressing and adjusting issues within agglomerated spaces to adapt to the spatial form of economic development in the future information age. Urban agglomerations and cities transition from performing agglomerative functions to serving as platforms that provide services.

Driven by the combined forces of the COVID-19 pandemic and accelerating advances in artificial intelligence, urban agglomerations have undergone a profound process of spatial and functional reshaping48. This transformation is not merely superficial but rather indicative of fundamental changes across multiple dimensions, encompassing spatial configurations, inter-city functional hierarchies, and the everyday behavioral patterns of urban residents49. In this emergent context, the conventional term “urban agglomeration” increasingly falls short of capturing the evolving relational dynamics among cities within a region. As a result, there is a growing theoretical imperative to adopt a revised conceptual vocabulary, often marked by the prefix “post-”, to articulate these paradigm shifts50,51. The use of the prefix “post-” in urban and regional studies has long served as a critical theoretical instrument to denote structural transitions that rupture, transcend, or radically reconfigure dominant spatial logics. From post-industrial cities52. to post-Fordist spatial regimes, and from post-socialist urbanism to post-metropolitan regionalism, the “post-” prefix consistently signals both a temporal break and an epistemological reorientation. It does not merely imply succession but rather marks a fundamental departure from established principles of urban organization, functional centrality, and spatial rationality.

Adopting the concept of the post-urban agglomeration provides a more precise lens through which to interrogate the evolving territorial logics of contemporary urban regions53. It underscores the imperative to reconceptualize urban form not merely as a material or morphological phenomenon, but as a dynamic socio-technical system in continuous transformation. This shift calls for a move away from static spatial models toward multi-scalar, processual modes of analysis capable of capturing the fluid and contingent interplay between decentralization, digital infrastructures, and emerging geographies of spatial belonging.

Method

This study adopts the Pearson correlation coefficient for analyzing urban correlations due to its simplicity and accuracy. Its results provide an intuitive representation of spatial distribution relationships in geography and interactions between variables in economics54. By comparing the inter-city correlations within the Great Lakes urban agglomeration across different periods, the degree of decentralization within the urban agglomeration can be assessed. If the correlations between cities weaken, it implies greater independence among them. The correlation of population growth rates between cities reflects the degree of agglomeration between non-central cities and central cities, while the correlation of median individual income growth rates indicates whether the urban agglomeration relies on industries in the central cities.

In terms of specific research methods, this study employs time series analysis, Geographic Information System (GIS) technology, and the Pearson correlation coefficient to conduct a detailed analysis of the spatial data of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration. GIS is one of the key tools for data model visualization in geography. It allows for the clear overlay of spatial elements and statistical results at multiple levels, facilitating the analysis of urban agglomeration changes in terms of spatial processes and characteristics.

This study contrasts the operational mechanisms of urban agglomerations between the information technology era and the AI era, with the aim of highlighting the societal significance of the decentralized development model underpinning the reshaping of post-urban agglomerations. Geographic Information Systems (GIS) are employed to analyze the demographic and economic interconnections within urban agglomerations, offering not only distinct advantages in spatial analysis but also enabling a more profound level of inquiry55.

First, GIS provides an explicit spatial framework that integrates diverse datasets—ranging from demographic and economic indicators to transportation networks and land-use configurations—within a unified geographical reference system. This allows for a more precise visualization and quantification of inter-city dynamics than traditional statistical approaches alone. Second, GIS supports multi-scalar analysis: it not only captures inter-urban linkages at the macro level, but also reveals interactions at meso- and micro-scales, such as commuting flows, trade connections, and spatial accessibility56. For example, techniques such as network analysis, spatial autocorrelation, and flow mapping can uncover the latent dependencies and asymmetric relations that shape the functional integration of urban agglomerations. Finally, by facilitating scenario modeling and simulation, GIS enhances the explanatory depth of urban research, enabling scholars to project spatial evolution trends of urban agglomerations under diverse socioeconomic and technological transformations—for instance, the rise of artificial intelligence or the widespread adoption of remote work practices57.

Conducting correlation analysis on the cities within an urban agglomeration can reflect the degree of interconnectedness among the cities in the agglomeration. This study employs the following two indicators for correlation analysis: the urban population growth rate and the median individual income growth rate. Correlation analysis of the urban population growth rate can reflect spatial relationships at the macro level, whereas that of the median individual income growth rate can reflect spatial relationships at the micro level within the urban agglomeration. The strength of the correlation can intuitively indicate the degree of cohesion among cities. Commonly used correlation analysis often employs Pearson’s correlation coefficient, which is widely used to measure the degree of correlation between two variables. Study area and data

Study area

The Great Lakes urban agglomeration is one of the earliest developed and few urban agglomerations in the world to have undergone a complete evolution from a monocentric structure to a polycentric structure. This agglomeration has long served as a model for other developing countries in constructing urban agglomerations. The core of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration comprises cities in the northeastern United States, including cities in Michigan, Wisconsin, Ohio, Illinois, and Indiana, as well as cities in southern Ontario in Canada, all centered around the Great Lakes. The total area of this agglomeration is approximately 245,000 square kilometers, encompassing two cities with populations exceeding ten million and six cities with populations exceeding one million. Owing to its advantageous geographic location, this urban agglomeration has leveraged the superiority of its natural environment to develop an efficient internal transportation network and has become a significant economic region in the world (Fig. 1b).

This study focuses on the Great Lakes urban agglomeration as the research area, aiming to explore the spatial reshaping process involved in the transition from a centralized urban agglomeration model to a decentralized post-urban agglomeration model through an in-depth analysis of the case area. The formation of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration began in the 1950 s with the development of the urban corridor (Chipitts) between Chicago and Pittsburgh. This corridor played a pivotal role in the early development phase of the urban agglomeration, linking the upstream and downstream industries of the manufacturing sector in the Great Lakes region rapidly and effectively through the transportation corridor model. From 1920 to 1960, Chicago, as the monocentric city of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration, experienced rapid expansion along major highways, including Interstates 94, 66, and 55 (Fig. 1a). During this period, other cities surrounding Chicago did not exhibit significant spatial expansion. Chicago established corridors connecting other cities in all directions, effectively organizing the upstream and downstream of the manufacturing sector through an advanced transportation network, which led to the formation of an urban agglomeration centered on Chicago, with Detroit and Cleveland serving as production hubs, Pittsburgh serving as a raw materials processing center, and Toronto and other locations serving as suppliers of agricultural raw materials. By fully utilizing the abundant natural resources and unique geographic location of the Great Lakes region, the manufacturing industry of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration gradually emerged and demonstrated significant economic vitality during its spatial agglomeration evolution. At its peak in the 1960 s, the GDP of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration was comparable to that of all of Japan. The influence of the urban agglomeration extended into various sectors, including agriculture, industry, and retail, across many states in the United States and neighboring provinces in Canada. It is noteworthy that, owing to the considerable influence of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration. Ontario in neighboring Canada, particularly the Toronto region, has been drawn into its sphere of interaction. Ontario has established a corridor extending from Windsor to Toronto that links directly with the Great Lakes urban agglomeration, while the governments of both countries have sought to strengthen this integration through agreements such as NAFTA, which encourage cooperative development.

In the Great Lakes urban agglomeration, Chicago, as the monocentric city, rapidly concentrated resources through its economic strength within a short period, making urban expansion a defining spatial characteristic of the early development of the agglomeration. The rationale behind this spatial agglomeration is threefold: (1) agglomeration meets the needs of manufacturing industry development by providing greater efficiency and more collaborative channels; (2) the spatial expansion of the central city benefits all tiers of cities during this phase, as noncentral cities establish factories to meet the needs of the central city, creating significant employment opportunities; and (3) spatial agglomeration offers economic efficiency. Although spatial agglomeration is not the only means for the economic development of an urban agglomeration at the spatial level, given the transportation and communication conditions of the industrialization era, concentrating around the central city became a practical choice for small and medium-sized cities within the region.

At the macro level, the spatial expansion model of the monocentric city provided momentum for the overall development of the urban agglomeration but also resulted in the “siphoning” of spatial resources from noncentral cities by the central city. Chicago, as the monocentric city, could freely choose to collaborate with other cities of various scales through its commercial influence. However, for noncentral cities, the limitation of having only one central city option within the spatial scope inevitably led to a passive, one-sided partnership with the central city’s industries. This spatial model, after reaching a certain development stage, caused an imbalance in the development speeds between the central and noncentral cities within the urban agglomeration, resulting in the polarization and collapse of spatial development. While the spatial expansion of the central city brought about economic benefits to other cities, it also siphoned off resources, for example, talent, labor, and independent markets from ordinary cities. Once the economic spillover capacity of the monocentric city was weakened, the noncentral cities closely linked to it were the first to suffer the impact.

Polycentricity essentially remains a form of agglomeration, representing an optimization of the original monocentric spatial model of urban agglomerations. This transformation aims to mitigate the vulnerability of the entire system to the economic or demographic fluctuations of a single city, thereby enhancing overall resilience. Since the late twentieth century, major cities across the Great Lakes region have progressively developed metropolitan configurations through the amalgamation of central municipalities with surrounding jurisdictions. In Canada, Toronto consolidated with adjacent areas to establish the Greater Toronto Area, while in the United States a number of core cities, including Cleveland, Pittsburgh, Indianapolis, Detroit, and Milwaukee, expanded their metropolitan boundaries to extend functional reach. Through these processes, each state or province became anchored by a central metropolitan hub, thereby facilitating the broader transition of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration from a monocentric to a polycentric structure. Changes in the median age growth rate of urban populations can reflect, from one perspective, the trend of young people dispersing from central cities to surrounding noncentral cities. The changes in the median age of the population in the central cities of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration provide evidence of this dispersal of young people (Fig. 2). Compared with the changes in the United States’ median population age, from 2011 to 2019, the median age growth rate in the central cities of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration was 0.09%, which was significantly lower than the United States’ median age growth rate of 0.37%, indicating that the Great Lakes urban agglomeration still attracted young people. During the same period, Chicago’s median age growth rate was 0.59%, which was much higher than that of other central cities, reflecting that young people chose primarily to cluster in central cities other than Chicago.

Data source

The population data and median individual income data for cities in the Great Lakes urban agglomeration in the United States include primarily demographic statistics provided by the United States Census Bureau58 and population data from the United States Bureau of Labor Statistics59. These sources offer crucial information on the population size, ethnic composition, and distribution of residents across cities in the Great Lakes region, which is essential for analyzing urban social structures and population mobility. This study focuses on age demographics, particularly changes in average age in central and non-central cities, to understand urban population dynamics and social structures. Industrial and information talent rating data are sourced from the research institution CBRE60 to observe changes in industrial structure and spatial dynamics within cities in the urban agglomeration in recent years. Data from Statistics Canada61 provide information on population, industry, and income in Ontario, Canada, providing insights for the Canadian government. The remote sensing data used in this study were obtained from the ArcGIS Online Map Viewer62.

Results

Phenomenon of decentralization in the great lakes urban agglomeration

Although young populations have increasingly migrated from Chicago to other central cities since the financial crisis, the average population growth rate in most central cities within the Great Lakes urban agglomeration continues to decline, with the exceptions of Indianapolis and Toronto. This study focuses on cities within the Great Lakes urban agglomeration with an average population exceeding 100,000 from 1990 to 2020. A further analysis of population growth rates in the Great Lakes region from 1990 to 2020 reveals that the overall average population growth rate for the region was 0.4%. However, the average population growth rates for central cities with populations exceeding 100,000 (0.07%) and for non-central cities (0.06%) were both lower than the regional average, indicating a persistent demographic stagnation across the urban system (Figure. 3).

Population growth is one of the most tangible indicators of spatial agglomeration, which, in turn, serves as the foundation for the economic development of urban agglomerations. The economic growth of central cities functions as the driving engine of urban agglomerations. However, when central cities begin to lose their ability to attract and concentrate population from surrounding non-central cities, the overall economic performance of the urban agglomeration becomes a critical indicator for assessing shifts in its spatial structure (Figure. 4).

After the collapse of the dot-com bubble in 2001, the population and GDP growth trajectories of central cities within the Great Lakes urban agglomeration began to diverge, as population levels declined even while GDP continued to expand. Although certain years saw population increases, these were primarily a result of sharp population declines in preceding years. After 2012, despite ongoing population loss in the central cities of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration, per capita income in these cities exhibited an overall upward trend. Between 2019 and 2020, per capita income growth in the Great Lakes urban agglomeration reached twice the national average in the United States. Even Detroit, the city most severely affected by the financial crisis, experienced an approximately 8% increase in resident income in 2020 compared to the time of its bankruptcy. Thus, from both a macro perspective of demographic shifts and a micro perspective of individual income dynamics, the economic development of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration no longer relies on spatial agglomeration. Instead, the region has transitioned into a post-urban agglomeration era, characterized by a decentralized spatial structure.

During the 2007–2008 financial crisis and its aftermath (2005–2010), the average correlation coefficient of population growth rates among central cities in the Great Lakes region of the United States reached 0.74. The correlation between central and non-central cities remained strong at 0.82, while non-central cities also exhibited a relatively high correlation of 0.86. These patterns indicate that the population growth rates across the Great Lakes urban agglomeration maintained a high degree of overall cohesion during this period. However, in the 2015–2019 period, the correlation of population growth rates among central cities declined sharply, dropping from 0.74 to 0.45. The most notable change was the correlation between Toronto and Chicago, which fell from 0.77 to 0.06, indicating an almost complete decoupling. The correlation between central and non-central cities also weakened during this period, with the most pronounced decline occurring among non-central cities, where the correlation dropped to 0.56 (Figure. 5a). This suggests that regional population interaction patterns have become increasingly complex.

The correlation of median individual income reflects whether an urban agglomeration has a dominant industry and indicates whether the industrial structures of individual cities are dependent on the central city’s dominant industry or possess their own diversified industries (Figure. 5b). In the first phase, the correlation among central cities in the Great Lakes urban agglomeration was 0.8, with the correlation among central cities within the United States reaching as high as 0.9. In contrast, the correlation of median individual income among residents of non-central cities was only 0.6. Compared with the first phase, the correlation of median individual income growth rates among central cities began to decline in the second phase. The overall correlation of median individual income among residents of various cities in the Great Lakes urban agglomeration from 0.7 decreased to 0.5, whereas the correlation of median individual income among central cities decreased to 0.45. The decline in the median individual income correlation indicates that the single industrial chain centered on the central cities in the Great Lakes urban agglomeration is beginning to dissipate, and diversified industries are gradually forming in the central cities. The level of dependence of non-central cities on central cities is decreasing, and instead, the relationships among non-central cities are strengthening.

On a spatial level, there are two modes of population movement under the agglomeration pattern: one is the influx of the external population into the urban agglomeration, and the other is the migration of the population from non-central cities to central cities within the agglomeration. When the external population flows into the urban agglomeration, the population in the central cities tends to collectively increase, with growth rates decreasing from central to non-central cities. This mode of population movement results in a highly unified positive correlation in population growth among cities within the urban agglomeration. The second mode involves the migration of the population from non-central cities to central cities. Under this mode, the population growth rate of non-central cities exhibits a strong negative correlation with that of central cities, with higher negative correlations for non-central cities located closer to central cities. These two modes of population movement result in a certain degree of regularity in the spatial pattern of population growth within the urban agglomeration. However, during the 2016–2019 period, the population growth rates of cities in the Great Lakes urban agglomeration did not exhibit these two patterns. Instead, irregular spatial population movements were observed, particularly in the spatial relationships between non-central cities and nearby central cities. For example, Peoria and Erie are located on the east and west sides of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration, respectively, with Chicago, Detroit, and Cleveland acting as central cities. However, the correlation of population growth rates between Peoria and Erie was greater than their correlations with these central cities. This irregular pattern can be attributed to decentralization effects in the post-urban agglomeration era (Fig. 6a and b).

Despite the overall economic rise in the Great Lakes urban agglomeration during the 2016–2019 period, the spatial correlation of median individual income growth rates continued to decline. Both the correlations among central cities and between central cities and surrounding non-central cities tended to be low. However, because more residents chose to live in non-central cities during the post-urban agglomeration era, there were exceptions in which the correlation of income growth between some non-central cities increased in the second period. The most notable case is between Buffalo, located at the northeastern edge of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration, and Green Bay, located at the northwestern edge. Despite having no direct transportation links, the correlation of income growth between these two cities strengthened in the second period. Moreover, the overall correlation of the median individual income among the cities in the agglomeration began to weaken, indicating that in the post-urban agglomeration era, the Great Lakes urban agglomeration no longer relies on the industries of central cities but is moving toward diversified industries in each city (Fig. 6c and d).

Discussion and limitation

Discussion

The preceding analysis reveals a consistent weakening of traditional agglomeration effects among cities in the Great Lakes urban agglomeration, as evidenced by the declining correlations between population growth and median household income dynamics. These empirical patterns suggest a gradual loosening of inter-city functional dependencies and a reconfiguration of spatial interconnectivity.

Based on the above analysis results, the post-urban agglomeration refers to a decentralized spatial structure that emerges in the era of AI-driven technological transformation. In this context, the integration of human and AI technologies allows key urban elements, including population, industries, and capital, to detach from their dependence on specific central cities, thereby reshaping the traditional urban agglomeration model. This decentralization is closely linked to the rise of remote work, the development of distributed industrial systems, the widespread application of artificial intelligence in both production and daily life, and the comprehensive upgrading of information infrastructure. The spatial reshaping occurs as population mobility, industrial development, and social connections are no longer constrained by physical space. Consequently, each city within the agglomeration can leverage its unique value, gradually moving away from both monocentric and polycentric spatial structures. In this process, the traditional agglomeration effects of urban agglomerations are replaced by decentralization.

In the post-urban agglomeration era, the correlation between population and median individual income growth rates among cities has become one of the key indicators for assessing the impact of future spatial decentralization. This indicator is based on the premise that in the post-urban agglomeration era, the entire urban agglomeration, as well as individual cities and residents, is influenced by information-driven work patterns.

Limitation

This study provides an innovative exploration of the concepts of “post-urban agglomerations” and “decentralization,” offering a pioneering summary that advances theoretical understanding of spatial restructuring in the AI era. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged in order to guide further inquiry.

First, the paper introduces the novel concept of the post-urban agglomeration and links it to the phenomenon of decentralization. Such a conceptual innovation requires considerable space for definition and theoretical framing. Given the constraints of article length, the emphasis on conceptual elaboration inevitably limited the breadth of empirical analysis. As a result, cross-sectional comparisons, longitudinal perspectives, and multidimensional empirical evidence were not fully developed. Future research should strive to balance conceptual clarity with empirical richness, for example, by incorporating diverse data sources such as social network datasets, business registration information, and mobility records. These approaches would not only strengthen the empirical grounding of the concept but also enhance the external validity of conclusions, ensuring that the post-urban agglomeration is not solely a theoretical abstraction.

Second, the main contribution of this paper lies in identifying decentralization as a pathway to conceptualizing post-urban agglomerations. Accordingly, the methodological design has leaned toward a macro-phenomenological orientation, while devoting less attention to mechanism-oriented analysis. Specifically, Pearson correlation and GIS-based methods served as intuitive and efficient tools to reveal spatial trends in intercity relationships with respect to economic, demographic, and industrial variables. However, the Pearson correlation coefficient has notable constraints: it captures only linear associations, is highly sensitive to extreme values and temporal fluctuations, and does not distinguish between correlation and causality. As a result, it oversimplifies the complex, nonlinear, and multi-scalar dynamics of intercity linkages. To move beyond descriptive tendencies, future research should adopt more diverse methodological frameworks, such as causal inference models (e.g., Granger causality tests), nonlinear statistical approaches (e.g., mutual information entropy), and network-based analyses. These tools would provide more robust insights into the mechanisms underlying decentralization in post-urban regions.

Third, this study primarily relied on pre-pandemic data to ensure stability and comparability. While this choice minimized distortions caused by the extraordinary volatility of the COVID-19 period, it also restricted the analysis from capturing the profound changes reshaping urban systems during and after the pandemic. The pandemic altered commuting patterns, accelerated the adoption of remote work, and intensified the rise of digital economies, all of which strongly influenced decentralization processes. Analyses confined to pre-pandemic data may therefore lag behind ongoing spatial transformations. Future research should systematically track post-pandemic datasets, focusing on mobility dynamics, industrial restructuring, and the redistribution of residential spaces. This will be crucial to test and refine the reliability of the post-urban agglomeration concept under dynamic and uncertain conditions.

Conclusion

This study first reviews the scholarly literature on urban agglomerations, tracing their evolution from the early stages of development to the multiple challenges they face in the contemporary era. Building on this foundation, an analysis of macro-level data, including urban population and GDP trends across the Great Lakes region over the past three decades, reveals that, following the 2008 financial crisis, the population agglomeration effect within the region’s urban system has gradually weakened, even as its economic growth exhibited signs of recovery. Against the backdrop of the transition from the information era to the AI era, this study employs correlation tests in conjunction with GIS-based spatial analysis to examine the spatial characteristics and formative mechanisms of decentralization within post-urban agglomerations. On this basis, the following conclusions are:

Firstly, an analysis of population and GDP growth in major cities within the Great Lakes urban agglomeration reveals that, under the influence of decentralization, urban economic development is no longer dependent on the agglomeration effect of the urban agglomeration. Driven by AI technology, individual mobility within space and personal value creation have become more significant contributors to economic growth in the post-urban agglomeration era.

Secondly, the degree of intercity connectivity within the Great Lakes urban agglomeration has declined, exhibiting non-patterned spatial transformations without a central orientation. This shift has weakened the macro-spatial manifestation of First Law of Geography64. A comparative analysis of the correlation between recent population growth and per capita income growth in the Great Lakes urban agglomeration reveals a continuous decline in intercity connectivity in the AI era. Simultaneously, the dependence among central cities has weakened, and non-central cities are progressively disengaging from the influence of central cities. These trends indicate a significant transformation in the spatial structure of the Great Lakes urban agglomeration, marking its transition into the post-urban agglomeration era. In the future, the degree of decentralization is expected to deepen further, solidifying decentralization as the operational mechanism of the post-urban agglomeration spatial structure.

Third, during the pandemic lockdowns, the Great Lakes urban agglomeration not only accelerated the process of spatial reshaping through mobility restrictions, but more importantly, it demonstrated through practice that decentralization can provide economic viability and resilience in the face of sudden disruptions during the post-urban agglomeration era. Cities within the post-urban agglomeration system have increasingly relied on the information industry to reduce their dependence on the resource concentration capacity of central cities. Non-central cities have gradually transformed from being mere resource suppliers into self-sustaining entities with diversified development trajectories.

Finally, spatial decentralization in the post-urban agglomeration era fundamentally represents a pursuit of spatial equity, addressing the polarization and collapse dynamics between central and non-central cities that arise from traditional agglomeration-based spatial structures. As workplaces are no longer constrained by fixed locations, individuals—facilitated by digital technology and remote work—are granted greater spatial mobility and autonomy within the post-urban agglomeration region. This shift ensures that all cities and individuals within the post-urban agglomeration framework have equal opportunities for spatial development under a decentralized spatial paradigm.
