Contextual Bandit Optimization with Pre-Trained Neural Networks
Mikhail Terekhov

Abstract
Bandit optimization is a difficult problem, especially if the reward model is high-dimensional. When rewards are modeled by neural networks, sublinear regret has only been shown under strong assumptions, usually when the network is extremely wide. In this thesis, we investigate how pre-training can help us in the regime of smaller models. We consider a stochastic contextual bandit with the rewards modeled by a multi-layer neural network. The last layer is a linear predictor, and the layers before it are a black box neural architecture, which we call a representation network. We model pre-training as an initial guess of the weights of the representation network provided to the learner. To leverage the pre-trained weights, we introduce a novel algorithm we call Explore Twice then Commit (E2TC). During its two stages of exploration, the algorithm first estimates the last layer’s weights using Ridge regression, and then runs Stochastic Gradient Decent jointly on all the weights. For a locally convex loss function, we provide conditions on the pre-trained weights under which the algorithm can learn efficiently. Under these conditions, we show sublinear regret of E2TC when the dimension of the last layer and number of actions K K are much smaller than the horizon T T . In the weak training regime, when only the last layer is learned, the problem reduces to a misspecified linear bandit. We introduce a measure of misspecification ε 0 \varepsilon_{0} for this bandit and use it to provide bounds O ⁡ ( ε 0 ​ d ​ K ​ T + ( K ​ T ) 4 / 5 ) O(\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{d}KT+(KT)^{4/5}) or O ~ ​ ( ε 0 ​ d ​ K ​ T + d 1 / 3 ​ ( K ​ T ) 2 / 3 ) \widetilde{O}(\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{d}KT+d^{1/3}(KT)^{2/3}) on the regret, depending on regularization strength. The first of these bounds has a dimension-independent sublinear term, made possible by the stochasticity of contexts. We also run experiments to evaluate the regret of E2TC and sample complexity of its exploration in practice.

Chapter 1
Introduction

In machine learning, empirical progress often outpaces careful theoretical analysis. Practitioners have collected a wide array of advice concerning model architectures, hyperparameter tuning, and the required amount of data, while theorists are struggling to justify the choices formally.

Pre-training
is one of the well-established practices in the engineer’s toolbox that is yet to see a theoretical treatment. In this thesis, we formalize the benefits of pre-training with a focus on applications to contextual multi-armed bandits.

Large neural network are typically pre-trained on a general task and then fine-tuned on another one, for which data is scarce. The classic example comes from computer vision, where we pre-train a classifier on ImageNet
[
9
]
with millions of images, and refine the weights on a smaller dataset for a medical imaging task. To comply with the task specification, like the number of classes, we need to change the last layer’s architecture and retrain it completely. The rest of the weights only have to be adjusted slightly.

The reasons for the wide adoption of pre-training are clear. Data for specialized tasks is expensive, so we would like to rely on the more general, cheaper data as much as possible. Pre-trained weights can also come from a model learned in an unsupervised fashion. The hope is that intermediate layers of the network learn some “general features” that encode meaningful information about the input sample. These features should be easier to use for a simple classifier than the original input. On top of that, the success of large language models on a variety of tasks out of the box
[
6
]
makes pre-training even more appealing compared to training a smaller model.

From a theoretical perspective, pre-training is less understood. How would we quantify the influence of the pre-trained weights on the performance after fine-tuning? The answer naturally depends on some notion of “quality” of these weights relative to the task at hand. In this thesis, we formalize this intuition. Depending on the size of the neural network, theorists employ different techniques to explain its generalization ability. In the regime of extremely wide networks, we now know that Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) in the parameter space can be viewed as following the so-called kernel gradient in the function space, with the inner product dictated by the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)
[
19
]
. When the networks are smaller, we can instead rely on assumptions on the loss function. Classical optimization theory can only provide guarantees of convergence of SGD to the minimum for very limited classes of losses, such as convex
[
36
]
or strongly convex
[
33
]
functions. Unfortunately, loss landscapes that arise during neural network training are highly non-convex
[
27
]
. A weaker assumption that we could make is that the loss is only
locally
convex around its minimum. In general, this is not sufficient: how would we know that SGD is initialized close enough to the minimum that the trajectory falls into the convex basin of the loss? This is where pre-training comes into the theoretical story. A key idea in this thesis is that by imposing restrictions on the pre-trained weights, we can ensure that SGD at initialization does fall into the convex basin of the loss and stays there during training.

Contextual bandit optimization
is concerned with maximizing the cumulative reward of a learning algorithm over a dataset. Data samples, also referred to as
contexts
, are shown one by one, and the learner must choose an
action
from a given set and receive a
reward
. Algorithm’s goal can be stated as minimizing regret, which is the expected difference of the sum of received rewards from its theoretical maximum. Crucial to solving this problem is to balance the trade-off of exploration versus exploitation. Exploration takes the form of choosing more diverse actions, also called “arms”. It gives the algorithm valuable information about their potential rewards, but it also detracts from the cumulative reward, since the algorithm is not exploiting the arms it already knows to be good.

In the contextual bandit setting, analysis in the NTK regime can be used to show sublinear regret when training finite-width neural networks on the observed rewards. NN-UCB
[
21
]
is the first algorithm to achieve this. Because of the limitations of NTK, however, it requires that the width of the network is polynomial in the horizon of the problem. The regret bound for NN-UCB scales as
O(T^{(2d-1)/2d})
, where
T
is the horizon and
d
is the dimension of the context. This thesis solves a similar problem as
[
21
]
. We also work in a contextual bandit setting, with the rewards modeled by a neural network trained by SGD. However, we are lifting the restriction on the width of the network, replacing it by assumptions on the loss function.

We approach the bandit problem using an Explore-then-commit technique (ETC). In terms of the attained regret guarantees, ETC-type algorithms are known to be suboptimal compared to optimistic algorithms
[
24
]
. Moreover, they are not naturally anytime algorithms, i.e. the learner must know the stopping time before it selects the first arm. In spite of these deficiencies, we chose to focus on ETC for two key reasons. First, they are easier to analyze. Random variable dependency structures in optimistic algorithms are more involved than those in ETC, where in the exploration phase the actions and rewards are i.i.d. Before dealing with this technical challenge, it is meaningful to look into the peculiarities of pre-training in a simplified setup. Second, i.i.d. action-reward pairs during exploration provide data for a classical learning problem, which might be of independent interest in non-adaptive applications. Bounds on the sample complexity of learning from pre-trained weights are the bulk of the results of this thesis. These bounds can be translated into regret bounds for ETC using the technique described in Section
4
.

The pre-trained network provides us only with a feature map, therefore we would still need to tune the last year from scratch. The initialization of the last layer weights plays a crucial role as it can lead the SGD path in or out of the convex basin of the loss. To solve this problem, we introduce an algorithm we call
Explore Twice then Commit (E2TC)
. The name reflects the two stages of exploration that the algorithm performs. In the first stage, the weights of the last layer are estimated from the random action-reward pairs using Ridge regression. In the second stage, pre-trained weights and the last layer are jointly refined with SGD. After exploration, the algorithm “commits” to the found weights and chooses actions greedily until the end of the episode. The core result of this thesis is that under suitable conditions on the loss, the algorithm can achieve a regret of
\widetilde{O}(T^{4/5})
in the stochastic context setup. This is worse than the typical
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
regret of optimism-based algorithms, but it compares favorably to the
\widetilde{O}(T^{(2d-1)/2d})
of NN-UCB. We highlight that out results hold under different conditions than that of NN-UCB. Our assumptions include realizability of the reward function, boundedness of the weights, and convexity of the mean squared error in a basin around the true weights. We also note that NN-UCB works with adversarial contexts, while we only consider the stochastic case.

The thesis is organised as follows. In the following section, we review the related work on neural contextual bandits and adjacent topics. In Chapter
2
, we introduce the bandit setup and the main algorithm formally. We also discuss the techniques for translating guarantees for learning in the exploration phase into the regret of ETC algorithms. Chapter
3
contains a detailed analysis of the Ridge regression used in the first stage of exploration, based on the work
[
18
]
. Without the second stage of exploration and training, our algorithm solves a misspecified linear bandit problem, for which we show a regret bound of
\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{d}KT+(KT)^{4/5})
, where
K
is the number of actions,
d
is the dimension of the last layer and
\varepsilon_{0}
is a measure of misspecification of the pre-trained weights. This regret guarantee contains a linear term
1

For adversarial linear bandits with
\varepsilon
-misspecification in
\infty
-norm, a lower bound of
\Omega(\varepsilon\min\{T,K\})
was shown in
[

. Hence, for large numbers of arms, linear dependence on
T
is unavoidable in general.
, but it does not rely on the convexity of the loss. In Chapter
4
, we make a detour into high-probability guarantees for SGD. Using a time-uniform version of the Hoeffding-Azuma inequality

]
, we show that with high probability, the SGD trajectory will stay in a convex basin of a generally non-convex loss under a suitable choice of the learning rate. Next, in Chapter
5
, we piece together the analyses of Ridge regression and of SGD to provide guarantees for E2TC. We also study the optimal choices of hyperparameters for the algorithm. Finally, in Chapter

1
Related Work

NN-UCB comes from a larger body of work on neural contextual bandits. Most of the results in this area are in the NTK regime, and only consider very wide neural networks. An established non-asymptotic analysis
[
1
]
reduces training and inference with a wide neural network to kernel regression with Gaussian Processes (GP). Bandits with reward functions sampled from a GP are better understood
[
37
,
7
]
, with contextual
[
23
]
and misspecified
[
5
]
cases having been studied. Among the neural contextual bandit algorithms is EE-Net
[
3
]
, which models both the reward function and the exploration bounty with neural networks and has a
O(\sqrt{T\log T})
regret bound. In addition to a polynomial lower bound on the width of the network, the analysis of EE-Net requires a strong condition on the contexts: individual actions in each context should be sampled i.i.d. from some distribution. This assumption is not satisfied, for example, in a classification setup. It is stronger than asking that entire contexts are sampled i.i.d., while the actions can be dependent, as we do.

An empirical study
[
34
]
compares several approaches to approximate Bayesian inference combined with Thompson Sampling in the contextual bandit setup. It reveales that “while it deserves further investigation, it seems that decoupling representation learning and uncertainty estimation improves performance”. In particular, the study analyzes an algorithm that trains a deep representation network, mapping actions to meaningful representations. These representations are then used by a Bayesian linear regression to estimate the reward. This is equivalent to using a neural network to regress the reward, where uncertainty is modeled only for the linear last layer’s weights. Neural-LinUCB
[
40
]
is a algorithm for neural contextual bandits built on this insight. It performs LinUCB-style exploration in the last layer of the learned model and trains the representation network with SGD. The algorithm achieves an
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
regret, again requiring the width of the network to grow polynomially with
T
.

The philosophy of
deep representation and shallow exploration
, exhibited in the above two works, is in line with the approach of this thesis. In our case, the pre-trained network provides representation, which might or might not be fine-tuned, while the last layer is trained from scratch and thus induces the bulk of our uncertainty about the weights. The effect of performing one step of SGD on the representation was carefully analyzed in
[
2
]
in the case of two-layer networks. The authors theoretically show that with a single gradient descent step makes the Ridge regression on the new representation outperform a wide range of kernelized Ridge regression predictors. This conclusion is mirrored in this thesis in the analysis of E2TC, which now makes many update steps on the features, and in the experiments, which show that “unfreezing” the representation network weights brings in significant improvement in practice.

Outside the NTK regime, it is common to make assumptions on the shape of loss function. Recently,
[
30
]
introduced the GO-UCB algorithm, operating in a setup rather similar to ours. GO-UCB trains a neural network using the mean squared error, and its
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
regret guarantee is dependent on the local strong convexity of the loss around the minimum and on a strong condition on the Hessian called “self-concordance” of the loss at the minimum. These conditions are still not enough to guarantee convergence. To provide a reasonable initialization for the parameters, GO-UCB relies on an Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) oracle, which solves the intractable global optimization problem.

Arguably, even local strong convexity is unreasonable to expect when training deep neural networks. Strong convexity implies that the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{min}(H)
of the Hessian of the loss is bounded from below by a constant
\mu
. This constant is then used to set the learning rate for training, and the regret guarantees typically have a factor of
1/\mu
. Empirical studies of the Hessian in deep learning
[
35
,
11
,
41
]
, however, show that the Hessian of the loss during training and at the minimum has a large nullspace. This nullspace appears, for example, when the network architecture is overparameterized, i.e. different parameters correspond to the same mapping of inputs to outputs. Any ReLU network is overparameterized because of the
positive scale-invariance
of ReLU:

\forall\lambda>0,\ x\in\mathbb{R}\qquad\text{ReLU}(\lambda x)=\lambda\text{ReLU}(x).

(1.1)

Thus, scaling the parameters of a fully-connected layer by some
\lambda>0
and the parameters of the next one by
1/\lambda
does not change the mapping given by the network. Even in the case when the problem is under-parameterized and Hessian does not have a nullspace, it is unclear how would the algorithm obtain a realistic lower bound
\mu
.

Unlike strong convexity, non-strong local convexity at the minimum will necessarily hold. This makes it more natural to expect that it will hold locally around the minimum, although this is not strictly guaranteed. For under-parameterized networks, one expects the local minima of the loss to be isoloated and surrounded by a region where the loss is convex. In the over-parameterized regime, the set of minimizers of the loss will generally be a manifold, and local convexity around each point of this manifold cannot hold unless the manifold is an affine subspace. In this case, a better framework is provided by the
Polyak-Łojasiewicz
(PŁ) condition. A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
\mu
-PŁ iff

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\qquad\lVert\nabla f(x)\rVert^{2}\geq 2\mu\left(f(x)-\min_{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x^{\prime})\right).

(1.2)

This condition is a strict relaxation of
\mu
-strong convexity, and it also implies exponential convergence of gradient descent
[
20
]
. In
[
29
]
, the authors argue that the (slightly modified) PŁ condition is satisfied for losses of over-parameterized networks, connect this condition to the minimal eigenvalue of the NTK matrix, and show that SGD converges exponentially to the optimum in expectation. This thesis focuses on the under-parameterized regime and non-strong convexity, but we believe the results can be extended to PŁ losses and thus cover the over-parameterized regime as well.

The regime when the pre-trained weights are kept fixed, and only the last layer is trained was dubbed
weak training
by
[
1
]
. Weak training in the bandit setup corresponds to misspecified contextual linear bandits. For adversarial contexts, the authors of
[
25
]
show that a modification of LinUCB achieves the regret bound
O(\varepsilon T\sqrt{d\log T}+d\sqrt{T}\log T)
. Here,
\varepsilon
is the maximum possible misspecification in the reward. For stochastic contexts, we can relax the
\varepsilon
to a different measure of misspecification, based on the expected difference in the reward instead of the
\infty
-norm distance. It also turns out that we can substitute the dependence on
d
in the sublinear term by a dependence on
K
, the number of actions. This thesis focuses on the case
K\ll d
, where this substitution is beneficial. At the same time, the dependence on
T
of our bounds is worse (
T^{4/5}
instead of
\sqrt{T}
), because our algorithm is a flavor of ETC.

We evaluate the quality of the pre-trained weights based on how close they are to the true weights with a certain distance measure. One can instead look into the data distribution from which these weights were generated, and compare the distribution to the one provided by the bandit. This approach was taken by
[
42
]
, who introduce a formal measure of distance between these distributions and a no-regret algorithm that utilizes the pre-training data directly. This approach is conceptually attractive, but limited in its coverage. For us, the interesting cases of pre-trained weights come from tasks that do not correspond to the task the bandit is trying to solve, e.g. they were trained in an unsupervised fashion.

2
Problem Formulation

We consider the following contextual bandit setup. An agent observes a random sequence of contexts
C_{1},\ldots,C_{T}
, each context providing features for
K
actions:
C_{t}=(X_{t,1},\ldots,X_{t,K})
. Individual features
X_{t,i}\in\mathcal{X}
come from an arbitrary set
\mathcal{X}
. The actions at step
t
are associated with unknown random rewards
(r_{t,1},\ldots,r_{t,K})
. The agent selects an action
A_{t}\in[K]
and observes the reward
r_{t,A_{t}}
. An optimal action at step
t
will be denoted as

A_{t}^{*}\in\argmax_{a\in[K]}\mathbb{E}[r_{t,a}].

(2.1)

We define the pseudo-regret of the agent on the time segment from
t_{1}
to
t_{2}
as

R_{t_{1}:t_{2}}=\sum_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left(r_{t,A_{t}^{*}}-r_{t,A_{t}}\right),

(2.2)

We will also use notation
R_{t}:=r_{t,A_{t}^{*}}-r_{t,A_{t}}
for immediate pseudo-regret at step
t
. Agent’s regret on the segment
t_{1}:t_{2}
is
\mathbb{E}[R_{t_{1}:t_{2}}]
, where the expectation is taken over the observed contexts, associated rewards, and randomness of the policy. The objective of the agent is to minimize
\mathbb{E}[R_{1:T}]
.

We will approximate the rewards using a neural network mapping input features to predicted rewards:

x\mapsto\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta}(x),

(2.3)

where
x\in\mathcal{X}
are the input features,
\varphi_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the
representation network
, parameterized by
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
.
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the weights of the linear layer on top of the representation network. The joint set of weights of the prediction network will be denoted by
\omega=(\mathbf{w},\theta)
. Before seeing the contexts, the agent is given
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
, the weights of the pre-trained representation network. An initial guess of the weights for the last layer is
not
provided. The dimension
d
of the last layer weights plays an important role in our analysis and appears in the regret bounds. In contrast, the dimension
d_{0}
, which would usually be much larger than
d
, does not appear in the analysis — only the
2
-norm
\lVert\theta\rVert
of the representation weights matters. We will call this bandit problem a
stochastic contextual bandit with pre-training
.

Now we will discuss the assumptions that we make about the process generating the contexts and the rewards. We begin with the contexts:

Assumption 1
(Context distribution).

C_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}_{C}
are i.i.d. samples from a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{C}
over
\mathcal{X}^{K}
. Furthermore, individual actions
X_{t,a}
have the same marginal distributions
\mathcal{D}_{X}
over
\mathcal{X}
:

\forall t\in[T],\ a\in[K]\qquad X_{t,a}\sim\mathcal{D}_{X}.

(2.4)

Note that this assumption is weaker than requiring that individual actions within the context are independently distributed. For example, if we consider classification in the bandit setup, the contexts are the incoming data samples, and they are often i.i.d., while the actions are given by pairs of the data sample and the chosen class, and thus are not independent. The second part of the assumption is there purely for convenience. If the incoming contexts
C_{t}
are i.i.d., but the marginal distributions of actions are not identical, we can make them identical by randomly permuting the actions in the context.

Next, we will assume that our reward model is realizable:

Assumption 2
(Realizability).

There exist true weights
\mathbf{w}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
and a distribution
\mathcal{H}
such that the random reward
r_{t,a}
corresponding to input features
X_{t,a}
is given by

r_{t,a}=\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t,a})+\eta_{t},\quad\eta_{t}\sim\mathcal{H},

(2.5)

where
\eta_{t}
are i.i.d. zero-mean noise variables coming from
\mathcal{H}
.

Realizability gives us a general way of defining the “quality” of the pre-trained weights
\theta_{0}
. We can, in some appropriate way, define the difference between
\theta_{0}
and
\theta^{*}
and provide regret bounds based on this difference. Depending on whether we only learn the last layer or also fine-tune the representation weights
\theta
, the exact difference definition will change.

Finally, for technical convenience, we will require that all objects in our regret model are bounded and differentiable:

Assumption 3
(Regularity).

We have
\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert\leq B_{\mathbf{w}}
and
\lVert\theta^{*}\rVert\leq B_{\theta}
for some
B_{\mathbf{w}},B_{\theta}>0
. For each
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\varphi_{\theta}(x)
is differentiable w.r.t.
\theta
. Its Jacobian is given by
\partial\varphi_{\theta}(x)/\partial\theta=J_{\theta}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{0}}
. For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
such that
\lVert\theta\rVert\leq B_{\theta}
, it holds that
\lVert\varphi_{\theta}(x)\rVert\leq B_{\varphi}
and
\lVert J_{\theta}(x)\rVert_{2}\leq L_{\varphi}
for some
B_{\varphi},L_{\varphi}>0
. Finally,
|\eta_{t}|\leq B_{\eta}
a.s. for all
t
.

Under this assumption and realizability, the rewards will also be bounded, and we will use
B_{r}
for this bound for brevity:

|r_{t,a}|\leq B_{r}:=B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}+B_{\eta}\qquad\text{a.s.}

(2.6)

For an example bandit setup that approximately satisfies these assumptions, the reader is invited to look at Section
13
, where we evaluate E2TC on MNIST classification.

Algorithm

We attack the bandit problem described above using an algorithm which we call
Explore Twice then Commit (E2TC)
. As the name suggests, it proceeds in three stages. The details are presented in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Explore Twice then Commit (E2TC) algorithm for pre-trained bandit problems.

In the first stage,
T_{1}
random actions
A_{t}\sim U([K])
are selected and the rewards from these actions collected. The input features
X_{t,A_{t}}
selected during this stage will for brevity be denoted as
X_{t}
, and the corresponding observed rewards — by
r_{t}
. Since the actions are chosen independently and uniformly, Assumption
1
guarantees that
X_{t}
are i.i.d. according to
\mathcal{D}_{X}
. We use Ridge regression with regularization strength
\lambda
to compute
\mathbf{w}_{0}
, an initial estimate of the weights of the last layer. We use
misspecified
representations
\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})
in the regression. The problem that we are solving is an instance of
random design Ridge regression
, and we can adapt the analysis
[
18
]
to provide guarantees for
\mathbf{w}_{0}
. These guarantees are given in terms of the covariance of representations. For a given
\theta
, this covariance is defined as

\Sigma(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\varphi_{\theta}(X)^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta}(X)\right].

(2.7)

Here and in the following, the expectation w.r.t.
X
implies that
X\sim\mathcal{D}_{X}
. Ridge regression proceeds by providing an estimate of the covariance at the pre-trained weights. This estimate is given by

\widehat{\Sigma(\theta_{0})}=\frac{1}{T_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t}).

(2.8)

Using it, Ridge regression estimates the weights of the last layer as

\mathbf{w}_{0}=(\widehat{\Sigma(\theta_{0})}+\lambda I)^{-1}\frac{1}{T_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}r_{t}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t}).

(2.9)

In the second stage, we perform
T_{2}
more random actions and collect the rewards. We will use notation
\widetilde{X}_{t}
for the features selected during this stage.
\widetilde{X}_{t}
are also i.i.d. according to
\mathcal{D}_{X}
. The observed rewards are
\tilde{r}_{t}
. We run SGD on the joint weights
\omega=(\mathbf{w},\theta)
of the prediction network, initializing it at
(\mathbf{w}_{0},\theta_{0})
. SGD minimizes the
true risk

\mathfrak{R}:\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{w}}\times\mathcal{B}_{\theta}\to\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w},\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X,r}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta}(X)-r\right)^{2}\right]

(2.10)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)\right)^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\left[\eta^{2}\right],

(2.11)

where
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{w}}=\{\mathbf{w}:\ \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\leq B_{\mathbf{w}}\}
is the domain of the weights of the last layer,
\mathcal{B}_{\theta}=\{\theta:\ \lVert\theta\rVert\leq B_{\theta}\}
is the domain of the weights of the representation network,
r=\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)+\eta
is the random reward associated with the feature
X
and
\eta\sim\mathcal{H}
is the noise independent of
X
. At step
t
, gradient estimators of the risk at the current weights
(\mathbf{w}_{t},\theta_{t})
are given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}=2(\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}_{t}\varphi_{\theta_{t}}(\widetilde{X}_{t})-\tilde{r}_{t})\varphi_{\theta_{t}}(\widetilde{X}_{t}),

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}=2(\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}_{t}\varphi_{\theta_{t}}(\widetilde{X}_{t})-\tilde{r}_{t})J_{\theta_{t}}(\widetilde{X}_{t})^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}_{t}.

(2.13)

Under Assumptions
2
and
3
, these estimators are bounded and unbiased, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\mid\mathbf{w}_{t},\theta_{t}\right]=\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}_{t},\theta_{t}),\quad\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}\mid\mathbf{w}_{t},\theta_{t}]=\nabla_{\theta}\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}_{t},\theta_{t})

(2.14)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\|\leq D_{\mathbf{w}}:=(4B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}+2B_{\eta})B_{\varphi}\qquad\text{a.s.,}

(2.15)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}\|\leq D_{\theta}:=(4B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}+2B_{\eta})L_{\varphi}B_{\mathbf{w}}\qquad\text{a.s.}

(2.16)

The algorithm updates the weights using the rules

\mathbf{w}_{t+1}\leftarrow\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{w}}}(\mathbf{w}_{t}-\zeta_{\mathbf{w}}(\widehat{\Sigma(\theta_{0})}+\lambda I)^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}),\quad\theta_{t+1}\leftarrow\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{\theta}}(\theta_{t}-\zeta_{\theta}\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}).

(2.17)

Separate learning rates
\zeta_{\mathbf{w}}
and
\zeta_{\theta}
can be used to update
\mathbf{w}
and
\theta
in practice. Projection
\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}
onto a set
\mathcal{B}
is defined in the standard way:

\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}(x)=\argmin_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert.

(2.18)

Since
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{w}}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\theta}
are convex, projection onto them is unique. An important subtlety in (
2.17
) is that the update step for
\mathbf{w}
is multiplied on the left by
(\widehat{\Sigma(\theta_{0})}+\lambda I)^{-1}
. Doing this using a general positive-definite matrix is called
pre-conditioning
of SGD and it is sometimes used to improve convergence of SGD in practice. It also leads to different theoretical guarantees, an effect we discuss in more detail in Section
10
. For now, it suffices to say that the guarantees for Ridge regression cannot be plugged into the guarantees for vanilla SGD, but with this pre-conditioning, it becomes possible. SGD outputs the estimated weights
\overline{\omega}=(\overline{\mathbf{w}},\overline{\theta})
as the average of the trajectory:

\overline{\mathbf{w}}=\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\mathbf{w}_{t},\quad\overline{\theta}=\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\theta_{t}.

(2.19)

We will provide a high-probability upper bound on the
suboptimality gap
of these estimates compared to
(\mathbf{w}^{*},\theta^{*})
:

\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\mathbf{w}},\overline{\theta})-\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}^{*},\theta^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\overline{\theta}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)\right)^{2}\right].

(2.20)

As we argue in the next section, such guarantees translate into upper bounds on the regret of E2TC.

4
Low risk to low regret

Suboptimality gap in the form (
2.20
) is clearly a measure of quality of the approximation of
(\mathbf{w}^{*},\theta^{*})
by
(\overline{\mathbf{w}},\overline{\theta})
. An intuition behind the main result in this section is guided by this observation. When the approximation is good, greedily selecting an action according to the approximate weights cannot be too much worse than selecting the optimal action. Since the risk function deals with the mean squared error, however, we have to take a square root to translate it into instant regret, which is measured as absolute difference.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let E2TC be run on a stochastic contextual bandit with pre-training. Let Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
hold. Assume also that the weights
(\overline{\mathbf{w}},\overline{\theta})
after the two exploration phases satisfy the following bound with
\mathbb{P}\geq 1-\delta
:

\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\mathbf{w}},\overline{\theta})-\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}^{*},\theta^{*})<\varepsilon(T_{1},T_{2},\delta).

(2.21)

Then the regret of E2TC will be bounded by

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1:T}\right]\leq 2B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}T\delta+2B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}(T_{1}+T_{2})+K(T-T_{1}-T_{2})\sqrt{\varepsilon(T_{1},T_{2},\delta)}.

(2.22)

For a given high-probability bound of the form (
2.21
), this theorem provides a recipe for selecting
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
by minimizing (
2.22
). As an example, SGD for convex functions (assuming
T_{1}=0)
will give us a guarantee of the form

\varepsilon=O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{T_{2}}}\right).

(2.23)

Then, for
K\ll T
, we can select
T_{2}=(KT)^{4/5}\log^{1/5}T
and
\delta=T_{2}/T
to get a bound

\mathbb{E}[R_{1:T}]=O\left((KT)^{4/5}\log^{1/5}T\right).

(2.24)

For strongly-convex functions
[
33
]
, we can instead get a bound of the form

\varepsilon=O\left(\frac{\log((\log T_{2})/\delta)}{T_{2}}\right),

(2.25)

leading to a regret guarantee
\mathbb{E}[R_{1:T}]=\widetilde{O}((KT)^{2/3})
for an appropriate
T_{2}
.

Proof
(Theorem
2.1
).

We will use notation
\overline{\omega}=(\overline{\mathbf{w}},\overline{\theta}),\ \omega^{*}=(\mathbf{w}^{*},\theta^{*})
, and
\omega_{t}=(\mathbf{w}_{t},\theta_{t})
throughout the proof. Let the event
S
(from “success”) be

S=\left\{\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\omega})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})<\varepsilon(T_{1},T_{2},\delta)\right\}.

(2.26)

Its complement will be called
F
. By assumption,
\mathbb{P}[F]\leq\delta
.
Next, given an arbitrary action
A_{t}
, we can bound its immediate regret by

\mathbb{E}[R_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t,A^{*}_{t}})-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t,A_{t}})]\leq 2B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}.

(2.27)

In steps starting from
T_{1}+T_{2}+1
, the action is chosen as

A_{t}=\argmax_{a\in[K]}\overline{\mathbf{w}}\varphi_{\overline{\theta}}(X_{t,a}),

(2.28)

so we can bound the immediate regret using the quality of the estimate
\overline{\omega}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[R_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t,A^{*}_{t}})-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t,A_{t}})\right]

(2.29)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t,A^{*}_{t}})-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t,A_{t}})|\ A_{t}\neq A_{t}^{*}\right]\mathbb{P}\left[A_{t}\neq A_{t}^{*}\right]

(2.30)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t,A^{*}_{t}})-\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\overline{\theta}}(X_{t,A^{*}_{t}})+\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\overline{\theta}}(X_{t,A_{t}})-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t,A_{t}})

\displaystyle\qquad+\underbrace{\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\overline{\theta}}(X_{t,A^{*}_{t}})-\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\overline{\theta}}(X_{t,A_{t}})}_{\leq 0,\text{ since $A_{t}$ maximizes $\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\overline{\theta}}$}}|\ A_{t}\neq A^{*}_{t}\big]\mathbb{P}\left[A_{t}\neq A_{t}^{*}\right]

(2.31)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{a=1}^{K}\left|\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t,a})-\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\overline{\theta}}(X_{t,a})\right|\right].

(2.32)

If S holds, then the cumulative regret in the “commit” phase of the algorithm can be bounded with the help of (
2.32
).
\mathbb{E}\left[R_{T_{1}+T_{2}+1:T}\mid S\right]=

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\sum_{t=T_{1}+T_{2}+1}^{T}\left(\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t,A^{*}_{t}})-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t,A_{t}})\right)\ \right|S\right]

(2.33)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\sum_{t=T_{1}+T_{2}+1}^{T}\sum_{a=1}^{K}\left|\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t,a})-\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\overline{\theta}}(X_{t,a})\right|\ \right|S\right]

(2.34)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\left(\sum_{t=T_{1}+T_{2}+1}^{T}\sum_{a=1}^{K}\left|\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t,a})-\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\overline{\theta}}(X_{t,a})\right|\right)^{2}\ \right|S\right]}

(2.35)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{K(T-T_{1}-T_{2})\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\sum_{t=T_{1}+T_{2}+1}^{T}\sum_{a=1}^{K}\left(\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t,a})-\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\overline{\theta}}(X_{t,a})\right)^{2}\ \right|S\right]}.

(2.36)

Analyzing the randomness in (
2.36
) carefully, we see that it is coming from two independent sources:
\overline{\omega}
is random, and it depends on
C_{1},\ldots,C_{T_{1}+T_{2}}
and the random actions selected during exploration. The distribution of
\overline{\omega}
is conditioned on the event
S
.
C_{T_{1}+T_{2}+1},\ldots,C_{T}
, on the other hand, are independent from
\overline{\omega}
and from
S
. Thus, we can rewrite (
2.36
) as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R_{T_{1}+T_{2}+1:T}\mid S]\leq\sqrt{K(T-T_{1}-T_{2})}\times

(2.37)

\displaystyle\times\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\overline{\omega}\sim p(\overline{\omega}|S)}\mathbb{E}_{C_{T_{1}+T_{2}+1},\ldots,C_{T}}\left[\sum_{t=T_{1}+T_{2}+1}^{T}\sum_{a=1}^{K}\left(\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t,a})-\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\overline{\theta}}(X_{t,a})\right)^{2}\right]}.

Here we notice that the expectation w.r.t.
C_{t}
of each term in (
2.37
) only depends on the marginal distribution of
X_{t,a}
, which is
\mathcal{D}_{X}
by Assumption
1
. Together with (
2.20
) and the definition of
S
, this gives us

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R_{T_{1}+T_{2}+1:T}\mid S]

\displaystyle\leq K(T-T_{1}-T_{2})\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\overline{\omega}\sim p(\overline{\omega}|S)}\left[\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\omega})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})\right]}

(2.38)

\displaystyle\leq K(T-T_{1}-T_{2})\sqrt{\varepsilon(T_{1},T_{2},\delta)}.

(2.39)

Using (
2.27
),

\displaystyle\!\!\mathbb{E}[R_{1:T}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[R_{1:T}\mid F]\mathbb{P}[F]+\left(\mathbb{E}[R_{1:T_{1}+T_{2}}\mid S]+\mathbb{E}[R_{T_{1}+T_{2}+1:T}\mid S]\right)\mathbb{P}[S]

(2.40)

\displaystyle\leq 2B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}T\delta+2B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}(T_{1}+T_{2})+K(T-T_{1}-T_{2})\sqrt{\varepsilon(T_{1},T_{2},\delta)}.

(2.41)

\square

In the rest of the thesis, we will focus on upper bounds on the suboptimality gap, translating them into regret guarantees using this theorem. Providing such bounds is equivalent to solving the sample complexity problem, formulated as “how many data samples do we need to bring the suboptimality gap below a certain threshold”. When we are not in the bandit setup, but instead simply do regression with a pre-trained representation network from i.i.d. data, a bound of the form (
2.21
) is also useful. Given
T_{0}
data samples and a desired probability of success
\delta
, we should minimize
\varepsilon(T_{1},T_{0}-T_{1},\delta)
w.r.t.
T_{1}
to learn how many samples to spend on the initialization of
\mathbf{w}
, and how many — on SGD.

5
Empirical risk minimization

Guarantees for SGD can be given in terms of a bound on the suboptimality gap (
2.20
). We directly optimize the true risk
\mathfrak{R}
, but we only use estimators of its gradients. In the thesis we focus on this approach. However, a mirrored approach is also popular in the literature, under the name of
empirical risk minimization

[
36
]
. In this section, we will briefly discuss how it can be used to provide regret bounds for stochastic contextual bandits with pre-training similar to the ones that we have.

Given
T_{2}
data samples
(\widetilde{X}_{t},\tilde{r}_{t})_{t=1}^{T_{2}}
, we define empirical risk as

\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}(\mathbf{w},\theta)=\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}(\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta}(\widetilde{X}_{t})-\tilde{r}_{r})^{2}.

(2.42)

We can compute the true gradients of
\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}
and optimize it with regular gradient descent (GD), but now the risk itself is an approximation. Given sufficient computational resources, the minimum of the empirical risk can be found with arbitrary precision. Then, we use a
uniform law
, stating that with
\mathbb{P}\geq 1-\delta
,

\forall\omega\qquad|\mathfrak{R}(\omega)-\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}(\omega)|\leq\hat{\varepsilon}(\delta)

(2.43)

for some
\hat{\varepsilon}(\delta)
. Let
\widehat{\omega}\in\argmin_{\omega}\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}(\omega)
be the weights minimizing the empirical risk. Now it is easy to show that, with
\mathbb{P}\geq 1-\delta
,

\mathfrak{R}(\widehat{\omega})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})\leq 2\hat{\varepsilon}(\delta).

(2.44)

Naturally, the catch here is “sufficient computational resources”. We focus on SGD instead of this approach because full-batch gradient descent (required to compute the full gradient of
\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}
) is very rare in practice. We note in passing that SGD on
\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}
with multiple passes through the data can also be used and there are results providing suboptimality gap bounds in this case without the need for a uniform bound
[
28
]
. This direction can be interesting for future research.

Uniform laws for risk functions associated with general classes of losses can be derived through
Rademacher complexity
. For a class of functions
\mathcal{F}\subset\left\{f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\right\}
, it is defined as

\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})=\mathbb{E}_{X,\varepsilon}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}f(X_{i})\right|\right],

(2.45)

where
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. samples from
\mathcal{D}_{X}
and
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
are i.i.d. samples from
U[\left\{-1,1\right\}]
. The class of function relevant to us is

\mathcal{F}=\left\{x\mapsto(\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta}(x)-r)^{2}\right\},

(2.46)

where
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\leq B_{\mathbf{w}}
,
\lVert\theta\rVert\leq B_{\theta}
, and
|r|\leq B_{r}
. Any
f\in\mathcal{F}
satisfies
\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}\leq(B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}+B_{r})^{2}
. Theorem 4.10 in
[
39
]
now gives us that

\mathbb{P}\left[\forall\omega\quad|\mathfrak{R}(\omega)-\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}(\omega)|\leq 2\mathcal{R}_{T_{2}}(\mathcal{F})+(B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}+B_{r})^{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{T_{2}}}\right]\geq 1-\delta.

(2.47)

Rademacher complexity of neural networks has been extensively studied, with one of the recent results being
[
13
]
. Authors of this paper improve the dependence of Rademacher complexity on the depth of the network, but for us the most important part is that it depends on
n
as

\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})=O(n^{-1/2})

(2.48)

as long as the depth of the network is kept fixed and Frobenius norms of parameters are bounded and some assumptions on activation functions are satisfied. In this case, we require that the input features come from a space
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{in}}
with bounded norms. Note that the paper actually bounds the complexity of the class

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}=\left\{x\mapsto\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta}(x)\right\},

(2.49)

but the contraction lemma (Lemma 26.9 in
[
36
]
) and Lipschitzness of
x\mapsto(x-a)^{2}
defined on a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}
give us that
\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})=O(\mathcal{R}_{n}(\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}))
. Substituting (
2.48
) into (
2.47
) and the result into (
2.44
) we get that, with
\mathbb{P}\geq 1-\delta
,

\mathfrak{R}(\widehat{\omega})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})=O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{T_{2}}}\right).

(2.50)

This is the same asymptotics as what we will show for SGD in Chapter
4
when only one pass is made through the data, and the true risk
\mathfrak{R}
is (locally) convex.

It turns out that the asymptotic bound (
2.50
) can actually be improved. Theorem C.1 in
[
30
]
, itself adapted from lecture notes
[
32
]
, shows that the suboptimality gap for mean squared losses can be bounded as

\mathfrak{R}(\widehat{\omega})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})=O\left(\frac{\log(T_{2}/\delta)}{T_{2}}\right),

(2.51)

where
\widehat{\omega}
is the exact minimizer of the empirical risk
\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}
. This improvement over (
2.50
) could potentially be adapted to the more realistic case of an inexact minimizer of
\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}
and lead to a reduction in regret bounds from
O(T^{4/5})
that we currently have to
O(T^{2/3})
, but efficient optimization methods of
\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}
would still be required.

Chapter 3
Linear estimation of the last layer

In this chapter we provide guarantees on the initial value
\mathbf{w}_{0}
of the last layer weights that we compute in Algorithm
1
using Ridge regression. In this and the following chapters, we will use notation

\Sigma_{0}=\Sigma(\theta_{0}),\quad\Sigma_{\lambda}=\Sigma(\theta_{0})+\lambda I,\quad\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}=\widehat{\Sigma(\theta_{0})},\quad\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}=\widehat{\Sigma(\theta_{0})}+\lambda I.

(3.1)

For convenience, we repeat the equations that define these matrices and
\mathbf{w}_{0}
:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)^{\intercal}\right],\quad\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}=\frac{1}{T_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t}),

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{0}=\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\frac{1}{T_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}r_{t}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t}).

(3.3)

Here,
X_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}_{X}
are i.i.d. samples that correspond to the random features of the actions that we choose during the first phase of exploration in E2TC.

We would like to provide a high-probability bound on “closeness” between
\mathbf{w}_{0}
and
\mathbf{w}^{*}
, for some appropriate definition of closeness. Since we are using the misspecified weights
\theta_{0}
, we can’t expect this closeness to go to zero with
T_{1}
, but only down to some constant that corresponds to the cost of misspecification. Two notions of closeness will be relevant to us:
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}
and
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}
. The first expression is easier to bound. In fact, in the bandit literature, this quantity is bounded
for any
sequence of
\{X_{t}\}_{t}
, w.h.p. over the noise
\eta_{t}
. In the following section, we provide a bound on this quantity. The second quantity has been analyzed in the literature on
random design Ridge regression
. We consider it in Section
7
. Note that we cannot bound
\lVert\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{2}
. This is a fundamental limitation: if
\mathcal{D}_{X}
is only supported on a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we cannot learn anything about the projections of
\mathbf{w}^{*}
onto directions orthogonal to this subspace. To work around this limitation, as we will see, we can perform pre-conditioned SGD, guarantees for which are given in terms of matrix-weighted distance from
\mathbf{w}^{*}
to
\mathbf{w}_{0}
.

6
Fixed design Ridge regression

Due to realizability, the rewards are generated as
r_{t}=\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X_{t})+\eta_{t}
. Our Ridge estimate (
3.3
) relies on
\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})
. Misspecification comes from the discrepancy between
\varphi_{\theta_{0}}
and
\varphi_{\theta^{*}}
. Let

\Delta\varphi(x)=\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(x)-\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(x),\quad\Sigma_{\delta}=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\Delta\varphi(X)\Delta\varphi(X)^{\intercal}\right].

(3.4)

With this notation,

\displaystyle r_{t}=\left(\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})+\Delta\varphi(X_{t})\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}+\eta_{t},

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{0}=\mathbf{w}^{*}-\lambda\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{*}+\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\frac{1}{T_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}\left(\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})\Delta\varphi(X_{t})^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}+\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})\eta_{t}\right).

(3.6)

Let us now focus on the suboptimality gap of
\omega_{0}=(\mathbf{w}_{0},\theta_{0})
:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\left(\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{0})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})\right)}

\displaystyle\quad=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}_{0}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)\right)^{2}\right]}

(3.7)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left((\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*})^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\right)^{2}\right]}+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\Delta\varphi(X)\right)^{2}\right]}

(3.8)

\displaystyle\quad=\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}+\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{\delta}}.

(3.9)

In the first equality we used (
2.11
). The first term in the above is the main character of Section
7
. The second term has a somewhat intuitive interpretation: it measures the average discrepancy between the true feature map
\varphi_{\theta^{*}}
and the initial one
\varphi_{\theta_{0}}
in the direction of the true weights
\mathbf{w}^{*}
. As it turns out, this term is an appropriate measure of misspecification of
\theta_{0}
compared to
\theta^{*}
, and we can derive the bounds on
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}
and
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}
using it. We will use notation

\varepsilon_{0}^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{\delta}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}(\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)-\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X))\right)^{2}\right].

(3.10)

Note that requiring a small
\varepsilon_{0}
is weaker than, for example, requiring small
\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\lVert\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)-\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)\rVert^{2}\right]
or
\lVert\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}\rVert^{2}
, although the latter will be required for SGD analysis later.

Now, we substitute (
3.6
) into the distance we are trying to bound:

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\quad=\left\|-\lambda\mathbf{w}^{*}+\frac{1}{T_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}\left(\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})\Delta\varphi(X_{t})^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}+\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})\eta_{t}\right)\right\|_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}

(3.11)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}+\left\|{\frac{1}{T_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})\Delta\varphi(X_{t})^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}}\right\|_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}+\left\|{\frac{1}{T_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})\eta_{t}}\right\|_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}.

(3.12)

The first term here can be bounded by observing that
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\leq\lambda^{-1}I
, so

\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}\leq\sqrt{\lambda}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert.

(3.13)

The second term in (
3.12
) is the cost of misspecification. The following lemma bounds it in terms of
\varepsilon_{0}
.

Lemma 3.1
(The cost of misspecification).

For all
\delta>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{T_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}\left(\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})\Delta\varphi(X_{t})^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)\right\|_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}<\sqrt{d\left(\varepsilon_{0}^{2}+\varepsilon_{\delta}\right)}\right]\geq 1-\delta,

(3.14)

where

\varepsilon_{\delta}=4B_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}B_{\varphi}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2T_{1}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}.

(3.15)

Proof
.

We define the empirical estimator of
\Sigma_{\delta}
as

\widehat{\Sigma}_{\delta}=\frac{1}{T_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}\Delta\varphi(X_{t})\Delta\varphi(X_{t})^{\intercal}.

(3.16)

With this notation,

\displaystyle\left\|{\frac{1}{T_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}\left(\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})\Delta\varphi(X_{t})^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)}\right\|_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\frac{1}{T_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}\left|\Delta\varphi(X_{t})^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}\right|\lVert\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}

(3.17)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{T^{2}_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}\left(\Delta\varphi(X_{t})^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}\lVert\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}^{2}}

(3.18)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=\frac{\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\delta}}}{\sqrt{T_{1}}}\sqrt{\tr\left[\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})^{\intercal}\right]}

(3.19)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=\frac{\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\delta}}}{\sqrt{T_{1}}}\sqrt{T_{1}\tr\left[I-\lambda\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\right]}\leq\sqrt{d}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\delta}}.

(3.20)

In (
3.20
), we used that
\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}=\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}-\lambda I
and the definition of
\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}
. Since
\mathbf{w}^{*}
is not random, Hoeffding’s inequality is sufficient to replace
\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\delta}}
by
\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{\delta}}
. Let

Y_{t}=(\Delta\varphi(X_{t})^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*})^{2}.

(3.21)

These are i.i.d. random variables with mean
\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]=\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{\delta}}^{2}
. Their empirical mean is

\frac{1}{T_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}Y_{t}=\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\delta}}^{2}.

(3.22)

We also have that
0\leq Y_{t}\leq 4B_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}B_{\varphi}^{2}
a.s. Hence, we can apply Hoeffding’s inequality to
Y_{t}
. Introducing an event
E_{\delta}
, bound
\varepsilon_{\delta}
, and a probability of failure
\delta
, we have

\displaystyle E_{\delta}=\left\{\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert^{2}_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\delta}}-\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert^{2}_{\Sigma_{\delta}}<\varepsilon_{\delta}\right\},\quad\mathbb{P}\left[E_{\delta}\right]\geq 1-\delta,

(3.23)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\delta}=4B_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}B_{\varphi}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2T_{1}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}.

(3.24)

When
E_{\delta}
holds, we can continue (
3.20
) as

\sqrt{d}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\delta}}<\sqrt{d\left(\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert^{2}_{\Sigma_{\delta}}+\varepsilon_{\delta}\right)}=\sqrt{d(\varepsilon_{0}^{2}+\varepsilon_{\delta})}

(3.25)

and arrive at (
3.14
).

\square

The next lemma bounds the third term in (
3.12
). Since the proof closely follows the exposition at the beginning of Chapter 20 in
[
24
]
, we defer it to Appendix
8.A
.

Lemma 3.2
(Noise in well-specified linear bandits).

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\|{\frac{1}{T_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})\eta_{t}}\right\|_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}<\varepsilon_{\eta}\right]\geq 1-\delta,

(3.26)

where

\varepsilon_{\eta}=2\sqrt{\frac{d\log 6+\log(1/\delta)}{T_{1}}}.

(3.27)

We are now ready to continue the chain of inequalities (
3.12
). Using (
3.13
), Lemma
3.1
, and Lemma
3.2
, we have, with
\mathbb{P}\geq 1-2\delta
,

\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}\leq\sqrt{d(\varepsilon_{0}^{2}+\varepsilon_{\delta})}+\varepsilon_{\eta}+\sqrt{\lambda}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert.

(3.28)

This is the result we needed. Asymptotically, it reads as

\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}^{2}=\widetilde{O}\left(\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d+\frac{d}{\sqrt{T_{1}}}+\lambda\right),

(3.29)

where
\widetilde{O}
hides the dependence on
\log(1/\delta)
. At this point, we could also convert this bound to one on
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}
by using a bound on
\lVert\Sigma_{0}-\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}\rVert_{2}
, provided by Lemma
8.2
. With a suitable
\lambda
, this would give a guarantee
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}^{2}=O(\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d+d/\sqrt{T_{1}})
. This turns out to be suboptimal. In the next section, we use more advanced techniques to show that this bound can be improved to
\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d+d/T_{1})
or to
\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d+1/\sqrt{T_{1}})
with high probability, depending on the regularization.

7
Random design Ridge regression

The approach we outlined in the last paragraph of the previous section relies on a bound on
\lVert\Sigma_{0}-\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}\rVert_{2}
in covariance estimation. As it turns out, we can instead rely on the
relative
error in covariance estimation
\lVert\Sigma_{\lambda}^{1/2}\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\Sigma_{\lambda}^{1/2}\rVert_{2}
. This idea is exploited in the work
[
18
]
, on which we base the analysis in this section.

When the weights of the feature map
\varphi
are fixed at
\theta_{0}
, the estimate
\mathbf{w}_{0}
given by (
3.3
) is solving the Ridge regression problem

\mathbf{w}_{0}=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}}\frac{1}{T_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{1}}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})-r_{t}\right)^{2}+\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^{2}.

(3.30)

The way our algorithm is designed,
\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X_{t})
are i.i.d. random variables for
t
from
1
to
T_{1}
. Therefore, the problem can be framed as a
random design
ridge regression. This problem is studied in the literature, with the goal of bounding the difference of the estimate
\mathbf{w}_{0}
to the minimizer
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}
of the true square error. In our notation, this minimizer is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{\mathbf{w}}\mathbb{E}_{X,\eta}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)-\eta\right)^{2}\right]

(3.31)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)\right)^{2}\right].

(3.32)

Authors of
[
18
]
give a high-probability upper bound on the
excess risk
of
\mathbf{w}_{0}
, which can be conveniently expressed as

\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}_{0}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)\right)^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)\right)^{2}\right]\\
=\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}.

(3.33)

Note that the ambiguity of
\argmin
in (
3.31
) is such that all minimizers produce the same excess risk landscape: for any
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and two minimizers
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{1},\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{2}
of (
3.31
), it holds that

\lVert\mathbf{w}-\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{1}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{w}-\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{2}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2},

(3.34)

because
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{1}-\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{2}
lies in the nullspace of
\Sigma_{0}
. The bounds on the excess risk in
[
18
]
are given in terms of the
effective dimension
of the problem,

d_{p,{\lambda}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{j}+{\lambda}}\right)^{p}\quad\text{for $p\in\{1,2\}$},

(3.35)

where
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j}
are the eigenvalues of
\Sigma_{0}
. It always holds that
d_{p,\lambda}\leq d
, but we try to preserve
d_{p,\lambda}
whenever possible, because sometimes this allows to substitute explicit
d
by a bound on the norm of
\mathbf{w}^{*}
or
\varphi_{\theta}(X)
. The following inequalities will be helpful for this:

d_{1,\lambda}\leq\frac{\tr\Sigma_{0}}{\lambda},\quad d_{2,\lambda}\leq\frac{\tr\Sigma_{0}}{2\lambda},\quad\tr\Sigma_{0}=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\lVert\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\rVert^{2}\right]\leq B_{\varphi}^{2}.

(3.36)

Apart from the notions of effective dimension above, we will use another two. The first is introduced in
[
18
]
. The second we introduce ourselves.

\tilde{d}_{1,\lambda}=\max\left\{d_{1,\lambda},1\right\},\qquad\hat{d}_{\lambda}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{j}+\lambda}\right)^{2}\leq d.

(3.37)

We will denote the unit eigenvector of
\Sigma_{0}
corresponding to
\lambda_{i}
as
\mathbf{v}_{i}
.
\Sigma_{0}
admits a spectral decomposition

\Sigma_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}.

(3.38)

Using the definition (
3.10
) of
\varepsilon_{0}
, bounds on the excess risk (
3.33
) translate into bounds on our excess loss of the estimated weights. We first note that by definition of
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}
,

\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)\right)^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)\right)^{2}\right]=\varepsilon_{0}^{2}.

(3.39)

Next, continuing (
3.7
),

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{0})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})}

\displaystyle\quad=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}_{0}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)\right)^{2}\right]}

(3.40)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}_{0}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)-\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\right)^{2}\right]}+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)\right)^{2}\right]}

(3.41)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}+\varepsilon_{0}.

(3.42)

The term
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}
is analyzed in
[
18
]
by decomposing it into three parts, each corresponding to an error coming from a specific source. The first source is the
regularization
error that stems from the introduction of
\lambda
. Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)\right)^{2}\right]+{\lambda}\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^{2}\right\}

(3.43)

\displaystyle=\left(\Sigma_{0}+{\lambda}I\right)^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)^{\intercal}\right]\mathbf{w}^{*}

(3.44)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)^{\intercal}\right]\mathbf{w}^{*}}{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}\mathbf{v}_{i}

(3.45)

be the minimizer of the true Ridge loss. If we introduce

\alpha_{i}=\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)^{\intercal}\right]\mathbf{w}^{*},

(3.46)

\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}
can be expressed as

\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}\mathbf{v}_{i}.

(3.47)

We also introduce the conditional expectation of
\mathbf{w}_{0}
,

\mathbf{w}^{c}=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\mathbf{w}_{0}|X_{1},\ldots,X_{T_{1}}\right].

(3.48)

The error is now split into three terms:

\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}\leq 3\left(\underbrace{\|{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}\|}^{2}_{\Sigma_{0}}}_{=:\varepsilon_{rg}}+\underbrace{\|{\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}-\mathbf{w}^{c}\|}^{2}_{\Sigma_{0}}}_{=:\varepsilon_{bs}}+\underbrace{\|{\mathbf{w}^{c}-\mathbf{w}_{0}\|}^{2}_{\Sigma_{0}}}_{=:\varepsilon_{vr}}\right),

(3.49)

where
\varepsilon_{rg}
is the regularization error (coming from the introduction of
{\lambda}
),
\varepsilon_{bs}
is the bias error, introduced by the randomness of the design, and
\varepsilon_{vr}
is the variance error, coming from noise
\eta_{t}
. Alternatively to the above decomposition, we could bound the excess risk using (
3.9
) as

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{0})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}+\varepsilon_{0}

(3.50)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{3\|\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}\|^{2}_{\Sigma_{0}}+3\varepsilon_{bs}+3\varepsilon_{vr}}+\varepsilon_{0}.

(3.51)

Both
\|\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}\|^{2}_{\Sigma_{0}}
and
\|\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}\|^{2}_{\Sigma_{0}}
can be bounded in terms of
\lambda
and
\varepsilon_{0}
, as the following two lemmas show.

Lemma 3.3
(Regularization error in presence of misspecification).

\varepsilon_{rg}=\|\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\|_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\hat{d}_{\lambda}.

(3.52)

When misspecification is not present, i.e.
\theta_{0}=\theta^{*}
, we have
\mathbf{w}^{*}=\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}
, and we can similarly bound
\varepsilon_{rg}\leq\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert^{2}/4
, hence the name of the lemma.

Proof
.

Let us for now assume that
\Sigma_{0}
is nonsingular. Then,
\lambda_{i}>0
for all
i
, and the inverse of
\Sigma_{0}
is expressed as

\Sigma_{0}^{-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}.

(3.53)

The minimizer of (
3.31
) is now unique and given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)^{\intercal}\right]\mathbf{w}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}\mathbf{v}_{i}.

(3.54)

\alpha_{i}
can be bounded as follows. Using
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)^{\intercal}\right]\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)^{2}+2\left(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\Delta\varphi(X)^{\intercal}\right]\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)^{2}.

(3.55)

Remembering that
\Sigma_{0}=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)^{\intercal}\right]
and that
\mathbf{v}_{i}
is its eigenvector,

\displaystyle=2\lambda_{i}^{2}\left(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)^{2}+2\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\Delta\varphi(X)^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}\right]^{2}

(3.56)

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda_{i}^{2}\left(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)^{2}+2\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\right)^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\Delta\varphi(X)^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)^{2}\right].

(3.57)

Using the definitions of
\mathbf{v}_{i}
and
\varepsilon_{0}
once again,

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda_{i}^{2}\left(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\lambda_{i}.

(3.58)

With the help of decompositions (
3.47
) and (
3.54
), we can express
\varepsilon_{rg}
as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{rg}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\left(\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}-\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}\right)^{2}

(3.59)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\lambda^{2}\alpha_{i}^{2}}{\lambda_{i}\left(\lambda+\lambda_{i}\right)^{2}}

(3.60)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\lambda^{2}\lambda_{i}}{\left(\lambda+\lambda_{i}\right)^{2}}\left(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\left(\lambda+\lambda_{i}\right)^{2}}

(3.61)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\hat{d}_{\lambda}.

(3.62)

In the last inequality we used that for
a,b>0
,
ab/(a+b)^{2}\leq 1/4
. As long as
\lambda>0
,
\hat{d}_{\lambda}
has a finite limit when
\lambda_{i}\to 0
. Taking this limit, we can see that the bound holds for singular
\Sigma_{0}
as well.

\square

A similar bound could be given for the weighted distance
\|\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\|_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}
:

Lemma 3.4
(Regularization error w.r.t.
\mathbf{w}^{*}
).

\|\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\|_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d_{2,\lambda}.

(3.63)

Compared to Lemma
3.3
, the effective dimension
\hat{d}_{\lambda}
is replaced by
d_{2,\lambda}
. As we shall see later, both bounds can be useful in different contexts.

Proof
.

Using (
3.47
) once again,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\|_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\left(\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}-\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)^{2}

(3.64)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{(\lambda+\lambda_{i})^{2}}\left(\alpha_{i}-(\lambda+\lambda_{i})\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)^{2}

(3.65)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\lambda_{i}\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda+\lambda_{i})^{2}}\left(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{(\lambda+\lambda_{i})^{2}}(\alpha_{i}-\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*})^{2}

(3.66)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{(\lambda+\lambda_{i})^{2}}(\alpha_{i}-\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*})^{2}.

(3.67)

In the first inequality, we used
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
. In the second — that
\lambda\lambda_{i}/(\lambda+\lambda_{i})^{2}\leq 1/4
. We will now focus on the remaining sum. Using the definition (
3.46
) of
\alpha_{i}
,

\displaystyle(\alpha_{i}-\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*})^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\big(\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)^{\intercal}\right]-\lambda_{i}I\big)\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)^{2}

(3.68)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\big(\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)^{\intercal}\right]-\lambda_{i}I+\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\Delta\varphi(X)^{\intercal}\right]\big)\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)^{2}

(3.69)

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}-\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}^{*}+\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\Delta\varphi(X)^{\intercal}\right]\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)^{2}

(3.70)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\Delta\varphi(X)^{\intercal}\right]\mathbf{w}^{*}\right)^{2}

(3.71)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\right)^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\Delta\varphi(X)\right)^{2}\right]

(3.72)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\lambda_{i}.

(3.73)

In (
3.70
) and (
3.73
), we used the definitions of
\lambda_{i}
and
\mathbf{v}_{i}
, and in (
3.73
) we also used the definition of
\varepsilon_{0}
. Substituting this into (
3.67
),

\|\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\|_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}\right)^{2}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d_{2,\lambda}.

(3.74)

\square

The bound on
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}
is given in
[
18
, Theorem 16]
. We adapted this theorem to our notation as Theorem
8.5
in the appendix. Our version introduces notation

\text{approx}_{\lambda}(X)=\left(\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\right).

(3.75)

The theorem relies on several conditions, which we will now verify in our setup. The first condition is that

\frac{\lVert\left(\Sigma_{\lambda}\right)^{-1/2}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\rVert}{\sqrt{d_{1,\lambda}}}\leq\rho_{\lambda}

(3.76)

holds almost surely for some
\rho_{\lambda}
. Since
\lVert\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\rVert\leq B_{\varphi}
, it is satisfied with

\rho_{\lambda}=B_{\varphi}/\sqrt{d_{1,\lambda}\lambda}.

(3.77)

Another condition concerns the boundedness of misspecification. The following lemma states this condition and provides a constant
b_{\lambda}
with which it is satisfied.

Lemma 3.5
(Bounded misspecification).

\delta_{\text{ms}}=\frac{\lVert\Sigma_{\lambda}^{-1/2}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\left(\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\right)\rVert}{\sqrt{d_{1,\lambda}}}\leq b_{\lambda}

(3.78)

almost surely, where

b_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{1,\lambda}\lambda}}\left(1+2\sqrt{2}\right)B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\lambda}(2+\sqrt{2})B_{\varphi}^{2}.

(3.79)

In the final bound on the excess risk,
b_{\lambda}
enters as a
O(b_{\lambda}^{2}d_{1,\lambda}/n^{2})
term. This lemma allows us to write this as

O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda n^{2}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d_{1,\lambda}}{\lambda^{2}n^{2}}\right).

(3.80)

The leading term coincides with the case of bounded
\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\rVert
, considered in
[
18
, Remark 4]
.

Proof
.

Let
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to be specified later. We have that

\displaystyle\delta_{ms}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{\lambda}\left|\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\right|

(3.81)

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{\lambda}\left(\left|\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)-\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\vphantom{\mathbf{w}^{\lambda\intercal}}\right|+\left|(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda})^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\right|\right)

(3.82)

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{\lambda}\left(B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}+B_{\varphi}\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}\rVert+\|{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}}\|_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\lVert\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)\rVert_{\left(\Sigma_{\lambda}\right)^{-1}}\right)

(3.83)

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{\lambda}\left(B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}+B_{\varphi}\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}\rVert+\rho_{\lambda}\sqrt{d_{1,\lambda}}\|{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}}\|_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\right).

(3.84)

A similar derivation is presented in
[
18
, Remark 4]
with
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}=\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}
. The authors then proceed to bound

\|{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}}\|_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\leq\sqrt{\lambda}\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\rVert

(3.85)

and thus get an expression for
b_{\lambda}
in terms of
\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\rVert
. Whenever
\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\rVert
is bounded, this is a valid technique. However, an examination of (
3.54
) reveals that the components of
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}
in the eigenbasis of
\Sigma_{0}
depend on
\lambda_{i}^{-1}
, so without additional assumptions on the spectrum of
\Sigma_{0}
, we cannot bound
\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\rVert
. Yet the largest components come from directions with small
\lambda_{i}
. The components of
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}
along these directions also contribute little to the value of
\|{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}}\|_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}
. We can exploit this informal observation in the following manner.

Let us divide the set of indices
[d]
into two subsets:

I_{0}=\left\{i\in[d]:\ \lambda_{i}\leq\lambda\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad I_{1}=\left\{i\in[d]:\ \lambda_{i}>\lambda\right\}.

(3.86)

One of them can be empty without affecting the proof. In this case, a sum over an empty set is assumed to be
0
. We set
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
to

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}=\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}\mathbf{v}_{i}.

(3.87)

That is, we take
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}
, but set some of its components to zero. The size of
I_{1}
can be bounded by the effective dimension:

|I_{1}|=\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\frac{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}<2\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}\leq 2d_{1,\lambda}.

(3.88)

Now, the norm of
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}\leq 2\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\left(\left(\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}\right)^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}{\lambda_{i}}\right)<2\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert^{2}+4\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d_{1,\lambda}}{\lambda}.

(3.89)

Above, we used (
3.58
) and the definition of
I_{1}
. It remains to deal with
\|{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}}\|_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}
. Taking into account the definition of
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
and (
3.47
),

\displaystyle\|{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}}\|_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{2}}{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}+\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\left(\lambda+\lambda_{i}\right)\left(\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}-\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}\right)^{2}

(3.90)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{2}}{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}+\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{2}\lambda^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}\left(\lambda+\lambda_{i}\right)}.

(3.91)

Using the bound (
3.58
) on
\alpha_{i}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\|{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}}\|_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}}{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}\left(\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}\right)^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}

(3.92)

\displaystyle\qquad+2\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}\left(\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{i}\right)^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\lambda_{i}(\lambda+\lambda_{i})}.

(3.93)

By definitions of
I_{0}
and
I_{1}
, we can bound
\lambda_{i}^{2}\leq\lambda^{2}
in the first sum and
\lambda^{2}<\lambda_{i}^{2}
in the fourth. Merging the first sum with the third sum and the second with the fourth,

\displaystyle<2\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d_{1,\lambda}.

(3.94)

Finally, we can substitute (
3.89
) and (
3.94
) into (
3.84
). When taking the square root, we use
\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
.

\displaystyle\delta_{ms}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{\lambda}\left(B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}+B_{\varphi}\left(\sqrt{2}B_{\mathbf{w}}+2\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{\frac{d_{1,\lambda}}{\lambda}}\right)+\rho_{\lambda}\sqrt{d_{1,\lambda}}\left(\sqrt{2\lambda}B_{\mathbf{w}}+\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{2d_{1,\lambda}}\right)\right)

(3.95)

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{\lambda}\left((1+\sqrt{2})B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}+\rho_{\lambda}\sqrt{2\lambda d_{1,\lambda}}B_{\mathbf{w}}\right)+\varepsilon_{0}\rho_{\lambda}\sqrt{d_{1,\lambda}}\left(2\frac{B_{\varphi}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}+\rho_{\lambda}\sqrt{2d_{1,\lambda}}\right)

(3.96)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{1,\lambda}\lambda}}\left(1+2\sqrt{2}\right)B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\lambda}(2+\sqrt{2})B_{\varphi}^{2}.

(3.97)

In the final equality, we substituted
\rho_{\lambda}=B_{\varphi}/\sqrt{\lambda d_{1,\lambda}}
.

\square

We are now ready to use Theorem
8.5
and provide regret bounds for the explore-then-commit algorithm with misspecified features. As mentioned before, this theorem provides all bounds in terms of the effective dimensions
d_{p,\lambda}
. We can bound the effective dimensions using (
3.36
) or simply as
d_{p,\lambda}\leq d
. It turns out that these two techniques lead to different regret bounds (the first with
\lambda=T_{1}^{-1/2}
, and the second with
\lambda=O(\log T_{1}/T_{1})
). The first bound has a dimension-independent sublinear term! The bounds are given in the two theorems that follow. The proofs for both theorems are deferred to Appendix
8.B
, since they are not very insightful. Both proofs mostly consist of verifying the conditions for Theorem
8.5
and substituting the specific hyperparameters into the bounds this theorem provides.

Theorem 3.6
(E2TC with
T_{2}=0
, data-poor regime).

Let E2TC be run with
{T_{2}=0}
(only the first stage of exploration). Let Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
hold. There exist
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and a universal constant
C>0
such that, whenever
T_{1}>N_{0}
, with
\lambda=T_{1}^{-1/2}
, the following statements hold simultaneously:

1.

With
\mathbb{P}\geq 1-C/\sqrt{T_{1}}
,

\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}=O\left(\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d+\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{1}}}\right).

(3.98)

2.

Regret of E2TC the following asymptotics:

R_{1:T}=O\left(TT_{1}^{-1/2}+T_{1}+\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{\min\{\hat{d}_{\lambda},d_{2,\lambda}\}}KT+T_{1}^{-1/4}KT\right).

(3.99)

When
T\geq K^{4}
, we can select
T_{1}=(KT)^{4/5}
to get

R_{1:T}=O\left(\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{\min\{\hat{d}_{\lambda},d_{2,\lambda}\}}KT+(KT)^{4/5}\right).

(3.100)

Otherwise, with
T_{1}=KT^{4/5}
,

R_{1:T}=O\left(\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{\min\{\hat{d}_{\lambda},d_{2,\lambda}\}}KT+KT^{4/5}\right).

(3.101)

Since our analysis targets applications with small
K
(e.g. a form of few-class classification), the first regret bound is the main one.

The constant
N_{0}
is rather large: it depends on
B_{\varphi}
approximately as
B_{\varphi}^{4}
due to the restrictions of the bound from
[
18
]
. To resolve this issue, we can scale
\lambda
up by a factor of
B_{\varphi}^{2}
. This will make
N_{0}
independent of
B_{\varphi}
, but the factor will also show up in the regret bound. While it doesn’t change the asymptotics, in practice this approach makes the guarantees we get much weaker.

We left two notions of the effective dimension (
\hat{d}_{\lambda}
and
d_{2,\lambda}
) in the regret bound on purpose.
d_{2,\lambda}
allows us to discard the dimension of the nullspace of
\Sigma_{0}
. In networks with ReLU activations, this can amount to a large fraction of
d
. On the other hand, using
\hat{d}_{\lambda}
can lead to sublinear regret in strongly convex losses, as we show at the end of this section.

The next theorem shows that if we can afford to introduce a factor of
d
in the sublinear term of the regret, then the
T
dependency of this term can be cut to
T^{2/3}
.

Theorem 3.7
(E2TC with
T_{2}=0
, data-rich regime).

Let E2TC be run with
T_{2}=0
(only the first stage of exploration). Let Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
hold. Let
a>0
. There exist
N_{0}(a)\in\mathbb{N}
such that, whenever
T_{1}>\max\{N_{0},d^{1/a}\}
, with

\lambda:=\frac{7B_{\varphi}^{2}\log T_{1}}{T_{1}},

(3.102)

the following statements hold simultaneously:

1.

With
\mathbb{P}>1-4/\sqrt{T_{1}}
,

\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}=\widetilde{O}\left(\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d+\frac{d}{T_{1}}\right).

(3.103)

2.

Regret of E2TC has the following asymptotics:

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1:T}\right]=O\left(\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{d}KT+\frac{T}{\sqrt{T_{1}}}+T_{1}\vphantom{+KT\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{T_{1}}}+\frac{d^{1/4}\log^{1/4}T_{1}}{\sqrt{T_{1}}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log T_{1}}{T_{1}}}\right)}\right.\\
\left.+KT\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{T_{1}}}+\frac{d^{1/4}\log^{1/4}T_{1}}{\sqrt{T_{1}}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log T_{1}}{T_{1}}}\right)\right).

(3.104)

Depending on the relative size of
T
,
d
, and

, we should use different choices for
T_{1}
. In the best case scenario, we can choose
T_{1}=d^{1/3}(KT)^{2/3}
and get

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1:T}\right]=O(\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{d}KT)+\widetilde{O}(d^{1/3}(KT)^{2/3}).

(3.105)

For

and

, our previous theorem provides better regret bounds, hence “data-poor” and “data-rich” regimes in the names of the theorems.

Strong convexity makes weak learning achieve sublinear regret

Let us focus on Theorem
3.6
. It is easy to see that the Hessian of
\mathfrak{R}
w.r.t.
\mathbf{w}
at
\theta_{0}
is constant and given by

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\mathbf{w}^{2}}\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w},\theta_{0})=2\Sigma_{0}.

(3.106)

Let us assume that the loss is strongly convex at
(\mathbf{w}^{*},\theta_{0})
w.r.t.
\mathbf{w}
, and that the strong convexity parameter is bounded from below by
\mu
. In this case, the lowest eigenvalue of the Hessian is bounded by the same value:

\lambda_{min}(2\Sigma_{0})\geq\mu.

(3.107)

We can use this to bound
\hat{d}_{\lambda}
, which, we remind the reader, is defined in (
3.37
):

\hat{d}_{\lambda}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\lambda_{i}}\leq\frac{2d\lambda}{\mu}.

(3.108)

Taking into account that Theorem
3.6
sets
\lambda=O(T_{1}^{-1/2})
, the regret bound (
3.99
) can be rewritten as

R_{1:T}=O\left(\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{d/\mu}T_{1}^{-1/4}KT+T_{1}^{-1/4}KT\right).

(3.109)

Selecting
T_{1}=(KT)^{4/5}
again, we arrive at

R_{1:T}=O\left((\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{d/\mu}+1)(KT)^{4/5}\right).

(3.110)

The regret is sublinear in
T
, even though we only learn the last layer of the network! We believe that this counterintuitive result points that
strong convexity should not be considered as a framework for neural network learning
. Even if the risk is strongly convex, it is unrealistic to expect that a lower bound on
\mu
will be known.

Chapter 4
High-probability guarantees for stochastic gradient descent

In this chapter we study high-probability bounds on the suboptimality gap that can be achieved with Stochastic Gradient Descent in the case of convex risk. The expected value of the gap can be bounded using standard techniques, described e.g. in
[
36
, Chapter 14]
. In Section
8
, we show how this can be generalized to a high-probability bound using the Hoeffding-Azuma inequality. Although we believe this result has been demonstrated before, we could not find a proper reference for it, so we decided to derive it ourselves. In Section
9
, we examine the more nuanced case of a risk function that is only locally convex. In this situation, we provide a restriction on the learing rate under which we can guarantee that SGD does not escape the convex basin, given that it is initialized in it. Finally, in Section
10
, we introduce the specific flavor of SGD that E2TC uses, namely preconditioned SGD. We discuss the intuition behind it and how it leads to different guarantees compared to vanilla SGD.

8
Globally convex loss

Let
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{P}
be a closed convex set. Projection
\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}
onto it is defined as

\forall\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{P},\quad\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}(\omega)=\argmin_{\widetilde{\omega}\in\mathcal{B}}\lVert\widetilde{\omega}-\omega\rVert^{2}.

(4.1)

Let
\mathfrak{R}:\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined over this set as
\mathfrak{R}(\omega)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(\omega,\mathbf{z})\right]
. This is a generalization of the mean squared risk (
2.11
) that we use in E2TC. Here,
\mathcal{D}
is the data distribution over some set
\mathcal{Z}
, and
\ell
is the loss function that depends on the model parameters
\omega\in\mathcal{B}
and a random data sample
\mathbf{z}
. We consider the stochastic gradient descent (SGD) to minimize
\mathfrak{R}
as described in
[
36
, Chapter 14]
. From an initialization
\omega_{1}\in\mathcal{B}
it proceeds as
\omega_{t+1}:=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}(\omega_{t}-\zeta\mathbf{v}_{t})
, where
\mathbf{v}_{t}=\nabla_{\omega}\ell(\omega_{t},\mathbf{z}_{t})
is an unbiased estimator of
\nabla\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{t})
,
\zeta>0
is the learning rate, and
\{\mathbf{z}_{t}\}
are iid samples from
\mathcal{D}
. After
T
updates, the algorithm outputs

\overline{\omega}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\omega_{t}.

(4.2)

Let
\omega^{*}\in\argmin_{\omega\in\mathcal{B}}\mathfrak{R}(\omega)
.
[
36
, Theorem 14.8]
bounds
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\omega})\right]-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})
under certain conditions. Here, we will adapt the proof under the same conditions, but instead obtain a high-probability bound on
\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\omega})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that
\mathfrak{R}
is convex,
\lVert\mathbf{v}_{t}\rVert\leq D
a.s., and the domain
\mathcal{B}
is a
B_{\omega}
-ball:

\mathcal{B}=\left\{\omega:\ \lVert\omega\rVert\leq B_{\omega}\right\}.

(4.3)

Then, for any
\delta>0
, with probability not less than
1-\delta
,

\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\omega})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})<\frac{\lVert\omega^{*}-\omega_{1}\rVert^{2}}{2\zeta T}+\frac{\zeta}{2T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{t}\rVert^{2}+4DB_{\omega}\sqrt{\frac{2}{T}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}.

(4.4)

Compared to the bound on
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\omega})\right]-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})
from
[
36
]
, this one has an extra term
4DB_{\omega}\sqrt{2(\log(1/\delta))/T}
. The rest of the bound is the same. If we set
\zeta=2B_{\omega}/(D\sqrt{T})
and bound
\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert\leq D,\ \lVert\omega^{*}-\omega_{1}\rVert\leq 2B_{\omega}
, this theorem implies that with
\mathbb{P}>1-\delta
,

\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\omega})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})<\frac{2DB_{\omega}}{\sqrt{T}}\left(1+2\sqrt{2\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right).

(4.5)

We will use a lemma from the original proof:

Lemma 4.2
(Lemma 14.1 in
[
36
]
).

Let
\mathbf{v}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{v}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
be a sequence of vectors and
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{P}
be a convex set. We define a sequence
\omega_{t}
recursively. Let
\omega_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
be arbitrary and

\omega_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}(\omega_{t}-\zeta\mathbf{v}_{t})

(4.6)

for
t>1
. Then, for any
\omega^{*}\in\mathcal{B}
,

\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\omega_{t}-\omega^{*})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{t}\leq\frac{\lVert\omega^{*}-\omega_{1}\rVert^{2}}{2\zeta}+\frac{\zeta}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{t}\rVert^{2}.

(4.7)

The original lemma omits the projection
\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}
, but subsequent discussion in
[
36
, Section 14.4.1]
shows that the lemma holds with projection as well. The lemma was modified to accommodate nonzero
\omega_{1}
without affecting the proof. If we further assume that
\lVert\mathbf{v}_{t}\rVert\leq D
for all
t
, then, with the learning rate
\zeta=2B_{\omega}/(D\sqrt{T})
, we get

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\omega_{t}-\omega^{*})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{t}\leq\frac{2B_{\omega}D}{\sqrt{T}}.

(4.8)

Proof
(Proof of Theorem
4.1
.).

Let
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t}
be a filtration defined as

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{t}),

(4.9)

where
\mathbf{z}_{t}\in\mathcal{Z}
are the iid random data samples used to compute stochastic gradients
\mathbf{v}_{t}=\nabla_{\omega}l(\omega_{t},\mathbf{z}_{t})
. With this definition, the sequence
\{\omega_{t}\}_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}
-predictable and the sequence
\{\mathbf{v}_{t}\}_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}
-adapted. We now define the
\mathcal{F}
-adapted sequence
\{a_{t}\}_{t}
as

a_{t}=\left(\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{t})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})\right)-(\omega_{t}-\omega^{*})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{t}.

(4.10)

Since
\mathfrak{R}
is convex,

\mathbb{E}\left[a_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]=\left(\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{t})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})\right)-(\omega_{t}-\omega^{*})^{\intercal}\nabla f(\omega_{t})\leq 0.

(4.11)

This means that
a_{t}
form a supermartingale difference sequence. Furthermore, bounded domain of
\mathfrak{R}
and
\lVert\mathbf{v}_{t}\rVert\leq D
(from which follows Lipschitzness of
\mathfrak{R}
with parameter
D
) allow us to write

|a_{t}|\leq 4DB_{\omega}\quad\text{a.s.}

(4.12)

Therefore, Hoeffding-Azuma inequality (Lemma
8.3
) is applicable:

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{t})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})\right)<\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\omega_{t}-\omega^{*})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{t}+4DB_{\omega}\sqrt{2T\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right]\geq 1-\delta.

(4.13)

Assume that the event in (
4.13
) holds. Then, we can employ Lemma
4.2
to finish the proof:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\omega})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{t})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*}))

(4.14)

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\omega_{t}-\omega^{*})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{t}+4DB_{\omega}\sqrt{\frac{2}{T}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}

(4.15)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lVert\omega^{*}-\omega_{1}\rVert^{2}}{2\zeta T}+\frac{\zeta}{2T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{t}\rVert^{2}+4DB_{\omega}\sqrt{\frac{2}{T}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}

(4.16)

The first step here relies on the convexity of
\mathfrak{R}
. The third — on Lemma
4.2
.

\square

9
Locally convex loss

The analysis above requires
\mathfrak{R}
to be convex on its entire domain. Often, this is an unrealistic assumption. We can relax it by requiring
\mathfrak{R}
to only be convex
locally
, in a region around
\omega^{*}
. We will call this region the
convex basin
around
\omega^{*}
. However, if we want SGD to converge to
\omega^{*}
, we still have to require that it is initialized within the convex basin. Now we have an additional worry: we need to avoid the trajectory escaping the basin at any point. Without knowing where in the basin did we land, we cannot simply enforce the trajectory to stay within it. Instead, we can provide a high-probability statement that the trajectory will stay in the basin without any enforcement.

Theorem 4.3
(Containing SGD within a convex basin).

Let
\mathfrak{R}:\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined over a ball

\mathcal{B}=\left\{\omega:\ \lVert\omega\rVert\leq B_{\omega}\right\}

(4.17)

as before. Starting at a point
\omega_{1}\in\mathcal{B}
, we run SGD for
T
steps with constant stepsize
\zeta
. Assume that
\lVert\mathbf{v}_{t}\rVert\leq D
a.s. Let
\omega^{*}\in\argmin_{\omega\in\mathcal{B}}\mathfrak{R}(\omega)
and assume
\lVert\omega_{1}-\omega^{*}\rVert<\varepsilon
. Further, assume that
\mathfrak{R}
is convex within the region

\mathcal{C}=\left\{\omega:\lVert\omega^{*}-\omega\rVert<\varepsilon_{c}\right\}\subset\mathcal{B}

(4.18)

for some
\varepsilon_{c}>\varepsilon
. Finally, assume that for some
\delta>0
,

\!\!\!\!\!\zeta^{2}D^{2}T+5(\zeta^{2}D^{2}+4\zeta DB_{\omega})\sqrt{T\left(\log\log(eT(2\zeta^{2}D^{2}+8\zeta DB_{\omega})^{2})\right)_{+}+T\log(2/\delta)}\\
<\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}.

(4.19)

Then, with probability
\mathbb{P}\geq 1-2\delta
, the entire trajectory of SGD will be contained within
\mathcal{C}
(
\forall t\ \omega_{t}\in\mathcal{C}
) and the suboptimality gap will be bounded as

\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\omega})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})<\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\zeta T}+\frac{\zeta D^{2}}{2}+4DB_{\omega}\sqrt{\frac{2}{T}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}.

(4.20)

Note that if
\varepsilon_{c}>2\varepsilon
, a simple solution would be to project SGD onto the known region

\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}=\left\{\omega:\lVert\omega_{0}-\omega\rVert\leq\varepsilon\right\},

(4.21)

since in this case
\omega^{*}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
and
\mathfrak{R}
is convex on
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
. However, this stops working when
\varepsilon_{c}<2\varepsilon
. What is more, this approach requires accurate knowledge of
\varepsilon
, while the lemma above simply asks for enough data as
\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}
becomes smaller. The precise relationship between the epsilons,
\zeta
,
T
, and
\delta
is captured by (
4.19
). Asymptotically, as
\delta\to 0,\ \zeta\to 0,\ T\to\infty
, it asks for

O\left(\zeta^{2}T+\zeta\sqrt{T\left(\log\log(\zeta^{2}T)\right)_{+}+T\log(1/\delta)}\right)<\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}.

(4.22)

One possible choice of
\zeta
that satisfies this requirement for large
T
is

\zeta=O\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}}{\sqrt{T\log(1/\delta)}}\right).

(4.23)

Thus, we adjust the asymptotics of
\zeta
by a factor of
(\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2})/\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}
compared to the case where
\mathfrak{R}
is convex on the entire domain. Despite the different
\zeta
, for a fixed
\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}
asymptotically we get the same high-probability bound as (
4.5
). Epsilons enter the high-probability guarantee as

\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\omega})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})<O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{T}}\Bigg(1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}}{\log(1/\delta)}\Bigg)\right).

(4.24)

However, selecting
\zeta
according to (
4.23
) might require a large
T
to satisfy (
4.19
). It also requires a known lower bound on
\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}
. If we are willing to sacrifice the asymptotics to make Theorem
4.3
work in the small
T
and unknown
\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}
regime, we can make
\zeta
decay faster in
T
.

Internally, the theorem depends on a high-probability uniform bound for a maringale with bounded differences. We rely on the time-uniform Hoeffding-Azuma inequality, in the form it was presented in
[
22
]
. This form was itself adapted from the recent work
[
17
]
, which presents a new method to derive so-called
curved
uniform bounds for martingales.

Proof
(Theorem
4.3
).

Assume that during the first
t-1
steps, the projection operator
\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}
was never involved, i.e. that

\forall k\leq t-1\quad\lVert\omega_{k}-\zeta\mathbf{v}_{k}\rVert<B_{\omega}.

(4.25)

Let
\widetilde{\omega}_{t+1}=\omega_{t}-\zeta\mathbf{v}_{t}
. Note that we have
\omega_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}\widetilde{\omega}_{t+1}
. Then,

\displaystyle\lVert\widetilde{\omega}_{t+1}-\omega^{*}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\omega_{t}-\omega^{*}\rVert^{2}+\zeta^{2}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{t}\rVert^{2}+2\zeta(\omega^{*}-\omega_{t})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{t}.

(4.26)

Since by assumption for
k<t
we have
\omega_{k+1}=\omega_{k}-\zeta\mathbf{v}_{k}
, we can apply the above equation recursively and get

\lVert\widetilde{\omega}_{t+1}-\omega^{*}\rVert^{2}=\lVert\omega_{1}-\omega^{*}\rVert^{2}+\zeta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{k}\rVert^{2}+2\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\omega^{*}-\omega_{k})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{k}.

(4.27)

Now the way forward becomes more clear. We know that
\lVert\omega_{1}-\omega^{*}\rVert^{2}<\varepsilon^{2}
, and we need to show that the two sums at all times stay below
\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}
. Let us introduce

a_{k}=\zeta^{2}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{k}\rVert^{2}+2\zeta(\omega^{*}-\omega_{k})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta^{2}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{k}\rVert^{2}+2\zeta(\omega^{*}-\omega_{k})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right].

(4.28)

It is an
\mathcal{F}
-adapted martingale difference sequence. We also know that

|a_{k}|\leq 2\zeta^{2}D^{2}+8\zeta DB_{\omega}=:B_{a}\quad\text{a.s.}

(4.29)

Let
S_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}a_{k}
. By Lemma
8.4
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\forall t\quad S_{t}<\frac{5B_{a}}{2}\sqrt{t\left((\log\log etB_{a}^{2})_{+}+\log(2/\delta)\right)}\right]\geq 1-\delta.

(4.30)

We will denote the event in the above as
E
. We will also introduce a shorthand

B_{\delta}(t)=\frac{5B_{a}}{2}\sqrt{t\left((\log\log etB_{a}^{2})_{+}+\log(2/\delta)\right)}.

(4.31)

Whenever
E
holds, we can write for all
t

\displaystyle\zeta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{k}\rVert^{2}+2\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\omega^{*}-\omega_{k})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{k}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad<\sum_{k=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta^{2}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{k}\rVert^{2}+2\zeta(\omega^{*}-\omega_{k})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]+B_{\delta}(t)

(4.32)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\zeta^{2}D^{2}t+2\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\omega^{*}-\omega_{k})^{\intercal}\nabla\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{k})+B_{\delta}(t).

(4.33)

Let
F_{t}
be the event defined as

F_{t}=\left\{\forall k\leq t\quad\omega_{k}\in\mathcal{C}\right\}\ \cap\ \left\{\forall k<t\quad\omega_{k}-\zeta\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{C}\right\}.

(4.34)

Note that
F_{t}
implies that the projection
\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}
was not involved during the first
t-1
steps, since
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{B}
. Therefore, whenever
F_{t}
holds, we can use (
4.27
). The events
\{E\cap F_{t}\}_{t}
form a nested sequence. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[E\cap F_{T}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[E\cap F_{1}\right]\prod_{t=1}^{T-1}\mathbb{P}\left[E\cap F_{t+1}|E\cap F_{t}\right]

(4.35)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[E\right]\prod_{t=1}^{T-1}\mathbb{P}\left[F_{t+1}|E\cap F_{t}\right].

(4.36)

In the above, we used that by the conditions of the theorem,
F_{1}
always holds. We will now focus on the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}\left[F_{t+1}|E\cap F_{t}\right]
. Assuming
E
and
F_{t}
hold, we use (
4.27
) and (
4.33
) as

\displaystyle\lVert\widetilde{\omega}_{t+1}-\omega^{*}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\omega_{1}-\omega^{*}\rVert^{2}+\zeta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{k}\rVert^{2}+2\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\omega^{*}-\omega_{k})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{k}

(4.37)

\displaystyle<\lVert\omega_{1}-\omega^{*}\rVert^{2}+\zeta^{2}D^{2}t+2\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\omega^{*}-\omega_{k})^{\intercal}\nabla\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{k})+B_{\delta}(t).

(4.38)

Since
F_{t}
holds,
\mathfrak{R}
is convex at
\omega_{k}
for all
k\leq t
, so
(\omega^{*}-\omega_{k})^{\intercal}\nabla\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{k})\leq\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{k})
. Taking into account that
\omega^{*}\in\argmin_{\omega\in\mathcal{B}}\mathfrak{R}(\omega)
,

\displaystyle\lVert\widetilde{\omega}_{t+1}-\omega^{*}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle<\lVert\omega_{1}-\omega^{*}\rVert^{2}+\zeta^{2}D^{2}t+B_{\delta}(t)

(4.39)

\displaystyle<\varepsilon^{2}+(\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2})=\varepsilon_{c}^{2}.

(4.40)

In the last inequality, we used (
4.19
) and the definition (
4.29
) of
B_{a}
. It follows from (
4.40
) that
\omega_{t+1}=\widetilde{\omega}_{t+1}
, and, together with
F_{t}
, this gives that
F_{t+1}
also holds. Overall, we have shown that
\mathbb{P}\left[F_{t+1}|E\cap F_{t}\right]=1
. Substituting into (
4.36
), we get

\mathbb{P}\left[F_{T}\right]\geq\mathbb{P}\left[E\cap F_{T}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[E\right]\geq 1-\delta.

(4.41)

Once we know that
F_{T}
holds, SGD stays in the convex basin of
\mathfrak{R}
, and Theorem
4.1
becomes applicable (our SGD takes the same steps as SGD on a restriction
\left.\mathfrak{R}\right|_{\mathcal{C}}
). Choosing the same probability of failure
\delta
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\left.\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\omega})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})<\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\zeta T}+\frac{\zeta D^{2}}{2}+4DB_{\omega}\sqrt{\frac{2}{T}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\quad\right|B_{T}\right]\geq 1-\delta.

(4.42)

Together with (
4.41
), this gives the statement of the theorem.

\square

10
Preconditioning in E2TC

Now we can discuss the SGD variant that is performed by E2TC. During the second stage of exploration, Algorithm
1
jointly refines the parameters
\omega=(\mathbf{w},\theta)
. It minimizes the risk function
\mathfrak{R}
, which we provide here for a quick reference:

\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w},\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{X,r}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta}(X)-r\right)^{2}\right].

(4.43)

Here,
r=\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)+\eta
is the random reward from action
X
, and
\eta\sim\mathcal{H}
is zero-mean noise. The function is defined over a product of closed convex sets
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{w}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\theta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
. Given a random action-reward pair
(\widetilde{X}_{t},\tilde{r}_{t})
, we get the following unbiased gradient estimates w.r.t. parameters
\mathbf{w}_{t}
and
\theta_{t}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left.\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\right|_{\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}_{t}}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}_{t}\varphi_{\theta_{t}}(\widetilde{X}_{t})-\tilde{r}_{t}\right)^{2}=2(\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}_{t}\varphi_{\theta_{t}}(\widetilde{X}_{t})-\tilde{r}_{t})\varphi_{\theta_{t}}(\widetilde{X}_{t}),

(4.44)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left.\nabla_{\theta}\right|_{\theta=\theta_{t}}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}_{t}\varphi_{\theta_{t}}(\widetilde{X}_{t})-\tilde{r}_{t}\right)^{2}=2\left(\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}_{t}\varphi_{\theta_{t}}(\widetilde{X}_{t})-\tilde{r}_{t}\right)J_{\theta}(\widetilde{X}_{t})^{\intercal}\mathbf{w}_{t}.

(4.45)

It is worth reminding here that
J_{\theta}(X)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{0}}
is the Jacobian of
\varphi_{\theta}(X)
w.r.t.
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
. By Assumption
3
, its norm is bounded by
\lVert J_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\rVert_{2}\leq L_{\varphi}
. Overall,
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
and
\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}
can be bounded almost surely by
D_{\mathbf{w}}
and
D_{\theta}
respectively, defined in (
2.15
) and (
2.16
).

SGD in E2TC is initialized at
\omega_{0}=(\mathbf{w}_{0},\theta_{0})
, where
\mathbf{w}_{0}
is produced by Ridge regression. Results from Chapter
3
give us high-probability bounds on the suboptimality gap
\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{0})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})
. Unfortunately, this cannot be directly plugged into the bounds in Theorem
4.1
or
4.3
. If the risk was Polyak-Łojasiewicz (strongly convex functions are also included in that class, but they are not as relevant for us, as we discuss at the end of Section
7
), then a low suboptimality gap of
\omega_{0}
would actually imply that it is close in
2
-norm to some minimizer
\omega^{*}\in\argmin_{\omega}\mathfrak{R}(\omega)

[
10
]
. Convex functions do not necessarily satisfy this property.

Although Ridge regression cannot provide a reasonable bound on
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert
, in Section
6
we have seen that it does give us a bound (
3.28
) on the matrix-weighted norm
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}
, which behaves asymptotically as

\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}^{2}=\widetilde{O}\left(\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d+\frac{d}{\sqrt{T_{1}}}+\lambda\right).

(4.46)

This behaviour is as expected: there is a constant term
O(\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d)
that comes from misspecification, a term decaying with
T_{1}
, and the cost of regularization.

To get a bound on SGD suboptimality in terms of this norm, we need to modify the algorithm. Instead of
\mathfrak{R}
, we can minimize a similar loss
\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}:\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}}\times\mathcal{B}_{\theta}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}},\theta)=\mathfrak{R}\left(\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1/2}\widetilde{\mathbf{w}},\theta\right).

(4.47)

We introduced a change of variables

\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}=\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{1/2}\mathbf{w},\quad\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}}=\left\{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}:\ \widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1/2}\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{w}}\right\}.

(4.48)

Under this change, the 2-norm transforms into the desired matrix norm. Note that we used the regularized sample covariance
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}
, as opposed to true covariance, because this is the matrix that is available when running the algorithm. Thus, results from Section
7
are not directly applicable, and we have to rely on the slightly worse bounds from Section
6
. The rule also requires that
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}
is invertible, which is why we cannot use
\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}
, even though this would give a better asymptotic bound than (
4.46
).

Running SGD on
\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}
is not equivalent to running it on
\mathfrak{R}
. The trajectory on
\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}
will be generated by the following update rules:

\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t}-\zeta_{\mathbf{w}}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}),\quad\theta_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{\theta}}(\theta_{t}-\zeta_{\theta}\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}),

(4.49)

where
\zeta_{\mathbf{w}},\ \zeta_{\theta}>0
are the learning rates corresponding to the last layer and to other parameters, respectively;
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
and
\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}
are the stochastic gradient estimates. The update rule of
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
can be represented in the original space of
(\mathbf{w},\theta)
as

\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{w}}}(\mathbf{w}_{t}-\zeta_{\mathbf{w}}\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1/2}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}).

(4.50)

Since
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
is an unbiased gradient estimator of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}
, it must satisfy

\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}|\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t},\theta_{t}\right]=\nabla_{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}}\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{t},\theta_{t})=(\widehat{\Sigma(\theta_{0})}+\lambda I)^{-1/2}\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}_{t},\theta_{t}).

(4.51)

Now, let
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
be a gradient estimate of
\mathfrak{R}
, i.e. a random variable such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}|\mathbf{w}_{t},\theta_{t}\right]=\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}_{t},\theta_{t}).

(4.52)

Then,

\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}=(\widehat{\Sigma(\theta_{0})}+\lambda I)^{-1/2}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}

(4.53)

satisfies (
4.51
), and therefore provides an unbiased gradient estimator for
\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}
. Substituting it into (
4.50
), together with the unchanged rule for
\theta
, we get an update rule of the form

\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{w}}}(\mathbf{w}_{t}-\zeta_{\mathbf{w}}\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}{\mathbf{v}}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}),\quad\theta_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{\theta}}(\theta_{t}-\zeta_{\theta}\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}).

(4.54)

This is the update rule that we use in E2TC. In general, performing updates of the form
\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\mathbf{w}_{t}-\zeta_{\mathbf{w}}A^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
instead of
\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\mathbf{w}_{t}-\zeta_{\mathbf{w}}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
is called
preconditioning
of SGD, and it is sometimes used to improve convergence of the algorithm in practice. As we shall see in the following chapter, the preconditioned variant with the update rule (
4.54
) is also helpful theoretically. In our case, it provides bounds suitable for analysis after the first phase of E2TC.

The coordinate transformation given by (
4.47
) and (
4.48
) hints at how the modified guarantees materialize. Let us briefly consider the general setting of a globally convex loss function from Section
8
again. If we use updates of the form
\omega_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}(\omega_{t}-\zeta A^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{t})
, we can apply Theorem
4.1
to
\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}(\widetilde{\omega})=\mathfrak{R}(A^{-1/2}\widetilde{\omega})
, where
\widetilde{\omega}=A^{1/2}\omega
. We would get a high-probability bound that translates back into the space of
\omega
as

\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\omega})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})<\frac{\lVert\omega^{*}-\omega_{1}\rVert_{A}^{2}}{2\zeta T}+\frac{\zeta}{2T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{t}\rVert_{A^{-1}}^{2}+4DB_{\omega}\sqrt{\frac{2}{T}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}.

(4.55)

Note how the matrix-weighted norms appeared in this bound compared to (
4.55
), which only uses
2
-norms.

Chapter 5
Analysis of the main algorithm

Previous chapters provide the building blocks for analyzing the performance of the entire E2TC. Here, we bring these blocks together. We show sublinear regret assuming that the risk is locally convex around the minimum and that the pre-trained weights are, in a certain sense, close enough to the optimal ones. We require a special shape of the convex basin for our results to be applicable. Our main result, Theorem
5.1
, provides regret and suboptimality gap bounds without hard-coded hyperparameter choices. This result can therefore be used in various situations as a template for hyperparameter tuning. Under the constraints of the theorem, one can optimize the desired bound w.r.t. the hyperparameters of E2TC. In Section
12
, we perform this optimization and show how theory dictates optimal hyperparameter choices for minimal sample complexity and regret. We arrive at a regret bound of
\widetilde{O}((KT)^{4/5})
in the regime
d=O(T^{2/5})
, and to a family of dimension-dependent bounds for
d
that scales faster than
T^{2/5}
.

11
Main result

Convex loss functions that are not strongly convex occupy the central place in this thesis. Analogously to Theorem
4.3
, we can show that if SGD is initialized at a point
\omega_{0}=(\mathbf{w}_{0},\theta_{0})
“sufficiently close” to a true minimizer
\omega^{*}=(\mathbf{w}^{*},\theta^{*})
, and the loss is convex in a basin around
\omega^{*}
, then with high probability the trajectory of SGD will not escape the basin and the suboptimality gap of the final estimate will be small. Since we can only show that
\mathbf{w}_{0}
is close to
\mathbf{w}^{*}
in terms of the matrix norm distance
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}
or
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}
, the shape of the convex basin has to be adjusted accordingly. The initial weights
\theta_{0}
of the representation network also have to be close to
\theta^{*}
. Since
\theta_{0}
is provided to the algorithm from outside, we will simply require that the
2
-norm distance
\varepsilon_{\theta}=\lVert\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}\rVert
is small. Note that
\varepsilon_{0}
given by (
3.10
) can be bounded by
\varepsilon_{\theta}
under Assumption
3
:

\varepsilon_{0}\leq B_{\mathbf{w}}L_{\varphi}\varepsilon_{\theta}.

(5.1)

However, we believe it is instructive to keep both
\varepsilon_{\theta}
and
\varepsilon_{0}
when formulating the result, since the bound (
5.1
) can be rather loose.

The main theorem intentionally provides a general statement without committing to a hyperparameter selection. In Section
12
, we provide a less general and more clear statement which follows from this theorem when hyperparameters are chosen in a certain way. Before presenting the theorem, we discuss its assumptions in detail. These assumptions are made in addition to the three basic assumptions we presented in Section
2
.

The convexity requirement on the risk function reads as follows:

Assumption 4
(Local convexity of
\mathfrak{R}
).

Let
\mathfrak{R}:\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{w}}\times\mathcal{B}_{\theta}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by (
4.43
) on the domains

\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{w}}=\left\{\mathbf{w}:\ \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\leq B_{\mathbf{w}}\right\},\quad\mathcal{B}_{\theta}=\left\{\theta:\ \lVert\theta\rVert\leq B_{\theta}\right\}.

(5.2)

There exists
0<\varepsilon_{c}<1
such that
\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w},\theta)
is convex in the region
\mathcal{C}
around
(\mathbf{w}^{*},\theta^{*})\in\argmin_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{w}},\theta\in\mathcal{B}_{\theta}}\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w},\theta)
. The region is defined as

\mathcal{C}=\left\{(\mathbf{w},\theta):\ \lVert\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}+\lVert\theta-\theta^{*}\rVert_{2}^{2}<\varepsilon_{c}^{2}\right\}\subset\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{w}}\times\mathcal{B}_{\theta}.

(5.3)

The convex region
\mathcal{C}
is a ball around
\omega^{*}
with respect to a metric generated by a special norm which we will denote
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{c}
. For
\omega=(\mathbf{w},\theta)
, it is defined as

\lVert\omega\rVert_{c}^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}+\lVert\theta\rVert^{2}.

(5.4)

The requirement for
\mathfrak{R}
to be convex in the ball
\mathcal{C}
can be seen as somewhat contrived, especially because of the dependency of the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{c}
on
\theta_{0}
through
\Sigma_{0}
. However, there is no clear way to get rid of this dependency, because the linear estimate
\mathbf{w}_{0}
is not in general constrained along the nullspace of
\Sigma_{0}
. If the projection of
\mathbf{w}_{0}
onto this nullspace can be arbitrary, we need the projection of
\mathcal{C}
to be the entire nullspace as well. Otherwise, there is no guarantee that
\mathbf{w}_{0}
lands into the basin. A helpful way to think about this requirement is as follows. When
(\mathbf{w},\theta)\in\mathcal{C}
, we have that
\lVert\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}<\varepsilon_{c}
and
\lVert\theta-\theta^{*}\rVert<\varepsilon_{c}
. Therefore, we know from (
3.9
) that
\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w},\theta_{0})-\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}^{*},\theta^{*})<\varepsilon_{c}+\varepsilon_{0}
. When
\mathfrak{R}
is
L_{\theta}
-Lipschitz w.r.t.
\theta
, we also know that
|\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w},\theta)-\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w},\theta_{0})|<2L_{\theta}\varepsilon_{c}
. Note that
\theta_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
, since
\varepsilon_{\theta}<\varepsilon_{c,\theta}
. Together, these facts give us that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w},\theta)-\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}^{*},\theta^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w},\theta)-\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w},\theta_{0})+\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w},\theta_{0})-\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}^{*},\theta^{*})

(5.5)

\displaystyle<(2L_{\theta}+1)\varepsilon_{c}+\varepsilon_{0}.

(5.6)

Thus, the convexity condition can be replaced by a (stronger) requirement that
\mathfrak{R}
is convex in the region
\mathcal{C}_{l}
of low loss, which is somewhat more intuitive:

\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{C}_{l}=\left\{(\mathbf{w},\theta):\ \mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w},\theta)-\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}^{*},\theta^{*})<(2L_{\theta}+1)\varepsilon_{c}+\varepsilon_{0}\right\}.

(5.7)

Assumption
4
imposes
\varepsilon_{c}<1
without loss of generality. If it does not hold, we can rescale the parameter space appropriately and adjust the constants
B_{\mathbf{w}}
and
B_{\theta}
that bound the size of the domain. At the same time,
\varepsilon_{c}<1
significantly simplifies the exposition of the result.

SGD only has a chance of initializing within the convex basin
\mathcal{C}
if the provided pre-trained weights
\theta_{0}
are good enough. The following assumption formalizes this requirement:

Assumption 5
(Quality of
\theta_{0}
).

The provided
\theta_{0}
satisfies

2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d+\varepsilon_{\theta}^{2}<\varepsilon_{c}^{2},

(5.8)

where

This result tells the following story about optimizing a locally convex

The theorem introduces an extra hyperparameter

The lower bound

\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert^{2}_{\Sigma_{0}}<\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}

{T_{1}\to\infty}

5.11

to get

. This justifies the dependency of
N_{1}
on
\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}
. Asymptotics (

) of

and

can be slightly improved, at the expense of becoming too large to be handled efficiently. The tighter asymptotics of
N_{1}
are given by (

Note that if we only perform linear estimation of
\mathbf{w}_{0}
and omit SGD, Theorem
3.6
asks for

T_{1}=1/\lambda^{2}
. The bound

in Theorem
5.1

.
N_{1}
also depends inversely on
\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}

becomes larger than
T
(or when assumptions (
5.11

3
to estimate
\mathbf{w}_{0}
, but then we have to incur the linear regret coming from misspecified weights
\theta_{0}
.

We also impose that the learning rates for
\zeta_{\mathbf{w}}
and
\zeta_{\theta}
here should be equal. This requirement can be lifted. Consider a function
\overline{\mathfrak{R}}
in the transformed space, defined as

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}},\theta):=f\left(\frac{\zeta_{\mathbf{w}}}{\zeta_{\theta}}\widetilde{\mathbf{w}},\theta\right).

(5.18)

Here, we introduced a coordinate transformation

. As one can easily verify, running SGD on

with learning rates
\zeta_{\mathbf{w}}
for

with a single learning rate

\mathfrak{R}
was locally convex around its minimum,

will keep this property, albeit with a different convex region. Note how this coordinate transformation is similar to the transformation (

12
Hyperparameter tuning

In this section we select hyperparameters for E2TC. Theorem
5.1
poses a constrained optimization problem of minimizing the bound (
5.14
) with respect to the hyperparameters under the constraints (
5.11
) and (
5.12
). In addition to setting the learning rate
\zeta
and the regularization parameter
\lambda
, we are interested in the optimal distribution of a fixed exploration time
T_{\mathrm{exp}}=T_{1}+T_{2}
between the two stages of exploration. This question is important both to obtain optimal rates of the regret in the bandit setup and to get sample complexity bounds with a fixed amount of data. The following result shows the asymptotic behavior of the optimal hyperparameters satisfying the constraints of Theorem
5.1
.

Theorem 5.2
(Hyperparameter tuning for E2TC).

Let
T_{\mathrm{exp}}=T_{1}+T_{2}
be the total number of steps that E2TC spends in the two exploration phases. Suppose assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
, and
5
hold. Define
\Delta\varepsilon\coloneqq\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d-\varepsilon_{\theta}^{2}
. Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Choose the hyper-parameters such that
T_{1}<T_{\mathrm{exp}}
and

\displaystyle T_{1}=O\left(\left(1+\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d\right)\frac{d^{\alpha}T_{\mathrm{exp}}^{\beta}\log(d/\delta)\log(1/\delta)}{\Delta\varepsilon^{3}}\right),

(5.19)

\displaystyle\ T_{2}=T_{\mathrm{exp}}-T_{1}

(5.20)

\displaystyle\lambda=O\left(\frac{\Delta\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}\right),\ \zeta=O\left(\frac{\Delta\varepsilon}{\sqrt{T_{2}\log(1/\delta)}}\right),

(5.21)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon_{\theta}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d\right).

(5.22)

Then the constraints (
5.11
) and (
5.12
) are met and E2TC satisfies

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\mathbf{w}},\overline{\theta})-\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}^{*},\theta^{*})=O\Bigg(

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{T_{2}}}\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d+\varepsilon_{\theta}^{2}}{\Delta\varepsilon}+\frac{\Delta\varepsilon}{\log(1/\delta)}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\Delta\varepsilon^{2}d^{1-\alpha/2}}{(1+\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d)\log(d/\delta)\sqrt{T_{2}}T_{\mathrm{exp}}^{\beta/2}}\Bigg)

(5.23)

with probability not less than
1-10\delta
.

For the purposes of this theorem, the
O
-notation hides
B_{\mathbf{w}},B_{\theta},B_{\varphi},B_{\eta},D_{\mathbf{w}},D_{\theta}
, and constant factors. The asymptotic expressions for
T_{1},\ \lambda,\ \zeta
should be understood in a sense that there exist functions
\lambda(\Delta\varepsilon,\delta)
,
\zeta(\Delta\varepsilon,T_{2},\delta)
, and
T_{1}(\Delta\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},d,\delta)
with the provided asymptotics such that, for large enough
T_{\mathrm{exp}}
and
d
and for small enough
\delta
, when the hyperparameters are selected according to these functions, the suboptimality bound will satisfy (
5.23
). We would like to contrast this interpretation to the
incorrect
idea that any selection of hyperparameters with the provided asymptotics would lead to the suboptimality bound (
5.23
).

T_{1}
can only be selected according to (
5.19

T_{\mathrm{exp}}
. This is why we left two hyperparameters in the new theorem — the powers
\alpha,\beta
in the expression for
T_{1}
can be adjusted based on the situation. For example, if

, we are in the data-rich regime. In this case, selecting
\alpha=2
avoids the dependency of the bound (
5.23
) on
d
altogether. In many cases, we can expect this to work, because

is the dimension of the last layer
\mathbf{w}
, not of the entire parameter space of
(\mathbf{w},\theta)
.

We can compare the bound (
5.23
) to (
4.24

d
and comes from the need to estimate the first layer from scratch. The last term is the only one with explicit dependence on
d
. We note here that the requirements of the theorem enforce

\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d+\varepsilon_{\theta}

Proof
.

First, we replace the bound (
5.14
) by a slightly looser one, using
(a+b+c)^{2}\leq 3(a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2})

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\mathbf{w}},\overline{\theta})-\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}^{*},\theta^{*})<\frac{1}{2\zeta T_{2}}

\displaystyle\left(3\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d+3\varepsilon_{\delta}d+3\varepsilon_{\eta}^{2}+3B_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}\lambda+\varepsilon_{\theta}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{D_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}\zeta}{2\lambda}+\frac{\zeta D_{\theta}^{2}}{2}+Q_{\delta}

(5.24)

where we also used

\frac{3B_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}\lambda}{2T_{2}\zeta}+\frac{D_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}\zeta}{2\lambda}.

+\frac{B_{\mathbf{w}}D_{\mathbf{w}}\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{T_{2}}}+Q_{\delta},

5.12

\zeta

\delta\to 0

2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d+\frac{B_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}}{2}\lambda<\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}<\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon_{\theta}^{2}.

T_{1}=\Omega\left(\frac{\log(d/\delta)}{\lambda\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{16B_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}+4D_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}\zeta^{2}/\lambda}{T_{1}}\left(B_{\varphi}^{2}\log\frac{2d}{\delta}+\sqrt{B_{\varphi}^{4}\log^{2}\frac{2d}{\delta}+2B_{\varphi}^{4}T_{1}\log\frac{2d}{\delta}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=O\left(\lambda\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}+\sqrt{\lambda}\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}+\zeta^{2}\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}+\frac{\zeta^{2}\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)

5.26

O\Big(\zeta\sqrt{T_{2}}+\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}\zeta\sqrt{T_{2}}+\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}\sqrt{\zeta}T_{2}^{1/4}+\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}\zeta^{2}+\frac{\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}\zeta^{3/2}}{T_{2}^{1/4}}\\

\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}

\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon_{\theta}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d\right).

(5.33)

With this bound on the suboptimality gap at hand, it is easy to show a regret guarantee:

Corollary 5.3
(Regret of E2TC).

Let the assumptions of Theorem
5.2
hold. In addition, assume that the parameters related to sizes of convex regions (
\varepsilon_{0}
,
\varepsilon_{\theta}
,
\varepsilon_{c}
,
\Delta\varepsilon
) are asymptotically
O(1)
. Assume also that
\zeta,\lambda
are selected according to (
5.21
), and
T_{1}
— according to (
5.19
) (without yet fixing
\alpha
and
\beta
). Then, the regret of E2TC admits a bound

R_{1:T}=\widetilde{O}(T_{1}+T_{2}+KTT_{2}^{-1/4}+KT\sqrt{d}T_{1}^{-1/4}T_{2}^{-1/4}).

(5.41)

When
K=O(T^{1/5})
and
d=O(T^{2/5})
, we can select
T_{1}=d^{2},T_{2}=(KT)^{4/5}
and get a bound
R_{1:T}=\widetilde{O}((KT)^{4/5})
.

Proof
.

As long as hyperparameters are selected according to Theorem
5.2
, we know that the conditions for the regret bound will be satisfied. Hence, the suboptimality gap
\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\mathbf{w}},\overline{\theta})-\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}^{*},\theta^{*})
will be with high probability bounded by (
5.39
). We can now use Theorem
2.1
to translate this risk
bound into a bound on regret, and arrive at (
5.41
).

\square

Depending on how
d
and
K
scale with
T
, we can select different
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
and achieve different regrets. For example, we can assume that
d=\Theta(T^{\gamma})
for some
\gamma>0
and
K=O(1)
. Depending on
\gamma
, the regret bounds can be:

1.

\gamma\leq\frac{2}{5}
. Select
T_{1}=O(d^{2}),T_{2}=O(T^{4/5})
, achieve
R_{1:T}=O(T^{4/5})
.

2.

\frac{2}{5}<\gamma<1
. Select
T_{1}=T_{2}=O(T^{(\gamma+2)/3})
, achieve
R_{1:T}=O(T^{(\gamma+2)/3})
.

3.

\gamma\geq 1
. Sublinear regret cannot be reached.

We note that if
\gamma>1
, the hidden dimension of the last layer of the network is larger than the number of data samples. In this regime, we should not expect local convexity of the loss anyway. NTK- or Polyak-Łojasiewicz-based analysis would be more appropriate. Alternatively, we can fall back to the lazy training analysis from Chapter
3
. If the misspecification
\varepsilon_{0}
is small enough, the regret bound of
O(\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{d}KT+(KT)^{4/5})
might still be useful.

Chapter 7
Conclusion

The main goal of this thesis was to provide an initial theoretical framework to study the effects of neural network pre-training in bandit learning scenarios. Utilizing methods from linear bandits and convex learning literature, we formalized the benefits of pre-trained weights for stochastic contextual bandit learning. We derived the precise requirements on the pre-trained weights under which we can relax global convexity of the loss function, an assumption common in the literature, to local convexity around the minimum. For realizable rewards and locally convex losses, we designed an algorithm, which we called E2TC, that achieves a regret of
\widetilde{O}((KT)^{4/5})
in the regime
d=O(T^{2/5}),\ K=O(T^{1/5})
. Here,
K
is the number of actions,
T
is the horizon, and
d
is the dimension of the representation provided by the pre-trained network.

We also provided guarantees for weak learning in stochastic contextual bandit setting, when only the linear last layer of the network is updated and the pre-trained representation weights are kept fixed. We showed how Ridge regression can be used to achieve a regret bound of
O(\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{d}KT+(KT)^{4/5})
or
O(\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{d}KT)+\widetilde{O}(d^{1/3}(KT)^{2/3})
, depending on the regularization strength. Here,
\varepsilon_{0}
is a measure of misspecification of the pre-trained weights that is less restrictive than infinity-norm distance between the representations, traditionally used for adversarial bandits with misspecification. When no misspecification is present (
\varepsilon_{0}=0
), the first bound becomes dimension-independent! This result is only possible due to the stochasticity of contexts and the
K^{4/5}
dependence of the bound on the number of actions. For both bounds, no assumption on the shape of the loss function was made, and they can be used both in under- and over-parameterized learning scenarios.

These results advertise a certain way of thinking about pre-training. Instead of relying on polynomially overparameterized networks required by NTK, we look at the problem through the lens of classical optimization, relaxing its overly restrictive assumptions with the help of pre-trained weights. We believe that much further progress can be made along this direction. Our results were based on an explore-than-commit technique, hence the regret bounds that scale worse than
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
. With an algorithm based on confidence bounds, we could probably reach this target. This approach should be applicable in the adversarial context setup too, albeit under more restrictive misspecification assumptions. When the contexts are stochastic, it was shown that regret logarithmic in
T
is achievable for linear bandits
[
12
,
4
]
. This dependence might be extendable to the more general setting of regrets modelled by neural networks.

We base our analysis of E2TC on local convexity of the risk function around its minimum. When the problem is over-parameterized, this framework is inadequate
[
29
]
. Instead we could rely on the Polyak-Łojasiewicz property of the loss. In this setting, the shape of the region around the minima of the loss could be simplified, as a small loss at a point implies its closeness to some minimizer
[
10
]
. We believe that our result that SGD stays in the convex basin w.h.p. from Section
9
can be extended to cover PŁ functions. This would immediately translate into an analogue of Theorem
5.1
in the over-parameterized regime, assuming the reward is locally PŁ.

By now, machine learning community has largely embraced the bitter lesson. As Richard Sutton concisely puts it in
[
38
]
, “The biggest lesson that can be read from 70 years of AI research is that general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin.” This effect has recently manifested itself even more explicitly through the scaling laws in large language models. We now know that scaling the size of the model translates directly into its performance on next word prediction, as long as available data is scaled accordingly
[
16
]
. This dynamic gave rise to the phenomenon of foundation models, giant neural networks trained on large amounts of unsupervised data. Fine-tuning these models is an increasingly ubiquitous trend in machine learning. We hope that the new theory will reflect this shift, and that pre-training will make its way into the guarantees for our algorithms.

\mathbb{P}[F]<4e^{-\nu}=\frac{4\tilde{d}_{1,\lambda}}{\max\left\{T_{1},e^{3}\tilde{d}_{1,\lambda}\right\}}\leq\frac{4\tilde{d}_{1,\lambda}}{T_{1}}\leq\frac{4\max\left\{1,B_{\varphi}^{2}/\lambda\right\}}{T_{1}}=O(T_{1}^{-1/2}).

We will now verify that the conditions of Theorem

\nu>\max\left\{0,2.6-\log\tilde{d}_{1,\lambda}\right\}.

(8.18)

This holds whenever

\displaystyle T_{1}\geq\frac{6B_{\varphi}^{2}}{\lambda}(\log\tilde{d}_{1,\lambda}+\nu)=6B_{\varphi}^{2}\sqrt{T_{1}}\log\max\left\{T_{1},e^{3}\tilde{d}_{1,\lambda}\right\}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{T_{1}}\geq 6B_{\varphi}^{2}\log T_{1}\right)\ \land\ \left(\sqrt{T_{1}}\geq 6B_{\varphi}^{2}(\log\tilde{d}_{1,\lambda}+3)\right).

(8.20)

The first inequality here is satisfied for all sufficiently large
T_{1}
. We note that this introduces a dependency of
N_{0}
on
B_{\varphi}
. To see that the second inequality holds as well, we remember that
\tilde{d}_{1,\lambda}\leq\max\left\{1,B_{\varphi}^{2}/\lambda\right\}
, so it suffices to have

\sqrt{T_{1}}\geq 6B_{\varphi}^{2}\left(\max\left\{0,\log(B_{\varphi}^{2}\sqrt{T_{1}})\right\}+3\right).

(8.21)

This also holds for sufficiently large
T_{1}
. Requirement (
8.107
) is satisfied for
\rho_{\lambda}
such that
\rho^{2}d_{1,\lambda}=B_{\varphi}/\lambda
, according to (
3.77
). Requirement (
8.109
) is given to us by Lemma
3.5
.

Now, we will provide the asymptotic bounds for individual terms used in Theorem
8.5
. First, the reader is reminded of the notation

\text{approx}_{\lambda}(X)=\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X).

(8.22)

Its squared expected value is bounded as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\text{approx}_{\lambda}(X)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}^{\lambda\intercal}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)\right)^{2}\right]+\lambda\|\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}\|^{2}-\lambda\|\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left(\mathbf{w}^{*}\varphi_{\theta_{0}}(X)-\mathbf{w}^{*\intercal}\varphi_{\theta^{*}}(X)\right)^{2}\right]+\lambda\|\mathbf{w}^{*}\|^{2}-\lambda\|\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq B_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}/\sqrt{T_{1}}+\varepsilon_{0}^{2}.

(8.25)

Here we used the definitions of
\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}
and of
\varepsilon_{0}
.
Next,

\log\tilde{d}_{1,\lambda}+\nu=\log\max\left\{T_{1},e^{3}\tilde{d}_{1,\lambda}\right\}\leq\log\max\left\{e^{3},T_{1},e^{3}B_{\varphi}\sqrt{T_{1}}\right\}=O(\log T_{1}).

Substituting this into (
8.112

\delta_{s}=O(T_{1}^{-1/4}\sqrt{\log T_{1}}).

(8.27)

Analogously, since

\nu=O(\log T_{1})
. Lemma

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varepsilon_{bs}&=O\left(\left(\frac{1}{T_{1}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}{\sqrt{T_{1}}}+\frac{1}{T_{1}^{3/2}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d}{T_{1}}\right)\log T_{1}\right.\\

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{\log T_{1}}{T_{1}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\log^{2}T_{1}}{\sqrt{T_{1}}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d\log T_{1}}{T_{1}}\right).

We used (
3.80

Before approaching

\displaystyle\delta_{f}\sqrt{d_{1,\lambda}d_{2,\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{4B_{\varphi}^{2}d_{1,\lambda}d_{2,\lambda}/\lambda-d_{2,\lambda}^{2}}{T_{1}}}\left(1+\sqrt{8\nu}\right)+\frac{4\nu\sqrt{B_{\varphi}^{4}d_{2,\lambda}/\lambda+d_{2,\lambda}^{2}}}{3T_{1}}

This brings us to (

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{vr}=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{1}}}+\frac{\sqrt{\log T_{1}}}{T_{1}^{3/4}}+\frac{\sqrt{\log T_{1}}}{T_{1}^{3/4}}+\frac{\log^{3/4}T_{1}}{T_{1}^{7/8}}+\frac{\log T_{1}}{T_{1}}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{1}}}\right).

(with appropriate

Bringing (

This proves the first statement of the theorem. Moving to the risk relevant to us using (

\sqrt{\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{0})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})}=O\left(\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{\hat{d}_{\lambda}}+\frac{1}{T_{1}^{1/4}}\right).

Alternatively, using (

Now we use Theorem
2.1

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R_{1:T}]=O\left(TT_{1}^{-1/2}+T_{1}+\varepsilon_{0}\sqrt{\min\{\hat{d}_{\lambda},d_{2,\lambda}\}}KT+T_{1}^{-1/4}KT\right).

\square

Appendix 8.C
Proof of Theorem

We begin by showing that with
\mathbb{P}>1-4\delta
, we will have
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma(\theta_{0})}<\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}
. For this, we once again turn to the Ridge regression analysis from Section
7
. We would like to apply Theorem
8.5
like we did to prove Theorem
3.6
. Theorem
8.5
provides its results in terms of constants
\rho_{\lambda}
and
b_{\lambda}
. As we have seen in Section
7
, they can be selected as follows. First,
\rho_{\lambda}
is such that
\rho_{\lambda}^{2}d_{1,\lambda}=B_{\varphi}^{2}/\lambda
. Next, by Lemma
3.5
,
b_{\lambda}
satisfies

\frac{b_{\lambda}^{2}d_{1,\lambda}}{T_{1}^{2}}=O\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d}{\lambda^{2}T_{1}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\lambda T_{1}^{2}}\right).

(8.53)

One condition for the theorem is that

T_{1}\geq\frac{6B_{\varphi}^{2}}{\lambda}\log\frac{\tilde{d}_{1,\lambda}}{\delta},

(8.54)

where
\tilde{d}_{1,\lambda}
is defined in (
3.37
). Since
\tilde{d}_{1,\lambda}<\min\{d,\max\{B_{\varphi}^{2}/\lambda,1\}\}
, this condition holds for
T_{1}>N_{1}
if
N_{1}
satisfies (
5.13
). The theorem also asks for
\nu>\max\{0,2.6-\log\tilde{d}_{1,\lambda}\}

or, equivalently, when
\delta<\exp(-2.6)
. Thus, Theorem
8.5
is applicable. With
\mathbb{P}\geq 1-4\delta
, the bound (
8.115
) on
\varepsilon_{bs}
and the bound (
8.116
) on
\varepsilon_{vr}
hold. Here,
\varepsilon_{bs}
and
\varepsilon_{vr}
are respectively the bias and variance errors of Ridge regression, introduced in (
3.49
). They are used to bound the distance
\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{0}\rVert_{\Sigma(\theta_{0})}
:

\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{0}\rVert_{\Sigma(\theta_{0})}<\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}\rVert_{\Sigma(\theta_{0})}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{bs}}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{vr}}.

(8.55)

The first term is bounded by Lemma
3.4
as

\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}^{\lambda}\rVert_{\Sigma(\theta_{0})}\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}B_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d_{2,\lambda},

(8.56)

where we used that, by Assumption
3

\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert<B_{\mathbf{w}}
. Bounds (
8.115
) and (
8.116
) give us the following assymptotics for
T_{1}\to\infty
and
\delta,\lambda\to 0
:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{bs}=O\left(\frac{l}{T_{1}}\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}{\lambda}+\lambda+\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d\right)+\frac{l^{2}}{T_{1}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}d}{\lambda^{2}}\right)\right),

(8.57)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varepsilon_{vr}=O\Bigg(\frac{d_{p,\lambda}}{T_{1}}+\frac{d_{p,\lambda}l^{1/2}}{\lambda^{1/2}T_{1}^{3/2}}+\frac{d_{p,\lambda}^{1/2}l}{T_{1}^{2}\lambda^{1/2}}+\frac{d_{p,\lambda}l}{T_{1}^{2}}+\frac{d_{p,\lambda}^{1/2}l^{1/2}}{T_{1}}\\
+\frac{d_{p,\lambda}^{1/2}l^{3/4}}{\lambda^{1/4}T_{1}^{5/4}}+\frac{d_{p,\lambda}^{1/4}l}{\lambda^{1/4}T_{1}^{3/2}}+\frac{d_{p,\lambda}^{1/2}l}{T_{1}^{3/2}}\Bigg),\end{split}

(8.58)

where

is the effective dimension with asymptotics
d_{p,\lambda}=O(\min\{d,1/\lambda\})
and
l=\log(1/\delta)
. To get the bound in (
8.55

\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}
, we need that
\sqrt{\varepsilon_{bs}}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{vr}}<\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}
. Asymptotically, this requires that the square root of each term is below
\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}
, which happens for large enough
T_{1}
. More specifically, there exists
N_{1}(\delta,\lambda,\varepsilon_{0},d,\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}})
with asymptotics

\begin{split}N_{1}=\Omega\Bigg(\max\Bigg\{\frac{l}{\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}},\ \frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}l}{\lambda\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}},\ \frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}dl}{\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}},\ \frac{l}{\lambda^{1/2}\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}},\ \frac{\varepsilon_{0}d^{1/2}l}{\lambda\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}},\frac{d_{p,\lambda}}{\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}},\\
\frac{d_{p,\lambda}^{2/3}l^{1/3}}{\lambda^{1/3}\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{4/3}},\ \frac{d_{p,\lambda}^{1/2}l^{1/2}}{\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}},\ \frac{d_{p,\lambda}^{2/5}l^{3/5}}{\lambda^{1/5}\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{5/8}},\ \frac{d_{p,\lambda}^{1/6}l^{2/3}}{\lambda^{1/6}\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{4/3}},\ \frac{d_{p,\lambda}^{1/3}l^{2/3}}{\Delta\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{4/3}}\Bigg\}\Bigg)\end{split}

(8.59)

such that, whenever

, we have
\lVert\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{0}\rVert_{\Sigma(\theta_{0})}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}
. The expression (

) is clearly inconvenient to handle. By using the fact that

Now we turn to the SGD stage of the algorithm. The remaining part of the proof follows the structure of the proof of Theorem
4.3
. There are two differences. First, we have to separately consider that
\mathbf{w}
and
\theta
remain close to
\mathbf{w}^{*}
and

\mathbf{w}

.

We introduce a filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T_{2}}

\quad\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{T_{1}},\widetilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{t}).

(8.60)

We remind the reader here that
(X_{t},r_{t})_{t=1}^{T_{1}}
is the data used for Ridge estimation of
\mathbf{w}_{0}
, and
(\widetilde{X}_{t},\tilde{r}_{t})_{t=1}^{T_{2}}
is the data used for SGD. With this notation,
\mathbf{w}_{0}=\mathbf{w}_{1}
is
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable. In general,
\mathbf{w}_{t}
and
\theta_{t}

\mathcal{F}_{t-1}

\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
and
\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}
are
\mathcal{F}_{t}

(8.61)

Let us assume that until step
t
, SGD both in
\mathbf{w}
and
\theta
was contained in the convex basin and that the projection was not involved. Mathematically speaking, we assume that

\displaystyle\forall k\leq t\ (\mathbf{w}_{k},\theta_{k})\in\mathcal{C}\quad\text{and}\quad\forall k<t\ (\mathbf{w}_{k}-\zeta\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k},\ \theta_{k}-\zeta\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{k})\in\mathcal{C}.

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}=\mathbf{w}_{t}-\zeta\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
and
\hat{\theta}_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\zeta\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}

\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{w}}}(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1})
and
\theta_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_{\theta}}(\hat{\theta}_{t+1})
. As we shall see, under certain conditions that hold with high probability,
(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1},\hat{\theta}_{t+1})\in\mathcal{C}

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}=\mathbf{w}_{t+1}
and
\hat{\theta}_{t+1}=\theta_{t+1}

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}

\mathcal{C}
requires bounding the following quantity:

\displaystyle\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}\leq\lVert\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}-\Sigma_{0}\rVert_{2}\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert^{2}+\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}^{2}.

(8.63)

We will now use Lemma
8.2
to bound
\lVert\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}-\Sigma_{0}\rVert_{2}

\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert^{2}\leq 2\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert^{2}+2\zeta^{2}\lVert\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\rVert^{2}\leq 8B_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}+2\frac{\zeta^{2}}{\lambda}D_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}.

(8.64)

Introducing

S_{\delta}:=\frac{8B_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}+2D_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}\zeta^{2}/\lambda}{T_{1}}\left(B_{\varphi}^{2}\log\frac{2d}{\delta}+\sqrt{B_{\varphi}^{4}\log^{2}\frac{2d}{\delta}+2B_{\varphi}^{4}T_{1}\log\frac{2d}{\delta}}\right),

(8.65)

we get, with
\mathbb{P}\geq 1-\delta

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}\\
&\quad<S_{\delta}+\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}^{2}\end{split}

(8.66)

\displaystyle\quad=S_{\delta}+\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}^{2}+\zeta^{2}\lVert\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\rVert^{2}_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}+2\zeta(\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{t})^{\intercal}\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}.

(8.67)

Using
\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}=I-\lambda\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}<S_{\delta}+\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}^{2}\\
&\qquad+\zeta^{2}\lVert\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\rVert^{2}_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}+2\zeta(\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{k})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}-2\lambda\zeta(\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{k})^{\intercal}\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}.\end{split}

(8.68)

By the assumption (
8.62
), projection was not involved until step
t

8.68

\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}<S_{\delta}+\lVert\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\zeta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\lVert\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\rVert^{2}_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}+2\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{k})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}-2\lambda\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{k})^{\intercal}\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}.

Using Lemma
8.2
again to switch from
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}^{2}
to
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}
, we get with
\mathbb{P}\geq 1-2\delta

\displaystyle\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}<2S_{\delta}+\lVert\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}

(8.70)

\displaystyle\quad+\zeta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\lVert\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\rVert^{2}_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}+2\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{k})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}-2\lambda\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{k})^{\intercal}\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}.

A similar recursive derivation for
\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{2}^{2}
gives us that

\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\zeta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\lVert\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\rVert_{2}^{2}+2\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{k})^{\intercal}\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}.

(8.71)

We can express the last sum from this equation and substitute it into (
8.70
):

\displaystyle\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}<2S_{\delta}+\lVert\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}+\lambda(\lVert\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{2}^{2}-\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\zeta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\lVert\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\rVert^{2}_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}+2\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{k})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}+\lambda\zeta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\lVert\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(8.72)

Now the first and the last sum can be combined using

\lVert\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\rVert^{2}_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{0}}+\lambda\lVert\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\lVert\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\rVert^{2}_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}=\lVert\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\rVert^{2}_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}.

(8.73)

Substituting this into (
8.70
),

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}<2S_{\delta}+\lVert\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}+4B_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}\lambda\\
&\quad\qquad+\zeta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\lVert\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\rVert^{2}_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}+2\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{k})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}.\end{split}

(8.74)

Terms in the first sum in (
8.74
) can be bounded as
\lVert\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}^{2}<D_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}/\lambda
. The last sum in this expression will be bounded using time-uniform Hoeffding-Azuma inequality. Let

\displaystyle a_{k}:=2\zeta(\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{k})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}-\mathbb{E}\left[2\zeta(\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{k})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right].

(8.75)

It is an
\mathcal{F}
-adapted martingale difference sequence, which can be bounded as

|a_{k}|\leq 8B_{\mathbf{w}}D_{\mathbf{w}}\zeta\quad\text{a.s.}

(8.76)

Let
E_{\mathbf{w}}
be an even defined as

E_{\mathbf{w}}:=\left\{\forall t\quad\sum_{k=1}^{t}a_{k}<20B_{\mathbf{w}}D_{\mathbf{w}}\zeta\sqrt{t\left((\log\log(64eB_{\mathbf{w}}D_{\mathbf{w}}t\zeta^{2}))_{+}+\log(2/\delta)\right)}\right\}.

(8.77)

By Lemma
8.4
,
\mathbb{P}[E_{\mathbf{w}}]\geq 1-\delta
. The bound in
E_{\mathbf{w}}
is itself upper-bounded by

P_{\delta}^{\mathbf{w}}:=20B_{\mathbf{w}}D_{\mathbf{w}}\zeta\sqrt{T_{2}\left((\log\log(64eB_{\mathbf{w}}D_{\mathbf{w}}T_{2}\zeta^{2}))_{+}+\log(2/\delta)\right)}.

(8.78)

With this notation, whenever
E_{\mathbf{w}}
holds, we have for all
t\leq T_{2}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}\leq\lVert\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}+4B_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}\lambda+2S_{\delta}+\zeta^{2}T_{2}\frac{D_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}}{\lambda}+P^{\mathbf{w}}_{\delta}\\
+2\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{k})^{\intercal}\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}_{k},\theta_{k}),\end{split}

(8.79)

where we used that, by definition of
\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}
, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]=\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}_{k},\theta_{k})
.

Now we will turn to
\lVert\hat{\theta}_{t+1}-\theta^{*}\rVert
. We would like to remind the reader that
\hat{\theta}_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\zeta\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}
, and that we are still operating under the assumption (
8.62
). Since no preconditioning is involved with respect to
\theta
, analyzing its trajectory is significantly easier. We have

\lVert\hat{\theta}_{t+1}-\theta^{*}\rVert^{2}=\lVert\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\rVert^{2}+\zeta^{2}\lVert\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}\rVert^{2}+2\zeta(\theta^{*}-\theta_{t})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}.

(8.80)

Applying this recursively to
\lVert\theta_{t}-\theta^{*}\rVert^{2}
,

\lVert\hat{\theta}_{t+1}-\theta^{*}\rVert^{2}=\lVert\theta_{1}-\theta^{*}\rVert^{2}+\zeta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\lVert\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}\rVert^{2}+2\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\theta^{*}-\theta_{k})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{k}.

(8.81)

The last sum enjoys a uniform high-probability bound analogous to (
8.77
). Let

P_{\delta}^{\theta}:=20B_{\theta}D_{\theta}\zeta\sqrt{T_{2}\left((\log\log(64eB_{\theta}D_{\theta}T_{2}\zeta^{2}))_{+}+\log(2/\delta)\right)}.

(8.82)

By Lemma
8.4
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\forall t\quad\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\theta^{*}-\theta_{k})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{k}<\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\theta^{*}-\theta_{k})^{\intercal}\nabla_{\theta}\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}_{k},\theta_{k})+P^{\theta}_{\delta}\right]\geq 1-\delta.

(8.83)

Substituting this into (
8.81
) and using
\lVert\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}\rVert\leq D_{\theta}
,

\lVert\hat{\theta}_{t+1}-\theta^{*}\rVert^{2}<\lVert\theta_{1}-\theta^{*}\rVert^{2}+\zeta^{2}T_{2}D_{\theta}+2\zeta\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\theta^{*}-\theta_{k})^{\intercal}\nabla_{\theta}\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}_{k},\theta_{k})+P^{\theta}_{\delta}.

(8.84)

Let, as elsewhere in the thesis,
\omega=(\mathbf{w},\theta)
be the combined vector of parameters of the network. We will also define the combined gradient estimator
\mathbf{v}_{t}=(\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t};\ \mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{d+d_{0}}
. With this notation,
\omega^{*}=(\mathbf{w}^{*},\theta^{*})
and
\omega_{k}=(\mathbf{w}_{k},\theta_{k})
. The two sums in (
8.79
) and (
8.84
) add to

\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\theta^{*}-\theta_{k})^{\intercal}\nabla_{\theta}\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}_{k},\theta_{k})+\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\mathbf{w}^{*}-\mathbf{w}_{k})^{\intercal}\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathfrak{R}(\mathbf{w}_{k},\theta_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\omega^{*}-\omega_{k})^{\intercal}\nabla\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{k}).

(8.85)

By assumption (
8.62
),
\omega_{k}\in\mathcal{C}
for
k\leq t
. Therefore,

(\omega^{*}-\omega_{k})^{\intercal}\nabla\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{k})\leq\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{k})\leq 0,

(8.86)

where the last inequality holds since
\omega^{*}\in\argmin_{\omega}\mathfrak{R}(\omega)
. Thereforee, when adding the inequalities (
8.79
) and (
8.84
), we get

\displaystyle\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}+\lVert\hat{\theta}_{t+1}-\theta^{*}\rVert^{2}\leq\lVert\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}+\lVert\theta_{1}-\theta^{*}\rVert^{2}

(8.87)

\displaystyle\quad+4B_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}\lambda+2S_{\delta}+\zeta^{2}T_{2}\left(\frac{D_{\mathbf{w}}}{\lambda}+D_{\theta}\right)+P^{\mathbf{w}}_{\delta}+P^{\theta}_{\delta}.

Whenever condition (
5.12
) holds, the terms on the second line of this expression are bounded by
\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}-\varepsilon_{\theta}^{2}
. At the same time,

\lVert\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}+\lVert\theta_{1}-\theta^{*}\rVert_{2}^{2}<\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}+\varepsilon_{\theta}^{2}.

(8.88)

Together, this gives

\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}+\lVert\hat{\theta}_{t+1}-\theta^{*}\rVert^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}+\varepsilon_{\theta}^{2}+\varepsilon_{c}^{2}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}-\varepsilon_{\theta}^{2}=\varepsilon_{c}^{2}.

(8.89)

We have, as promised, shown that
(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1},\hat{\theta}_{t+1})\in\mathcal{C}
. Since
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{w}}\times\mathcal{B}_{\theta}
, this means that projection will not be used, and that at step
t
SGD stays within the convex basin. Therefore, whenever the used high-probability bounds hold, the entire SGD trajectory will stay in
\mathcal{C}
.

Since the function is convex in the domain where SGD operates, we can use the technique from the proof of Theorem
4.1
to show a bound on the suboptimality gap.
Let
c_{t}
be defined as

c_{t}=\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{t})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})-(\omega_{t}-\omega^{*})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{t}.

(8.90)

When
\mathfrak{R}
is convex in the region containing
\omega^{*}
and all
\omega_{t}
, it satisfies

\mathbb{E}[c_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{t})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})-(\omega_{t}-\omega^{*})^{\intercal}\nabla\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{t})\leq 0

(8.91)

The martingale difference
\tilde{c}_{t}=c_{t}-\mathbb{E}[c_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
is almost surely bounded as

|\tilde{c}_{t}|<4B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}+2B_{\mathbf{w}}D_{\mathbf{w}}+2B_{\theta}D_{\theta}.

(8.92)

We can apply Lemma
8.3
to it and get, with
\mathbb{P}\geq 1-\delta

\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\left(\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{t})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})\right)<\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\left((\omega_{t}-\omega^{*})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{t}+\mathbb{E}[c_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\right)+Q_{\delta},

(8.93)

where

Q_{\delta}:=(4B_{\mathbf{w}}B_{\varphi}+2B_{\mathbf{w}}D_{\mathbf{w}}+2B_{\theta}D_{\theta})\sqrt{\frac{2}{T_{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}.

(8.94)

In total, we used
5
high-probability bounds in the proof and
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\Sigma_{0}}^{2}<\varepsilon_{\mathbf{w}}^{2}
holds with
\mathbb{P}\geq 1-4\delta
. Thus, with
\mathbb{P}\geq 1-9\delta
, we have
\mathbb{E}[c_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq 0
. Combining this with the above bound, we get

\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\left(\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{t})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})\right)<\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}(\omega_{t}-\omega^{*})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{t}+Q_{\delta}\right]\geq 1-10\delta.

(8.95)

Now we only need to decompose the sum on the right. First, since we now know that with high probability, projection was not involved in the algorithm, so

\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}^{2}-2\zeta(\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{*})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}+\zeta^{2}\lVert\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}^{2}.

(8.96)

Applying this recursively,

\lVert\mathbf{w}_{T_{2}+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}^{2}-2\zeta\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}(\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{*})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}+\zeta^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\lVert\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}^{2}.

(8.97)

We can express the first sum from this expression and substitute it into the second sum in (
8.95
). With
\mathbb{P}>1-10\delta
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(\overline{\omega})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}(\mathfrak{R}(\omega_{t})-\mathfrak{R}(\omega^{*}))

\displaystyle<\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}(\omega_{t}-\omega^{*})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}_{t}+Q_{\delta}

(8.98)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}(\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{*})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}+\frac{1}{T_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}(\theta_{t}-\theta^{*})^{\intercal}\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}+Q_{\delta}.

(8.99)

We express the first sum here from (
8.97
) and the sum — from (
8.81
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\frac{1}{2\zeta T_{2}}\left(\lVert\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}^{2}-\lVert\mathbf{w}_{T_{2}+1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}^{2}\right)+\frac{\zeta}{2T_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\lVert\mathbf{v}^{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}^{-1}}^{2}\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{2\zeta T_{2}}\left(\lVert\theta_{1}-\theta^{*}\rVert^{2}-\lVert\theta_{T_{2}+1}-\theta^{*}\rVert^{2}\right)+\frac{\zeta}{2T_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{2}}\lVert\mathbf{v}^{\theta}_{t}\rVert^{2}+Q_{\delta}\end{split}

(8.100)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\zeta T_{2}}\left(\lVert\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}^{2}+\varepsilon_{\theta}^{2}\right)+\frac{\zeta P^{\mathbf{v}}_{\delta}}{2T_{2}}+\frac{\zeta D_{\theta}^{2}}{2}+Q_{\delta}.

(8.101)

Finally, we substitute the bound (
3.28
) from Section
6
for
\lVert\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{*}\rVert_{\widehat{\Sigma}_{\lambda}}
(the reader is reminded here that we use notation
\mathbf{w}_{0}=\mathbf{w}_{1}
and
\theta_{0}=\theta_{1}
for convenience) and get the last statement of the theorem.

\square

Appendix 8.D
Hyperparameter choices in experiments

Network architecture

In both MNIST and wine quality experiments, the representation network
\varphi_{\theta}
is a fully-connected network with one hidden layer and GELU activations
[
15
]
after both the hidden and the output layer. Thus, the overall reward model
x\mapsto\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\varphi_{\theta}(x)
is a network with two hidden layers, whose dimensions we will denote as
[d_{h},d]
.

Initialization

Whenever the weights of a fully-connected layer had to be initalized, we used i.i.d. samples from
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{1}{d_{in}}\right)
for the entries of the kernel matrix, where
d_{in}
is the dimension of the input of this layer. This prevents the activations from exploding during the forward pass. If the layer has a bias (i.e. it is not the last layer), bias weights were zero-initialized.

MNIST experiment

Regularization factors in the loss (
6.2
) were selected as
c_{1}=10,\ c_{2}=1,c_{3}=0
. Such aggressive regularization was possible because it did not influence the accuracy of the network, which stayed at
99.2\%
correct classifications. The hidden dimensions of the network were
d_{h}=d=128
. For the bandit runs with or without the fine-tuned network, the data for digits 5-9 was divided into two equal parts. One was used for hyperparameter search, the other — to evaluate the bandit algorithm. Hyperparameters for all algorithms were searched on a grid
\zeta_{\mathbf{w}},\zeta_{\theta}\in\left\{10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2},10^{-1}\right\}
and
\lambda\in\left\{10^{-3},10^{-2},5*10^{-1},10^{-1}\right\}
. For each tuple
(\zeta_{\mathbf{w}},\zeta_{\theta},\lambda)
, the optimal
T_{2}
was selected with the procedure described in Section
13
. We used
T_{1}=128
.

Wine quality experiment

Regularization factors were
c_{1}=0.1,\ c_{2}=0.1,\ c_{3}=0.01
. For pre-training on white wine, we set
\zeta_{\mathbf{w}}=\zeta_{\theta}=:\zeta
and searched through tuples of
(d_{h},d,\zeta)
on a grid
d_{h},d\in\left\{2,5,10,20,50,100\right\},\ \zeta\in\left\{10^{-1},10^{-2},10^{-3},10^{-4}\right\}
. For the main experiment on the red wines, we set
\zeta_{\theta}=0.01
and searched through the grid
\zeta_{\mathbf{w}}\in\left\{10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2}\right\},\ \lambda\in\left\{10^{-1},10^{0},10^{1},10^{2}\right\}
.

External results

In this section we provide the external results that this thesis relies on. Most of the original notation from the sources was adapted to our needs.

Lemma 8.1
(Sphere covering number, Lemma 20.1 in
[
24
]
).

There exists a set
C_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|C_{\varepsilon}|<(3/\varepsilon)^{d}
such that for all
\mathbf{x}\in S^{d-1}
there exists a
\mathbf{y}\in C_{\varepsilon}
with
\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert\leq\varepsilon
.

Lemma 8.2
(Estimation of second momentum, adapted from
[
39
]
).

Let
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
be i.i.d. random vectors such that
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\intercal}\right]=\Sigma
and
\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}\rVert<B
a.s. Let the empirical estimator of
\Sigma
be

\widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\intercal}.

(8.102)

Then, for all
\delta>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{2}<\frac{1}{n}\left(B^{2}\log\frac{2d}{\delta}+\sqrt{B^{4}\log^{2}\frac{2d}{\delta}+2B^{2}\lVert\Sigma\rVert_{2}n\log\frac{2d}{\delta}}\right)\right]\geq 1-\delta.

(8.103)

Proof
.

This lemma follows from
[
39
, Corollary 6.20]
. This corollary states that

\mathbb{P}\left[\|\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon\right]\leq 2d\exp\left[-\frac{n\varepsilon^{2}}{2B^{2}(\lVert\Sigma\rVert_{2}+\varepsilon)}\right].

(8.104)

In the book, it is formulated for zero-mean vectors
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, but this is never used in the proof, and only allows us to state that
\widehat{\Sigma}
is a
covariance
estimator of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. We don’t need that, and instead work with the second momentum of the noncentered
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. To get the bound in terms of
\delta
, we simply solve the quadratic equation w.r.t.
\varepsilon
.

\square

Let

|X_{k}|\leq B
a.s. for all
t

\delta>0

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}<B\sqrt{2T\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right]\geq 1-\delta.

(8.105)

where
