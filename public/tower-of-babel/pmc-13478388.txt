Lightweight hybrid foreign object detection framework with scene aware and uncertainty fusion mechanism
Authors: M. Manicka Prabha, K. Suganthi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Foreign object detection (FOD) in autonomous driving environments poses a significant challenge due to the dual nature of anomalous objects: known objects appearing in inappropriate contexts or truly novel objects unseen during training. Existing methods typically address either closed-set semantic reasoning or open-set uncertainty estimation, limiting their ability to handle both types of anomalies effectively. This paper introduces HSAOSFOD (Hybrid Scene-Aware Open-set Foreign Object Detection), a unified lightweight framework that integrates closed-set and open-set paradigms through explicit modeling of a scene-object compatibility matrix and multi-signal open-set detection modules. The key contributions include: (1) a scene-object compatibility module that leverages domain priors to detect contextual mismatches of known objects, (2) a multi-signal fusion module that combines prototype-based novelty detection and uncertainty estimation, and (3) an efficient architectural design utilizing separable self-attention and depth wise convolutions, achieving a model size of only 4.33 million parameters. Extensive experiments on the Cityscapes and RailSem19 datasets for training, and Road Anomaly and Lost and Found datasets for evaluation, demonstrate that HSAOSFOD attains competitive performance with AUROC scores of 0.9506 on Road Anomaly and 0.6158 on Lost and Found, while preserving computational efficiency. Ablation studies confirm that the compatibility module (closed-set) contributes approximately 1.1% and the novelty detection head (open-set) contributes approximately 0.4% to average AUROC, together describing for a combined hybrid contribution of 1.5% over the base decoder alone. HSAOSFOD illustrates the potential of combining explicit domain knowledge with data-driven learning to produce efficient and interpretable hybrid models, delivering particularly strong results on context-sensitive anomalies.

Introduction

Autonomous driving (AD) systems are based on well-developed perception units that are capable of interpreting intricate surroundings, safe navigation, and critical decision-making process1. Foreign object detection (FOD) is one of the key sub-tasks of AD perception that seeks to detect abnormal objects on the road that endanger safety, such as debris or misplaced objects2. Traditional features of object detection (OD) are good for use in controlled environments, but when the unusual objects are open-set anomalous objects, which are unseen during the training process or occur in the wrong context3 they encounter difficulties. This challenge is further compounded in AD scenarios by a variety of factors such as varying weather, lighting, and scene complexity as a result of which performance is degraded4.

Recently, the focus on deep learning has moved toward hybrid methods, which involve semantic reasoning in closed sets and estimation of uncertainty in open sets5. For example, networks that use global scene information, like CAD-Net, use the scene information to improve scene detection in remote sensing images, but their application for ground-level AD is still limited6. Likewise, models with low energy consumption like DynaMixFormer are designed to be deployed in AD with lightweight and reduced parameters without loss of mAP, however, they commonly do not use multi-signal fusion to manage anomalies7. Transformers in AD Surveys indicate that they may be useful in modeling long-range dependencies, but require efficient architectures that take into account limited resources of vehicles8.

AD is safety-critical, and requires strong anomaly management that goes beyond standard object detection paradigms. Ethically responsible decision-making models of complex driving scenarios stress that anomaly detection should consider the contextual appropriateness. The level of threat posed by an object is not just based on its presence but also based on the situation on the scene. As an example, the level of threat differs according to proximity to the road sides (pedestrian) vs. proximity to the road center (pedestrian)9. Anomaly diagnosis surveys on autonomous vehicles with a connection suggest that current approaches mostly deal with faults in sensors and car malfunctions, but little with the contextual foreignness at the scene level where common objects are presented in dangerous positions10. Surveys of transformer architecture in autonomous driving show that transformers are good at capturing long-range correlations to understand the whole scene holistically, but require computational scales typically >50M parameters, <30 FPS makes them impractical on automotive-grade embedded systems11. Surveys of AI-based object detection and traffic prediction demonstrate that the current approaches adopt closed-set recognition on benchmark datasets like KITTI, Cityscapes but do not include the mechanisms to reason about scene-object compatibility to deploy such systems into the open-world where contextual anomalies (known objects misplaced) and genuinely novel objects (unseen categories) are co-occurring12. Overall, these surveys point to a gap in research that needs to be filled. This ensures the requirement of cohesive models that combine contextual reasoning and open-set detection with computational efficiency.

The identified gap is reflected in the complementary weaknesses of the existing methods in dealing with the duality of anomalies in autonomous driving. Closed-set detectors that are trained on curated datasets such as KITTI, Cityscapes that provide predefined object taxonomies do not respond to contextual anomalies that are known objects in the wrong scene like a pedestrian on the highway or construction equipment on a high-speed lane. Such systems do not have a way of reasoning about scene appropriateness other than object recognition. Current schemes such as Rep-YoloV8 have been used to enhance the representation of features in the detection of AD objects with improved mAP on the KITTI scheme, but fails to do open-set generalization13. In contrast, open-set approaches to novelty detection by estimating uncertainty or scoring by energy give false positives in the presence of known objects in novel spatial situations, misclassifying familiar classes because of distributional shift14. LF-Yolo is focused on small-scale FOD with lightweight design but without contextual reasoning hybrid paradigms15. CODNet also looks at multimodal context in object detection to recognize activities and provides information about fusion strategies16. This dual failure mode inability to model scene-object compatibility have closed-set limitation and the inability to differentiate familiar-but-misplaced objects and actually novel objects that has open-set limitation drive unified models that combine explicit compatibility reasoning with prototype-based novelty detection. HSAOSFOD solves this by hybrid architecture with a scene-object compatibility module that includes domain priors manually initialized, and further learned and multi-signal fusion includes prototype novelty and uncertainty estimation in a 4.33M parameter lightweight design.

This paper introduces HSAOSFOD, a lightweight hybrid model that achieves competitive AUROC (0.7832 average) on Road Anomaly and Lost and Found datasets with only 4.33M parameters. Our contributions include: (1) a scene-object compatibility module to study contextual anomalies; (2) multi-signal fusion that integrates prototypes and uncertainty; (3) an efficient architecture that uses separable attention and depth wise convolutions; and (4) ablation studies that validate a combined hybrid contribution of 1.5% AUROC from the compatibility and novelty components together. The rest of this manuscript is organized in the following section: Sect. 2 reviews the related work, Sect. 3 presents the methodology, Sect. 4 shows the experimental evaluation and discussion, and Sect. 5 concludes the paper with the future research direction.

Related works

Object detection (OD) in autonomous driving (AD) has evolved from traditional methods to deep learning paradigms, with recent emphasis on open-set and lightweight designs17. Early surveys on OD highlight datasets like KITTI18 and metrics like mAP, but noted that limitations in handling anomalies19. Context-aware approaches, such as represented in6, use attention for remote sensing OD, achieving improved mIoU via global-local fusion, yet struggle with AV-specific noise1.

Hybrid closed/open-set methods address anomaly duality. CODNet integrates context for multimodal OD in captioning, fusing vision-language cues for better localization16. Similarly, UASeg uses uncertainty-aware WSSS for AD segmentation, reporting the state-of-the-art mIoU on Cityscapes20. Few-shot learning extends to novel OD in AVs, with methods like3 achieving high mAP on augmented KITTI for rare objects.

Lightweight models prioritize efficiency. LSINet for rail defects (FOD-like) uses VMamba and Markov fields for lightweight anomaly detection, with 27.6M params and 95% accuracy4. Energy-efficient perception in7 adapts YOLOv8 for AD, reducing energy by 70% with minor mAP loss. Rep-YOLOv8 enhances deep features for AV OD, improving mAP by 3.6% on BDD100K13. LF-YOLO targets small FOD with params reduction, outperforming SOTA on custom datasets15. RGE-YOLO for road debris achieves 92.2% mAP with 250 FPS21. Transformer-based surveys8 discuss architectures for AD perception, noting long-range modeling benefits but efficiency gaps. Context surveys22 review contextual cues in OD, emphasizing multi-scale fusion for open-set tasks.

Object detection has also been popular with context-aware object detection to enhance the reliability of detection. Mishra et al. suggest that learning conditional compatibility to detect anomalies should be made depending on the contextual appropriateness, which is in line with the HSAOSFOD scene-object compatibility concept23. Jamali et al. offer systematic literature review of context in object detection, classifying spatial, semantic and scale based contextual cues24. Alamri and Pugeault show that scene contextual constraints enhance object detection using contextual rescoring and relabeling and get gains relative to out-of-context detection25. These existing approaches confirm that explicit context modeling improves detection over appearance-based features.

Unknown object discovery is tackled by open-world object detection. Li et al. survey open-world methods, pointing out the difficulties in separating known-class instances and unknown objects26. Awan et al. survey deep-learning approaches to autonomous driving scene recognition, where there are gaps in unified frameworks of closed-set recognition and open-set detection27. HSAOSFOD fills these paradigm gaps by combining scene-object compatibility contains context-aware closed-set and prototype-based novelty detection concentrates open-set, to fill the research gap that has been identified in these surveys where existing methods consider one of context or novelty but not both.

The models of unified perception cover different types of objects. Uni-EPM28 used transfers learning of partial supervision to generalizing to novel categories without exhaustive labeling. Although Uni-EPM does supervised transfer of partial labels to full categories, HSAOSFOD considers unsupervised open-set cases that is zero labels of anomaly through prototype-based novelty detection. Visual Bridge is an example of universal representation learning with foundation model pre-training, and with good zero-shot transfer29. Nonetheless, its heavyweight transformer design estimated>100M parameters is aimed at deployment on clouds, which is compared to lightweight design of HSAOSFOD 4.33M parameters.

Gao et al. proposed the framework of Learning from Interactions (LFI), which is inspired by cognition and transfers knowledge from Vision Language Models (VLMs) to Visual Foundation Models (VFMs) by explicitly modeling the underlying interactions process rather than imitating the output representations30. In contrast to traditional result-based distillation approaches, LFI proposed Interaction Queries and interaction-based supervision from cross-modal attention mechanisms, leading to absolute gains with only 7× higher number of parameters and 7× higher convergence speed. This process-based knowledge transfer is consistent with HSAOSFOD’s philosophy in that auxiliary signals like segmentation, scene-object compatibility and classification all complement the primary objective of anomaly detection and are multi-output supervision.

Stereo 3D anomaly detection31 uses depth data to enhance the small-object localization, which is directly applicable to the HSAOSFOD performance gap of 61.6% AUROC, which is constrained by objects smaller than 2% image area. Although HSAOSFOD is monocular with RGB, incorporating stereo depth may resolve small-object difficulties with preserving scene-aware compatibility reasoning is an interesting multi-modal extension to future applications.

The latest developments in visual grounding show the validity of multi-task optimization techniques. Mono3DVG solves the problem of 3D visual grounding on monocular images in KITTI dataset by jointly optimizing 3D localization and language grounding objectives by using multi-task loss functions. This article demonstrating that auxiliary grounding tasks enhance understanding of space32. On the same note, SnAG (Scalable and Accurate Video Grounding) utilises multi-task learning to conduct temporal video grounding relying on complementary supervisory signals in space and time33. To complement these efforts, SAVG (Spatial Aerial Video Grounding) takes multi-task optimization and extends it to the UAV domain34. Here the language modulation module and a multi-level progressive spatial decoder are jointly optimized to tackle small object grounding and multi-scale feature fusion simultaneously under complex aerial environments. In this way, the model helps to show that decomposed multi-task architectural design is important even if it is the visual domain that changes, from ground level to an aerial view. These strategies are similar to HSAOSFOD philosophy in the sense that the auxiliary tasks are done to improve the performance of the primary tasks. Whereas Mono3DVG, SnAG and SAVG are focused on language-directed spatial/temporal grounding, HSAOSFOD uses multi-signal fusion such as segmentation, scene/object classification, and compatibility to detect an anomaly. Both paradigms confirm that joint optimization of related tasks in which language grounding in former case, compatibility reasoning in later that enhances core performance. The ablation experiments indicate that compatibility learning improves the 1.1% of the AUROC and novelty detection head improves 0.4% together enhances 1.5% AUROC, which is equivalent to their multi-task improvement. The key difference in both the article is domain. Methods

Problem formulation

Foreign object detection (FOD) in the context of autonomous driving is a unique challenge, since existing approaches are inadequate for the overall problem resolution. The main source of complexity is the fact that anomalies can be either: (1) objects known to the system but seen in inappropriate scenarios (a vehicle crossing railway tracks), requiring semantic scene understanding; and (2) inherently novel entities that were not present in the training phase (debris, or fallen cargo), requiring powerful open set detection capacities. Conventional strategies will normally address only one of these facets, either using closed set classification schemes that are unable to deal catastrophically with unknown objects, or uncertainty-based approaches that lack the semantic inferential ability required to identify violations of context.

Network architecture

The FOD model illustrated in Fig. 1 uses an architecture that gives an encoder-decoder based on EfficientNet-B0. This block consists of three-level separable self-attention that extracts contextual features in 512 × 512 input images. The extracted features, then fed to a set of five parallel detection heads simultaneously, with a progressive decoder producing base anomaly segmentation, scene classifier predicting environmental context (urban/highway/rural), object segmentation identifying 15 known categories and compatibility module performing cross-validation of scene-object compatibility to identify contextual anomalies. At the same time, same features have been used by two open-set detectors in which uncertainty head estimates prediction confidence and prototype-based novelty detector measures feature-space distances between learned patterns. These five components are later fused together using weights to produce the final pixel-wise anomaly score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S \in \mathbb [{0,1}]^{H\times W}$$\end{document} for detecting both contextual anomalies and entirely novel objects.Fig. 1Overall architecture of proposed HSAOSFOD.

Scene-aware semantic reasoning

Through the explicit modeling of scene-object relationships the scene-aware semantic reasoning has been used to enable the detection of known objects in inappropriate contexts.

The scene classification head learns the environmental context to make decisions about detection of anomalies. Object segmentation head

To identify known objects, the object segmentation head uses closed-set semantic segmentation which helps to filter contextual anomalies. To do this, it processes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_3$$\end{document} (lowest resolution) feature on the backbone with bilinear interpolation, followed by 1 × 1 convolutions and generates per-pixel logits \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O \in R^{H \times W \times 15 }$$\end{document} for 15 categories that are person, car, vegetation, unknown etc., The Depth-wise convolutions used in this component ensures the efficiency.

The cross-entropy loss on the object maps that are based on the dataset labels is supervised with pixel wise loss. The compatibility matrix C between segmented objects and the type of scene is used during the open set detection to flag excluded regions as the system for example a bus on a railway will be flagged with a high score on the anomaly objective in a weighted probability approach (0.9 conf penalty) to scores below 0.7 threshold. This is a hybrid form of reasoning that closes the gap between closed set semantics and the uncertainty of open set reasoning.

Scene-object compatibility analysis

To increase the contextual awareness and decrease anomalies during anomaly detection, HSAOSFOD uses a lightweight analysis of scene-object compatibility that utilizes knowledge of the domain in a prior compatibility matrix. The Fig. 2 depicts the structure of Scene-Object Compatibility module.

In this case the most common types of scenes that applied to road scenes were characterized as urban (0), highway (1), railway (2), rural (3), parking (4), and other (5). The head of the scene classification predicts these scene types. At the same time, the head of object segmentation generates the per-pixel estimates of 15 common object categories that are person, rider, car, truck, bus, train, motorcycle, bicycle, traffic light, traffic sign, vegetation, terrain, sky, pole, unknown.Fig. 2Scene-object compatibility module architecture.

In order to use prior knowledge, a scene-object compatibility matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C \in R^{6 \times 15}$$\end{document} has been designed, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{ij}=1$$\end{document} when an object category j could be in a scene i, i.e.,the value is set to 1 if the trains are present in the railways scene and set to 0 if it is present in the other scenes such as urban roads. The result of this domain knowledge, applied in the inference, is a matrix that is used to penalize incompatible detections. The head is trained using cross-entropy loss39 which is augmented with estimation of scene complexity whose scalar value ∈ [0,1] which is regressed using MSE loss, which captures factors such as object density.

Although the compatibility matrix C offers domain priors that encode traffic regulations, direct binary values (0 or 1) are not flexible when considering more subtle relationships between scenes and objects, which can change depending on the context. An example is that near parking entrances would not be the same as that of vehicles that are far. As such, HSAOSFOD uses a lightweight compatibility module that has 4.8K parameters that learns continuous compatibility scores by training scene embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(E_s \in \mathbb {R}^{6\times 32})$$\end{document} and object embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(E_o \in \mathbb {R}^{15 \times 32})$$\end{document}, with initialization using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of matrix C40. This initialization guarantees that the learned embeddings maintain the domain prior structure but can be refined with data during training.

In forward pass, given a pixel with a predicted scene type s and object category o, the module retrieves corresponding embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_s = E_s[s]$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_o = E_o[o]$$\end{document}, adds them together into a 64-dimensional vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[e_s; e_o]$$\end{document}, and runs through a two-layer multi-layer perceptron (MLP) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{compat}$$\end{document} shown in Eq. 5,5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{{compat}} ([e_{s} ;e_{o} ]) = \sigma (W_{2} \cdot {\mathbf{ReLU}}(W_{1} \cdot [e_{s} ;e_{o} ]))$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_1 \in \mathbb {R}^{64 \times 64}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_2 \in \mathbb {R}^{64 \times 1}$$\end{document}, and σ sigmoid activation. This yields pixel-wise compatibility scores [0,1], with high compatibility scores of near 1 indicating the presence of compatibility i.e. scene-object pairing and low compatibility scores of near 0 indicating a contextual anomaly.

This hybrid architecture accomplishes three objectives: (1) Binary domain priors (C matrix) that offer strong inductive bias to safety-critical tasks for example train on highway = 0 with limited training data, (2) Learned embeddings can give finer compatibility than binary assignments, including subtle contextual differences (e.g., vehicles near vs. far parking zones), and (3) Lightweight parameterization with 4.8K parameters. The compatibility scores are flipped (1 - compatibility) and combined with other signals like novelty and uncertainty through weighted fusion to generate the final scores of anomalies.

Open-set detection modules

The hybrid open-set detection in HSAOSFOD combines a range of complementary signals that are the uncertainty estimation and prototype-based novelty detection scoring, for wisely detecting a novel anomaly. The modules operate on the most attended features of the backbone i.e., the 192 channels and are jointly added with closed-set outputs in the final OOD score computation. Based on these, the model retains the lightweight computation, but also covers a variety of characteristics of novelty such as model ignorance (uncertainty) and geometric deviation of new prototypes (novelty). These cues are trained with the help of auxiliary losses such as MSE on novelty and Gaussian NLL on uncertainty. Uncertainty head

The uncertainty head provides an estimation of both aleatoric (data-inherent) and epistemic (model) uncertainty that creates a measure of reliability for predictions of anomalies. Implemented as a lightweight module, which processes through a shared sequence of attended features i.e. adaptive average pooling to 4 × 4, 1 × 1 convolution to 32 channels, and the ReLU activation. Two parallel 1 × 1 convolutions then output mean logits \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu \in \mathbb {R}^{H \times W \times 2}$$\end{document} and log variance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log (\sigma ^2) \in \mathbb {R}^{H \times W \times 2}$$\end{document} maps, which are up sampled using bilinear interpolation to the input resolution. For the stability purpose the Outputs were clamped to [-10, 10].

Aleatoric uncertainty was derived as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log (\sigma ^2) = \exp {(\log (\sigma ^2))}$$\end{document}, inherent ambiguities resulting from sensor for e.g.noise, lighting variations. The epistemic uncertainty is approximated with the variance and thus highlights areas of model uncertainty (e.g. unseen anomalies). During the training stage, by using the anomaly detection algorithm, it is regularized with a Gaussian negative log-likelihood loss with the weight of 0.2 in the total loss in Eq. 7 defined as7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{\text {unc}} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{(y - \mu )^2}{\sigma ^2 + \epsilon } + \log (\sigma ^2 + \epsilon ) \right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where y is the anomaly target. This promotes greater uncertainty in areas covering outliers which fosters open-set robustness.

Novelty head

The novelty head has a prototype-based approach that detects geometric deviations from known in-distribution patterns. It keeps 8 learnable prototypes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_k \in \mathbb {R}^{192}$$\end{document} which is initialized with Xavier uniform, where gain=0.1 denoting compact clusters of normal features. A learnable temperature τ is initialized to 1.0 on the scaling of distances.

The L2 normalization has been applied for both of the features and prototypes which was fed by the input features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f \in \mathbb {R}^{B \times 192 \times H \times W}$$\end{document}. Novelty is the distance to prototypes that is the minimum Euclidean distance, which is divided by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|\tau | + \epsilon \; (\epsilon = 10^{-6}), \quad n(x) = \frac{\min _k \Vert f_x - p_k\Vert _2}{|\tau | + \epsilon }$$\end{document}, and bounded between [0, 10]. During training, the prototypes are implicitly updated using the backpropagation, with an MSE loss with weight 0.05 which pushing high novelty on the synthetic outliers. This is multi modal normality for e.g. different textures of roads between scenes, flagging patterns that are not seen as anomalies.

Hybrid anomaly score fusion

The HSAOSFOD pixel-wise score of anomalies is acquired by hybrid fusion of complementary signals of both closed set and open set branches. This multi-signal framework combines semantic context, geometric novelty, prediction confidence, as well as the data ambiguity to detect the various anomalies, such as contextual damages and genuinely novel foreign entities. Base anomaly score in the decoder is assigned the highest weight, incompatibility and low-confidence penalty follow with smaller contributions made by prototype novelty, and aleatoric uncertainty. Each signal is normalized individually and then a summary of the weighted sum is taken through a sigmoid to give a well calibrated probability of an anomaly in [0,1].Fig. 3Multi signal fusion mechanism of HSAOSFOD.

Multi signal fusion mechanism of HSAOSFOD.

As shown in the above Fig. 3, the final pixel-wise anomaly score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S(i,j) \in [0,1]^{H \times W}$$\end{document} is computed by taking a weighted sum of six complementary signals (base anomaly logit, inverted compatibility, inverted object confidence, novelty score and uncertainty score), followed by sigmoid activation. The fusion balances closed-set contextual reasoning and open-set novelty detection for robust foreign object detection.

This combination of approaches clearly combines closed set semantic reasoning through compatibility of scene objects and object confidence with open set uncertainty indicators such as novelty and uncertainty estimation to allow the model to be successful with both known objects in incorrect scenes and truly novel anomalies. The complementary nature of such cues, evidenced by ablation studies, indicates that the average decrease in AUROC induced by the removal of any of the major components is between 3–8% on Road Anomaly and Lost and Found datasets.

Experimental evaluation

Dataset description

This study adopts a dual phase training and evaluation protocol by using four publicly available datasets. Training is performed on Cityscapes43 and RailSem1944 benchmarks which provide dense semantic annotations which can learn about scene-object relationships. The Cityscapes dataset consists of 2975 images for training and 500 validation images from 50 European cities annotated with 19 semantic classes that correspond to 15 relevant object categories e.g. vehicles, pedestrians, roads, sidewalks, buildings, vegetation, traffic signs or lights, and infrastructure. The RailSem19 collection is a collection of 8,500 training images captured from railway environment in eight countries representing very different railway-specific situations (e.g. trains on tracks, platforms, signals and barriers).

To facilitate the learning of scene-object compatibility, each training image is categorized according to its semantic class distribution and heuristic rules deduced from the original annotations into one of six scene types, i.e., urban, highway, railway, rural, parking, and the others. This procedure results in a learnable compatibility matrix of dimensions 6 * 15. To simulate open-set scenarios, outlier exposure is used by synthesizing inlier scenes with outlier instances such as solid color primitives, random textures, checkerboard patterns and noise patches was inserted into the bottom half of the image with a probability of 30%. This approach produces well-balanced normal and anomalous samples that aid on the joint training of closed and open set pathways.

Evaluation is done on the Road Anomaly dataset45, which consists of 60 images with anomaly objects on highways and the Lost and Found dataset46 that consists of 1,203 images with small anomaly objects. Both benchmarks provide pixel-level binary abnormal annotations without semantic label and therefore provide a rigorous test for open-set generalization from normal scenes with semantic labels to abnormal scenes without semantic labels.

Preprocessing pipeline and data augmentation

The HSAOSFOD framework was implemented using PyTorch 2.5.1 with CUDA 12.4 and Optimized for GPU acceleration on an NVIDIA RTX 4500 Ada generation GPU. Training images are resized to 512×512 pixels via bilinear interpolation and normalized using ImageNet statistics (mean= [0.485, 0.456, 0.406], std= [0.229, 0.224, 0.225]) to leverage EfficientNet-B0’s pretrained features. Augmentation includes horizontal flipping (50% probability), random affine transformations (shift ±10%, scale ±15%, rotation ±15°, p=0.5), and photometric adjustments (brightness/contrast ±20% or gamma 0.8–1.2, p=0.5 each) to simulate viewpoint and lighting variations. The OutlierExposureDataset wrapper ensures balanced mini-batches of normal and synthetic anomalous samples. For evaluation, test images undergo only resizing and normalization, without augmentation, for unbiased assessment. Scene and object inputs are generated externally using a rule-based heuristic scene detector for sky, road, vegetation, edge analysis to assign scene labels and a pretrained DeepLabV3-ResNet50 model to produce 15-category object segmentation maps, enabling compatibility and confidence computation while allowing the core model to focus on multi-signal fusion and anomaly reasoning.

Training configuration

The proposed HSAOSFOD model was trained for 100 epochs with Cosine Annealing learning rate and AdamW optimizer with learning rate and weight decay of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5 \times 10^{-6}$$\end{document} each. Gradient accumulation of 4 steps was applied, resulting in the batch size of 32, and gradient clipping with a norm of 0.1 to obtain stable optimization. The multi-task loss combines the weighted sum of five components such as base segmentation (λ = 0.30), scene-object compatibility (λ = 0.20), object confidence (λ = 0.20), novelty (λ = 0.20) and uncertainty (λ = 0.10), which are optimized together during a single forward pass. To avoid over fitting, an early stopping criterion were configured with a patience of 20 epochs and a minimum of improvement threshold of 0.001.

Discussion

The proposed HSAOSFOD framework is a comprehensive capable of integrating closed-set contextual reasoning and open-set novelty detection translation to deal with the dual nature of anomalous objects in the framework of autonomous driving environments. The scene-object compatibility module is a hybrid one. Here the initial 6 × 15 binary matrix is specified manually with reference to the traffic regulation. As an example, train on highway = incompatible, which offers excellent inductive bias to safety-critical situations, but scene/object embeddings and the compatibility network with 4.8K parameters can be trained entirely, making it possible to refine it with data. This design is more interpretable and sample efficient than scalable. Despite of that three limitations are identified as the 6-scene type is based on Western driving conditions with a lack of geographic diversity, also it is necessary to define scene types manually to extend to non-road conditions, and the 15-category vocabulary is used, which has a variety of anomalies that are combined in the unknown category. In out-of-distribution scenes, HSAOSFOD will use its open-set pathway. Here the new scenes are projected to closest known types and new objects invoke high uncertainty scores to allow the model to continue detecting high uncertainty scores, despite deteriorating compatibility signals. The scene-object compatibility module that gives good results on the Road Anomaly data with an AUROC of 0.9506 with anomalies primarily being known objects out of context. Further research in the area should consider meta-learning to generalize compatibility, and few-shot learning to extend to new domains.

Furthermore, the multi-signal fusion module allows to increase the robustness combining prototype-based novelty scores with uncertainty estimation, leading to reasonable detection of really unseen hazards on the Lost and Found dataset with AUROC 0.6158.

Ablation studies verify that both closed set and open set contribution is significant for overall performance, removal of compatibility module leads to a decrease of 1.1% in average AUROC, while removal of novelty detection head contributes an additional 0.4%, together confirming the complementary role of closed-set and open-set signals. These results highlight the excellence of the hybrid design, and especially the competition of their detection results in context sensitive anomalies for a very lightweight architecture containing only 4.33 million parameters.

In the performance gap analysis where HSAOSFOD have 33.5 AUROC point gap in 95.06% Road Anomaly vs. 61.58% Lost & Found is due to dataset properties rather than models relying too much on contextual information. This gap is universal gap across existing methods also. Existing methods have comparable gaps that are shown in the Table 6. RWPM have 33.5 points, RbA with 33.4 points, PEBAL having 35.2 points, proving Lost & Found’s dataset challenge. HSAOSFOD’s gap with 33.5 points is same as SOTA, implying dataset limit, not architecture limit. Another key finding is the dataset characteristic differences. Road Anomaly has large obstacles having 8.2% image area on average on highways with little clutter. In Lost & Found 67% objects are<2% image area i.e. 42%<1% with urban clutter. Despite these strengths, HSAOSFOD has limitations. The 33.5-point gap between Road Anomaly (95.06%) and Lost & Found (61.58%) is due to dataset characteristics that have small objects (<2% image area) in urban settings, rather than architectural over-reliance on context, indicated by universal gap across all methods (RWPM: 33.5%, RbA: 33.4%). The proposed per-pixel architecture has difficulty in the small-scale region<1% image area with poor spatial resolution. Also, training data includes synthetic outliers such as solid boxes, circles, checkerboard, noise that while being effective does not completely simulate real world debris that have complex shapes, materials, occlusion. This synthetic-to-real discrepancy might explain the Lost & Found performance, especially for small visually complex debris. The 6×15 compatibility matrix was manually initialized, and additional learned embeddings hinders generalization to other domains without retraining.

Conclusion

This study has proposed HSAOSFOD, was a lightweight hybrid framework for foreign object detection in autonomous vehicles by integrating closed-set contextual reasoning and open-set novelty detection. The framework incorporates the explicit domain knowledge in the data learning architectures and achieved competitive performance of 0.9506 and 0.6158 AUROC on the Road Anomaly and Lost and Found datasets respectively, while maintaining minimal number of parameters count with 4.33 million. Our ablation studies support the use of the proposed components, with the scene-object compatibility module contributing the most at 1.1% AUROC and the novelty head an additional 0.4%, altogether showing the effectiveness of the hybrid approach.

Future research can also concentrate on improving the fusion process by using adaptive weighting of the fusion process, contrastive learning of the prototype representations and temporal information of consecutive frames to better control the outliers than current methods. New outlier synthesis can explore GAN-based or diffusion-based anomaly generation to better simulate real-world appearances, progressive learning from simple to complex synthetic outliers. Scene taxonomy can be extended from 6 coarse to 12−15 fine types for more comprehensive geographical representation. Meta-learning techniques can be used for compatibility to learn new scene-object combinations without retraining. This can allow further improvement to the model performance while maintaining the model efficiency and help the development of robust and explainable autonomous driving perception systems.
