Digital Monitoring of Adverse Reactions During Paediatric Food Oral Immunotherapy in a Real‐World Cohort
Authors: Tiina Kauppila, Heidi Sandström, Anna Kaarina Kukkonen, Kati Palosuo, Mika J Mäkelä
Published: 2026

In the last 20 years, oral immunotherapy (OIT) has substantially changed the management of food allergies (FA) [1]. OIT increases the threshold for previously allergenic food proteins in IgE‐mediated FA, although severe allergic reactions, while rare, remain possible [1]. In Finland, the first milk OIT study began in 2005, followed by peanut and egg OIT studies [2]. We aimed to assess the use of digital monitoring during oral immunotherapy, including adverse events, and their association with treatment outcomes. All patients starting OIT after August 2019 were recruited to the Safety of OIT for Food Allergy study with digital follow‐up. Digital follow‐up was implemented through the Health Village portal, where a digital care pathway specifically for OIT was developed to support patients and their families throughout the treatment process [3].

Patients or parents were asked to sign in to the digital care pathway and report side effects or accidental exposures to the food for which OIT was planned. The pathway provided guidance based on side‐effect severity using a traffic light model: green for mild symptoms, orange for caution and dose reduction, and red for severe reactions requiring adrenaline. Use of the pathway was voluntary; all families were informed about the system at treatment initiation and encouraged to use it. Initial access required parental secure digital identification, after which a mobile application could be used. Standard care at the clinic continued, and patients were reminded to use the pathway during visits, and data were collected from both the pathway and patient records. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Helsinki University Hospital.

A total of 80 patients started OIT between August 2019 and November 2024. All patients had IgE‐mediated peanut, egg, or milk allergy, and their allergy was confirmed with an open food challenge (OFC). Most patients participated in egg OIT (n = 35), and fewer in peanut (n = 23) or milk (n = 22) OIT. The median age at initiation of OIT was 10 years (interquartile range [IQR] 7.3–13.0); 60% of participants were male, and 55% had asthma. Patients had a six‐month follow‐up on an avoidance diet, using a digital diary to record accidental reactions to the foods they were about to start in OIT. A total of 18 patients reported reactions during this period, five of whom had more than one reaction. Egg caused reactions in 10 patients, including one with three different mild reactions. Milk‐induced reactions occurred in eight patients, four of whom experienced up to three recurrent episodes. Most reactions were mild; three patients visited the emergency room during the six‐month period (one egg‐related and two milk‐related reactions). No accidental reactions were reported among peanut OIT patients during follow‐up.

Desensitisation outcomes were good among egg OIT participants, with 80% of patients achieving the full maintenance dose at the one‐year follow‐up. In peanut and milk OIT, the percentage of patients achieving full maintenance dose was 43% and 45%. During the first year of OIT, four patients did not sign in at all to the digital pathway, nine patients signed in but made no notes, and thirteen patients made notes without using the traffic lights. User experiences were not systematically collected; however, families occasionally reported barriers to use, mainly related to login difficulties. Few patients were highly committed to the digital pathway, as three patients reported almost daily whether they had symptoms or not. Among all patients, the median number of notes in the digital pathway was 17 (mean 57 notes), demonstrating that some patients and their families were committed to using it while several families signed in only a few times.

Due to small sample size, we combined the results from all participants to evaluate the effects of utilizing the digital pathway to support OIT (Table 1). Patients were categorised according to their maintenance dose at the one‐year follow‐up visit (full maintenance dose defined as desensitised, and partial desensitisation combined with discontinued patients). We compared the number of notes entered in the digital pathway between these outcome groups, and no significant differences were observed (Table 1). Similarly, the distribution of traffic‐light–classified side effects did not differ between the groups. In contrast, patients' allergen‐specific IgE levels to the OIT food, both to whole allergen extracts (milk, egg white, peanut) and to the corresponding allergen molecules (Bos d 8, Gal d1, Ara h 2 and Ara h 6) varied according to OIT outcome (Table 1), consistent with a previous report [3].

A total of eight anaphylactic reactions, defined as an emergency room visit with documented anaphylaxis or the use of adrenaline for severe reactions, were recorded among the study participants (one egg OIT, four milk OIT and three peanut OIT related reactions). However, two of these reactions were related to accidental exposure to the allergenic OIT food while patients were receiving small protein doses during the buildup phase (one egg OIT and one milk OIT). Discontinuation was lowest in egg OIT (14%), while similar rates were observed in milk and peanut OIT (27% and 26%, respectively).

Our study has limitations. We report observations from the digital pathway and did not perform a comparison with other reporting methods. Previous studies have demonstrated positive outcomes using mobile applications to support OIT and have found web‐based reporting to be superior to email [4, 5]. In our study, outcomes did not differ according to the level of engagement with the digital pathway, which may have multiple explanations. Patients who were most motivated to use digital follow‐up may also have been the most adherent to OIT despite experiencing side effects. Furthermore, we were unable to determine whether the digital pathway was noted by the patient themselves or by a parent. Digital tools will likely play an increasing role in patient care in oral immunotherapy, but their optimal implementation and impact on safety require further study.

In conclusion, engagement with digital monitoring during the first year of food oral immunotherapy was moderate and varied between patients. Severe accidental reactions occurred both before and during OIT, highlighting the importance of ongoing safety monitoring. Digital reporting of adverse events was not associated with immunotherapy outcomes, which were instead related to baseline allergen‐specific IgE levels.
