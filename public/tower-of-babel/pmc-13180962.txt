Effect of obesity and endocrine therapy on the prognosis of premenopausal women with HR+HER2-breast cancer: a multi-center retrospective study
Authors: Weibin Lian, Chengye Hong, Chuan Wang, Debo Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obesity’s impact on breast cancer prognosis may depend on molecular subtype, yet there is limited research on premenopausal women with HR+HER2- breast cancer. In this study, we included 5,094 premenopausal women with HR+HER2- early-stage breast cancer who underwent radical surgery at 42 breast centers across China between 2016 and 2021. Patients were categorized into four BMI groups: underweight (UW), normal weight (NW), overweight (OW), and obese (OB). Using propensity score matching (PSM) to adjust for confounding factors, we compared disease-free survival (DFS) across BMI groups with Kaplan–Meier curves and performed multivariate Cox regression analysis to evaluate the effect of obesity on prognosis. Our results showed that obese patients had significantly worse DFS compared to those who were UW, NW, or OW (p = 0.013). After adjusting for clinicopathologic factors and treatment, obesity remained an independent prognostic factor for DFS in premenopausal HR+/HER2- patients (p = 0.043). After 1:1 PSM, OW/OB patients receiving selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) alone had significantly worse DFS compared to UW/NW patients (p = 0.022), whereas OW/OB patients treated with SERMs or aromatase inhibitors (AIs) in combination with ovarian function suppression (OFS) showed no significant difference in DFS from UW/NW patients. In addition, Restricted Cubic Splines (RCS) analysis demonstrated that the risk of recurrence increases with BMI in OW/OB patients. Collectively, these findings indicate that obesity may act as an independent adverse prognostic factor for DFS in premenopausal women with HR+/HER2− breast cancer. Introduction

Body mass index (BMI), a widely used metric for categorizing weight and obesity, has been implicated in breast cancer incidence. Studies have demonstrated that obesity increases the risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus by promoting insulin resistance and elevates serum estrogen levels through the upregulation of aromatase. Elevated circulating glucose has also been shown to activate the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), a key signaling pathway in breast cancer pathogenesis1. A growing body of research has explored the association between BMI and prognosis in early-stage breast cancer. A meta-analysis of 73 cohort studies found that being overweight or obese is a risk factor for overall survival (OS) in breast cancer patients compared to those of normal weight2. However, the relationship between obesity and breast cancer prognosis remains variable and controversial across different molecular subtypes and menopausal statuses. A prospective randomized controlled trial identified obesity as an independent prognostic factor in estrogen receptor-positive (ER +) breast cancer in younger patients but not in ER-negative disease3. Similarly, an analysis by Sparano et al., based on multiple prospective randomized controlled trials, found that higher BMI within the overweight (OW) and obese (OB) categories was associated with poorer disease-free survival (DFS) and OS in hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative (HR+HER2-) breast cancer, but not in HER2-overexpressing or triple-negative breast cancer4. Data from South Korea further corroborated these findings, indicating that obese patients with HR+HER2- breast cancer have worse OS and breast cancer-specific survival (BCSS) compared to those with normal weight (NW)5.

Hormone receptor-positive (HR+) early breast cancer patients receive different adjuvant endocrine therapies depending on their menstrual status. Retrospective analyses from the ATAC trials suggested that obese patients treated with anastrozole had a worse prognosis than those without obesity, a pattern not observed in the tamoxifen-treated group6. A prospective analysis from the ABCSG-12 trial showed that obesity significantly affected the efficacy of anastrozole and goserelin in premenopausal patients7. Additionally, a high BMI at diagnosis appears to be associated with an increased risk of recurrence in postmenopausal women receiving aromatase inhibitors (AIs). In a similar study, Harborg et al. enrolled 13,230 postmenopausal women with breast cancer and found that obesity was linked to a higher risk of recurrence compared to patients with a healthy weight8. However, few studies focus exclusively on premenopausal women, and most are single-institution retrospective studies. Ozaki et al. reported that a high BMI was associated with a worse prognosis in premenopausal patients with HR+breast cancer who received adjuvant tamoxifen (TAM)9. These findings indicate that the impact of BMI on the efficacy and prognosis of adjuvant endocrine therapy in premenopausal breast cancer remains controversial. More comprehensive, multi-center data are needed to further explore the relationship between BMI and prognosis in specific subtypes of premenopausal breast cancer.

In this study, we utilized data from multiple breast center to investigate the effect of BMI on the prognosis of premenopausal patients with HR+HER2- early breast cancer. Our aim is to determine whether obesity serves as an independent risk factor for prognosis in premenopausal breast cancer. Additionally, we seek to understand the impact of BMI on prognosis across various endocrine therapy regimens.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

At diagnosis, 198 (3.9%) patients were underweight (UW), 3690 (72.5%) were NW, 1061 (20.8%) were OW, and 145 (3.8%) were OB. Older patients (> 40 years) were more likely to be overweight or obese at diagnosis compared to those who were underweight or normal weight. Obese patients presented with a higher disease stage (Stage III) and a greater proportion of lower ER expression (≤ 50%). Additionally, more OW and OB patients received chemotherapy and radiotherapy. BMI and Prognosis

After a median follow-up of 38 months, DFS events were observed across different BMI categories. Specifically, DFS events occurred in 156 of 3,690 patients (4.2%) in the NW group, 54 of 1061 patients (5.1%) in the OW group, 13 of 145 patients (9.0%) in the OB group, and 10 of 198 patients (5.1%) in the UW group. As shown in Fig. 2, obese patients exhibited significantly worse disease-free survival compared with normal-weight, underweight, and overweight patients (p = 0.013). When stratified by whether patients received OFS as part of their adjuvant endocrine therapy, we found that DFS in OW/OB patients who received selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) without OFS was significantly worse than that of UW/NW patients (Fig. 3A p = 0.007). After 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) by age, tumor size, and N stage (Supplementary Table 1), DFS remained significantly worse in OW/OB patients treated only with SERMs compared to UW/NW patients (Fig. 3B p = 0.022). Discussion

HR+HER2- is the most common molecular subtype of breast cancer. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have specifically explored the relationship between BMI and prognosis in premenopausal women with HR+HER2- breast cancer. Using multi-center data, we found that obesity increases the risk of breast cancer recurrence in premenopausal patients with HR+HER2- subtype. Interestingly, obesity appears to have a greater impact on recurrence risk in patients treated with SERMs alone, but not in those receiving OFS as part of adjuvant endocrine therapy. Moreover, the risk of recurrence progressively increases with BMI above the normal range.

A growing body of research has identified high BMI as a risk factor for postmenopausal breast cancer8,10,11. However, few studies have focused on the relationship between BMI and prognosis in premenopausal breast cancer. Ozaki et al. analyzed 3,380 cases of premenopausal women with HR+breast cancer and found that high BMI was associated with worse BCSS in patients receiving adjuvant TAM9. Another single-center retrospective analysis from Taiwan showed that BMI was an independent prognostic factor for OS and DFS in breast cancer patients under the age of 5012. Previous studies have also shown that BMI is not an independent prognostic factor for HER2-positive or triple-negative breast cancer, suggesting that the effects of obesity on breast cancer prognosis may be subtype-dependent. To avoid confounding factors from other subtypes, our study exclusively included premenopausal patients with HR+HER2- breast cancer. Based on an analysis of 5,094 cases from multiple centers, we demonstrated that obesity is an independent risk factor for DFS, even after adjusting for clinicopathological and treatment factors. The mechanisms by which high BMI leads to poor prognosis in premenopausal HR+HER2- breast cancer remain unclear, but several factors may contribute. First, high BMI is often associated with hormonal changes, particularly increased estrogen levels, which may promote the growth and proliferation of HR+breast cancer cells. Premenopausal patients with HR+HER2- breast cancer typically receive TAM treatment. Sahar et al. demonstrated that circulating exosomes derived from obese women are more likely to induce tumorigenesis and tamoxifen resistance in MCF7 cells compared to those from women of normal weight13. Second, obesity can upregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines, adipokines, and angiogenic factors, all of which are closely linked to the proliferation and invasive behavior of breast cancer14. Additionally, high BMI increases circulating levels of insulin and insulin-like growth factor-1, promoting tumor cell proliferation and invasion15. In vivo experiments have shown that an obese microenvironment elevates local leptin levels, activating the PI3K, ERK1/2, and STAT3 pathways through leptin receptors, ultimately accelerating tumor progression16. Another study by Linares et al. showed that leptin not only increases breast cancer cell proliferation in vitro but also reduces tamoxifen efficacy17. Recently, a preplanned analysis from the PALLAS trial demonstrated a significant impact of BMI on side effects, dose reductions, early treatment discontinuation, and relative dose intensity, suggesting that BMI may affect prognosis by influencing drug efficacy and tolerability18.

Our study also suggested that BMI did not affect the prognosis of premenopausal women with HR+HER2- breast cancer who received OFS as part of their endocrine therapy. An exploratory analysis from the ABCSG12 trial showed that overweight premenopausal patients treated with OFS and aromatase inhibitors (AI) had a significantly higher risk of disease recurrence compared to normal-weight patients. However, BMI did not influence the prognosis of patients who received OFS and TAM7. Ozaki et al. reported similar findings, showing that premenopausal patients with HR+breast cancer and BMI ≥ 25 who were treated with TAM had significantly worse BCSS compared to those with BMI < 25. These results suggest that the addition of OFS may mitigate the negative impact of BMI on the prognosis of premenopausal breast cancer treated with TAM. Unfortunately, our study could not further analyze the effect of BMI on prognosis in patients receiving OFS in combination with TAM. Longer follow-up is needed to determine whether adding OFS to TAM offers a prognostic benefit for premenopausal breast cancer patients with high BMI. Moreover, chemotherapy-induced amenorrhea or menopause may be associated with better prognostic outcomes, potentially diluting the impact of OFS on prognosis19. Unfortunately, we do not have these data in our cohort for further analysis. Overall, although our findings suggest that OFS may attenuate the adverse prognostic impact of obesity in premenopausal patients, this observation should be interpreted with caution. Given the non-randomized nature of treatment allocation and the potential for residual confounding despite PSM and multivariable adjustment, this result should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than indicative of a causal relationship.

Conclusion

Obesity may serve as an independent prognostic factor for DFS in premenopausal women with HR+HER2- breast cancer, and that OFS may counteract the adverse impact of obesity in patients receiving SERMs.

Methods

Patients

All data for this study were obtained from the Shanghai Jiaotong University Breast Cancer Database (SJTU-BCDB) (http://47.100.125.104:8080/), which compiles breast cancer information from 42 centers across China. Our study included premenopausal female patients who underwent radical breast surgery for stage I–III HR+HER2- primary breast cancer between January 2016 and December 2021. The study protocol was approved by the independent ethics committee of Quanzhou First Hospital, affiliated with Fujian Medical University, and complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective design of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

All patients received adjuvant endocrine therapy with SERMs or SERMs/AI combined with OFS. Exclusion criteria included a history of breast cancer or other invasive cancers, male breast cancer, bilateral breast cancer, and patients who either did not receive endocrine therapy or had missing information on endocrine treatment. Patients lacking BMI records or follow-up data were also excluded. BMI was classified into the following categories: underweight (UW), BMI < 18.5 kg/m2; normal weight (NW), 18.5–24.9 kg/m2; overweight (OW), 25.0–29.9 kg/m2; and obese (OB), ≥ 30 kg/m2.

Definition

Hormone receptor (HR) positivity was defined as estrogen receptor (ER) and/or progesterone receptor (PR) expression ≥ 1% of tumor cell nuclei by immunohistochemistry, in accordance with current ASCO/CAP guidelines. HER2 negativity was defined as an immunohistochemistry (IHC) score of 0 or 1+, or IHC 2+with negative fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH). The primary endpoint of this study was disease-free survival (DFS), defined as the time from radical breast surgery to the date of recurrence, metastasis, or death due to breast cancer. Premenopausal status was determined using clinical criteria. Patients were considered premenopausal if they had regular menstrual cycles at the time of diagnosis or had experienced menstruation within the previous 12 months. When necessary, serum hormone levels—including estradiol, follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinizing hormone were measured to confirm premenopausal status. In this study, the typical duration of SERM therapy was 5 years, in accordance with standard clinical guidelines. For patients at high risk or per physician recommendation, SERM therapy was extended up to 10 years. OFS was primarily achieved using LH-RH agonists, administered as a subcutaneous injection every 28 days. The duration of OFS was maintained for at least 2–5 years, depending on the patient’s risk factors.

Statistical analysis

Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used to assess differences in clinicopathological characteristics and treatment information across BMI categories. Kaplan–Meier curves were employed to compare DFS between BMI groups. A multivariable Cox regression model was used to calculate hazard ratios for DFS by BMI category. The multivariable models included covariates such as age at diagnosis, tumor size, nodal status, Ki67 index, progesterone receptor (PR) status, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and OFS. To minimize potential confounding, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to balance baseline clinicopathological characteristics between comparison groups. Propensity scores were estimated using a multivariable logistic regression model incorporating age at diagnosis, tumor size and nodal status. Patients were matched in a 1:1 ratio using nearest-neighbor matching without replacement, with a predefined caliper width of 0.2 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an SMD < 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance. Additionally, a multivariable Cox regression model was applied with BMI as a continuous variable to explore the relationship between BMI and prognosis in premenopausal women with HR+HER2- breast cancer. In addition, multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to further adjust for endocrine therapy type and other potential confounders when evaluating DFS. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were utilized to visualize the association between continuous BMI and hazard ratios for breast cancer recurrence. A two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version 4.2.3.
