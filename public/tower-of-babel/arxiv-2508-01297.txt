Unified description of cuprate superconductors by fractionalized electrons emerging from integrated analyses of photoemission spectra and quasiparticle interference
Shiro Sakai 1,2 , Youhei Yamaji 3,4 , Fumihiro Imoto 5 , Tsuyoshi Tamegai 6 , Adam Kaminski 7,8 , Takeshi Kondo 9 , Yuhki Kohsaka 2,10 , Tetsuo Hanaguri 2 , and Masatoshi Imada 1,6

Abstract
Electronic structure of high-temperature superconducting cuprates is studied by analyzing experimental data independently obtained from two complementary spectroscopies, one, quasiparticle interference (QPI) measured by scanning-tunneling microscopy and the other, angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) and by combining these two sets of data in a unified theoretical analysis. Through explicit calculations of experimentally measurable quantities, we show that a simple two-component fermion model (TCFM) representing electron fractionalization succeeds in reproducing various detailed features of these experimental data: ARPES and QPI data are concomitantly reproduced by the TCFM in full energy and momentum spaces. The measured QPI pattern reveals a signature characteristic of the TCFM, distinct from the conventional single-component prediction, supporting the validity of the electron fractionalization in the cuprate. The integrated analysis also solves the puzzles of ARPES and QPI data that are seemingly inconsistent with each other. The overall success of the TCFM offers a comprehensive understanding of the electronic structure of the cuprates. We further predict that a characteristic QPI pattern should appear in the unoccupied high-energy part if the fractionalization is at work. We propose that integrated-spectroscopy analyses offer a promising way to explore challenging issues of strongly correlated electron systems.



I
Introduction

The mechanism of high-temperature superconductivity in cuprates
Bednorz and Müller 1986
remains a major challenge in condensed matter physics
Keimer et al. 2015
. In nearly 40 years since its discovery, an enormous amount of experimental data has been reported and accumulated by using a variety of experimental tools. Since each experimental result has often been analyzed independently, a comprehensive and consistent understanding based on a unified picture is often lacking.
To resolve such disjointed analyses and sometimes inconsistent conclusions, it is important to combine results from different but complementary experimental tools, especially energy (
\omega
) and momentum (
\mathbf{k}
) resolved spectroscopies, and analyze together on the same grounds in an integrated fashion using a unified theoretical framework to reach a consistent understanding of the electronic structure.

As one of the major experimental tools, angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES)
Damascelli et al. 2003
;
Sobota et al. 2021
has been developed and improved in step with the advances in the research history of the cuprates.
A great advantage of ARPES is its accessibility to a momentum-resolved single-particle spectral function, while its applicable scope is limited to the occupied part of the spectra.
Although the unoccupied part of the spectra may hold a key to unlocking the mechanism of the high-temperature superconductivity, as well as the anomalous metallic behavior above the critical temperature (
T_{\text{c}}
), no high-resolution photoemssion technique has been established for this purpose: Inverse photoemission spectroscopy, which can in principle measure the unoccupied spectra, has not yet reached an energy resolution sufficiently high to discuss the above issues.

Another experimental technique that can access the single-particle excitation spectra is the scanning tunneling spectroscopy/microscopy (STS/STM)
Fischer et al. 2007
.
This technique can resolve the real-space structure of a sample surface in an atomic resolution, and measure the local density of states (LDOS) at each position for both sides of the Fermi energy at a high energy resolution.
While atomic-scale modulation in the LDOS occurs within a unit cell, larger-scale modulation can be observed when a defect or impurity exists on the sample surface.
This is owing to the interference effect between electron waves before and after the scattering by the defect or impurity, and is called quasiparticle interference (QPI) effect
Hasegawa and Avouris 1993
;
Crommie et al. 1993
.
The QPI pattern observed for the cuprates shows various interesting signals
Hoffman et al. 2002
;
McElroy et al. 2003
;
Hanaguri et al. 2004
;
Hanaguri et al. 2007
;
Kohsaka et al. 2008
;
Lee et al. 2009
;
Alldredge et al. 2012
;
He et al. 2014
;
Fujita et al. 2014
;
Machida et al. 2016
;
Gu et al. 2019
;
Yu et al. 2019
, while it requires theoretical analyses to interpret them as a structure of the single-particle excitation spectrum.

These experimental techniques have indeed revealed various anomalous electronic structures in the cuprates, e.g., the momentum-dependent pseudogap
Ding et al. 1996
and superconducting gap
Shen et al. 1993
including the arc-like truncated Fermi surface
Norman et al. 1998
;
Shen et al. 2005
;
Kondo et al. 2009
;
Kondo et al. 2013
, distinct growth of the pseudogap from the superconducting gap
Tanaka et al. 2006
;
Kondo et al. 2007
, asymmetric energy dependence of the density of states (DOS) between occupied and unoccupied sides
Hanaguri et al. 2004
in contrast to the electron-hole symmetry expected in the superconducting phase, and the dopant-induced spatial inhomogeneity
Pan et al. 2001

as well as the stripe and checkerboard type inhomogeneity
Hanaguri et al. 2004
;
Kohsaka et al. 2007
.
For the occupied spectra, comparisons have been made
between the QPI results and autocorrelation of the ARPES data
Markiewicz 2004
;
Cheng and Su 2005
;
McElroy et al. 2006
;
Chatterjee et al. 2006
;
Fujita et al. 2020
.
These analyses have shown that most of the strong QPI signals can be attributed to scatterings between tips of banana-shaped constant-energy contours of the spectral function, namely, endpoints of arc-like conspicuous spectral intensity.
However, a discrepancy
between ARPES and QPI/STM results has been pointed out and remains puzzling:
For instance, the dispersion of a QPI signal (dubbed
\mathbf{q}_{7}
below) related to the
d
-wave superconducting gap seems to cut the Fermi energy at a finite momentum
Kohsaka et al. 2008
;
Lee et al. 2009
;
Vishik et al. 2009
, unlike the clear
d
-wave gap structure observed in ARPES.

To consider these issues, we would first point out that the experimentally measured QPI spectra do not necessarily have the same spatial dependence, or equivalently the same momentum-space structure, as the LDOS: While the tunneling spectrum is proportional to the LDOS at each spatial position, the normalization factor could depend on the position, giving a spatial dependence different from that of the LDOS. Therefore, a more explicit calculation of the experimentally measurable quantities, beyond the LDOS, is necessary to discuss the QPI pattern.

Here, by such an explicit calculation of the QPI pattern, we show that a simple theoretical model, derived from an electron fractionalization due to a strong correlation effect, gives a consistent explanation for many essential features of the observed results of ARPES, STM/STS and QPI, resolving the puzzles and showing the power of integrated spectroscopy analysis. It renders support for the validity of the fractionalization as well.
The model, which we call the two-component Fermion model (TCFM)
Sakai et al. 2016a
;
Sakai et al. 2016b
, materializes the fractionalization in the simplest form of a one-body Hamiltonian involving a measurable quasiparticle and a hidden fermion, which hybridize with each other.

The concept of the electron fractionalization was proposed in various contexts such as the spin-charge separation in 1D Tomonaga-Luttinger liquid
Tomonaga 1950
;
Luttinger 1963
, solitons in polyacetylene
Heeger et al. 1988
, fractional quantum Hall state
Laughlin 1983
and slave-boson or slave-fermion approaches for doped Mott insulators
Sachdev 2003
;
Kotliar and Ruckenstein 1986
. The present fractionalization is distinct from any of these proposals and represents two bistable fermion excitations splintered off from one fermion to describe physics around the Mott insulator such as the cuprates.

The cuprates are often modeled by doped Mott insulators and by the two-dimensional (2D) Hubbard model as one of the simplest models. The 2D Hubbard model has been extensively studied before without paying serious attention to the fractionalization by the cluster extension
Kotliar et al. 2001
;
Maier et al. 2005
of the dynamical mean-field theory (cDMFT)
Metzner and Vollhardt 1989
;
Georges et al. 1996

and related methods, for the ARPES spectra

Huscroft et al. 2001
;
Maier et al. 2002
;
Sénéchal and Tremblay 2004
;
Civelli et al. 2005
;
Maier et al. 2005
;
Sénéchal et al. 2005
;
Kyung et al. 2006
;
Aichhorn et al. 2007
;
Kancharla et al. 2008
;
Civelli et al. 2008
;
Liebsch and Tong 2009
;
Okamoto et al. 2010
;
Chen et al. 2012
;
Gull et al. 2013
;
Kohno 2014
, electronic Raman spectroscopy
Lin et al. 2012
;
Sakai et al. 2013
;
Loret et al. 2016
;
Loret et al. 2017
, optical conductivity
Lin et al. 2009
;
Ferrero et al. 2010
;
Sakai et al. 2013
, and STS
Ferrero et al. 2009
;
Sakai et al. 2010
;
Sordi et al. 2012
;
Sakai et al. 2013
.
These analyses were able to capture some aspects of the cuprates.

Previous cDMFT studies on the 2D Hubbard model have also revealed the existence of self-energy poles, which naturally accounts for the emergence of the Mott gap in the Mott insulator and the pseudogap in the low-energy region of the underdoped normal state, both of which are beyond the scope of the perturbative treatments of the electron correlation and are manifestations of the Mott physics
Stanescu and Kotliar 2006
;
Civelli 2009
;
Kyung et al. 2009
;
Sakai et al. 2009
;
Sakai et al. 2010
;
Gull and Millis 2015
;
Sakai et al. 2018
. Furthermore, the cDMFT studies in the
d
-wave superconducting phase revealed an unexpected cancellation of the poles of normal and anomalous self-energies in the electron Green’s function
Sakai et al. 2016a
;
Sakai et al. 2016b
. However, the nature and root of the self-energy poles had been highly nontrivial at the time of the discovery of the cancellation.

TCFM was first proposed
Sakai et al. 2016a
;
Sakai et al. 2016b
to offer a unique way of understanding the above mentioned emergence of the self-energy poles and their enigmatic cancellation in the superconducting state discovered in the cDMFT studies.
The self-energy poles can be interpreted as a consequence of the hybridization of a quasiparticle with a hidden fermion degree of freedom, which is correctly described by the TCFM.
For a review, see also Ref.
Sakai 2023
.

Furthermore, without relying on the Hubbard model, the machine-learning analysis of the ARPES data has shown the cancellation of the self-energy peaks
Yamaji et al. 2021
, suggesting the dominance of the fractionalization.
The TCFM with the fractionalized electrons has also predicted an enhancement of high-energy excitonic signal below
T_{\rm c}
in resonant inelastic X-ray spectroscopy
Imada 2021
, which has been evidenced experimentally
Singh et al. 2022
.

It has been argued
Imada and Suzuki 2019
that the two fermions in TCFM can be interpreted as the conventional quasiparticle and the incoherent part of the same electron, respectively, which emerge as a consequence of bistability ascribed to a direct consequence of the Mottness. The bistability directly generates emergent attraction and the Cooper pair. In
ab initio
calculations of the cuprates, the emergence of the bistability and resultant effective attraction were evidenced counterintuitively in the local repulsive interaction term, where the local emergent attraction has shown one-to-one correspondence to the calculated
d
-wave superconducting long-ranged order
Schmid et al. 2023
.

Since the electron fractionalization offers an entirely different picture from the conventional electronic structure, it is desired to test its validity by thorough and combined analyses using
independent spectroscopy tools. In this paper, we explore the consistency of ARPES and QPI in their integrated analyses by employing TCFM to further critically examine the validity of the electron fractionalization and its microscopic basis. For this purpose, we first determine the TCFM parameters so as to reproduce the ARPES experimental result
Palczewski et al. 2010
measured for an optimally-doped Bi
2
Sr
2
CaCu
2
O
8+δ
(Bi2212).
We then calculate the QPI pattern based on the same model, and finally compare it with the experimental results for the same material.
For this comparison, we have calculated experimentally measurable QPI spectra beyond the LDOS.
We find that the TCFM can reproduce the main features of both the ARPES and QPI experimental results at the same time and solve the known puzzle of the seemingly inconsistent results between the ARPES and QPI data on the quasipaticle dispersions mentioned above.

We further find that the available experimental QPI data are not consistent with the single-component description without fractionalization but are accounted for by the TCFM, evidencing the electron fractionalization. We predict another possible evidence for the fractionalization to be observed in the future QPI measurements for the high-energy region.

II
Model

As is outlined in Sec.
I
, we consider the following TCFM Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}\left[\epsilon_{c\mathbf{k}}c_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+\epsilon_{d\mathbf{k}}d_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}^{\dagger}d_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+V_{\mathbf{k}}(d_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+c_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}^{\dagger}d_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}^{\phantom{\dagger}})\right]

\displaystyle-\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left[D_{c\mathbf{k}}c_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow}c_{-\mathbf{k}\downarrow}+D_{d\mathbf{k}}d_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow}d_{-\mathbf{k}\downarrow}+\text{h.c.}\right],

(1)

where
c
and
d
represent a quasiparticle and the hidden fermion, respectively.

\epsilon_{c(d)\mathbf{k}}
and
D_{c(d)}
denote the normal and anomalous dispersions of
c
(
d
), and
V_{\mathbf{k}}
denotes the hybridization between them.
Integrating out the
d
degrees of freedom from
H
, we obtain an effective action for
c
(quasiparticle) degrees of freedom, where their normal and anomalous dispersions are modified by

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\text{nor}}_{\text{TCFM}}(\mathbf{k},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}(\omega+\epsilon_{d\mathbf{k}})}{\omega^{2}-\epsilon_{d\mathbf{k}}^{2}-D_{d\mathbf{k}}^{2}},

(2)

and

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\text{ano}}_{\text{TCFM}}(\mathbf{k},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{V_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}D_{d\mathbf{k}}}{\omega^{2}-\epsilon_{d\mathbf{k}}^{2}-D_{d\mathbf{k}}^{2}},

(3)

respectively
Sakai et al. 2016b
;
Sakai et al. 2016a
.
These modifications can be interpreted as a self-energy for
c
.

Namely, as far as there is only a single self-energy pole in the low-energy region, the correlation effects in a many-body system (such as the Hubbard model and first-principles one for the cuprates) can be mapped onto a
one-body
Hamiltonian of Eq. (
1
), where the low-energy dynamics of the quasiparticle (
c
) can be described by the “bare” dispersions (
\epsilon_{c}
and
D_{c}
) and the self-energy corrections, Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
).
In other words, the presence of a self-energy pole in a many-body system manifests a fractionalization of an electron to a quasiparticle (continuously connected from the bare electron through a renormalization) and a hidden fermion (corresponding to a self-energy pole, i.e., incoherent excitation).
In the particular case relevant to high-
T_{\text{c}}
cuprates, which are doped Mott insulators, this hidden fermion may correspond to an electron trapped around doped holes, namely, weakly bound exciton
Imada and Suzuki 2019
, that appears as the incoherent part of the carrier in experimental measurements such as ARPES, optics and transport, because such a state can reduce the interaction energy and hence be a low-energy excitation
Yamaji and Imada 2011a
;
Yamaji and Imada 2011b
.
As was introduced in Sec.
I
, the
ab initio
calculations of the cuprates support the emergence of the bistable excitations, one, the conventional quasiparticle represented by
c
and the other, the hidden fermion
d
, because the local Coulomb energy has double-well type dependence on the carrier density with the negative curvature in between, which induces emergent local attraction between carriers as well
Schmid et al. 2023
.
A more detailed physical picture is discussed in Refs.
Sakai et al. 2016a
;
Sakai et al. 2016b
;
Sakai et al. 2018
;
Imada and Suzuki 2019
;
Schmid et al. 2023
.

In this study, we do not address the origin of
d
but utilize the Hamiltonian, Eq. (
1
), to calculate the QPI pattern.
However, just in case, we note that the origin of the two components has nothing to do with the separation of Cu-
d
and O-
p
contributions nor the bilayer structure of Bi2212. It has been established that the single-band description by the antibonding orbital of strongly hybridized Cu-

and O-
p
orbitals is enough for the analysis of low-energy physics of cuprates

and the two components emerge from the strong correlation effect of this single-band degree of freedom.

We determine the model parameters in Eq. (
1
) by fitting the ARPES experimental result presented in Sec.
IV
.
More concretely, we employ

\displaystyle\epsilon_{c(d)\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=t_{c(d),0}-2t_{c(d),1}(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y})

\displaystyle-4t_{c(d),2}\cos k_{x}\cos k_{y}-2t_{c(d),3}(\cos 2k_{x}+\cos 2k_{y})

(4)

with

(

) denoting the onsite potential, and the nearest-, second-, and third-neighbor hoppings of the

(

The hybridization,

\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{k}}=V_{0}+V_{1}(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y})+2V_{2}\cos k_{x}\cos k_{y},

(5)

is parametrized in a similar way.
We assume the
d
-wave superconductivity by

\displaystyle D_{c(d)\mathbf{k}}=\frac{1}{2}D_{c(d),0}(\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y}).

(6)

The detailed procedure for the fitting is described in Appendix
A.2
.

While the TCFM Hamiltonian introduced in Eq. (
1
) captures the most singular structure of the self-energy,
interactions among

To take these effects into account, we phenomenologically add less-singular components in the self-energy of

The candidates for the origin of these additional self-energies

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{r},eV)\equiv\derivative{I}{V}=eI_{\text{set}}\frac{\rho(\mathbf{r},eV)}{\int_{0}^{eV_{\text{set}}}\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)d\omega},

\displaystyle L(\mathbf{r},eV)\equiv\frac{V}{I}\derivative{I}{V}=\frac{V}{I}g(\mathbf{r},eV).

VI
Summary and Outlook

We have conducted an integrated analysis of combined ARPES, STM and QPI experimental data with the help of the TCFM theory based on the electron fractionalization.
The TCFM fitted with ARPES data reproduces the
QPI patterns even for details, which makes a substantial step forward toward a unified understanding of the high-
T_{c}
cuprates.
In particular, a distinctive QPI pattern obtained by the TCFM, which is not found in the conventional electronic structure without the fractionalization, agrees with the experimental data supporting the unconventional fractionalization of electrons taking place in the cuprates. At the same time, the seemingly controversial data between ARPES and QPI for the quasiparticle dispersion are understood by considering the structure of the TCFM solutions.

These results are obtained by explicitly calculating the experimentally-measurable quantities, such as the differential conductance, which has often been replaced with the local density of states in the literature but does not agree with it.
We have also revealed that the relative-sign structure can hold a key to unraveling the pseudogap physics.

We also point out that the background intensity in ARPES is related to the extinction in QPI.
Its microscopic origin, as well as that
of the marginal Fermi liquid, is left for future studies. These elements have been taken into account as phenomenological self-energies and are desired to be derived microscopically from the interactions of
c
and
d
as well as those with other relevant degrees of freedom, such as excitons and magnetic/charge/phonon fluctuations, in an extended framework of the TCFM.

We have performed a combined analysis of ARPES, STM, and QPI. To further examine more quantitative reliability of the electron fractionalization theory, it would be important to combine other experimental data, for instance from neutron, optics, magnetic resonance, muon, electron energy-loss spectroscopy, and resonant inelastic X-ray scattering, with the help of machine learning analysis, which is left as important future studies.

The significance of the electron fractionalization lies in its link to the mechanism of the superconductivity itself, for which some proposals were made
Schmid et al. 2023
from ab initio calculations, though it is not fully understood. Further pursuit using integrated spectroscopy analyses is another intriguing future subject.

A.1
Form of self-energy

While the TCFM Hamiltonian has been successful in describing the low-energy structures of the spectral function and the self-energy
Sakai et al. 2016a
;
Sakai et al. 2016b
;
Yamaji et al. 2021
;
Imada 2021
;
Singh et al. 2022
;
Sakai 2023
in the underdoped region of the 2D Hubbard model at least qualitatively,
more detailed quantitative comparisons with the experimental spectra of the cuprates become possible by incorporating additional structures to
the self-energy
\Sigma_{\rm TCFM}
.
Here, along the line addressed in the end of Sec.
II
, we provide additional self-energy structures close to zero energy and at high energies.
The former is related to a so-called marginal Fermi-liquid (MFL) behavior
Varma et al. 1989
, which gives a linear-in-energy scattering rate around the Fermi energy.
The latter is related to the flat feature of the ARPES spectra for
\omega<-0.1
eV, mentioned in Sec.
IV
.
Indeed, the less-singular part of the self-energy
of the cuprate superconductors
can be decomposed into a
MFL-type component and the remaining flat structure, as explained later
Yamaji et al. 2021
.
In the following, we elaborate on these contributions one by one.

A.1.1
TCFM part

Equations (
2
) and (
3
) define the TCFM part of the self-energy, describing the correlation effect of our main interest.
They have poles at the same energies,
\omega=\pm\omega_{p\mathbf{k}}
with
\omega_{p\mathbf{k}}\equiv\sqrt{\epsilon_{d\mathbf{k}}^{2}+D_{d\mathbf{k}}^{2}}
.
The contributions of these poles are canceled out in the Green’s functions [see Eqs. (
34
) and (
35
)] at the pole energies but still significantly influence the quasiparticle dynamics
Sakai et al. 2016a
;
Sakai et al. 2016b
.

A.1.2
Marginal Fermi-liquid part

As examined in the literature,
the low-energy ARPES spectra, at least, along the nodal direction require a term proportional to
|\omega|
in the imaginary part of the normal self-energy
Smit et al. 2024
.
The non-analytic structure is indeed found
not only along the nodal direction
but also in the antinodal region
Yamaji et al. 2021
.
Even though a small deviation from the MFL was reported along the nodal direction
Smit et al. 2024
, the
\omega
-linear term is enough to capture the essential behaviors of the low-energy spectra in the entire Brillouin zone.
The non-analytical term has been examined to describe the momentum-independent anomalous behavior of the normal-state spectra known as the MFL behavior
Varma et al. 1989
.

As discussed in Ref.
Varma et al. 1989
, the MFL component of the self-energy can arise from electron scatterings by intensive low-energy bosonic fluctuations not contained in Eq. (
1
).
In Ref.
Yamaji et al. 2021
, the formation of the self-energy peaks causes such electron scatterings, giving the MFL component.
The excitonic excitations that are not taken into account in the TCFM Hamiltonian, Eq. (
1
), coupled to the
d
fermions, are possibly responsible for the MFL component of the self-energy.
However, the microscopic origin of the MFL is not well established.

Since it is observed universally in experiments, without specifying its origin, we model the MFL
component,
\Sigma_{\rm MFL}(\omega)
, with a soft cutoff as

\displaystyle{\rm Im}\Sigma_{\rm MFL}(\omega)=

\displaystyle-(1/\pi)\sqrt{\omega^{2}+(\pi/\tau_{0})^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\{1+\exp[(\omega-\Lambda)/\sigma_{\rm MFL}]\}^{-1}

\displaystyle\times\{1+\exp[-(\omega+\Lambda)/\sigma_{\rm MFL}]\}^{-1}.

(19)

Here, a small but finite inverse lifetime,
\tau_{0}^{-1}=1
meV is introduced.
The soft cutoff is defined by sigmoid functions with the step width
\sigma_{\rm MFL}=30
meV and the cutoff energy
\Lambda
=0.4 eV.
The real part of the
\Sigma_{\text{MFL}}
is obtained by the Kramers-Kronig transformation of
{\rm Im}\Sigma_{\text{MFL}}
.

A.1.3
Background part

As one can see in Fig.

This flat spectrum has been attributed to a broad structure of Im

;
Li et al. 2018
;
Yamaji et al. 2021

In particular, according to the machine-learning analysis of the ARPES spectrum at

Hereafter, this flat structure in

The possible origin of the background has been discussed in the literature.
For instance, when bosonic excitations are coupled to fermions, a typical self-energy structure appears at
|\omega|
larger than the bosonic energy scales through a mechanism different from the origin of the MFL.
A well-studied example is the self-energy generated by the electron-phonon couplings
Schrieffer et al. 1963

Scalapino et al. 1966

For
|\omega|
larger than the typical boson energy scale, flat structures appear in Im
\Sigma^{\text{nor}}

of the origin of the boson

Even in the electron gas, where plasmons play an important role, the broad structure in the self-energy was also found
Pavlyukh et al. 2016

In the spectral function of the Hubbard model, the incoherent continuum is found
between the quasiparticle dispersion and
the incoherent lower Hubbard band
Macridin et al. 2007
;
Sakai et al. 2010
;
Kohno 2014
;
Charlebois and Imada 2020
, which is possibly relevant
to the flat structure in ARPES.
Though electron-phonon couplings in the cuprates have been examined in detail
Li et al. 2021

Bok et al. 2010
.
The flat structure may alternatively be induced by the excitonic fluctuations, in addition to the MFL component.
In the TCFM picture, excitonic fluctuations may arise because it was proposed that the
d
fermions represent the fermionic degrees of freedom contained in the weakly bound excitons
Yamaji and Imada 2011b
;
Yamaji and Imada 2011a
;
Sakai et al. 2018
;
Imada and Suzuki 2019

However, the couplings between the
d
fermion and neither the exciton, phonon, nor the spin fluctuation are explicitly included in Eq. (
1

Therefore, we add the flat component to
{\rm Im}\Sigma^{\rm nor}_{\rm TCFM}
in the TCFM description of the ARPES spectra and QPI patterns to reproduce the ARPES flat structure without specifying its origin.

Irrespective of its origin the background component is nearly constant over the high-energy region for
\omega\lesssim-0.1
eV in Bi2212

.
Because the background is not contained in the TCFM Hamiltonian (which describes only a few low-energy spectral peaks), we incorporate this component as follows.

We derive the
\omega
dependence of this additional self-energy,
\Sigma_{\text{bg}}
, from the ARPES data at
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}_{\text{AN}}
, where we have high-resolution data.
The self-energy derived from the ARPES data
Yamaji et al. 2021
contains peak structures and MFL-like contributions as well.
Therefore, we need to subtract these contributions when we construct the background component to avoid double counting.
We start from the imaginary part of the total self-energy,
{\rm Im}\Sigma^{\rm tot}
, for the optimally doped Bi2212.
While the peak structures are canceled in

The real part of

To perform the Kramers-Kronig transformation, the imaginary part is interpolated using Gaussian process regression based on 512 normal distributions with a standard deviation of 0.01 eV, arranged at equal intervals from -0.4 eV to 0.4 eV, which naturally introduces a soft cutoff as shown in the inset of Fig.
16

Here, for simplicity, we assume electron-hole symmetry for

This background contribution is relevant to the so-called extinction of QPI signals, as shown in Fig.

The obtained self-energy is shown by blue dashed curves in Fig.

As the momentum dependence of this self-energy is not known, we assume the following simple form to account for the difference between nodal and antinodal regions, introducing a single fitting parameter

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\text{bg}}(\mathbf{k},\omega)=\Sigma_{\text{bg}}(\mathbf{k}_{\text{AN}},\omega)\left[1+c_{\text{bg}}h(\mathbf{k})\right]

(20)

with

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{k})=(\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y})^{2}-(\cos k_{\text{AN}x}-\cos k_{\text{AN}y})^{2}.

(21)

h(\mathbf{k})=0

is decomposed as

\displaystyle C\simeq

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\int_{E_{\rm cut}}^{0}|\tilde{I}_{\rm TCFM}(\mathbf{k},\omega)-f(\mathbf{k},\omega)|^{2}d\omega

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\int_{E_{\rm cut}}^{0}|\varepsilon(\mathbf{k},\omega)|^{2}d\omega,

(42)

where we assume that the noise
\varepsilon(\mathbf{k},\omega)
is random and independent at each
(\mathbf{k},\omega)
.
The second term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
42
) provides an estimate of the lower bound of
C
.

Here, we try to estimate the noise by fitting the experimental ARPES data with a Gaussian process model.
We employ Gaussian distributions with the standard deviation
\sigma=10
meV at each
\omega
point and
smoothly interpolate the noisy ARPES data.
Here, the ARPES data are taken at 301
\omega
points ranging from -0.405 eV to 0.195 eV.
The standard deviation
\sigma
of the Gaussian distributions is chosen to be the experimental resolution.
To avoid overfitting to the noise, we fit the ARPES data at five consecutive
\mathbf{k}
points by a single curve.
(There are 120
\mathbf{k}
points along each momentum cut shown in Fig.
1
.
Out of the 120 points, each locus indicated by an orange arrow contains 31-81
\mathbf{k}
points.)
We thus obtain a Gaussian process model,
\tilde{I}_{\rm GP}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
.
When the Gaussian process model is a decent approximation of
f(\mathbf{k},\omega)
, the following integral,

\displaystyle C_{\rm n}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\int_{E_{\rm cut}}^{0}|\tilde{I}_{\rm GP}(\mathbf{k},\omega)-\tilde{I}_{\rm ARPES}(\mathbf{k},\omega)|^{2}d\omega,

(43)

is a decent approximation of the integrated noise, i.e.,

\displaystyle C_{\rm n}\sim\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\int_{E_{\rm cut}}^{0}|\varepsilon(\mathbf{k},\omega)|^{2}d\omega.

(44)

The deviation of the TCFM spectrum from the inherent spectrum,
f(\mathbf{k},\omega)
, is given by the first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
42
).
Then, the standard deviation of the TCFM spectrum
is measured by
\sqrt{(C-C_{\rm n})/C_{\rm n}}
.
Fitting the ARPES data with the TCFM, we obtain the optimized cost function of
C=0.524
.
As Eq. (
43
) gives
C_{\rm n}=0.248
,
the standard deviation is
\sqrt{(C-C_{\rm n})/C_{\rm n}}\simeq 1.05
.
Although the number of the TCFM parameters is as small as 16, their optimization turns out to reproduce the inherent spectrum within almost one standard deviation in the full
\mathbf{q}-\omega
space.

Based on the electronic structure, i.e.,

To calculate the spatial modulation of the LDOS, we follow the method of a single-impurity scattering
Wang and Lee 2003
(Appendix

Dell’Anna et al. 2005
;
Choubey et al. 2014
;
Kreisel et al. 2015

Dalla Torre et al. 2016

Choubey et al. 2017

Because the experimentally measurable quantities are not the LDOS itself, we further calculate the quantities [

L
and
Z
defined in Eqs. (
63
), (
64
), and (
65
) below, respectively], which can be directly measured in experiments (Appendix
A.3.3

In the calculation, we place a single impurity at the origin.
Since this conserves the inversion symmetry, the Fourier-transformed quantities like

,

,

In experiments, however, these quantities can have a phase because the overall phase depends on the impurity position, which is difficult to locate experimentally, and because multiple impurities can contribute.

and

We therefore use

[defined by Eq. (

A.3.1
Projection onto a surface Wannier function

To make a better comparison with STM experiments, which have a spatial resolution of 0.1nm scale, we project the TCFM lattice Green’s function onto a Wannier function respecting the
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
symmetry
Choubey et al. 2014
;
Kreisel et al. 2015
;
Dalla Torre et al. 2016
;
Choubey et al. 2017
and thereby take into account the structure inside a unit cell.
Since STM measures a current from the surface of a cleaved sample, which consists of a Bi-O plane in the case of Bi2212, it essentially measures a tail of the Wannier function centering on the CuO
2
plane closest to the surface.
To represent this tail, we employ a
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
-symmetry Gaussian function,

\displaystyle w_{\mathbf{R}}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\equiv A\frac{(x-X)^{2}-(y-Y)^{2}}{a^{2}}\exp\left[-\frac{B(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R})^{2}}{2a^{2}}\right],

(45)

following Ref.
Dalla Torre et al. 2016
. Here,
a=1
is the lattice constant,
\mathbf{R}=(X,Y)
represents the coordinate of the Wannier center projected on the surface, and
\mathbf{r}=(x,y)
represents the continuous-space coordinate on the surface.
We choose
B=4
, with which the unperturbed DOS projected on the surface,
\rho_{0}
defined below, well reproduces the STM experimental result.
Although we choose
A=\sqrt{B^{3}/(\pi a^{2})}
, which normalizes Eq. (
45
), the conclusions obtained below do not essentially depend on this choice since
\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)
defined below is simply proportional to
A^{2}
and we are interested only in relative intensity.

With the Fourier transformation,
w_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{R}}w_{\mathbf{R}}(\mathbf{r})e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}
, we define

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{0}(\mathbf{r},\omega)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{N_{\mathbf{k}}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}|w_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}\hat{G}(\mathbf{k},\omega)

(46)

and

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{r},\omega)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{N_{\mathbf{k}}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}w_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\hat{G}(\mathbf{k},\omega),

(47)

in a real continuous space, where
N_{\mathbf{k}}
is the number of
\mathbf{k}
points.

A.3.2
Calculation of LDOS modulation

After the above preparations, we now calculate the LDOS at the surface, modulated by impurity scatterings
Wang and Lee 2003
;
Andersen and Hedeg

å

rd 2003
;
Capriotti et al. 2003
;
Pereg-Barnea and Franz 2003
;
Zhang and Ting 2003
. It is written as

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)=\rho_{0}(\mathbf{r},\omega)+\delta\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)

(48)

with

\displaystyle\rho_{0}(\mathbf{r},\omega)\equiv-\frac{1}{\pi}\imaginary[\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{0}(\mathbf{r},\omega)]_{11},

(49)

representing the nonperturbed part, and
\delta\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)
, representing the spatially-modulated part induced by an impurity.
In the lowest order of the nonmagnetic impurity potential,
\hat{V}_{\text{imp}}
, located at the origin, the spatially modulated part is given by
Wang and Lee 2003
;
Böker et al. 2020

\displaystyle\delta\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\imaginary\left[\hat{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{r},\omega)\hat{V}_{\text{imp}}\hat{\mathcal{G}}(-\mathbf{r},\omega)\right]_{11}.

(50)

For comparison with experiments, we calculate the Fourier transformation of Eq. (
48
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}(\mathbf{q},\omega)+\delta\rho(\mathbf{q},\omega).

(51)

Here,
\mathbf{r}
is summed over the whole real-space points, where we consider
N_{c}=4\times 4
points in each unit cell.
We have confirmed that the following results do not change qualitatively for a larger
N_{c}
.
Since
\rho_{0}(\mathbf{r},\omega)
is periodic with respect to the lattice constant
a
,
\rho_{0}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
is nonzero only at
(2\pi m/a,2\pi n/a)
with integers
m
and
n
.
Hence, we focus on
q_{x},q_{y}\in(-2\pi/a,2\pi/a)
, where
\rho(\mathbf{q},\omega)

is identical with
\delta\rho(\mathbf{q},\omega)
except for
\mathbf{q}=(0,0)
.
Note that, while
\rho(\mathbf{q},\omega)
and
\delta\rho(\mathbf{q},\omega)
can be complex
since the impurity placed at the origin preserves the inversion symmetry of the system.
Thus, for
\mathbf{q}\neq(0,0)
,
\rho(\mathbf{q},\omega)
is equal to

\displaystyle\delta\rho(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\pi N_{\mathbf{k}}N_{c}}\sum_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}w_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})w_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})^{\ast}e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\times[\hat{G}(\mathbf{k},\omega)\hat{V}_{\text{imp}}\hat{G}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle-\{\hat{G}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\omega)\hat{V}_{\text{imp}}\hat{G}(\mathbf{k},\omega)\}^{\ast}]_{11},

(52)

where
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
is summed over the
N_{c}
positions in the unit cell. We have used
w_{\mathbf{k}}(-\mathbf{r})=w_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})^{\ast}
and
w_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=w_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}
.

To further take into account a realistic form of the impurity, we employ a finite spread of the impurity potential in real space by replacing
\hat{V}_{\text{imp}}
with
\int\hat{V}_{\text{imp}}(\mathbf{r}_{0})d\mathbf{r}_{0}

Zhang and Ting 2003
;
Capriotti et al. 2003
;
Zhu et al. 2004
;
Nunner et al. 2006
.
This is reasonable because the source of the impurity potential in cuprates is considered to be located in the dopant layer away from the CuO
2
plane.
In fact, as we see later in Sec.
V.3
, this finite spread causes a noticeable decay of the QPI intensity as
|\mathbf{q}|
increases, consistently with the experimental QPI data.
Then, instead of Eq. (
50
), we start from

\displaystyle\delta\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)

\displaystyle\equiv-\frac{1}{\pi}\int d\mathbf{r}_{0}\imaginary\left[\hat{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0},\omega)\hat{V}_{\text{imp}}(\mathbf{r}_{0})\hat{\mathcal{G}}(-\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}_{0},\omega)\right]_{11},

(53)

and obtain

\displaystyle\delta\rho(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\pi N_{\mathbf{k}}N_{c}}\sum_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}w_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})w_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})^{\ast}e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\times[\hat{G}(\mathbf{k},\omega)\hat{V}_{\text{imp}}(\mathbf{q})\hat{G}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle-\{\hat{G}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\omega)\hat{V}_{\text{imp}}(\mathbf{q})\hat{G}(\mathbf{k},\omega)\}^{\ast}]_{11},

(54)

where
\hat{V}_{\text{imp}}(\mathbf{q})=\int\hat{V}_{\text{imp}}(\mathbf{r}_{0})e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{0}}d\mathbf{r}_{0}
.

Experimental data also indicate that both normal and anomalous (i.e., superconducting) components of
\hat{V}_{\text{imp}}
are necessary:
The importance of the anomalous component has been
highlighted in previous theoretical works as well

Cheng and Su 2005
;
Nunner et al. 2005
;
Nunner et al. 2006
.
We therefore decompose
\hat{V}_{\text{imp}}(\mathbf{r})
as
\hat{V}_{\text{imp}}(\mathbf{r})=V_{n}(\mathbf{r})\hat{\sigma}_{z}+V_{s}(\mathbf{r})\hat{\sigma}_{x}
with the Pauli matrices
\hat{\sigma}_{x}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{z}
.
Following Ref.
Sulangi et al. 2017
, to mimic the realistic impurity potential, we take the Yukawa form,

\displaystyle V_{n}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=V_{n0}\exp(-\frac{\sqrt{\rr^2+z_0^2}-z_0}{r_{\rm imp}})\frac{z_{0}}{\sqrt{r_{\rm imp}^{2}+z_{0}^{2}}},

(55)

\displaystyle V_{s}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=V_{s0}\left(\cos\frac{x}{a}-\cos\frac{y}{a}\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp(-\frac{\sqrt{\rr^2+z_0^2}-z_0}{r_{\rm imp}})\frac{z_{0}}{\sqrt{r_{\rm imp}^{2}+z_{0}^{2}}}.

(56)

with
r_{\rm imp}=0.5a
and
z_{0}=2a
.
Note that the
d
-wave form factor in
V_{s}
is necessary to make a finite contribution in Eq. (
54
) since
V_{s}
connects the normal and anomalous Green’s functions.

As the above impurity potentials, Eqs. (
55
) and (
56
), hold the inversion symmetry, their Fourier transformation,
V_{n}(\mathbf{q})
and
V_{s}(\mathbf{q})
, are real. In this case, Eq. (
54
) can be recast into

\displaystyle\delta\rho(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=V_{n}(\mathbf{q})\{\delta\rho_{GG}(\mathbf{q},\omega)-\delta\rho_{FF}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\}

\displaystyle+V_{s}(\mathbf{q})\{\delta\rho_{GF}(\mathbf{q},\omega)+\delta\rho_{FG}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\}

(57)

with

\displaystyle\delta\rho_{XY}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{\pi N_{\mathbf{k}}}\sum_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\mathbf{k}}w_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})w_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})^{\ast}e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\times\text{Im}[X(\mathbf{k},\omega)Y(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\omega)]

(58)

for
X,Y=G,F
.

The amplitude of the impurity potential is not known experimentally and we use small values within the realistic range, namely,
V_{n0}=0.1
eV and
V_{s0}=0.01
eV in the following unless otherwise mentioned.
Our main interest is in the relative intensity and sign of the QPI pattern in the energy-momentum space, which are insensitive to the impurity strength.
The small impurity strength ensures the validity of the lowest-order approximation in Eq. (
50
).
This choice of the small values of
V_{\{n0,s0\}}
is indeed justified by the good agreement with the experimental results as shown in Sec.
V
.

A.3.3
Calculation of experimentally measurable data

The experimental data obtained by STS are not directly connected to the LDOS,
\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)
, but require an appropriate analysis.
The STS measures the current
I
flowing between the tip and the sample when the bias voltage
V
is applied between them.
Assuming that the LDOS of the tip does not significantly depend on energy in the range of our interest, we can write the current at a lateral position
\mathbf{r}
on the sample surface as

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{r},z,V)=Ce^{-\kappa(\mathbf{r})z}\int_{0}^{eV}\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)d\omega,

(59)

where
\kappa(\mathbf{r})>0
describes the decay of the current against the tip-sample distance
z
and
C
is a constant.
In practice, when measuring the tunneling current and the differential conductance,
z
is adjusted to maintain a constant current
I_{\text{set}}
at a fixed bias voltage
V_{\text{set}}
at each
\mathbf{r}
through

\displaystyle I_{\text{set}}=Ce^{-\kappa(\mathbf{r})z_{\mathrm{cc}}(\mathbf{r};V_{\text{set}},I_{\text{set}})}\int_{0}^{eV_{\text{set}}}\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)d\omega,

(60)

where
z_{\mathrm{cc}}
denotes a constant-current topographic image obtained at the set-point voltage
V_{\text{set}}
and current
I_{\text{set}}
.
Accordingly, the measured current is

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{r},z_{\mathrm{cc}},V)=Ce^{-\kappa(\mathbf{r})z_{\mathrm{cc}}(\mathbf{r};V_{\text{set}},I_{\text{set}})}\int_{0}^{eV}\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)d\omega.

(61)

Combining Eqs. (
60
) and (
61
), we can eliminate the factor
Ce^{-\kappa(\mathbf{r})z_{\mathrm{cc}}}
, to obtain

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{r},eV;V_{\text{set}},I_{\text{set}})=\dfrac{I_{\text{set}}\int_{0}^{eV}\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)d\omega}{\int_{0}^{eV_{\text{set}}}\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)d\omega}.

(62)

The differential conductance
\frac{dI}{dV}
is then given by

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{r},eV;V_{\text{set}},I_{\text{set}})\equiv\frac{dI}{dV}=eI_{\text{set}}\frac{\rho(\mathbf{r},eV)}{\int_{0}^{eV_{\text{set}}}\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)d\omega}.

(63)

The presence of the denominator in Eq. (
63
), known as the set-point effect, prevents a direct comparison between
g(\mathbf{r},eV;V_{\text{set}},I_{\text{set}})
and
\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)
.
The following functions are used in the analysis of experimental data to mitigate the set-point effect:

\displaystyle L(\mathbf{r},eV)\equiv\frac{g(\mathbf{r},eV;V_{\text{set}},I_{\text{set}})}{I(\mathbf{r},eV;V_{\text{set}},I_{\text{set}})/V}=\frac{eV\rho(\mathbf{r},eV)}{\int_{0}^{eV}\rho(\mathbf{r},\omega)d\omega}

(64)

and

\displaystyle Z(\mathbf{r},eV)\equiv\frac{g(\mathbf{r},eV;V_{\text{set}},I_{\text{set}})}{g(\mathbf{r},-eV;V_{\text{set}},I_{\text{set}})}=\frac{\rho(\mathbf{r},eV)}{\rho(\mathbf{r},-eV)}.

(65)

For simplicity, we write
g(\mathbf{r},eV;V_{\text{set}},I_{\text{set}})
as
g(\mathbf{r},eV)
in the rest of the manuscript,
abbreviating
V_{\text{set}}
and
I_{\text{set}}
in the arguments.

To compare with the experimental results, we calculate the Fourier transformation of these quantities as

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{q},\omega)\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{r}}g(\mathbf{r},\omega)e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}},

(66)

\displaystyle L(\mathbf{q},\omega)\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{r}}L(\mathbf{r},\omega)e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}},

(67)

and

\displaystyle Z(\mathbf{q},\omega)\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{r}}Z(\mathbf{r},\omega)e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}.

(68)

According to the above Fourier transformations, the spatially unmodulated part will not contribute to the QPI signals at finite
\mathbf{q}
’s in the thermodynamic limit.
However, because of the finite size of the sample or more strictly the domain where the assumption of a single impurity is valid, the experimental data of
g(\mathbf{q},\omega)
and
L(\mathbf{q},\omega)
show strong intensities for non-zero but small
\mathbf{q}
’s, as seen in Figs.
9
(a,b) and
10
(a,b).
Although these
\mathbf{q}\simeq\mathbf{0}
structures are not of our interest, in order to make a comparison with the experimental data, we have added the following Gaussian functions to Eqs. (
66
) and (
67
), respectively:

\displaystyle A_{g}\bar{\rho}_{0}(\omega)\exp(-(q_x^2+q_y^2)/q_0^2),

(69)

\displaystyle A_{L}\frac{\bar{\rho}_{0}(\omega)}{\int_{0}^{eV}\bar{\rho}_{0}(\omega)d\omega}\exp(-(q_x^2+q_y^2)/q_0^2).

(70)

Here,
\bar{\rho}_{0}(\omega)
is the average of
\rho_{0}(\mathbf{r},\omega)
over the unit cell.
We used
A_{g}=2000
,
A_{L}=0.7
, and
q_{0}=0.5/a
to fit the experimental data.
Note that since Eq. (
70
) decays rapidly with
|\mathbf{q}|
, it does not affect the behavior for
