Learning In-context 𝒏 \boldsymbol{n} -grams with Transformers: Sub- 𝒏 \boldsymbol{n} -grams Are Near-stationary Points
Aditya Varre, Gizem Yüce, Nicolas Flammarion

Abstract
Motivated by empirical observations of prolonged plateaus and stage-wise progression during training, we investigate the loss landscape of transformer models trained on in-context next-token prediction tasks. In particular, we focus on learning in-context n n -gram language models under cross-entropy loss, and establish a sufficient condition for parameter configurations to be stationary points. We then construct a set of parameter configurations for a simplified transformer model that represent k k -gram estimators (for k ⩽ n k\leqslant n ), and show that the gradient of the population loss at these solutions vanishes in the limit of infinite sequence length and parameter norm. This reveals a key property of the loss landscape: sub- n n -grams are near-stationary points of the population cross-entropy loss, offering theoretical insight into widely observed phenomena such as stage-wise learning dynamics and emergent phase transitions. These insights are further supported by numerical experiments that illustrate the learning dynamics of n n -grams, characterized by discrete transitions between near-stationary solutions.

Introduction

Transformers
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
have become central to modern machine learning, due to their capabilities such as in-context learning (ICL)
(
Brown 2020
)
—the ability of models to perform new tasks by leveraging a few examples provided within the context, without the need for parameter updates or retraining.

Recent empirical studies have revealed that the dynamics that result in in-context learning abilities often deviate from a simple monotonic decrease in loss, exhibiting complex behaviors with plateaus such as grokking

(
Power et al. 2022
)
and stage-wise transitions
(
Olsson et al. 2022
)
where the training process frequently lingers in regions of slow progress before going through a sudden phase transition after which ICL abilities are acquired. Mechanistic interpretability studies suggest that after these plateaus, specific circuits, such as induction heads
(
Olsson et al. 2022
)
, or syntactic structures
(
Chen et al. 2024a
)
, are gradually learned. This raises a natural question:

Why does training linger at plateaus before developing such abilities?

The setting of
Edelman et al. 2024
provides an avenue to take a closer look at this question with the specialized task of learning in-context
n
-grams
(
Shannon 1948
;
Chomsky 1956
;
Brown et al. 1992
)
. Here, the training unfolds in a hierarchical, phase-wise manner. It begins with the transformer making uniform predictions, progresses through unigram and bigram predictions, and potentially generalizes to higher-order
n
-grams. Figure
1
illustrates how these training phases overlap with the losses of the sub-
n
-gram estimators.

Building on this observation, we provide a theoretical foundation for why training lingers at long plateaus—the loss landscape of transformers trained on in-context sequential data exhibit stationary points aligned with sub-hierarchical or sub-syntactic solutions. These points act as intermediate solutions where gradients vanish, causing training to stagnate before transitioning to the next hierarchical level.

Concretely, this paper explores the loss landscape of transformer models trained on in-context
n
-gram language models to predict the next token.
We show a sufficient stationarity condition for the cross-entropy loss that the sub-
n
-gram constructions satisfy, shedding light on the incremental and phase-wise learning phenomena observed during training for in-context
n
-grams.
Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

In Section
3
, we provide a sufficient condition for the solutions to be the stationary points of the cross-entropy loss in the next-token prediction task when the model’s score function depends solely on a sub-sequence of the input history. This characterization provides a powerful tool for analyzing the structure of the loss landscape for various tasks, including but not limited to
n
-grams.

•

In Section
4
, we demonstrate that a set of solutions representing sub-
n
-gram estimators are near-stationary points, by verifying that they satisfy the conditions sufficient for the gradient of the population loss to converge to zero in the infinite weight and sequence length limit.

•

In Section
5
, we present empirical evidence that illustrates the structural evolution of these models during training and how transitions between training phases are in alignment with the predictions of our theory.

2.2
In-context
\boldsymbol{n}
-grams task

We start with the definition of
n
-gram language model and discuss some estimators for this setting.

The
\bm{n}
-gram Language Model.
A language model
p_{\tau}
is an
n
-gram language model if it satisfies the two key assumptions:

(a)

Markov property: The conditional probability of a token depends only on the
(n{\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}}1)
-history rather than the entire history, i.e.,

\displaystyle p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle l}|x^{\scriptscriptstyle l-1}\right)=p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle l}|{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l-1}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-n+1}\right),\text{ for }l\geqslant n.

(b)

Time Homogeneity: The transition probabilities are independent of the position in the sequence. Formally, for all
t,l\in[T]
and sequences
s^{n}\in[\mathcal{S}]^{n}
,

\displaystyle p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle l}{=}s_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}|{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l-1}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-n+1}{=}s^{\scriptscriptstyle{n{-}1}}\right)=p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle t}{=}s_{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}|{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle t-1}_{\scriptscriptstyle t-n+1}{=}s^{\scriptscriptstyle{n{-}1}}\right).

The assumptions above distinguish
n
-grams from general language models by limiting the dependency range and assuming uniform transition dynamics over time. Although these assumptions restrict their expressive power, they retain certain key characteristics of natural language, such as causal dependence on the prior context. Under these assumptions, the probability of the sequence
x^{\scriptscriptstyle T}
can be expressed using the chain rule as

\displaystyle p_{\tau}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle T})=\prod_{l\in[T]}p_{\tau}({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle l}|x^{\scriptscriptstyle l-1})=\prod_{l\in[T]}p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle l}|{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l-1}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-n+1}\right).

2.3
Disentangled Transformer Architecture

Given an input sequence
x^{\scriptscriptstyle T}{\in}[\mathcal{S}]^{*}

E:[\mathcal{S}]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

The core of the transformer is the self-attention mechanism, which allows the model to weigh different parts of the input sequence based on learned similarity scores. Given a sequence embedding
r\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}

\displaystyle\text{SA}_{(Q,K,V)}\left({r}\right)=\bm{\sigma}\left(\left(r\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{K}r^{\top}\right)\right)r\mathbf{V},

where
\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}\right)

\bm{\sigma}\left(\cdot\right)
is defined as

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}\left(x\right)[i][j]=\begin{cases}\frac{exp(x_{ij})}{\sum_{k\leqslant i}exp(x_{ik})},\quad j\leqslant i\\
0,\qquad\qquad\qquad otherwise\end{cases}.

Traditionally, the outputs of self-attention layers are added to the residual stream. For ease of interpretation and analysis, we instead consider a disentangled architecture in which the outputs of individual heads and layers are concatenated rather than summed. This framework was proposed by

3
A Sufficient Stationary Condition for Population CE on Sequences

In this section, we analyze the gradient of the population next-token cross-entropy loss for sequential data. This analysis provides fundamental insights into the training dynamics. We begin by presenting a lemma that provides a closed-form expression for the gradient.

{lem-rest}

[]

Consider any parametric model
{p}_{\theta}(.):[\mathcal{S}]^{T}\to\Delta^{\mathcal{S}{-}1}
that maps a sequence of states to a probability vector on the states. The derivative of the population cross-entropy loss
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
with respect to a parameter
\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84544pt\partial_{\theta_{i}}\mathcal{L}(\theta)

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu{=}\mkern 1.5mu\underset{p_{\tau}\sim\mathcal{P}}{\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}}\,\underset{x^{\scriptscriptstyle T}\sim p_{\tau}}{\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}}\Bigl\langle{{p}}_{\theta}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle T}){-}p_{\tau}\left(\left..\right\rvert x^{\scriptscriptstyle T}\right),{\partial_{\theta_{i}}\log{{p}_{\theta}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle T})}}\Bigr\rangle,

where
\partial_{\theta_{i}}
is the partial derivative with respect to
\theta_{i}
and
\log(\cdot)
denotes component-wise logarithm.

The lemma
3
, presented for a fixed length
T
for notational simplicity, generalizes to sequences of arbitrary length. This result extends prior analyses such as
Bietti et al. 2024
and
Makkuva et al. 2024
.

The gradient expression in Lemma
3
is the expectation of an inner product between two key terms: (a) the
prediction residual
,
{{p}}_{\theta}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle T}){-}p_{\tau}\left(\left..\right\rvert x^{\scriptscriptstyle T}\right)
, representing the error between the model’s estimate and the true next-token distribution; and (b) the
score function
, which is the gradient of the model’s logits. In the following proposition, we demonstrate that when the score function’s structure depends only on a specific sub-sequence of the input, the population loss gradient simplifies significantly.

{prop-rest}

[]

For any
{\theta_{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
\partial_{\theta=\theta_{*}}\log{\bigl.{p}_{\theta}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle T})}=g\left(p_{\tau},{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}_{\scriptscriptstyle t}\right)
, i.e., the score is solely a function of the context
p_{\tau}
and the last
T{-}t{+}1
elements of the sequence
x^{\scriptscriptstyle T}
, the gradient of the population loss
\mathcal{L}
can be written as

\displaystyle\hskip-7.11317pt\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{*})

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu{=}\mkern 1.5mu\underset{p_{\tau}\sim\mathcal{P}}{\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}}\,\underset{x^{\scriptscriptstyle T}\sim p_{\tau}}{\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}}\Bigl\langle{{p}}_{\theta_{*}}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle T}){-}p_{\tau}\left(\left..\right\rvert{{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}_{\scriptscriptstyle t}}\right),g\left(p_{\tau},{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}_{\scriptscriptstyle t}\right)\Bigr\rangle.

Furthermore, if for such
{\theta_{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the model estimates the conditional probability of the next token
p_{\tau}\left(\left..\right\rvert{{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}_{\scriptscriptstyle t}}\right)
, i.e.,
{{p}}_{\theta_{*}}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle T}){=}p_{\tau}\left(\left..\right\rvert{{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}_{\scriptscriptstyle t}}\right)
almost surely for
p_{\tau}\sim\mathcal{P}
, then
\theta_{*}
is a stationary point.

Proposition
3
provides a sufficient condition for stationarity. It reveals that if the score function
g
depends only on the context and a suffix
{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}_{\scriptscriptstyle t}
, then the gradient is no longer driven by the error against the true probability conditioned on the full sequence,
p_{\tau}(.|x^{\scriptscriptstyle T})
, but rather by the error against the probability conditioned on the shorter suffix,
p_{\tau}(.|{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}_{\scriptscriptstyle t})
. Therefore, the population loss gradient vanishes when the model correctly learns to estimate the latter.

We leverage this proposition to formally show that the population loss gradient vanishes when the model correctly learns the true
k
-gram conditional probabilities. However, the proposition is not specific to the setting of
k
-grams and holds generally for cross-entropy loss in next-token prediction tasks. A generalization of this result, which applies to models that depend on any arbitrary subset of tokens (not just a contiguous suffix), is discussed in Appendix
C.2
.

4.3
Proof Sketch

Our proof hinges on applying the stationarity result from Proposition
3
. This requires establishing two key conditions for the parameter configuration: (a) demonstrating that model’s prediction
{{p}}_{\theta_{*}^{k}}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle T})
converges to the true conditional probability
p_{\tau}(\cdot\mid{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}_{\scriptscriptstyle T-k+2})
and (b) proving that the score function depends solely on the
(k{-}1)
-history.

It is easy to see that the first condition holds asymptotically. As
c\to\infty
, the model
{{p}}_{\theta_{*}^{k}}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle T})
implements the
k
-gram estimator (see Lemma
B.1
), and as
T\to\infty
, the
k
-gram estimator converges to the conditional probability (see Lemma
H.6
in the Appendix for the formal argument). Finally, to obtain non-asymptotic bounds on the gradient, we use these asymptotic computations while carefully controlling for the perturbation caused by non-asymptotic conditions.

The main technical challenge is proving the second condition: that the score function has a restricted dependency structure. Our argument proceeds by analyzing the structure of the model’s output derivatives. The model’s prediction is a weighted sum of one-hot vectors:

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle T})=\sum_{i=1}^{T}a^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\left(i,T\right)}e_{x_{i}},

(9)

where
a^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\left(i,T\right)}
are the attention scores in the second layer for key
i
and query
T
(see Appendix
B
for details). The derivative with respect to any parameter
{\theta_{(1)},\theta_{(2)}}
in the first or second layer involves the derivatives of these attention scores:

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial p_{\bm{\theta}}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle T})}{\partial\theta_{(1)}},\frac{\partial p_{\bm{\theta}}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle T})}{\partial\theta_{(2)}}}

\displaystyle={\sum_{i=1}^{T}\frac{\partial a^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\left(i,T\right)}}{\partial r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[i]}\frac{\partial r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[i]}{\partial\theta_{(1)}}e_{x_{i}},\sum_{i=1}^{T}\frac{\partial a^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\left(i,T\right)}}{\partial\theta_{(2)}}e_{x_{i}}}.

A crucial observation is that the softmax function has a self-bounding property: the magnitude of the derivative of a softmax output is bounded by (and scales with) the output value itself. Consequently, when an attention score is close to zero, its gradient will also be close to zero. This property will play a key role in our subsequent analysis.

At the
k
-gram estimator parameterized by
\bm{\theta}_{*}^{k}

\displaystyle a^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\left(i,T\right)}\approx\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|\mathsf{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}^{k}|}\ ,\quad&\text{for }i\in\mathsf{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}^{k},\\

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{\bm{\theta}}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle T})}{\partial\theta_{(2)}},\frac{\partial p_{\bm{\theta}}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle T})}{\partial\theta_{(1)}}\bigg|_{\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{*}^{k}}

\displaystyle{\approx}

4.4
Extensions and Perspectives

Beyond contiguous history.

Our main analysis focuses on contiguous
k
-gram histories (suffixes). However, as discussed previously in section
2.2
, the dependency on the last
k
tokens are not more important than the other
n{-}k
for the
n
-gram data generating process under a uniform prior on the transition matrices. Our framework can be extended to show that estimators matching
arbitrary
subsets of the
(n{-}1)
-history (e.g., matching only the
(\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}i)
-token for some
i>1
as a counterpart of the bigram estimator) also correspond to stationary points under certain assumptions. See Appendix
F.2
for details.

Towards general transformer architecture.

Our parameter construction, techniques and methodology extend naturally to a general transformer architecture. First, positional encoding, which was not explicitly used in the simplified model, can be incorporated using one-hot positional encoding. The concatenation of the attention head output and residual connections can be replaced with the addition. Additionally, the value matrix in the second layer can be incorporated in the architecture. However, in this case, the transformer’s output would no longer be in
\Delta^{\mathcal{S}-1}
, requiring normalization via softmax at the end.
This restriction on the value matrix can also be alleviated by using an MLP layer to approximate the logarithm (see Appendix
F.1
).

On the Emergence of Syntactic Structure.

Consider the behavior of a gradient-based method at a sub-n-gram, say
\bm{\theta}_{*}^{k}
. As it is a stationary point, training remains at this point for a prolonged period, leading to a plateau in the training curve. However, as training progresses, the model eventually escapes due to landscape curvature or stochastic noise, allowing it to learn a new syntactic structure— attending to
(-k)
-token—before reaching the next stationary point. This phenomenon is general and has been empirically reported—emergence of a syntactic structure
(
Chen et al. 2024a
;
Wei et al. 2022
)
, phase transitions
(
Olsson et al. 2022
;
Edelman et al. 2024
)
.
By carefully analyzing the loss landscape of a relevant yet simple in-context task, we demonstrate that the stationary points correspond to underlying syntactic structures. Consequently, our work provides insights into why these syntactic structures emerge following extended plateaus.

Limitations.

Our theoretical results hold in the asymptotic limit of infinite sequence length (
T\to\infty
) and parameter scale (
c\to\infty
). The dependence on
c
is mild, as the gradient decays exponentially for finite
c
.
However, there is an inherent difficulty in moving beyond the assumption of infinite sequence length. Existing works on theoretical analysis of learning in-context
n
-gram like sequences with transformers, such as
Rajaraman et al. 2024
and
Nichani et al. 2024
, also rely on this assumption. It is even unclear which estimator transformers learn at finite sequence lengths, even at the end of training. This makes it particularly challenging to determine what transformers exactly compute during intermediate stages of training.

5
Experimental Evaluation

In this section, we perform experiments on the disentangled transformer introduced in the previous section to examine the stage-wise learning behavior and analyze the different solutions the transformer learns during different stages of training. The code is available at
https://github.com/tml-epfl/sub-n-grams-are-stationary
.

Experimental Setup.

We train our model on data generated from a trigram (
n=3
) language model over a vocabulary of size
\mathcal{S}=5
. Each in-context sequence has a length of
T=32
, and the transition probabilities are drawn from a uniform Dirichlet prior,
\text{Dir}(\alpha\mathbf{1})
, with
\alpha=0.5
. The model is the two-layer simplified Transformer analyzed in our theory, with two heads in the first layer and an embedding dimension of
d=5
. We use one-hot token embeddings and train for
2^{14}
iterations using the Adam optimizer with a constant learning rate of
0.01
and a batch size of
128
. No weight decay is used. The test loss is evaluated on a separate set of
2^{16}
sequences.

Discussion.

To accurately predict the next token, the model needs to attend to the previous

n-1=2
tokens in the sequence. Figure
3(a)
shows that learning occurs in distinct phases, where the model remains in a plateau for an extended period before quickly jumping to the next one. In Figure
3(b)
, we illustrate the evolution of the attention maps from both heads in the first layer at various plateaus during training, providing a fine-grained view of the structure of the model at these stages.
Initially, the attention maps are uniform, and the model does not consider token history in its predictions. Upon reaching the first plateau, both attention heads focus on the previous token (the

(\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}1)
-token), and the model behaves like a bigram estimator at this stationary point. In the later stages of training, the second attention head learns to shift its focus to the

(\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}2)
-token, transforming the model into a trigram estimator. The same phenomenon holds for the general attention-only transformers, see Fig
4
.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we investigate the problem of learning in-context
n
-grams with transformers, specifically focusing on a simplified yet insightful setting to gain a deeper understanding of the dynamics of complex large language models. We first constructed specific parameter configurations within a simplified Transformer architecture that provably implement sub-
n
-gram estimators. Our main theoretical result then establishes that these configurations correspond to near-stationary points of the population cross-entropy loss. This finding creates a mechanistic link between the geometry of the loss landscape and the empirical observation of training plateaus corresponding to sub-
n
-grams: the model’s learning trajectory naturally pauses at these regions of vanishingly small gradient, which represent mastery of simpler, sub-syntactic skills, before eventually escaping to learn more complex dependencies.

Our analysis provides a foundational step, and several exciting avenues for future research remain. The theoretical results are derived under idealized conditions, namely for the population loss, in the limit of infinite sequence length, and without regularization. Extending these findings to the finite-sample regime and incorporating practical considerations like finite context windows and the effect of weight decay is an important direction. Furthermore, our work focuses on the static properties of the loss landscape. A complementary and crucial line of inquiry involves analyzing the training dynamics themselves. Characterizing how stochastic gradient-based methods navigate this structured landscape to escape plateaus and transition between solutions, as explored by
Nichani et al. 2024
, presents a key challenge for future research.

\displaystyle r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[i]=\sum_{h=0}^{k-1}s_{{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle i-h}}^{h}+\mathcal{O}(ie^{-c})\cdot\mathbf{1}.

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Q}_{2})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{2}r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[i]

\displaystyle=c\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{S}}s_{j}^{h-1}(s_{j}^{h})^{\top}\left[\sum_{h=0}^{k-1}s_{{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle i-h}}^{h}+\mathcal{O}(ie^{-c})\cdot\mathbf{1}\right],

\displaystyle=c\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}s_{{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle i-h}}^{h-1}+\mathcal{O}(ie^{-c})\cdot\mathbf{1}.

\displaystyle\left\langle{r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[t]},{(\mathbf{Q}_{2})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{2}r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[i]}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=c\left\langle{\sum_{h=0}^{k-1}s_{{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle t-h}}^{h}},{\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}s_{{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle i-h}}^{h-1}}\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(te^{-c}),

\displaystyle=c\left\langle{\sum_{h=1}^{k}s_{{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle t+1-h}}^{h-1}},{\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}s_{{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle i-h}}^{h-1}}\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(te^{-c}),

Now we use the fact that the embeddings

are orthogonal for

or

\displaystyle\left\langle{r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[t]},{(\mathbf{Q}_{2})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{2}r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[i]}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=c\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}\left\langle{s_{{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle t+1-h}}^{h-1}},{s_{{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle i-h}}^{h-1}}\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(te^{-c}),

\displaystyle=c\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}\mathbbm{1}\{{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle i-h}={x}_{\scriptscriptstyle t+1-h}\}+\mathcal{O}(te^{-c}).

If the

\displaystyle{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{t}\exp{\left\langle{\mathbf{K}_{2}r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[j]},{\mathbf{Q}_{2}r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[t]}\right\rangle}}\leqslant|\mathsf{M}_{t}|\exp\{(k-1)c\}+(t-|\mathsf{M}_{t}|)\exp\{(k-2)c\}.

\displaystyle\text{For }i\in\mathsf{M}_{t}^{k},\quad a^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\left(i,t\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp{(k-1)}c^{2}}{{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{t}\exp{\left\langle{\mathbf{K}_{2}r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[j]},{\mathbf{Q}_{2}r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[t]}\right\rangle}}}\geqslant\frac{\exp{(k-1)}c^{2}}{|\mathsf{M}_{t}|\exp\{(k-1)c\}+(t-|\mathsf{M}_{t}|)\exp\{(k-2)c\}},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathsf{M}_{t}|}-a^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\left(i,t\right)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\exp{(k-2)c}}{|\mathsf{M}_{t}|\exp{(k-1)c}}=\mathcal{O}(\exp\{{-}c\})\quad\text{o.w.}.

\displaystyle\text{For }i\in\mathsf{M}_{t}^{k},\quad a^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\left(i,t\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathsf{M}_{t}^{k}|}\ -\frac{t\mathcal{O}(\exp\{{-}c\})}{|\mathsf{M}_{t}^{k}|^{2}},

This completes the proof of the lemma.

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Q}_{2})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{c}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{S}}\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}s_{j}^{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}0}(s_{j}^{h})^{\top},

F.1
General Transformer Architecture

The results presented in the main text are given for a simplified transformer architecture. In this section, we discuss how the results can be extended to a general transformer architecture. We provide brief proof sketches for two extensions: adding a value matrix to the second layer and replacing the concatenation of head embeddings and the first-layer skip connection.

Token and position embeddings.

First we define some block embeddings in a dimension
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and later lift them into the dimension of the transformers, i.e.,
nd
and sequences upto length
T
.

\displaystyle\text{Token embeddings}\to s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{\mathcal{S}-1}\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}

\displaystyle\text{Position embeddings}\to p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{T-1}\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}.

The embeddings are mutually orthogonal, i.e.,

\displaystyle s_{i}\perp s_{j},\text{ for all }i\neq j\in[N].

\displaystyle p_{i}\perp p_{j},\text{ for all }i\neq j\in[T].

\displaystyle p_{i}\perp s_{j},\text{ for all }i\in[T],\,\,j\in[N].

For
h\in[k+1]
, we denote the
s_{i}^{h},p_{i}^{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}
, the lift of the block embeddings which are defined as the following,

\displaystyle s_{i}^{h}=x\begin{bmatrix}\overbrace{0_{d}\,\,0_{d}\,\,\cdot\,\,\cdot}^{{\text{h blocks}}}&s_{i}&\cdot&\cdot\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle p_{i}^{h}=\begin{bmatrix}\underbrace{0_{d}\,\,0_{d}\,\,\cdot\,\cdot\,}_{{\text{h blocks}}}&p_{i}&\cdot&\cdot\end{bmatrix}

The embedding layer maps to
q_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times nd}

where each row
i
is the embedding of
i^{\text{th}}
element in the sequence along with its positional embedding, i.e.,
q_{0}[i]=s_{x_{i}}^{0}+p_{i}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}
.

The attention layers.

The first layer has
k
attention heads and also has a skip connection.
For a head
h\in[k]
, let
Q_{1}^{(h)},K_{1}^{(h)},V_{1}^{(h)}
denote the query, key and value matrices. The forward pass on
q_{0}
writes

\displaystyle r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}=r_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}+\sum_{h=1}^{k}\bm{\sigma}\left(r_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}~(\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{(h)})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1}^{(h)}~r_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\top}\right)~r_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}~\left(\mathbf{V}_{1}^{((h))}\right)^{\top},

Here
\bm{\sigma}(.)
is a row-wise softmax operator with a causal masking. Note that here we are adding in comparison to the simplified architecture where we are concatenating.

The second layer has just one head and also no skip connection (the skip connection can be included and the value matrix should be scaled appropriately so that it is ignored after normalization). Let
K_{2},Q_{2},V_{2}
be the key, query and value matrices of the second layer. The forward pass for the second layer writes

\displaystyle r_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}=\bm{\sigma}\left(r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}~(\mathbf{Q}_{2})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{2}~r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\top}\right)~r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}~\left(\mathbf{V}_{2}\right)^{\top}.

Now that
r_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}
is not row wise normalized and the result does not give an output vector on the simplex, we have to normalize them using a softmax operator, i.e.,
\bm{\sigma}(r_{\scriptscriptstyle 2})
to get the final output. In this case, the counting algorithm (MLE) implemented by the attention-only transformer previously discussed cannot compute logits of the probability
\log{p_{\tau}}
, so that softmax normalization over
\log{p_{\tau}}
gives the true probability distribution
p_{\tau}
.
Hence, an MLP layer is used to compute the logits.

An MLP layer.

As discussed above, there is an MLP layer that computes the logarithm of its input. Let
M
be the set of parameters of the MLP layer and
m
be the function implemented,

does component wise logarithm

for some small epsilon.

Writing it together, the forward pass of the transformer writes,

\displaystyle r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}

\displaystyle=r_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}+\sum_{h=1}^{k}\bm{\sigma}\left(r_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}~(\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{(h)})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1}^{(h)}~r_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{\top}\right)~r_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}~\left(\mathbf{V}_{1}^{((h))}\right)^{\top},

\displaystyle r_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}

\displaystyle=\bm{\sigma}\left(r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}~(\mathbf{Q}_{2})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{2}~r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\top}\right)~r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}~\left(\mathbf{V}_{2}\right)^{\top},

\displaystyle r_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}^{+}

\displaystyle=m(Ur_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}),

\displaystyle{p}_{\bm{\theta}}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle t})

\displaystyle=\bm{\sigma}(r_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}^{+})

This model generalizes the simplified version by adding a value matrix in the second layer, moving beyond simple concatenation for head embeddings and the first-layer skip connection, and explicitly using positional encoding. While the simplified model’s representation results extend to this generalized version, proving that the gradients vanish requires additional work, particularly for the value matrices.

For the
t^{\text{th}}
token, the output after the second layer of the transformer can be written as,

\displaystyle r_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}[t]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{t}a^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\left(i,t\right)}\,\mathbf{V}_{2}r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[i],

where the attention scores in the second layer

\displaystyle a^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\left(i,t\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp{\left\langle{\mathbf{K}_{2}r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[i]},{\mathbf{Q}_{2}r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[t]}\right\rangle}}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{t}\exp{\left\langle{\mathbf{K}_{2}r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[j]},{\mathbf{Q}_{2}r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[t]}\right\rangle}}.

The final output probabilities are given by

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle t})=Ur_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}[t]=\sum_{i=1}^{t}a^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\left(i,t\right)}Ur_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}[i]=\sum_{i=1}^{t}a^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\left(i,t\right)}e_{x_{i}}.

Here, we show that the gradient with respect to
\mathbf{V}_{2}
vanishes. The others follow the same pattern as the simplified model and will be not be precisely computed here. The partial derivative with respect to gradient gives us,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial r_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}[t]}{\partial\mathbf{V}_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{t}a^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}_{\scriptscriptstyle\left(i,t\right)}\,I_{\mathcal{S}}\otimes r_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}[i],=I_{\mathcal{S}}\otimes\bar{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}_{\scriptscriptstyle t}

Define

\displaystyle(Q_{1}^{(h)})^{\top}K_{1}^{(h)}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}c_{h}\sum\limits_{l=h}^{T-1}p_{l-h}p_{l}^{\top}\text{ where }\quad\text{for}~h\in[k-1]\\

(33)

\displaystyle(Q_{2})^{\top}K_{2}

\displaystyle=c\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{S}}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{N}}s_{j}^{h-1}(s_{j}^{h})^{\top},

(34)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{S}}e_{j}(s_{j}^{0})^{\top}.

Intuitively, this is just an expansion on simplified transformer with lifting in the first layer and multiplication with value made explicit. Recalling

Note that we only consider estimators conditioned on the contiguous suffices. Consider again the simple example of bigram, which is conditioning on a single element in the
n
-history. There can be many such estimators, for example, conditioning on the first element in the
n
-history
{p}(x_{n}=.|x_{n-1})

n

To generalize this, we define

. For any

, we define a subsequence

\mathcal{N}
-history and the

-MLE estimator is given by,

\displaystyle\frac{\sum\limits_{l=0}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{N}}={x}^{\scriptscriptstyle t+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{N}}\}\,\mathbbm{1}\{x_{l}=i\}}{\sum\limits_{l=0}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{N}}={x}^{\scriptscriptstyle t+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{N}}\}}.

which compares the
\mathcal{N}
-history of the token

with the
\mathcal{N}
-history of all the tokens in the sequence.

-history, it must compare the
(-h)
-token of token
i
with the
(-h)
-token of the token
(t{+}1)
(not
t
) for

. This essentially requires comparing the
(-h)
-token of
i
with the
(-(h{-}1))
-token of
t
. As a result, an induction head must attend to the
(-(h{-}1))
-token in the first layer. When
\mathcal{N}
forms a suffix, this step isn’t explicitly necessary since

will also be in
\mathcal{N}
. Otherwise, it must be computed explicitly using additional heads or changing the attention matrix specifically depending on the last token.

When the inputs are of fixed length the later approach could be used. We specify the construction first and then give the intuition on why it would work.

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Q}_{2})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{c}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{S}}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{N}}s_{j}^{h}(s_{j}^{h})^{\top},

(36a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{c}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{S}}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{N}}s_{j}^{h}(s_{j}^{h})^{\top},

(36b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{1}^{(h)}

\displaystyle=c\sum\limits_{l=h}^{T{-}2}\mathsf{e}_{\scriptscriptstyle l{-}h}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}\left(\mathsf{e}_{\scriptscriptstyle l}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}\right)^{\top}+\mathsf{e}_{\scriptscriptstyle T{-}h{+}1}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}\left(\mathsf{e}_{\scriptscriptstyle T{-}1}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}\right)^{\top}~\text{for}~h\in\mathcal{N},

(36c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{1}^{(h)}

The main modification is in the attention matrix of the first layer, i.e., now head

but

.
The last token acts as a special token

and the attention matrix is modified accordingly. The gradients of the heads will vanish similar to the proof of Theorem

\displaystyle=p_{j}\mathrm{vec}\left(K\,q_{j}q_{t}^{\top}\right)^{\top}-p_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}\mathrm{vec}\left(Q\,q_{k}q_{t}^{\top}\right)^{\top},

\displaystyle=p_{j}\mathrm{vec}\left(K(q_{j}-\widehat{q})q_{t}^{\top}\right)^{\top}.

Substituting the following derivates,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{j}}{\partial K}

\displaystyle=p_{j}\mathrm{vec}\left(Qq_{t}(q_{j}-\widehat{q})^{\top}\right)^{\top},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{j}}{\partial Q}

\displaystyle=p_{j}\mathrm{vec}\left(K(q_{j}-\widehat{q})q_{t}^{\top}\right)^{\top}.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q_{t}^{+}}{\partial K}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\left(Vq_{j}\right)\otimes\frac{\partial K}{\partial p_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}p_{j}\left(Vq_{j}\right)\otimes\mathrm{vec}\left(Qq_{t}(q_{j}-\widehat{q})^{\top}\right)^{\top},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q_{t}^{+}}{\partial Q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\left(Vq_{j}\right)\otimes\frac{\partial p_{j}}{\partial Q}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}p_{j}\left(Vq_{j}\right)\otimes\mathrm{vec}\left(K(q_{j}-\widehat{q})q_{t}^{\top}\right)^{\top},

Coming to the derivatives wrt
q_{i}
, we have,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{j}}{\partial q_{i}}

\displaystyle=p_{j}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{j=i\}\left(K^{\top}Qq_{t}\right)^{\top}+\mathbbm{1}\{i=t\}(Q^{\top}Kq_{j})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt-p_{i}p_{j}\left(K^{\top}Qq_{t}\right)^{\top}-\mathbbm{1}\{i=t\}p_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}(Q^{\top}Kq_{k})^{\top}.

Writing the above expression on case by case basis, we get,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p_{j}}{\partial q_{i}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}p_{t}(1-p_{t})\left(K^{\top}Qq_{t}\right)^{\top}+p_{j}\left(Q^{\top}K(q_{j}-\widehat{q})\right)^{\top},\text{ when }i=j=t,\\
p_{j}(1-p_{j})\left(K^{\top}Qq_{t}\right)^{\top},\text{ when }i=j\neq t,\\
-p_{i}p_{j}\left(K^{\top}Qq_{t}\right)^{\top},\text{ when }i\neq j\quad\text{and}\quad i\neq t,\\
-p_{t}p_{j}\left(K^{\top}Qq_{t}\right)^{\top}+p_{j}\left(Q^{\top}K(q_{j}-\widehat{q})\right)^{\top},\text{ when }i=t\quad\text{and}\quad i\neq j,\\
\end{cases}

First computing the derivative wrt to
q_{i}
for
i\neq t
, we get,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q_{t}^{+}}{\partial q_{i}}

\displaystyle=p_{i}\,V+\sum_{j=1}^{t}\left(Vq_{j}\right)\otimes\frac{\partial p_{j}}{\partial q_{i}},

\displaystyle=p_{i}\,V+p_{i}\left(Vq_{i}\right)\otimes\left(K^{\top}Qq_{t}\right)^{\top}-p_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{t}p_{j}\left(Vq_{j}\right)\otimes\left(K^{\top}Qq_{t}\right)^{\top},

\displaystyle=p_{i}\,V+p_{i}\left(Vq_{i}\right)\otimes\left(K^{\top}Qq_{t}\right)^{\top}-p_{i}\left(V\widehat{q}\right)\otimes\left(K^{\top}Qq_{t}\right)^{\top},

\displaystyle=p_{i}\,V+p_{i}\left(V(q_{i}-\widehat{q})\right)\otimes\left(K^{\top}Qq_{t}\right)^{\top}.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q_{t}^{+}}{\partial q_{i}}

\displaystyle=p_{t}\,V+p_{t}\left(Vq_{t}\right)\otimes\left(K^{\top}Qq_{t}\right)^{\top}-p_{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}p_{j}\left(Vq_{j}\right)\left(K^{\top}Qq_{t}\right)^{\top}+\sum_{j=1}^{t}p_{j}\left(Vq_{j}\right)\otimes\left(Q^{\top}K(q_{j}-\widehat{q})\right)^{\top},

\displaystyle=p_{t}\,V+p_{t}\left(V(q_{t}-\widehat{q})\right)\otimes\left(K^{\top}Qq_{t}\right)^{\top}+\sum_{j=1}^{t}p_{j}\left(Vq_{j}\right)\otimes\left(Q^{\top}K(q_{j}-\widehat{q})\right)^{\top}.

This proves the lemma.

Appendix H
Higher Order Markov Chain

This section first recalls key properties of higher-order Markov chains and their stationary distributions. We then leverage these properties to establish that the
k
-gram estimator has a limit as the sequence length,
T
, approaches infinity.

Definition H.1

(Higher Order Markov Process)
.

A Markov process
p_{\tau}
of order
k
generates a sequence of random variables
{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2},\ldots\in\left[\mathcal{S}\right]
such that the conditional distribution of
{x}_{\scriptscriptstyle t+1}
given
x^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
depends only on
{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle t}_{\scriptscriptstyle t-k+1}
, i.e., for any
t\geqslant k
,

\displaystyle p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle t+1}\,\bigg|\,x^{\scriptscriptstyle t}\right)=p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle t+1}\,\bigg|\,{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle t}_{\scriptscriptstyle t-k+1}\right).

Now, we define the transition tensor with is given by
p_{\tau}
.

Definition H.2

(Transition Tensor)
.

The transition tensor of a Markov process of order
k
is a
k
-dimensional tensor
\mathcal{P}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}}\coloneqq p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}=i_{k+1}\,\bigg|\,x^{\scriptscriptstyle k}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right).

Now, we lift the process into the higher dimension and write it as a simple Markov process of order
1
. We create a Markov chain
{\Xi}
on the space
\left[S\right]^{k}
of order
1
Markov chain with transition matrix
\mathcal{T}
. Index the states by
{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\in\left[S\right]^{k}
. Now the transition probabilities for this are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\left({Y}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\bigg|{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right)=\begin{cases}\neq 0\quad\text{ if }Y^{k}=({i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2},{i}_{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}),\text{ for some }{i}_{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}\in\left[S\right],\\
=0\quad\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}.

The transition probabilities are explicitly given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\left({i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\bigg|{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right)=\mathcal{P}_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}}.

Stationary distribution.

Let
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|^{k}}
denote the stationary distribution of the chain
\Xi
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\pi^{\top}\mathcal{T}=\pi^{\top}.

In the summation form , for the coordinate indexed by
{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}
, we get that,

\displaystyle\sum_{{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}}\,\pi_{{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}}\cdot\mathcal{T}\left({i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\,\bigg|\,{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right)=\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}.

Note that
\mathcal{T}\left({i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\,\bigg|\,{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right)=0
whenever
{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\neq{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}
. Using this, we get,

\displaystyle\sum_{{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}}\,\pi_{{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}}\cdot\mathcal{T}\left({i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\,\bigg|\,{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}}\,\pi_{{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}}\cdot\mathcal{T}\left({i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\,\bigg|\,{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right)\left(\mathbbm{1}\biggl\{{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\neq{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\biggr\}+\mathbbm{1}\biggl\{{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\biggr\}\right),

\displaystyle=\sum_{{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}}\,\pi_{{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}}\cdot\mathcal{T}\left({i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\,\bigg|\,{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right)\mathbbm{1}\biggl\{{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\biggr\},

\displaystyle=\sum_{{j}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}\pi_{\left({j}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\right)}\mathcal{T}\left({i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\,\bigg|\,\left({j}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\right)\right),

\displaystyle=\sum_{{i}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}}\mathcal{T}\left({i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\,\bigg|\,{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right).

Using this expansion, we get the following stationarity condition,

\displaystyle\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{i}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}}\mathcal{T}\left({i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\,\bigg|\,{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right).

H.1
Generating Sequences with The Markov Chain
\Xi

In this subsection, we show the subsequences of length
k
follow the stationary distribution when generated from the Markov Chain
\Xi
. To generate sequences from the Markov Chain
\Xi
, we do the following generation,

a)

Generate a sequence of length
k
sampling from the stationary distribution
\pi
(say
{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}
)

b)

Sample the next token from the distribution
\mathcal{P}_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k},(.)}
say
{i}_{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}

c)

After generating a sequence
{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
of length
t>k
, the next token is generated by the sampling from the distribution
\mathcal{P}_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle t}_{\scriptscriptstyle t-k+1},(.)}

Lemma H.3

(Stationarity of
k
-tuples)
.

Let
x^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
be the random variable representing the sequences generated from
\Xi
, the distribution of subsequence
{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}
of length
k
is given by
\pi
.

Proof

We will prove this using induction. For the base case, observe that for
l=k
, the value is sampled from
\pi
by construction. Use the induction hypothesis, any
k
length subsequence of
x^{\scriptscriptstyle l}
obeys the law given by
\pi
. It remains to show that
{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l{+}1}_{\scriptscriptstyle l{-}k{+}2}
are distributed according to
\pi
.

\displaystyle p_{\tau}\left({x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l{+}1}_{\scriptscriptstyle l{-}k{+}2}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l{+}1}_{\scriptscriptstyle l{-}k{+}2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}}p_{\tau}\left(x^{\scriptscriptstyle l{+}1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l{+}1}\right),

\displaystyle=\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}}p_{\tau}\left(x^{\scriptscriptstyle l}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right)p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}={i}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}\;\bigg|\;x^{\scriptscriptstyle l}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right)

Using the Markov property of the sequence generation, we have,

\displaystyle p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}={i}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}\;\bigg|\;x^{\scriptscriptstyle l}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right)=p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}={i}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}\;\bigg|\;{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}\right).

Substuting this expression, we have,

\displaystyle p_{\tau}\left({x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l{+}1}_{\scriptscriptstyle l{-}k{+}2}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l{+}1}_{\scriptscriptstyle l{-}k{+}2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}}p_{\tau}\left(x^{\scriptscriptstyle l}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right)\mathcal{P}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}={i}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}\;\bigg|\;{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}\right),

\displaystyle=\sum_{{i}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}}\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l-k}}p_{\tau}\left(x^{\scriptscriptstyle l}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right)p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}={i}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}\;\bigg|\;{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}\right),

\displaystyle=\sum_{{i}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}}\left[\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l-k}}p_{\tau}\left(x^{\scriptscriptstyle l}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right)\right]p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}={i}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}\;\bigg|\;{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}\right).

Using the fact that
\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l-k}}p_{\tau}\left(x^{\scriptscriptstyle l}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right)=p_{\tau}\left({x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}\right)
. Using the induction hypothesis, we have that
p_{\tau}\left({x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}\right)=\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}}
.

\displaystyle p_{\tau}\left({x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l{+}1}_{\scriptscriptstyle l{-}k{+}2}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l{+}1}_{\scriptscriptstyle l{-}k{+}2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{i}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}}\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}}p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}={i}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}\;\bigg|\;{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}\right),

\displaystyle=\sum_{{i}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}}\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}}\mathcal{T}\left({i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+2}\;\bigg|\;{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}\right).

Using the Eq. (
37
),

\displaystyle p_{\tau}\left({x}^{\scriptscriptstyle l{+}1}_{\scriptscriptstyle l{-}k{+}2}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l{+}1}_{\scriptscriptstyle l{-}k{+}2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{i}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}}\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}}\mathcal{T}\left({i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+2}\;\bigg|\;{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+1}\right),

\displaystyle=\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle l-k+2}}.

This proves the hypothesis for length
l+1
. Hence, by induction, the hypothesis holds for any length.

Lemma H.4

(Stationarity of sub-
k
-tuples)
.

For any
t\geqslant 0
and
l\leqslant k
, we have the following shift invariant property for the marginals,

\displaystyle p_{\tau}\left(x^{\scriptscriptstyle l}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right)=p_{\tau}\left({x}^{\scriptscriptstyle t+l-1}_{\scriptscriptstyle t}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right)

Proof

Using Lemma
H.3
, we have that the distribution of the subsequence
{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle t+k-1}_{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is given by
\pi
for any
t\geqslant 0
, i.e.,

\displaystyle p_{\tau}\left(x^{\scriptscriptstyle k}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right)=p_{\tau}\left({x}^{\scriptscriptstyle t+k-1}_{\scriptscriptstyle t}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right)=\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}}.

Summing the above expression over all possible values of
{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}
, we get,

\displaystyle\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}}p_{\tau}\left(x^{\scriptscriptstyle k}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right)=\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}}p_{\tau}\left({x}^{\scriptscriptstyle t+k-1}_{\scriptscriptstyle t}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right)=\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}}\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}}.

We know that

\displaystyle\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}}p_{\tau}\left(x^{\scriptscriptstyle k}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{\tau}\left(x^{\scriptscriptstyle l}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right),

\displaystyle\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}}p_{\tau}\left({x}^{\scriptscriptstyle t+k-1}_{\scriptscriptstyle t}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{\tau}\left({x}^{\scriptscriptstyle t+l-1}_{\scriptscriptstyle t}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right)

This proves the lemma.

H.2
Lower-order Conditional Probabilities

The following lemma show that the lower order conditional probabilities are time homogenous and are given by the stationary distribution
\pi
.

Lemma H.5
.

For the sequences generated by the markov chain
\Xi
, for
l\leqslant k
, we have,

\displaystyle p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle T+1}=.\,\,\bigg|\,\,{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}_{\scriptscriptstyle T-l+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right)=p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}=.\,\,\bigg|\,\,x^{\scriptscriptstyle l}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right)=\frac{\sum\limits_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k{-}l}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}}{\sum\limits_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k{-}l}}\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}}}

Proof

For
l=k
, this holds due the Markov property of order k and

\displaystyle p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle T+1}=.\,\,\bigg|\,\,{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}_{\scriptscriptstyle T-k+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right)=p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}=.\,\,\bigg|\,\,x^{\scriptscriptstyle k}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\right).

Now, for
l<k
, we have,

\displaystyle p_{\tau}\left({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle T+1}={i}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}\,\,\bigg|\,\,{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}_{\scriptscriptstyle T-l+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{\tau}\left({x}^{\scriptscriptstyle T+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle T-l+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}\right)}{p_{\tau}\left({x}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}_{\scriptscriptstyle T-l+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right)},

Using the shift-invariant property of the marginals, we have that,

\displaystyle p_{\tau}\left({x}^{\scriptscriptstyle T+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle T-l+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{\tau}\left(x^{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}\right)=\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+2}}\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}},

\displaystyle p_{\tau}\left({x}^{\scriptscriptstyle T}_{\scriptscriptstyle T-l+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{\tau}\left(x^{\scriptscriptstyle l}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}\right)=\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}}\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}}.

This proves the lemma.

Consistency of the sub-k counting estimators.

Here, we use the ergodic properties of the chain
\Xi
to show that the lower order estimators convergence as

.

Lemma H.6
.

Consider the
l{+}1
-gram estimator
{\widehat{p}}\,_{l}
for
0<l\leqslant k-1
, then as
T\to\infty
,
{\widehat{p}}\,_{l}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle T})\to p_{\tau}(.|x^{\scriptscriptstyle l})
.

Proof

Using the ergodicity of the chain
\Xi

(
Penev 1991
)
, the
k{+}1-
gram estimator converges to the stationary distribution as it equivalent to counting the empirical frequency on the chain of higher order.
Now we write the
l-

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{l}({i}_{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}|{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle k-l+1})=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle j+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle j-l+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle k-l+1}\}}{\sum_{j=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle j}_{\scriptscriptstyle j-l+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle k-l+1}\}}

Now we can write the following expression as

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle j+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle j-l+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle k-l+1}\}=\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}\sum_{j=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle j+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle j-k+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\},

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle j}_{\scriptscriptstyle j-k+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle k-l+1}\}=\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}}\sum_{j=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle j}_{\scriptscriptstyle j-k+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}\}.

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle j+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle j-k+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\}}{T}\,\,\overset{t\to\infty}{\rightarrow}\,\,\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}},

\displaystyle\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle j+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle j-k+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\}}{T}\,\,\overset{t\to\infty}{\rightarrow}\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle j+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle j-l+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle k-l+1}\}}{\sum_{j=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle j}_{\scriptscriptstyle j-l+1}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle k-l+1}\}}=\frac{\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}}{\sum_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle l}}\pi_{{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}}}=p_{\tau}({x}_{\scriptscriptstyle l+1}={i}_{\scriptscriptstyle k+1}|x^{\scriptscriptstyle l}={i}^{\scriptscriptstyle k}_{\scriptscriptstyle k-l+1})

The last equality flows from the lemma
H.5
