Age (juvenile to aged) and sex effects on in vitro CYP2A6 enzyme activity in African green monkeys using nicotine and coumarin
Authors: Weilong Gu, Rachel F. Tyndale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
CYP2A6 is the principal human enzyme metabolizing coumarin and nicotine; it demonstrates wide variation from genetics and environmental regulation, complicating the determination of human developmental profile and sex-related differences. African green monkeys (AGMs), with comparable CYP2A6 activity to humans, of both sexes across 5 developmental stages (N = 56) were used to investigate age and sex effects on CYP2A6 activity using 7-hydroxycoumarin formation from coumarin, and cotinine formation from nicotine. Age and sex differences were determined using two-way ANOVAs and post hoc tests. Age impacted microsomal protein per gram liver (determined by cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase activity). Age and sex differences, with interactions, were observed in liver and body weight, whereby older AGMs and males were heavier for both. Pooled microsomes per age-sex group were used to determine the kinetic parameters for 7-hydroxycoumarin (overall Km = 1.5 ± 0.3 μM, Vmax = 0.34 ± 0.06 nmol/min/mg) and cotinine (overall Km = 37.4 ± 8.0 μM, Vmax = 1.36 ± 0.23 nmol/min/mg) formation. Age impacted the velocity of 7-hydroxycoumarin and cotinine formation (individual AGMs assessed at substrate concentrations at ∼Km and ∼10 × Km), at per milligram of microsomal protein, per gram liver, and per whole liver normalized by body weight, whereby juveniles and adolescents had higher velocities. Both age and sex impacted 7-hydroxycoumarin and cotinine formation per whole liver, but in contrast, older AGMs and males had higher velocities. In conclusion, age but not sex impacted CYP2A6 activity, which was higher in younger AGMs for essentially all denominators except following extrapolation to the whole liver, where activity was influenced by larger livers in adults and males.Significance StatementMicrosomal CYP2A6 activity was higher in younger African green monkeys (AGMs), with no sex differences. Once extrapolated to the whole liver, representing the total rate of metabolism, the velocity was faster in older AGMs and males, primarily due to age and sex differences in liver weight. There were significant age differences in CYP2A6 activity, where younger AGMs had higher velocity per milligram-microsomal protein, gram liver, and whole liver normalized to body weight levels, while older and male AGMs had higher whole liver velocity.

Introduction

CYP2A6 metabolizes many natural products and clinical drugs, including efavirenz, letrozole, and tegafur.1 Coumarin 7-hydroxylation is an established probe pathway for CYP2A6 activity.2 In addition, nicotine is metabolically inactivated (∼70%–80%) to cotinine, primarily by hepatic CYP2A6 (80%–90%) with a minor role (10%–20%) for CYP2B6.3,4 As a result, variation in CYP2A6 importantly alters smoking behaviors, response to cessation therapies, and tobacco-related diseases.5,6

CYP2A6 is highly variable among individuals, largely due to genetic variation in CYP2A6.1,7 Human CYP2A6 activity is also associated with other factors, including age and sex.8 Human CYP2A6 can be induced by estrogen,9 increasing CYP2A6 activity in women from adolescence to menopause, and by the use of estrogen-containing medications.10,11 However, whether CYP2A6 activity exhibits age/developmental and sex differences is unclear, and studies are inconsistent, likely because of the complex interaction of genetic, hormonal, and environmental factors. For example, there were age differences in cotinine formation velocity in one human liver bank (ages 0–87 years) but not another (ages 2–64 years).12,13 Likewise, nicotine clearance was higher in adults compared with elderly subjects (ages 22–76 years),14 but there was no age effect in a regression model of nicotine clearance (ages 18–70 years).15 Likewise, significant sex differences were not observed in CYP2A6 activity studies from human liver banks, although they trended toward higher activity in females.12,13 However, significantly higher nicotine metabolite ratio (NMR, the ratio of trans-3'-hydroxycotinine to cotinine), an in vivo indicator of CYP2A6 activity, has been reported in adult females in some but not all studies.10,16, 17, 18

Nonhuman primates, including African green monkeys (AGMs, vervets, Cercopithecus aethiops), can be used to test age/development and sex effects on CYP2A6 while minimizing genetic and environmental influences. The kinetics of CYP2A6-mediated coumarin 7-hydroxylation are similar between humans (Km of 2.1 μM and Vmax of 0.79 nmol/min/mg) and AGMs (Km of 2.7 μM and Vmax of 0.52 nmol/min/mg).19 Nicotine is also metabolized to cotinine in AGMs, by CYP2A6 with a minor role for CYP2B6.20,21 In addition, the nicotine plasma half-life is comparable between humans and AGMs (ranging from 1–4 h).22,23 These kinetic similarities between species are consistent with the high (>90%) amino acid sequence identity between human and AGM CYP2A6.24 In addition to CYP2A6-mediated coumarin and nicotine metabolism, AGMs have also been used to investigate CYP3A4-mediated l-deprenyl metabolism, CYP2B6-mediated bupropion metabolism, CYP2E1-mediated chlorzoxazone metabolism, and CYP2C9- and CYP3A4-mediated diclofenac metabolism.25, 26, 27, 28 In contrast, mice are poorer models as CYP2A5, the mouse ortholog, has lower amino acid sequence identity with human CYP2A6 and very fast cotinine and 7-hydroxycoumarin formation rates (Vmax approximately 3–4 fold higher).29, 30, 31 Likewise, rat is a very poor model for CYP2A6, as cotinine formation is mainly CYP2B1/2-mediated in rats.32 In addition, compared to mice and rats, AGMs are closer physiologically and developmentally to humans, which can strengthen their translational relevance.33

The lack of controlling for genetic variation, the inability to control for environmental regulation, and the age range tested (and interactions with sex) can make it challenging to assess developmental and sex differences in CYP2A6 in humans. Using the AGMs allows us to answer whether there are age and sex differences, and their interactions, in CYP2A6 activity across developmental stages while minimizing these influences. Given coumarin as a classic CYP2A6 probe substrate and nicotine as a pharmacologically relevant CYP2A6 substrate, the combined evaluation of the 2 substrates enables a more comprehensive understanding of CYP2A6-mediated metabolism, while differences between their metabolic patterns may provide insight into the relative roles of CYP2A6 and CYP2B6 by age and sex.

Specifically, the aims of this study were to evaluate the impact of age/development and sex on physiological factors and the kinetics of CYP2A6 metabolism (ie, 7-hydroxycoumarin from coumarin, and cotinine formation from nicotine) in AGM liver microsomes. Lastly, we extrapolated CYP2A6 activity from microsomal velocity to per gram liver velocity, to whole liver velocity, and to whole liver velocity normalized by body weight, providing further insight into age and sex differences in CYP2A6 function.

Material and methods

Materials

Coumarin and (–)-nicotine hydrogen tartrate salt from Sigma-Aldrich were used as substrates. Coumarin-d4 and 7-hydroxycoumarin-d5 from LGC Standards, nicotine-d4 and cotinine-d3 from Sigma-Aldrich were used as internal standards for analyses. NADPH and horse heart cytochrome c were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and HPLC-grade solvents were purchased from Caledon Laboratory Chemicals. All other chemicals were obtained from standard commercial sources.

Animals

AGMs (vervets; Chlorocebus sabaeus) were housed at the Behavioral Sciences Foundation in St. Kitts. AGMs were obtained from a large, nonendangered, and isolated colony with restricted genetic variability.34,35 Approximately equal numbers of male and female AGMs were selected across 5 stages of development. Developmental stages, analogous to human stages, were assigned by age ranges based on the previous AGM studies.36, 37, 38 The stages selected were juvenile, adolescent, adult, mature, and aged (N = 56, 4–8 AGMs of each age-sex group, see the demographics in Table 1).36, 37, 38 During the study period, all AGMs were maintained on standard rations of Purina monkey chow, supplemented with fresh fruit and vegetables twice daily, and drinking water was available ad libitum. AGMs were euthanized by exsanguination through the femoral artery under ketamine anesthesia. Livers were rapidly excised, immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80 °C until further analysis. Liver weight (g) and body weight (kg) were recorded during tissue collection. Developmental stage, age, and weight for the African green monkeys used in this study

Developmental stages, analogous to human stages, were assigned by age ranges based on the previous AGM studies.36, 37, 38 AGMs aged 15 years or older are classified as aged AGMs (exact ages were not available).

Liver microsome preparation

AGM liver microsomes were prepared as previously described.39 For each AGM, liver (∼1.5 g) was homogenized in 1.15% (w/v) KCl. After homogenizing, 500 μL of the homogenate was collected for cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase (POR) activity assay, and the remaining homogenate was centrifuged at 9000g for 20 minutes at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and then centrifuged at 100,000g for 60 minutes at 4 °C. The resulting microsomal pellet was resuspended in 250 mM sucrose. Protein concentrations were determined with the Bradford reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Bio-Rad Labs).

In addition to individual AGM microsomes, 500 μg of microsomal protein from each animal of the same age and sex group (eg, mature females) was mixed to form an age-sex pooled liver microsomal sample for initial Michaelis-Menten kinetic experiments. Individual and pooled microsomes were stored in aliquots at –80 °C until further use.

Determination of microsomal protein per gram liver

The POR activity was assessed in both the homogenate and microsomes prepared from each individual liver sample, and was used to estimate the microsomal protein per gram liver (MPPGL in mg/g), as previously described.40 The POR activity assay was assessed by the rate of cytochrome c reduction.41 The assays were performed in a total reaction volume of 220 μL, containing 0.3 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.7), 0.1 mM horse heart cytochrome c, and 10 μL of diluted homogenate or microsomes. The reaction was initiated by adding 20 μL of 10 mM NADPH. The rate of increase in absorbance at 550 nm (generated by the reduced form of cytochrome c) was used to determine the rate of cytochrome c reduction, using a BioTek Synergy MX microplate reader in kinetic mode. Absorbance was recorded for 5 minutes after the NADPH addition. MPPGL values (mg/g) were calculated as the ratio of the POR activity of homogenates (nmol/min/g liver) to the POR activity of microsomes (nmol/min/mg microsomal protein).

Microsomal incubation and in vitro linearity assays

As previously described,19,21 AGM liver microsomes were preincubated for 2 minutes at 37 °C in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH, 7.4), 2.5 μL of cytosol (source of aldehyde dehydrogenase, only used in the nicotine incubations), 1 mM NADPH in water to a final volume of 22.5 μL; reactions were initiated by the addition of 2.5 μL of coumarin or nicotine in water (ie, the substrate) for a total incubation volume of 25 μL. Reactions were stopped with 25 μL of methanol containing internal standards for coumarin (20 ng/mL of coumarin-d4, 7-hydroxycoumarin-d5) or nicotine (20 ng/mL of nicotine-d3, cotinine-d3, 3-hydroxycotinine-d3), respectively. The samples were then vortexed and stored at –80 °C prior to analysis.

The linear conditions for 7-hydroxycoumarin formation from coumarin and cotinine formation from nicotine were established. Based on extensive previous work with AGM,19,21 a range of protein concentrations for AGM liver microsomes (0, 0.025, 0.05, and 0.1 mg/mL) were incubated with coumarin (3 and 30 μM) for various durations for 7-hydroxycoumarin formation (0, 5, 10, and 15 minutes) or with nicotine (30 and 300 μM) for cotinine formation (0, 10, and 20 minutes). A protein concentration of 0.05 mg/mL and an incubation time of 10 minutes were determined to be linear for both metabolite formations (Supplemental Fig. 1) and were used for all subsequent assays.

Michaelis-Menten kinetics and velocity for 7-hydroxycoumarin and cotinine formation by AGM liver microsomes

Km, Vmax, and intrinsic clearance (CLint) for 7-hydroxycoumarin and cotinine formation for each age-sex group, using AGM pooled liver microsomes, were performed using coumarin at concentrations from 0.1 μM to 300 μM, and nicotine at concentrations from 0.3 μM to 3 mM. The age-sex group pooled microsomes were used to assess the kinetic parameters for 7-hydroxycoumarin and cotinine formation across developmental stages (ie, from juvenile to aged) and between sexes in AGMs, as these had not been previously characterized, and to determine Km values to guide individual velocity assessments. In addition, microsomal velocity (Vmic, nmol/min/mg) for each individual AGM was measured at substrate concentrations equivalent to ∼Km and ∼10 × Km for 7-hydroxycoumarin (ie, 2 and 20 μM coumarin, respectively) and cotinine (ie, 30 and 300 μM nicotine, respectively) formation. The microsomal velocity was measured at 2 substrate concentrations (∼Km and ∼10 × Km) to capture complementary aspects of CYP2A6 function. When the substrate concentration is at ∼Km, the velocity is around 50% Vmax, which is more sensitive to variation in enzyme affinity and catalytic efficiency; when the substrate concentration is at ∼10 × Km, the velocity is around 90% Vmax, which is more dependent on enzyme abundance, reflecting the maximum catalytic capacity of the enzyme.42, 43, 44

Analytical procedure

Concentrations of coumarin and its metabolite, 7-hydroxycoumarin, were measured using a liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) adapted from methods previously described.45 Samples were vortexed and then centrifuged at 12,500 rpm for 10 minutes, and 40 μL of supernatant was injected. In brief, the analysis was performed on an Agilent 1260 LC system coupled with an Agilent 6430 Triple Quadrupole system (Agilent Technologies). Analytes were separated on a Zorbax SB-C18 column, 2.1 × 150 mm, 5 μm (Agilent Technologies). The separation was performed at 35 °C using a gradient elution with 0.1% formic acid in Milli-Q water (mobile phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in HPLC-grade methanol (mobile phase B) at a flow rate of 0.40 mL/min. The gradient was as follows: 0–4 minutes, 20% mobile phase B; 4–6.5 minutes, linear increase to 50% mobile phase B; 6.5–7 minutes, linear decrease to 20% mobile phase B; 7–10 minutes, held at 20% mobile phase B. The mass spectrometer was operated with multiple ion monitoring under positive ion mode, and the following transitions were monitored: coumarin (m/z 147 → 91), coumarin-d4 (m/z 151 → 95), 7-hydroxy coumarin (m/z 163 → 107), and 7-hydroxy coumarin-d5 (m/z 168 → 112). The linear range, R2, and the limit of quantification (LOQ) were 1 to 1000 ng/mL, >0.99, and 1 ng/mL, respectively. In addition, for 7-hydroxycoumarin measurement, the assay precision (measured as coefficient of variation), assay accuracy, and quality control concentrations were 0.3%–4.7%, 100.1%–102.5%, and 1, 10, and 1000 ng/mL, respectively.

Concentrations of nicotine and its metabolite cotinine were measured using an LC-MS/MS method previously described.46 Samples were vortexed and then centrifuged at 12,500 rpm for 10 minutes, and 40 μL of each sample was injected. In brief, the analysis was performed on an Agilent 1260 LC system coupled with an Agilent 6430 Triple Quadrupole system (Agilent Technologies). Analytes were separated on a Synergy Polar-RP column, 4.6 × 150 mm, 4 μm (Phenomenex). The mobile phase consisted of a 10 mM ammonium acetate/0.1% acetic acid in Milli-Q water (mobile phase A) and 10 mM ammonium acetate/0.1% acetic acid in HPLC-grade methanol (mobile phase B) at a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min. The gradient was as follows: 0–6.5 minutes, linear increase from 20%–100% mobile phase B; 6.5–8 minutes, held at 100% mobile phase B; 8–8.1 minutes, linear decrease to 20% mobile phase B; 8.1–13 minutes, held at 20% mobile phase B. The mass spectrometer was operated with multiple ion monitoring under positive ion mode for nicotine (m/z 163 → 84), nicotine-d4 (m/z 167 → 84), cotinine (m/z 177 → 80), and cotinine-d3 (m/z 180 → 80). The linear range, R2, and the LOQ were 1 to 1000 ng/mL, >0.99, and 1 ng/mL, respectively. In addition, for cotinine measurement, the assay precision, assay accuracy, and quality control concentrations were 1.6%–5.6%, 97.1%–105.2%, and 1, 10, and 1000 ng/mL, respectively. For all microsomal incubations, the peak area of trans-3'-hydroxycotinine was below the LOQ of 1 ng/mL, suggesting that there was no trans-3'-hydroxycotinine formation under these conditions.

Extrapolation of in vitro metabolic activity

The velocity per gram of liver (Vg liver, nmol/min/g liver) was estimated as previously described,47 using the formula Vg liver = Vmic × MPPGL. The velocity for the whole liver (Vliver, nmol/min/liver) was estimated using the formula Vliver = Vg liver × liver weight. The velocity normalized to body weight (Vbody, nmol/min/kg) was estimated using the formula Vbody = Vliver/body weight.

Data and statistical analyses

Each sample was assessed in duplicate in each experiment and repeated on at least 3 independent days. Data collection and statistical analysis followed the recommended guidelines for experimental design and analysis in pharmacology. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.5.0; GraphPad Software Inc).

For each age-sex group of pooled microsomes, Michaelis-Menten parameters, Km, Vmax, and CLint values were calculated for each of 3 or more experiments using nonlinear regression for the classic Michaelis-Menten model (v = Vmax × S/[Km + S]), and then the means and SEs were calculated.

For the velocity measured in microsomes for each individual animal at substrate concentrations of ∼Km and ∼10 × Km, the means and SEs of 3 or more independent experiments were calculated for each animal. Age-sex group-level means and SDs were then determined from the individual data. As an internal check, these means were then compared with the corresponding age-sex group from pooled microsomal means to assess consistency. For any age-sex group showing a deviation more than 15% between the group-level mean and the pooled mean, further repeats were performed for all individual animals within the age-sex group and for the corresponding pooled liver microsomes until concordance was observed. In addition, all velocity experiments were repeated using a second batch of microsomes prepared from a new piece of liver for all 56 animals; velocities showed minimal variation (<15%) between the 2 microsomal batches.

To determine the effect of age and sex on individual parameters, a two-way ANOVA (age × sex) with Tukey’s multiple comparison post hoc test was performed. The analyzed parameters included physiological factors (ie, MPPGL, liver weight, body weight, or liver-to-body weight ratio), and the velocities (ie, Vmic, Vg liver, Vliver, or Vbody) for 7-hydroxycoumarin and cotinine formation by individual AGMs at ∼Km and ∼10 × Km substrate concentrations. Statistical significance was defined at P <.05. Data are expressed as mean ± SD.

Results

Age and sex effects on physiological factors

We first assessed the effects of age and sex on physiological factors in AGMs. For MPPGL, there was a significant main effect of age, with no main effect of sex, or age × sex interaction (see data illustrated in Fig. 1A, age-sex group means in Supplemental Table 1, and results of two-way ANOVA in Table 2). For MPPGL, Tukey’s post hoc tests indicated no significant differences among the groups (Fig. 1A). For liver weight, there were significant main effects of age and sex, with a significant age × sex interaction (Fig. 1B; Supplemental Table 1; Table 2). For liver weight, Tukey’s post hoc tests indicated that adult, mature, and aged AGMs had significantly higher liver weights than juvenile and adolescent AGMs within each sex, with males exhibiting significantly higher liver weights than females in adult and mature AGMs (Fig. 1B). For body weight, there were significant main effects of age and sex, with a significant age × sex interaction (Fig. 1C; Supplemental Table 1; Table 2). For body weight, Tukey’s post hoc tests indicated that adult, mature, and aged AGMs had significantly higher body weights than juvenile and adolescent AGMs within each sex, with males exhibiting significantly higher body weights than females in adult, mature, and aged AGMs (Fig. 1C). For liver-to-body weight ratio, there were significant main effects of age and sex, with no age × sex interaction (Fig. 1D; Supplemental Table 1; Table 2). Discussion

Age was a significant influence on CYP2A6 activity when measured as Vmic, Vg liver, and Vbody for both 7-hydroxycoumarin and cotinine formation, with higher velocity in younger AGMs. Age and sex both impacted Vliver for 7-hydroxycoumarin and cotinine formation, with higher Vliver in older and male AGMs. Km values for 7-hydroxycoumarin formation were relatively stable across ages in both sexes. However, Km values for cotinine formation essentially doubled from juvenile to adulthood. In terms of physiology, younger AGMs tended to have higher MPPGL than older AGMs, and both liver and body weight increased with age and exhibited significant sex differences during and after adulthood. As expected, the liver-to-body weight ratio tended to decrease from juvenile to aged AGMs (especially in males). Overall, in terms of CYP2A6 activity, we saw age/developmental effects on Vmic, Vg liver, and Vbody, and contrasting age and sex (and their interaction) effects when assessed as Vliver.

Age influenced microsomal velocity at lower (∼Km) and higher (∼10 × Km) substrate concentrations. The reaction velocity at a substrate concentration of 10 × Km is approximately 90% of Vmax,42 consistent with the significant correlations between Vmic at ∼10 × Km and Vmax (mean of each group) among different age-sex groups for both 7-hydroxycoumarin (ρ = 0.69, P < .05, n = 10) and cotinine (ρ = 0.82, P < .05, n = 10) formation. This effect of age on CYP2A6 activity has been seen in some human studies. For example, the urinary excretion of 7-hydroxycoumarin from coumarin (measured as a percentage of the coumarin dose given) was higher in younger (<25 years) compared with older (>65 years) humans.48 In addition, adults (ages 22–43 years) had higher nicotine clearance (measured as total clearance normalized to body weight in mL/min/kg) than elderly humans (ages 65–76 years).14 In contrast, significant age effects on cotinine formation (measured as microsomal velocity in nmol/min/mg) were not seen in a human liver bank (ages 2–64 years) or a regression model of in vivo nicotine clearance (ages 18–70 years).12,15 The ability to observe an age effect in AGMs on CYP2A6 activity might be due, at least in part, to having comparable sample sizes/age group, while human studies tended to oversample adults.

The similar patterns between Vg liver and Vmic suggested that the age-related differences stem from variation in CYP2A6 activity rather than in microsomal protein concentration (see below for further discussion). The higher Vbody in younger versus older AGMs, indicating greater metabolic capacity relative to body size, was consistent with significant correlations between Vbody and predicted whole liver intrinsic clearance normalized by body weight (CLint, body, Supplemental Table 6) for both 7-hydroxycoumarin (ρ = 0.74, P < .05, n = 10) and cotinine (ρ = 0.90, P < .05, n = 10) formation. In a physiologically based pharmacokinetic model for squirrel monkeys, nicotine clearance on a per body weight basis for adolescent monkeys was higher than that of adult monkeys.49 In contrast, due to the larger livers in older AGMs, age influenced the Vliver for 7-hydroxycoumarin and cotinine formation differently, whereby older AGMs had higher Vliver than younger AGMs. This was consistent with significant correlations between Vliver and predicted whole liver intrinsic clearance (CLint, liver, Supplemental Table 6) for both 7-hydroxycoumarin (ρ = 0.90, P < .05, n = 10) and cotinine (ρ = 0.67, P < .05, n = 10) formation.

When addressing the question of sex and/or estrogen effects on CYP2A6, it is notable that estrogen can induce CYP2A6 mRNA and protein expression (using an in vitro expression system), acting via the interaction with the estrogen response element.9 However, there was little evidence for sex impacting CYP2A6 activity in the AGMs. Significant sex differences were not observed on Vmic for either 7-hydroxycoumarin or cotinine formation, indicating no differences between females and males at any developmental stage in AGMs. In considering estrogen effects, there were: (1) no sex differences among adult male and female AGMs, and (2) no increase in velocity during or after adolescence among female AGMs, nor did velocity decline in aged female AGMs, when estrogen decreases.50 Our AGM data were consistent with a lack of sex differences observed in the ratio of urinary 7-hydroxycoumarin/coumarin (an in vivo indicator of CYP2A6 activity) among human young adults (ages 18–23 years) and in cotinine formation velocity (measured as microsomal velocity in nmol/min/mg) in human liver banks (ages 2–64 years; ages 0–87 years).12,13,51 Although sex differences were observed in Vliver for 7-hydroxycoumarin and cotinine formation, it was primarily due to the larger livers in older and male AGMs. Thus, in the absence of human genetic variation and environmental regulation (eg, exogenous estrogen, etc.), we were unable to demonstrate sex differences in CYP2A6 activity. Consistent with this, in humans, when assessed with other variables that impact CYP2A6 activity, the amount of variation attributable to sex was only 1.7%.11

There were some notable trends in the Km values, which differed by substrates. The overall mean values of Km for 7-hydroxycoumarin and cotinine formation were consistent with previous studies in adult male AGMs.19,21 7-Hydroxycoumarin formation showed relatively stable Km values for all age groups in both sexes (1.4 ± 0.2 μM in females, 1.5 ± 0.3 μM in males), indicating little impact of age or sex on the affinity of CYP2A6 for coumarin. However, for cotinine formation, the Km doubled from juvenile to adult (23–25 μM to 39–47 μM, respectively) AGMs in both sexes, which suggested that either the CYP2A6 changed or that additional cytochrome P450 enzymes contributed to cotinine formation later in life. In humans, expressed CYP2B6 had a >10-fold lower-affinity (Km = 132 μM) for nicotine compared with expressed CYP2A6 (Km = 11 μM),20 and CYP2B6 expression levels increased with age.52 In AGMs, higher CYP2B6 levels in adult animals increased the proportion of cotinine formation mediated by CYP2B6 in vitro.21 Taken together, the increasing Km in AGM for cotinine formation but not for 7-hydroxycoumarin formation, suggested that the proportion of cotinine formation mediated by CYP2B6, the lower-affinity enzyme, might increase after the juvenile period in AGMs. Further studies focusing on the specific evaluation of CYP2B6 across developmental stages (ie, from juvenile to aged) could help to explain the observed increase in Km values for cotinine formation in AGMs.

We also examined the impact of age and sex on physiological variables. MPPGL is a critical physiological scaling factor in in vitro–in vivo extrapolation as it bridges microsomal level cytochrome P450 activity to per gram liver and finally to whole liver metabolic capacity.53 MPPGL in AGMs (38.7 ± 21.2 mg/g, n = 56) was identical to MPPGL in humans (39.5 ± 21.6 mg/g, n = 128),47 and also consistent with the MPPGL value (45 mg/g) frequently used in physiologically based pharmacokinetic models for monkeys.54 Age influenced MPPGL, where younger AGMs tended to have higher MPPGL values than older AGMs, consistent with humans.53 Age, sex, and their interactions impacted liver and body weight, where males had substantially greater liver weight than females beginning in adulthood. Age and sex differences in liver weight have been demonstrated in cynomolgus monkeys and in humans.55,56 Liver-to-body weight ratio decreased with maturation (especially in males), consistent with the trend found in AGMs and humans.57,58

As males progress from adolescence to adulthood over a longer time, with larger physiological changes in both humans and monkeys,55,56 we explored differences among the male adults by age. When following a median split, the age ranged from 5.7 to 6.3 years (5.9 ± 0.3 years) in young adult males (n = 4) and from 7.7 to 8.8 years (8.2 ± 0.6 years) in adult males (n = 4) (P < .05). Among male AGMs only, a one-way ANOVA was performed for each of the parameters (all 4 physiological factors, Vmic, Vg liver, Vliver, and Vbody at ∼Km and 10 × Km) across 6 age groups (ie, juvenile, adolescent, young adult, adult, mature, and aged); there was a main effect of age, but post hoc tests indicated no significant differences between young adult and adult males.

Study limitations include that the age-sex group pooled microsomes were used to initially estimate Km and Vmax parameters, preventing statistical assessment. In addition, the estrous cycle for female AGMs was not tracked during the study; however, the variation observed among adult females was similar to that observed in adult males, and in juvenile and mature females, suggesting that the impact of the estrous cycle/estrogen was relatively small in AGMs, similar to that observed in humans. In addition, to avoid over-extrapolation and misleading interpretation, we did not extrapolate to in vivo clearance parameters as we did not have additional parameters (eg, hepatic blood flow for different ages/sexes).

In conclusion, there were significant age differences in CYP2A6 activity, where younger AGMs had higher velocity at the microsomal level, per gram liver level, and whole liver velocity normalized to body weight level. Age and sex impacted the whole liver velocity, where older and male AGMs had higher velocity, primarily due to larger livers. Sex had minimal impacts on CYP2A6 activity, including between sexes, and among females during maturation. These findings in AGMs highlight the importance of incorporating age- and sex-dependent physiological parameters, as well as age-dependent CYP2A6 activity, together when evaluating human CYP2A6 activity (eg, in vitro–in vivo extrapolations, population pharmacokinetic models, and physiologically based pharmacokinetic models).
