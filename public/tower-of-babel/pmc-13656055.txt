Why is there so much reluctance to use more crossbars in pectus surgery?
Authors: Miguel Lia Tedde
Published: 2026

D’Ambrosio et al. published a systematic review and meta-analysis aiming to compare outcomes of the minimally invasive repair of pectus excavatum (PE) using either crossed bars (CB) or parallel bars (PB) (1). This is a relevant topic, and we have previously addressed the subject (2).

The authors deserve recognition not only for addressing a topic that is relatively new in the minimally invasive repair of pectus excavatum (MIRPE) literature but, above all, because the study design was based on recommended methodology—even though there appear to have been issues with its implementation.

Given that the goal of a systematic review is to critically evaluate and provide the highest level of evidence for surgical decision-making, certain aspects require clarification. However, before addressing this specific topic, we would like to make a few additional comments.

The first point concerns the term “Nuss procedure”, which refers to the surgery’s original description. Since then, however, the literature has recorded nearly a hundred proposed modifications to the technique. Our group alone has published at least two technical modifications (3,4). Consequently, we have adopted a more comprehensive term—MIRPE—which, in our view, better describes a technique that has undergone so many modifications since its original description.

Regarding the choice of bar configuration—CB or PB—the authors state in the second paragraph of the Discussion that “current practice remains driven largely by surgeon experience, institutional experience and deformity-specific considerations...”

In our view, “deformity-specific considerations” should be the guiding factor here. The determination of which bar configuration to use depends on the type of PE the patient presents. Although mixed forms of PE can occur, generally speaking—and for educational purposes—we classify PE into two main types: “cup” (localized deformity) or “Grand Canyon” (deformity extending along the entire sternum). One aspect that helps classify PE is the shape of the sternum on sagittal views of chest CT scans. In general, “Grand Canyon” types feature a straightened sternum, whereas “Cup” types present a C-shaped (or banana-shaped) sternum.

This classification guides the placement of the metal bars. “Grand Canyon” cases are treated with PB because the straight, flat sternum associated with this type acts as a stable support for the bars. Conversely, “Cup” cases are treated with CB because the C-shaped sternum—due to the lack of adequate bony support—makes the PB (positioned at the point of deepest depression) prone to displacement, which would prevent correction of the deformity.

Another important aspect—often overlooked in discussions of current practice—is the type of material available to the surgeon for PE repair. The major challenge with MIRPE was ensuring adequate bar fixation to prevent displacement, which could lead to catastrophic consequences. Since we developed a fixing system (bridges) in Brazil to secure the bars (5,6), there have been no instances of bar displacement, a finding consistent with global literature. This surgical reliability has encouraged the use of CBs and enabled the development of procedures previously unperformed in our setting, such as minimally invasive repair of pectus carinatum (MIRPC) (6).

However, the most significant observation regarding the present study concerns the eligibility criteria for including publications in the meta-analysis. Four publications were selected (7-10), but a detailed analysis of these papers raises questions about the potential for overlapping cases across them.

The study by Sayan et al. includes 51 CB cases from 2005 to 2018 (7), whereas the study by Erzoz et al. includes 205 CB cases collected between 2012 and 2022 (8). It turns out that one of the surgeons (M.Y.), practicing at the same medical institution, is a co-author of both publications. It would not be unreasonable to suspect that some of the cases included in the publication by Erzoz et al. are duplicates.

A similar situation arises with the publication by Moon et al., which includes 36 CB cases from 2015 to 2018 (9), whereas the publication by Hyun et al. lists 157 CB cases from 2016 to 2019 (10). Once again, the same surgeon (H.J.P.), practicing at the same medical institution, is a co-author of both publications. This again raises the suspicion that there may be duplicate cases in the series reported by Hyun et al.

Furthermore, there is another confounding factor in Hyun’s case series (10). Of the 157 reported cases, 51 (33%) involved the implantation of bars in an “XI” configuration. In this setup, the patient receives two CBs and an additional bar positioned transversely in a cranial location; in other words, the configuration employs three metal bars. It does not seem reasonable to include a series of patients treated with three bars (XI format) in an analysis focused on patients treated with two bars (CBs or PBs).

Clarifying these facts is important from a methodological standpoint. This issue becomes even more significant considering that the initial dataset comprised only four publications. If duplicate cases are confirmed—as is suspected—it would be appropriate to re-run the statistical analysis and clarify the matter for readers, given that publications of this nature serve as evidence guiding surgical practice.

On the other hand, regardless of what such a statistical review might reveal, the study highlights an important point. Although the body of evidence regarding CBs is limited and few surgeons use them, the publication dates of the included studies indicate that CB usage has been increasing over time. Since these surgeons are likely the most experienced in the field, this suggests that surgeons who specialize in treating PE should give closer consideration to the use of CBs.

Once again, we commend the authors for their initiative in addressing a topic of such relevance to the future of pectus surgery worldwide.
