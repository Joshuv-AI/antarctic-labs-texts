Changing Worlds, Changing Drug Markets
Authors: Matilda Hellman
Published: 2026

Europe's drug markets are becoming more complex, fluid and unpredictable. This stands clear from the latest of Europe Union’s annual European Drug Report 2026: Trends and Developments (European Union Drugs Agency (EUDA), 2026b), published in June this year. Physical and sociocultural separation between illicit drugs, new psychoactive substances, and pharmaceuticals is increasingly breaking down: synthetic cannabinoids appear in vaping liquids; counterfeit medicines contain potent synthetic opioids; and stimulants, opioids, and cannabis products are sold in ever more diverse forms.

Europe has been witnessing a technological transformation of trafficking and supply, with criminal networks adapting rapidly through new trafficking routes, drones, semi-submersible vessels, designer precursor chemicals, and online social media and payment app coordination. The report describes how authorities face new pressure to employ methods and tools such as early-warning systems, drug-checking, wastewater monitoring, toxicology networks, and other real-time surveillance tools to detect abrupt market shifts.

Drones and High-Tech

In 2026, there has been some renewed media attention to drone use in drug business. Drones are currently mostly used as specific trafficking and logistics technology and are not to be regarded as something that has fundamentally transformed retail drug markets. Nonetheless, their use can be seen as symptomatic for a time where previous boundaries are broken down with the help of technological devices. Drones have, for example, been used for moving drugs across difficult borders as they can (potentially anonymously) carry small but valuable loads across borders and other controlled areas. Spanish authorities have seized large unmanned aerial vehicles (UAV) associated with cannabis trafficking. In 2022, they uncovered an operation involving both conventional UAVs and other more home-made unmanned vessels intended to move cannabis from Morocco (EUDA, European Union Agency for Law Enforcement Cooperation (Europol), 2023).

The Dutch police authorities have documented organized drone deliveries to prisons in the Netherlands, Belgium, and Luxembourg (Politie, 2024). In April 2026, there was a prosecution against a man accused of repeatedly using drones to deliver drugs and phones to prisons. (Openbaar Ministerie, 2026). In the UK, there was a sentence in March 2026 regarding drone delivery of drugs, phones, and other contraband into several prisons. Police estimated that the group behind these crimes could have been responsible for 75% of drone drops into London prisons between December 2024 and February 2025. The 2026 European Drug Report identifies a diversification of trafficking methods, describing increasingly fragmented systems involving, for example, also maritime transfers, semi-submersibles, and other forms of technological adaptation (EUDA, European Union Agency for Law Enforcement Cooperation (Europol), 2023). Although European authorities have tightened security at major ports such as Antwerp and Rotterdam, traffickers are responding by moving toward smaller ports, offshore transfers, smaller vessels, and more technologically mediated logistics.

The development fits into a broader transformation of drug markets from more territorial, vertically organized trafficking towards something increasingly resembling distributed logistics. The report describes trafficking networks as diversifying routes and methods resulting in more unpredictable systems involving containers and commercial logistics and smaller ports: speedboats and semi-submersibles are used together with GPS, encrypted communication, and other concealment technologies (EUDA, 2026a, 2026b). In July 2026, the Financial Times picked up on more sophisticated offshore drops, GPS spoofing, encrypted communications, and unmanned submersibles as security around major ports has increased (Financial Times, 2026).

This is a very different picture from the classic cartel controlling a particular territory and physically moving drugs along fixed routes: the logics of drug trafficking are indeed shifting from Scarface to Mission Impossible.

Synthetic and High Potency Products

A decade ago or so, drug-related harm could still be traced to specific drugs or from typical combinations of drugs. Harm in Europe now increasingly come from polysubstance use and from substances that the users do not necessarily know exactly what they are.

There is a growing prominence of synthetic substances and high-potency products: Synthetic cathinones, semi-synthetic cannabinoids, ketamine, and novel opioids such as nitazenes and orphines are no longer peripheral phenomena but more and more integrated into mainstream drug markets. Meanwhile, cocaine availability remains historically high, crack cocaine is becoming more visible in some cities, and cannabis markets are diversifying through legalization experiments, high-potency extracts, edibles, and semi-synthetic cannabinoid products.

The EUDA report also highlights emerging social concerns, particularly the recruitment of vulnerable young people into drug distribution and violence, alongside persistent public health challenges linked to overdose deaths and infectious diseases among people who inject drugs. In short, the defining characteristic of Europe's current drug situation is not a single “drug crisis” but a rapidly evolving ecosystem of overlapping substances, markets, and risks that is stretching existing public health, prevention, and law-enforcement responses.

The drug situation is as volatile as the world around it: technologies are outmaneuvering established systems with consequences for public health and crime control. It is part of a broader fragmentation and disruption of the European societies and democracies as we have understood them since the post-second world war era. The stakes are rising, and interventions and methods are larger and more sophisticated. The drug market is indeed a reflection of a new world order: it is increasingly difficult to grasp and puts new demands on how todeal with it.

In This Issue

This issue of the journal is truly international with contributions from different parts of Europe. A Mexican contribution is a brief essay by Marquez-Romero (2026) about tracing the opioid epidemic in Stephen King's work. He discusses how King's novels mirror the changing cultural normalization of pharmacological pain treatment: over the course of his writing, references shift from ordinary over-the-counter analgesics toward increasingly potent prescription opioids, reflecting broader societal acceptance of pharmaceutical solutions to pain. Kankainen and Tammi (2026) report a conference that marked the first joint Nordic mobilization of civil-society organizations representing people who use drugs around human rights. The event brought together activists, researchers and policymakers from across the Nordic countries and Europe in Oslo in April.

Lerkkanen et al. (2026) have inquired into the moralities in the Swedish government's commissioned drug policy inquiries. Also, Selin (2026) is concerned with the policy strategies and logics in his work on corporate involvement in Finland's gambling policy reform. Old and young aged people are in focus in two contributions. Ampim and Ågotnes (2026) have looked at how substance use among elderly is conceptualized in Norwegian policy frameworks. The study finds that Norwegian policies increasingly frame substance use in old age through broad narratives of active ageing, but in doing so largely overlook illicit drug use and risk treating older people with substance-use problems as a homogeneous group. Manoli et al. (2026) have performed a classic mystery shopper study in Cyprus with the depressing result that compliance with underage alcohol sales laws in Cyprus remains extremely low. In 2024, 85.5% of vendors sold alcohol without any age verification, with little improvement since 2015.

Medical use and misuse of addictive prescription drugs and mental health among adolescents in Estonia is the subject of a piece by Veskimäe et al. (2026) and Calvo et al. (2026) have studied service user perceptions of drug treatment in Barcelona. Syvertsen et al. (2026) write about acceptability of Internet-based therapy among Norwegian individuals struggling with gambling. Berge (2026) has provided a commentary on this.

Høiland et al. (2026) have conducted an observational study of the differences between patients with AUD referred to outpatient or residential treatment: The study finds that patients referred to residential alcohol treatment mainly differ from outpatients in having more severe alcohol use, whereas other differences, such as age and employment,are comparatively modest. The study is commented on by Guterstam (2026).
