Proteomics identifies complement protein signatures in patients with primary biliary cholangitis
Authors: Xiaolin Ma, Hang Dong, Junming Han, Ming Han, Teng Zhang, Meng Zhou, Xiude Fan, Luna Liu, Zhen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is a chronic autoimmune liver disease lacking reliable biomarkers for diagnosis or prognosis. To identify plasma complement biomarkers that improve diagnosis and prognosis of PBC. We analyzed large-scale proteomic data from the UK Biobank, concentrating on complement components linked to PBC. A total of 44 PBC patients, including 13 baseline cases and 31 incident cases, were evaluated alongside matched controls based on demographic factors. Proteomic analysis revealed significantly elevated levels of complement proteins, including complement receptor 1 (CR1), complement component 1q subcomponent A chain (C1QA), complement component 1q subcomponent-like 2 (C1QL2), complement component 7 (C7), and component 9 (C9) in PBC patients (P < 0.05). Among these, C1QA, C1QL2, C7, and CR1 demonstrated strong diagnostic potential. Elevated levels of CR1, complement receptor 1 (CR2), C1QA, C1QL2, and C7 were linked to increased risk of PBC onset (P < 0.05). Further analysis revealed that CR1, CR2, C1q and tumor necrosis factor-related protein 1 (C1QTNF1), C1q and tumor necrosis factor-related protein 5 (C1QTNF5), and C7 were associated with more severe liver outcomes (P < 0.05), while lower levels of complement component 5 (C5) and complement C1r subcomponent-like protein (C1RL) correlated with worse outcomes (P = 0.002). Notably, CR1 had the highest predictive accuracy for adverse outcomes (AUC = 81.85), outperforming traditional liver fibrosis markers such as the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index (AUC = 76.33). Complement components, particularly CR1, may serve as valuable biomarkers for diagnosing PBC and predicting its severity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03387-5.

Introduction

Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is a chronic autoimmune liver disorder characterized by the progressive destruction of the small and medium-sized bile ducts. This destruction results in cholestasis, marked by the intrahepatic accumulation of bile acids, which ultimately leads to hepatocyte injury, fibrosis, and, if untreated, cirrhosis [1]. PBC predominantly affects middle-aged women, with a global prevalence estimated between 2 to 40 cases per 100,000 women [2, 3]. Despite the increasing incidence of the condition, the precise pathogenesis of PBC remains poorly understood. Several factors, including genetic susceptibility, environmental influences, and immune dysregulation, have been implicated in the development of the disease [3–6]. This underscores the critical need for further research to uncover the mechanisms driving PBC and to identify reliable biomarkers for its diagnosis and management.

The complement system, a vital component of the innate immune response, comprises over 30 proteins that play crucial roles in immune defense mechanisms, including inflammation, opsonization, and the clearance of pathogens [7]. Dysregulation of complement activation has been implicated in several autoimmune diseases, including autoimmune liver diseases, suggesting that complement proteins may serve as plausible biomarkers in PBC [8, 9]. Aberrant complement activation may exacerbate inflammatory responses, contributing to tissue damage and disease progression. However, despite the recognized importance of the complement system, substantive evidence of actual complement activation in PBC remains limited [7]. Activated complement fragments, such as C3a, C4a, and C5b9, are not found to be elevated in the plasma of PBC patients, and immunohistochemical studies have not detected deposition of these activated fragments in the bile ducts [8, 10–12]. This suggests that traditional complement activation may not play a major role in cholangiocyte damage in PBC. However, the observed elevations in serum levels of complement components like C3 and C4 could indicate a heightened hepatic response to the underlying inflammation associated with the disease [11, 13, 14], reflecting the importance of systemic/hepatic complement dysregulation rather than robust local complement deposition at the bile duct. As PBC progresses to cirrhosis, a decrease in these complement protein levels may occur, likely reflecting the liver's reduced capacity for protein synthesis [13, 14]. Given these observations, the distinct patterns of complement component levels in PBC patients raise the possibility that specific alterations could serve as valuable biomarkers. These biomarkers may aid in diagnosing the disease, evaluating its progression, and predicting patient outcomes. The clinical utility of complement protein biomarkers lies in their potential to provide nuanced insights into disease activity and prognosis, addressing the limitations of standard diagnostic tools, such as alkaline phosphatase and antimitochondrial antibodies (AMA) [15, 16]. Traditional markers often fail to correlate with the extent of liver injury or disease progression, underscoring the need for alternative biomarkers that can more accurately reflect disease dynamics and guide therapeutic decisions.

In light of these considerations, investigating the role of complement proteins in PBC may provide novel insights into disease mechanisms and offer opportunities to identify new biomarkers with potential diagnostic and prognostic value. In this study, we analyze large-scale proteomic data to explore associations between complement components and PBC, with the aim of evaluating their potential relevance for disease monitoring and clinical management.

Methods

Study population

This study utilized the UK Biobank (UKB), a population-based prospective cohort comprising over 500,000 participants aged 37–73 years, recruited between 2006 and 2010. Participants underwent comprehensive baseline assessments, including physical measurements, health questionnaires, and biological sample collection. The diagnoses of disease are ascertained through linkage to hospital admission and mortality records, enabling long-term follow-up of participants based on their unique identification numbers. From this cohort, 53,022 individuals were selected for plasma proteomic analysis using the OLINK Explore 3072 platform [17]. For the present study, we applied the following exclusion criteria (Fig. 1A): participants missing proteomic data for more than 50% of the 27 complement molecules (n = 7940); participants with missing follow-up data (n = 115); those missing covariates such as age, sex, ethnicity, Townsend deprivation index (TDI), smoking, and alcohol consumption (n = 461); participants with other liver diseases (All liver diseases excluding primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), n = 232). At baseline, there were 13 participants identified as having primary biliary cholangitis (PBC). During the follow-up period, 31 additional participants developed PBC, resulting in a total of 44 PBC patients. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the relevant local ethics committees. Research involving the UK Biobank cohort was conducted under the general approval of the NHS National Research Ethics Service.

Outcome assessment

The outcome assessment for this study focused on three primary objectives: first, to evaluate the ability of complement components to distinguish between patients with PBC and non-PBC controls; second, to assess the incidence of PBC development among participants who were initially without the condition; and third, to analyze the risk of liver-related adverse outcomes in patients diagnosed with PBC during follow-up. These assessments aimed to elucidate the clinical utility of complement components in relation to PBC and its progression. The diagnosis of diseases was based on the 10th Revision of the World Health Organization’s International Statistical Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) codes recorded during hospitalization [18]. Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) was identified using the ICD code K743. Other liver diseases included all liver disease (ICD-10 codes from K70 to K77, excluding K743), along with viral hepatitis (B15–B19) and liver cancer (C22). Liver-related adverse outcomes were categorized as liver cirrhosis (K746, I850, I859, I982, I983, K766, K767), liver failure (K720, K721, K729), and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC; C220). Liver function indicators include alanine aminotransferase (ALT, U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, U/L), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT, U/L), alkaline phosphatase (ALP, U/L), total bilirubin (μmol/L), direct bilirubin (μmol/L), albumin (g/L), and platelets (PLT, 10⁹ cells/L). Aspartate Aminotransferase to Platelet Ratio Index (APRI) was calculated as the ratio of AST level (U/L) divided by the upper limit of normal for AST, multiplied by 100, and then divided by the platelet count (× 10⁹/L). Fibrosis-4 Index (FIB-4) was calculated as age (years) multiplied by AST (U/L), divided by platelet count (× 10⁹/L), and further divided by the square root of ALT (U/L) [19].

Exposure assessment

Plasma proteomic data were obtained from the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP), using non-fasting blood samples collected between 2007 and 2010. Plasma was prepared and stored at − 80 °C, followed by standardized quantification using the Olink Explore platform. This platform utilizes the Proximity Extension Assay (PEA), a highly sensitive and reproducible antibody-based proteomic method with minimal cross-reactivity. Proteomic measurements were conducted across 2923 unique proteins. Relative protein concentrations were reported as normalized protein expression (NPX) values, which were log2-transformed and subsequently inverse rank normalized to ensure data consistency. For this study, we specifically focused on 27 complement-related proteins.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations, while categorical variables were expressed as percentages. The difference of continuous variables between groups were conducted using t-tests, whereas categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests. PSM at a 1:3 ratio was employed to match PBC patients and controls by age, sex, ethnicity, TDI, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. The associations between differential complement molecules and the risk of PBC were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, focusing on the area under the curve (AUC) and the identification of optimal cut-off values. Based on these cut-off values, complement molecules were stratified into high and low expression groups. Logistic regression models were utilized to analyze the association between complement components and the risk of PBC. Cumulative risk curves were plotted, and Cox proportional hazards regression was conducted to assess the risk of developing PBC across these groups. Subsequent analyses focused on differential complement molecules associated with liver-related adverse outcomes among PBC patients. ROC analysis was performed to determine predictive accuracy, followed by cumulative risk curve plotting to explore associations between complement molecules and liver-related outcomes. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2), and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population

The study included a total of 44,274 participants, comprising 44,261 controls and 13 PBC patients prior to propensity score matching (PSM) (Table 1). Compared to the control group, the PBC group had a lower proportion of males (15.4% vs. 45.8%, P = 0.055), though this difference did not reach statistical significance. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of age, TDI, ethnicity, or smoking status (all P > 0.05). However, liver enzyme levels (AST, ALT, GGT, and ALP) were significantly elevated in the PBC group compared to controls (all P < 0.001), while albumin levels were notably lower (41.96 g/L vs. 45.15 g/L, P < 0.001). Markers of fibrosis, including FIB-4 and APRI, were also significantly higher in the PBC group (P < 0.001 for both). In terms of clinical outcomes, liver-related adverse events were substantially more common in PBC patients than in controls (30.8% vs. 0.8%, P < 0.001). However, mortality rates did not differ significantly between the two groups (23.1% vs. 10.5%, P = 0.304). Identification of complement components as distinguishing biomarkers for PBC

To assess the potential of complement components in distinguishing patients with PBC from non-PBC controls, we analyzed 27 complement components in a matched cohort of PBC patients and controls. Among these, five complement components—complement receptor 1 (CR1), complement component 1q subcomponent A chain (C1QA), complement component 1q subcomponent-like 2 (C1QL2), complement component 7 (C7), and complement component 9 (C9)—were significantly elevated in PBC patients compared to controls (all P < 0.05, Table S1, Fig. 1B). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated the strong diagnostic potential of these five complement components, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 74.90 for CR1, 93.69 for C1QA, 80.79 for C1QL2, 79.96 for C7, and 68.44 for C9 (Fig. 1C). The optimal cutoff values for these biomarkers were determined as 0.354 for CR1, 0.197 for C1QA, 0.190 for C1QL2, 0.107 for C7, and 0.356 for C9, allowing for stratification of participants into high- and low-expression groups based on these thresholds. These findings were further corroborated by logistic regression analysis, which revealed significant odds ratios (OR) for PBC risk associated with these biomarkers: 7.250 (95% CI: 1.797–29.258, P = 0.005) for CR1, 56.833 (95% CI: 6.136–526.404, P < 0.001) for C1QA, 20.714 (95% CI: 3.680–116.613, P < 0.001) for C1QL2, 11.917 (95% CI: 2.278–62.338, P = 0.003) for C7, and 4.640 (95% CI: 1.229–17.519, P = 0.024) for C9 (Fig. 1D). In summary, these results underscore the potential of CR1, C1QA, C1QL2, C7, and C9 as robust biomarkers for diagnosing and stratifying the risk of PBC.

Plasma complement components as predictive biomarkers for PBC onset

To investigate the predictive role of plasma complement components in assessing the risk of PBC, we excluded participants diagnosed with PBC at baseline and monitored the cohort for new cases during the follow-up period. Out of 44,493 participants, 31 incident cases of PBC were identified (Table 2). Compared to the non-PBC group, the PBC group had a significantly lower proportion of males (25.8% vs. 45.9%, P = 0.039). No significant differences were observed in age, TDI, or ethnicity (all P > 0.05). However, the PBC group showed significantly elevated liver enzyme levels, including ALT, AST, GGT, and ALP, as well as fibrosis markers (FIB-4 and APRI) (all P < 0.001). Additionally, albumin levels were significantly lower in PBC patients (43.50 g/L vs. 45.15 g/L, P = 0.001). Five components—CR1 (0.31 ± 0.55 vs. 0.06 ± 0.34, P = 0.004), complement receptor 2 (CR2, 0.31 ± 0.66 vs. 0.04 ± 0.53, P = 0.024), C1QA (0.20 ± 0.27 vs. 0.03 ± 0.24, P = 0.002), C1QL2 (0.35 ± 0.74 vs. 0.06 ± 0.49, P = 0.017), and C7 (0.39 ± 0.50 vs. − 0.01 ± 0.27, P < 0.001)—were found to be significantly elevated in PBC patients compared to controls (Table S2, Fig. 2A). Association of plasma complement components with liver-related outcomes in PBC

To explore the connection between plasma complement components and the risk of adverse liver-related outcomes in PBC patients, we assessed 27 complement components within a cohort of individuals with and without such outcomes.

Significant differences were observed in seven complement components: CR1 (0.71 ± 0.51 vs. 0.11 ± 0.40, P < 0.001), CR2 (0.65 ± 0.89 vs. 0.10 ± 0.40, P = 0.012), C1q and tumor necrosis factor-related protein 1 (C1QTNF1, 0.42 ± 0.46 vs. 0.03 ± 0.36, P = 0.007), C1q and tumor necrosis factor-related protein 5 (C1QTNF5, 0.40 ± 0.76 vs. − 0.12 ± 0.50, P = 0.015), complement C1r subcomponent-like protein (C1RL, − 0.25 ± 0.39 vs. 0.05 ± 0.21, P = 0.003), C5 (− 0.04 ± 0.11 vs. 0.07 ± 0.12, P = 0.006), and C7 (0.78 ± 0.57 vs. 0.22 ± 0.42, P = 0.002). Among these, CR1, CR2, C1QTNF1, C1QTNF5, and C7 were elevated in patients with adverse outcomes, while C1RL and C5 were reduced (Table S3, Fig. 3A). No significant differences were observed for other complement components. We evaluated the predictive performance of these seven complement components, as well as FIB4 and APRI, for adverse liver-related outcomes using ROC curve analysis. The AUC values for the complement components were 81.85 for CR1, 78.77 for CR2, 72.62 for C1QTNF1, 68.34 for C1QTNF5, 74.56 for C1RL, 76.63 for C5, and 77.78 for C7, indicating their strong predictive capabilities. In contrast, the AUC values for FIB4 and APRI were 76.33 and 71.60, respectively, highlighting their relatively weaker predictive power compared to the complement components (Fig. 3B). The optimal cutoff thresholds for the complement components were identified as 0.761 for CR1, 0.425 for CR2, 0.190 for C1QTNF1, -0.146 for C1QTNF5, -0.029 for C1RL, 0.007 for C5, and 0.435 for C7. For FIB4 and APRI, the thresholds were 1.507 and 0.372, respectively. Cumulative risk analyses revealed that individuals with higher levels of CR1, CR2, C1QTNF1, C1QTNF5, and C7, as well as lower levels of C1RL and C5, had significantly increased risks of adverse liver-related outcomes (Fig. 3C). These findings were further corroborated by Cox proportional hazards analysis, which confirmed the superior predictive capacity of these complement components (Fig. 3D). In contrast, the predictive performance of FIB4 and APRI, as reflected in their cumulative risk curves, was less robust. Discussion

This study, based on large-scale proteomic data from the UK Biobank, systematically investigated the role of complement molecules in PBC, focusing on their diagnostic, predictive, and prognostic potential. Our findings demonstrate that complement components, including CR1, C1QA, C1QL2, C7, and C9, are significantly elevated in PBC patients and exhibit high accuracy in distinguishing PBC from non-PBC individuals. For instance, C1QA achieved an AUC of 93.69, significantly outperforming traditional liver function markers. Furthermore, these complement proteins were strongly associated with PBC onset and adverse outcomes such as cirrhosis and liver failure, making them valuable tools for disease risk stratification and outcome prediction. Importantly, these results reveal the complement system not as a passive bystander but as an active participant in PBC pathogenesis, potentially driving disease progression through non-classical pathways and local immune responses.

The diagnostic utility of complement proteins lies in their ability to reflect disease-specific immune and inflammatory processes. PBC is characterized by chronic, autoimmune-mediated bile duct destruction and cholestasis. Although conventional markers such as ALP and AMA are widely used in PBC diagnosis, they may lack specificity in reflecting underlying immune dysregulation [20]. Our findings suggest that complement activation in PBC may involve both classical and non-classical pathways. CR1 and C1QA, as key upstream molecules, were significantly elevated in PBC. CR1 regulates complement activation and promotes immune complex clearance, suggesting a compensatory response to chronic biliary inflammation [21]. C1QA, part of the classical complement pathway, is crucial for recognizing and binding immune complexes, facilitating their clearance and initiating inflammatory responses [22]. It may also interact with disease-specific autoantibodies, linking complement activation to the autoimmune features of PBC. Furthermore, the increase in terminal complement components, such as C7 and C9, suggests subclinical activation of the terminal complement cascade. C7 is involved in the formation of the membrane attack complex, and C9 is essential for cell lysis. Their elevation indicates that this pathway is activated, which may contribute to bile duct injury and persistent inflammation by damaging bile duct epithelial cells [23, 24]. These observations suggest that complement proteins provide a more comprehensive picture of the pathological processes in PBC, which could improve diagnostic sensitivity.

Beyond their diagnostic value, complement molecules also play a role in predicting PBC onset. Elevated levels of CR1, CR2, C1QA, C1QL2, and C7 were significantly associated with a higher risk of developing PBC in non-PBC individuals, with hazard ratios ranging from 2.167 to 10.418. This underscores their utility as early biomarkers for PBC risk prediction, facilitating the identification of high-risk individuals for closer monitoring and timely intervention. Mechanistically, complement proteins may contribute to PBC pathogenesis through immune dysregulation, amplification of inflammatory responses, and bile duct microenvironment alterations. While the classical complement pathway appears inactive, indicated by the absence of elevated complement activation fragments [11, 12], the increased levels of C3 and C4 suggest heightened hepatic production likely due to inflammation [10, 11, 13, 14]. The elevation of C1QA and C1QL2, key activators of the classical complement pathway, indicates local immune activation in the context of chronic inflammation [25]. Elevated C1QA may imply that complement-mediated pathways could be inadvertently triggered by immune complexes formed with AMA and cholangiocytes [26]. Similarly, increased C1QL2 points to macrophage and dendritic cell involvement, potentially exacerbating the immune response [27]. Additionally, the increase in CR2 underscores the abnormal proliferation of B cells in PBC. Elevated CR2 levels highlight abnormal B-cell proliferation in PBC, potentially mediated by interactions with complement fragment C3d, which can enhance B-cell reactivity to self-antigens [28]. Collectively, these findings suggest that complement activation is not merely a secondary response but actively contributes to the initiation and progression of PBC.

In terms of prognosis, complement proteins showed significant potential for predicting severe liver-related outcomes such as cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Among these, CR1 exhibited the strongest prognostic performance, with an AUC of 81.85, surpassing traditional fibrosis scores such as FIB-4 (AUC = 76.33). The increase in CR1 stands out as a notable finding, given its critical role in regulating complement activation and its strong correlations with severe liver outcomes [29]. CR1 is known to regulate complement activation by inhibiting terminal complement complex formation and by serving as a cofactor for factor I in the cleavage of C3b and C4b. Elevated CR1 levels might indicate it is actively engaging in modulating the inflammatory response, serving as a marker for liver fibrosis if consistently overactive [21]. In our study, elevated levels of CR1, CR2, and C1QTNF proteins (e.g., C1QTNF1 and C1QTNF5) were associated with increased risk of severe outcomes, likely due to their role in driving inflammation and fibrosis. Specifically, C1QTNF proteins are known modulators of fibrogenic pathways, such as Transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) and Wnt signaling, which promote hepatic stellate cell activation and collagen deposition [30]. Conversely, reductions in C5 and C1RL levels were observed in patients with advanced disease, reflecting complement system exhaustion and impaired immune clearance, which may exacerbate liver dysfunction in late-stage PBC [22, 31]. These findings highlight the dynamic role of complement proteins in PBC progression and their potential as biomarkers for risk stratification and prognostication.

Importantly, our study has several strengths, including the robust sample size derived from a population-based cohort and the application of rigorous statistical analyses such as propensity score matching. These features enhance the credibility of our findings and mitigate the potential for confounding variables. Furthermore, the emphasis on complement proteins, an emerging area of biomarker research in autoimmune liver diseases, contributes significantly to the field. However, some limitations must be acknowledged. Notably, while our study establishes strong correlational evidence, the observational design does not permit us to infer direct causative relationships between complement dysregulation and PBC. In addition, the relatively small sample size of PBC cases and the lack of validation in an independent external cohort represent important limitations. Future studies should therefore include mechanistic investigations as well as validation in larger, independent populations to further elucidate these pathways.

Conclusions

Our study underscores the pivotal role of complement dysregulation in PBC, highlighting its potential as a diagnostic, predictive, and therapeutic target. Further exploration of complement pathways could revolutionize the management of PBC, offering opportunities for early diagnosis, risk stratification, and personalized therapeutic strategies to improve patient outcomes.
