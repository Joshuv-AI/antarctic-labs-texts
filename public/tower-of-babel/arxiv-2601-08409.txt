Global compressible Euler–Poisson limit of the ionic Vlasov–Poisson–Boltzmann system for all cutoff Potentials
Qin Ye † , Fujun Zhou ∗ and Weijun Wu ‡

Abstract
. The ionic Vlasov–Poisson–Boltzmann system is a fundamental model in dilute collisional plasmas. In this work, we study the compressible ionic Euler–Poisson limit of the ionic Vlasov–Poisson–Boltzmann system for the full range of cutoff potentials − 3 < γ ≤ 1 -3<\gamma\leq 1 . By employing a truncated Hilbert expansion together with a novel weighted H x , v 1 H^{1}_{x,v} – W x , v 1 , ∞ W^{1,\infty}_{x,v} framework, we prove that the solution of the ionic Vlasov–Poisson–Boltzmann converges globally in time to the smooth global solution of the compressible ionic Euler–Poisson system.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Problem Setup and Main Results

The dynamics of dilute ions result from the combined effects of free transport, binary collisions, and self-consistent electric fields. At the kinetic level, these mechanisms are mathematically captured by the
ionic Vlasov–Poisson–Boltzmann (IVPB) system
:

(1.1)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}F^{\varepsilon}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}F^{\varepsilon}-\nabla_{x}\phi^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{v}F^{\varepsilon}&=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}Q(F^{\varepsilon},F^{\varepsilon}),\\
\Delta\phi^{\varepsilon}&=e^{\phi^{\varepsilon}}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}F^{\varepsilon}\,{\mathrm{d}}v.\end{split}

Here,
F^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)\geq 0
denotes the number density function of ions at time
t\geq 0
, position
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and velocity
v=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The IVPB system arises as the massless-electron limit of the two-species Vlasov–Poisson–Boltzmann equations, in which electrons equilibrate instantaneously due to their much smaller mass, giving the Poisson-Boltzmann relation for the electric potential
[
1
]
. The exponential term
e^{\phi^{\varepsilon}}
constitutes a major analytical challenge compared to the classical electronic Vlasov–Poisson system.
The Boltzmann collision operator
Q(G_{1},G_{2})
for binary elastic collisions is

Q(G_{1},G_{2})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}}|(u-v)|^{\gamma}b_{0}(\alpha)\left\{G_{1}(v^{\prime})G_{2}(u^{\prime})-G_{1}(v)G_{2}(u)\right\}{\mathrm{d}}u{\mathrm{d}}\omega,\quad-3<\gamma\leq 1,

where the angular kernel satisfies the Grad cutoff
0\leq b_{0}(\alpha)\leq C|\cos\alpha|
with
\cos\alpha=\omega\cdot\frac{v-u}{|v-u|}
, and the post-collisional velocities

v^{\prime}=v-[(v-u)\cdot\omega]\omega,u^{\prime}=u+[(v-u)\cdot\omega]\omega
.

At the macroscopic level, dilute ion gases behave as an isentropic charged fluid, which can be modeled by the compressible ionic Euler–Poisson system:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho u)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\rho\left(\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u\right)

\displaystyle=-\nabla(K\rho^{5/3})-\rho\nabla\phi,

\displaystyle\Delta\phi

\displaystyle=e^{\phi}-\rho,

where
\rho(t,x)
and
u(t,x)
denote the ion density and velocity field, respectively, and
K>0
is a constant related to the equation of state (e.g., in the Thomas–Fermi approximation).
This system can be formally derived from a two-fluid Euler–Poisson model for ions and electrons by taking the massless-electron limit. In this regime, electrons relax instantaneously to a Boltzmann equilibrium, resulting in the semilinear Poisson–Boltzmann equation
\eqref{ep}_{3}
for the electric potential
[
10
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, the fluid dynamic limit of the IVPB system remains largely open. In this work, we rigorously derive the compressible ionic Euler–Poisson system (
1.2
) as the fluid dynamic limit of the IVPB system (
1.1
) when the Knudsen number
\varepsilon
tends to zero.

1.1

Applying the Taylor expansion to
\exp\{\sum_{n=1}^{2k-1}\varepsilon^{n}\phi_{n}\}

(1.4)

\displaystyle e^{\phi^{\varepsilon}}=

\displaystyle e^{\phi_{0}}\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{2k-1}\varepsilon^{n}B_{n}+\sum_{n=2k}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}H_{n}\right)\left(1+e^{\varepsilon^{k}\phi_{R}^{\varepsilon}}-1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\phi_{0}}\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{2k-1}\varepsilon^{n}B_{n}\right)+e^{\phi_{0}}\sum_{n=2k}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}H_{n}+e^{\phi_{0}}\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{2k-1}\varepsilon^{n}B_{n}+\sum_{n=2k}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}H_{n}\right)\left(e^{\varepsilon^{k}\phi_{R}^{\varepsilon}}-1\right),

where

(1.5)

\displaystyle B_{1}:

\displaystyle=\phi_{1},

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}+\cdots+n_{m}=n\\

1.2.
Difficulties and Innovations

The difficulties and innovations we encountered are as follows:

1.2.1.
Time Growth of
F_{n}
and
\phi_{n}

Since the remainder equation (
1.7
) involves the coefficients
F_{i}
and
\phi_{i}
for
i=0,\dots,2k-1
, it is essential to estimate it. Our approach combines mathematical induction with energy and elliptic estimates. A key difficulty arises from the fact that
|A_{0}|\sim 1
, so the term
\langle A_{0}V_{1},U_{1}\rangle
in (
A.11
) cannot be directly controlled by a Grönwall-type argument to yield the bound
\|U_{1}\|^{2}+\|V_{1}\|^{2}\leq C
.
To overcome this, we exploit integration by parts together with elliptic estimates and the Poisson equation for the
\varepsilon^{1}
-order term in (
1.6
), which gives

\displaystyle\langle A_{0}V_{1},\,U_{1}\rangle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{2}-\mathcal{U}_{1},

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{2}\sim\|\phi_{1}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}
and
\mathcal{U}_{1}\lesssim(1+t)^{-16/15}\big(\|U_{1}\|^{2}+\|\phi_{1}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\big)
; see (
A.12
) for details.
Moreover, the term
\frac{Q(F_{i},F_{j})}{\sqrt{\mu}}
in
\overline{A}
is treated using the macroscopic-microscopic decomposition, which allows us to obtain
\|w_{\lambda}\overline{A}\|_{H^{1}_{x,v}}\lesssim(1+t)^{i+j-2},
see (
B.3
) for details.

1.2.2.
Growth in
|v|

We identify three representative terms exhibiting velocity growth, denoted by
\mathcal{H}_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
.
First,

\mathcal{H}_{1}=\left\langle\frac{v\cdot\nabla_{x}\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{\mu}}\,f^{\varepsilon},\,f^{\varepsilon}\right\rangle.

By decomposing the velocity into high and low frequency and applying
L^{\infty}
estimates, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}\lesssim\;

\displaystyle\Big\|\langle v\rangle^{3}f^{\varepsilon}\mathbf{1}_{\{\langle v\rangle^{6-\gamma}\geq\kappa^{2}/\varepsilon\}}\Big\|_{\infty}\|f^{\varepsilon}\|+(1+t)^{-\frac{16}{15}}\left(\|\langle v\rangle^{3}\mathbb{P}f^{\varepsilon}\|+\|\langle v\rangle^{3-\gamma/2}\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon}\mathbf{1}_{\{\langle v\rangle^{6-\gamma}\leq\kappa^{2}/\varepsilon\}}\|_{\nu}\right)\|f^{\varepsilon}\|,

see the estimates of
\mathcal{S}_{1}
,
\mathcal{S}_{5}
,
\mathcal{S}_{8}
, etc. in Section 3.
Next,
\mathcal{H}_{2}
contains
\mathcal{H}_{2}^{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}^{2}

arising from (
3.26
) and (
3.35
), respectively, and

\mathcal{H}_{2}^{1}=\int\Big(\int(v-u_{0})\sqrt{\mu}f^{\varepsilon}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\Big)\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi_{R}^{\varepsilon}\,{\mathrm{d}}x,\qquad\mathcal{H}_{2}^{2}=-\varepsilon\int\Big(\int(v-u_{0})\sqrt{\mu}f^{\varepsilon}\,{\mathrm{d}}v\Big)\cdot\nabla_{x}^{3}\phi_{R}^{\varepsilon}\,{\mathrm{d}}x.

Although
\|\phi_{R}^{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{3}}
is controlled by
\|f^{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}}
(see Lemma
2.1
),
the estimates for
\mathcal{H}_{2}^{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}^{2}
are too large to allow closure of a Grönwall-type argument.
To overcome this difficulty, we derive energy estimates for the Poisson equation
\eqref{F_R}_{2}
, see Lemma
3.2
, to eliminate
\mathcal{H}_{2}^{1}
in (
3.27
) and
\mathcal{H}_{2}^{2}
in (

).
Finally, arising from (

), we consider

\mathcal{H}_{3}=\langle\nabla_{x}\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon},\varepsilon\nabla_{v}\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon}\rangle,\qquad-3<\gamma<0.

Note that the energy functional is taken as
\|\sqrt{\varepsilon}\nabla f^{\varepsilon}\|^{2}
.
Since the
\varepsilon
-coefficient is insufficient,
\mathcal{H}_{3}
cannot be directly absorbed into the energy.
Moreover, directly estimating
\|\langle v\rangle^{-\gamma}\nabla_{x}\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon}\|_{\nu}^{2}

would require
L^{2}_{x,v}
estimate to differentiating
\nabla_{x}\eqref{ipgg}
, leading to uncontrollable
\nabla_{x}^{2}f^{\varepsilon}
terms in

Thus, we use

\mathcal{H}_{3}\leq\eta\|\nabla_{v}\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon}\|_{\nu}^{2}+C_{\eta}\varepsilon^{2}\|\langle v\rangle^{-\gamma}\nabla_{x}f^{\varepsilon}\|_{\nu}^{2}+C_{\eta}\varepsilon^{2}\|f^{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.

This requires performing an
L^{2}_{x,v}
estimate on
\langle v\rangle^{-\gamma}\nabla_{x}f^{\varepsilon}
.
In particular, under the local Maxwellian background (cf. (
3.4
)), we obtain

(1.23)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\big\langle\nabla_{x}(Lf^{\varepsilon}),\,\varepsilon^{5/2}\langle v\rangle^{-2\gamma}\nabla_{x}f^{\varepsilon}\big\rangle\geq\;

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{1}\varepsilon^{3/2}}{2}\|\langle v\rangle^{-\gamma}\nabla_{x}f^{\varepsilon}\|_{\nu}^{2}-C_{\eta}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\|\sqrt{\varepsilon}f^{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}-\text{other terms}.

The appearance of the remainder term

explains why the energy estimates are formulated in terms of

1.2.3.
Local Maxwellian background
L(x,v)

The term
\nabla(L(x,v)f^{\varepsilon})
creates substantial analytical difficulties.
To overcome this issue, as Lemma
2.2
, we decompose

L(x,v)=L_{M}(v)+\text{other terms},

The remaining terms are controlled by separating the velocity variable into low and high frequency.
Combined with the coercivity of
L_{M}(v)
(cf. Lemma
B.1
):

(1.24)

\displaystyle\big\langle\langle v\rangle^{2\lambda_{1}}\partial_{v}^{\beta}(L_{M}f),\,\partial_{v}^{\beta}f\big\rangle\geq\|\langle v\rangle^{\lambda_{1}}\partial_{v}^{\beta}f\|_{\nu}^{2}-C_{\eta}\|f\|_{\nu}^{2}-\text{other terms},

which yields the desired dissipation. However, the energy functional is

\|f^{\varepsilon}\|^{2}+\|\sqrt{\varepsilon}\nabla f^{\varepsilon}\|^{2}
in this work.
Hence, only for
\varepsilon^{n}\nabla(Lf^{\varepsilon})
with
n\geq\tfrac{3}{2}
, the dissipation estimate (
1.24
) applies directly (cf. (
1.23
)).

To estimate
\nabla_{x}(Lf^{\varepsilon})
, we employ the macro-micro decomposition. Using the identity
\mathbb{P}\mathbb{P}^{\bot}\nabla_{x}f^{\varepsilon}=0
(cf. (
3.7
)), we derive

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\big\langle\nabla_{x}(Lf^{\varepsilon}),\,\varepsilon\theta_{0}\nabla_{x}f^{\varepsilon}\big\rangle\geq\delta_{1}\theta_{M}\|\nabla_{x}\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon}\|_{\nu}^{2}-\mathcal{H}_{4}-\mathcal{H}_{5}-\text{other terms}.

\mathcal{H}_{4}
is estimated as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{4}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sqrt{\mu}\nabla_{x}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\right)\Gamma\left(\sqrt{\mu},\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon}\right),\,\theta_{0}\nabla_{x}(\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon})\right\rangle\lesssim\delta_{0}\|\langle v\rangle^{2}\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon}\|_{\nu}^{2}+\delta_{0}\|\nabla_{x}(\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon})\|_{\nu}^{2},

see (
3.10
) for more details.

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{5}=\left\langle\sqrt{\mu}\nabla_{x}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\right)\Gamma\left(\sqrt{\mu},\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon}\right),\,\theta_{0}\nabla_{x}(\mathbb{P}f^{\varepsilon})\right\rangle,

which produces
\|\nabla_{x}(\mathbb{P}f^{\varepsilon})\|_{\nu}
via (
B.3
).
This term cannot be controlled by the energy functional
\|f^{\varepsilon}\|+\|\sqrt{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}f^{\varepsilon}\|
, and the dissipation
\frac{\delta_{1}\varepsilon^{3/2}}{2}\|\langle v\rangle^{-\gamma}\nabla_{x}f^{\varepsilon}\|_{\nu}^{2}
in (
1.23
).
Therefore, we apply integration by parts in
x
and use (
B.3
) to obtain:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{5}=

\displaystyle-\left\langle\theta_{0}\sqrt{\mu}\nabla_{x}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\right)\Gamma\left(\sqrt{\mu},\nabla_{x}(\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon})\right),\mathbb{P}f^{\varepsilon}\right\rangle-\text{l.o.t}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(1+t)^{-16/15}\|f^{\varepsilon}\|^{2}+\delta_{0}\|\nabla_{x}(\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon})\|_{\nu}^{2}+\delta_{0}\|(\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon})\|_{\nu}^{2},

see (
3.12
) for more details.
The presence of

explains why it is necessary to take
L^{2}_{x,v}-
estimate of
\sqrt{\varepsilon}\|\nabla_{x}f^{\varepsilon}\|
.
Other terms can be controlled by similar methods.

Finally, for the estimate of
\|\nabla_{v}f^{\varepsilon}\|
, we note the equivalence

\|f^{\varepsilon}\|+\|\sqrt{\varepsilon}\nabla_{v}f^{\varepsilon}\|\sim\|f^{\varepsilon}\|+\|\sqrt{\varepsilon}\nabla_{v}(\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon})\|,

which motivates us to estimate

\|\sqrt{\varepsilon}\nabla_{v}(\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon})\|
rather than
\|\sqrt{\varepsilon}\nabla_{v}f^{\varepsilon}\|

This approach
yields the required dissipation
\|\nabla_{v}(\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon})\|_{\nu}

C_{\eta}\|\mathbb{P}^{\bot}f^{\varepsilon}\|_{\nu}
can be absorbed (see (
3.3

\mathbb{P}^{\bot}\!\left(\frac{v-u_{0}}{\theta_{0}}\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi_{R}^{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\mu}\right)=0.

1.3.
Relevant literature

First, we provide some necessary background on ion dynamics. Plasma, composed of electrons and ions, is a prevalent state of matter in both natural and laboratory environments, such as lightning, the ionosphere, stellar coronae, accretion disks, and the interstellar medium
[
32
,
35
,
36
]
. Its macroscopic behavior is primarily governed by electromagnetic forces. For example, stellar atmosphere plasmas consist of electrons, protons, helium ions, and a small fraction of heavier ions
[
31
]
. From a kinetic perspective, plasma is described by statistical mechanics, which bridges single-particle dynamics and magnetohydrodynamics. Charged particles interact predominantly through Coulomb collisions within a sphere of radius equal to the Debye length. The collision frequencies for electron-electron, electron-ion, and ion-ion interactions can be expressed explicitly; see
[
35
]
.
Owing to the large ion-to-electron mass ratio, electron-electron and electron-ion collisions occur on much faster timescales than ion-ion collisions. Consequently, on the ion timescale, electrons can be regarded as being in thermal equilibrium, with their density following a Boltzmann distribution, see
[
1
,
9
,
14
]
. Substituting this relation into Poisson’s equation leads to the semilinear Poisson–Poincaré equation
\Delta\phi^{\varepsilon}=e^{\phi^{\varepsilon}}-\rho
in
\eqref{vpb}_{3}
. Interested readers may refer to
[
27
]
for a more detailed derivation.

Next, we state the ionic Vlasov–Poisson
system, which models the dynamics of ions from a kinetic perspective. The system is given by:

(1.25)

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f-\nabla\phi[f]\cdot\nabla_{v}f=0,\qquad-\Delta\phi[f]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,{\mathrm{d}}v-e^{\phi[f]},

where
f(t,x,v)
is the ion distribution function and
\phi[f]
is the self-consistent potential determined by the Poisson–Boltzmann relation.
The global existence of weak solutions for the ionic Vlasov–Poisson system was first established by Bouchut
[
2
]
. Subsequent works have investigated well-posedness, propagation of moments, and stability in the Wasserstein distance
[
1
,
14
,
9
]
, with a comprehensive survey available in
[
13
]
. Bardos et al.
[
1
]
rigorously derived the ionic Vlasov–Poisson system as the massless-electron limit of the coupled ion-electron Vlasov–Poisson equations, incorporating electron collisions via Boltzmann or BGK-type operators. The mean-field limit of the system was justified deterministically in
[
12
]
, and subsequently a probabilistic formulation was developed in
[
11
]
. In a complementary direction, Han-Kwan
[
20
]
analyzed the quasineutral limit, formally deriving macroscopic models including the compressible Euler and shallow-water equations. More recently, global-in-time existence results for Lagrangian and renormalized solutions have been obtained under initial data with
L^{1+}
integrability
[
4
]
, which also established the well-posedness of the Poisson–Boltzmann equation
-\Delta\phi=\rho-e^{\phi}
for density functions
\rho\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
with
p>1
.

To account for collisional effects, we introduce a Boltzmann-type or Landau-type collision operator
Q(f,f)
on the right-hand side in (
1.25
). Flynn and Guo
[
9
]
formally derived the Vlasov–Poisson–Landau system from a kinetic ion-electron model via the massless-electron limit. Flynn
[
8
]
proved the local well-posedness of the ionic Vlasov–Poisson–Landau system in
\mathbb{T}^{3}
for large initial data. Li-Yang-Zhong
[
29
]
constructed the existence and uniqueness of the strong solution to the ionic Vlasov–Poisson–Boltzmann system (
1.1
) in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In a related development, Li and Wang
[
27
]
established the global existence and exponential decay of classical solutions to (
1.1
) in
\mathbb{T}^{3}
.

Furthermore, we briefly review classical results on the compressible Euler–Poisson limit of the Boltzmann equation obtained via the Hilbert expansion method. This approach, originally introduced by Hilbert in 1912, is based on seeking a formal asymptotic expansion of solutions to the scaled Boltzmann equation in powers of the Knudsen number. A rigorous justification of the formal Hilbert expansion was first achieved through truncated asymptotic expansions. In the whole-space or periodic setting, Caflisch
[
3
]
derived the compressible Euler limit by means of a truncated Hilbert expansion, under the restrictive assumption that the initial remainder vanishes. This assumption was later removed by Lachowicz
[
24
]
, who incorporated initial layers into the expansion, thereby allowing for more general initial data. Guo et al.
[
18
]
also highlighted the restrictive assumption on the initial remainder in Caflisch’s framework. By employing the
L^{2}
–
L^{\infty}
method originally developed in
[
16
]
, they revised Caflisch’s proofs to obtain the compressible Euler–Poisson limit for hard-sphere case, and Jiang et al.
[
23
]
extended this to the Vlasov–Poisson–Boltzmann system with Fermi–Dirac statistics.
Using a similar methodology as
[
16
]
, Li et al.
[
26
]
extended the approach to soft potentials.
Once boundary effects are considered, deriving the compressible Euler limit requires accounting for boundary layers in the half-space problem. Sone
[
33
]
highlighted the need for viscous and Knudsen layers, while Guo, Huang, and Wang
[
17
]
, and Jiang, Luo, and Tang
[
22
,
21
]
treated specular, Maxwell, and diffuse reflection conditions. These studies mainly focus on hard-sphere kernels.

Up to now, due to the analytical challenges posed by the nonlinear Poisson equation,
the Euler–Poisson limit of the ionic Vlasov–Poisson–Boltzmann system (
1.1
) for the full range of cutoff potentials
-3<\gamma\leq 1
had not been investigated. In this paper, we completely resolve this problem.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{v}X(s;t,x,v)&=\partial_{v}X(t;t,x,v)+(s-t)\left.\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}s}\partial_{v}X(s;t,x,v)\right|_{s=t}+\frac{(s-t)^{2}}{2}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}}{{\mathrm{d}}s^{2}}\partial_{v}X(\tilde{s};t,x,v),\end{split}

Noticing

\partial_{v}X(t;t,x,v)=0,\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}s}\partial_{v}X(s;t,x,v)\right|_{s=t}=I_{3\times 3}
, combining (

\|\nabla^{2}_{x}\phi^{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}\lesssim 1
form (

) and (
B.14
), for
T_{0}
is small, we obtain

(B.16)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\frac{s-t}{2}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}}{{\mathrm{d}}s^{2}}\partial_{v}X(\tilde{s})\right|&\leq\left|\frac{s-t}{2}\right|\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\big\{\|\nabla^{2}_{x}\phi^{\varepsilon}(\tilde{s})\|_{{\infty}}\|\partial X(\tilde{s})\|_{{\infty}}\big\}\leq C\left|\frac{s-t}{2}\right|\leq\frac{CT_{0}}{2}\leq\frac{1}{8},\end{split}

which implies (
B.9
).
