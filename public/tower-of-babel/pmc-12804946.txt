Deep reinforcement learning for scheduling semiconductor cluster tools in varying configurations
Authors: Jongwon Choi, Seoung Bum Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional rule-based cluster tool scheduling in semiconductor manufacturing faces significant limitations, including inflexibility, reliance on domain-specific expertise, and suboptimal performance in dynamic and complex environments. These methods often struggle to adapt to varying process conditions and equipment configurations, which are common in modern fabrications (fabs). Furthermore, previous research has typically relied on simplified simulators of cluster tools, failing to capture the full complexity of real-world semiconductor manufacturing equipment. To address these challenges, this study examines the potential of deep reinforcement learning (DRL) for optimizing cluster tool scheduling. This research presents a comprehensive simulation environment that models a cluster tool system, including both vacuum (VTM) and atmospheric (ATM) robots. This study progressively evaluates DRL agents, starting with a single-agent deep Q-network (DQN) and advancing to a multi-agent DQN (MADQN) framework to schedule the combined VTM-ATM system. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed DRL agents consistently outperform traditional rule-based methods in terms of productivity and adaptability. In the complex multi-agent environment, the MADQN agent demonstrated robust performance across all tested configurations, achieving a productivity improvement of up to 8.9% over standard rule-based scheduling methods. These findings highlight the potential of DRL to overcome the limitations of existing scheduling methods and significantly enhance productivity in semiconductor manufacturing.Keywords: Semiconductor cluster tool, Scheduling, Semiconductor manufacturing, Deep reinforcement learningSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The growing importance of semiconductor productivity is driving a critical need for optimization in manufacturing. As the semiconductor industry continues to transition to smaller process nodes, such as 3 nm and 2 nm, achieving higher throughput while maintaining precision and yield becomes ever more critical. This growing complexity necessitates optimization across the entire semiconductor manufacturing process. Cluster tools, as the primary equipment used in wafer processing, are central to this optimization effort1.

Cluster tools have become the predominant equipment in modern semiconductor fabrications (fabs), playing a pivotal role in wafer processing. These tools consist of multiple single-wafer processing modules integrated with robotic wafer-handling systems. Cluster tools are widely adopted because they facilitate complex manufacturing processes, enabling the controlled, sequential processing of wafers with high precision1. Given their central role in modern fabs, optimizing the performance of cluster tools is essential for achieving the high levels of productivity demanded by today’s semiconductor industry2.

Effective and efficient scheduling in cluster tools is crucial for optimized semiconductor manufacturing. Scheduling determines how efficiently wafers move through various modules, directly impacting throughput, tool utilization, and overall fab performance. Inefficient scheduling can lead to delays, idle times, and bottlenecks, compromising both productivity and product quality. The challenge of scheduling lies in managing constraints such as wafer residency times, reentrant flows, and the need for periodic module cleaning and maintenance2. Developing advanced scheduling strategies that address these complexities is essential for maximizing equipment utilization, increasing wafer throughput, and minimizing processing times, all while ensuring smooth fab operation under varying conditions.

Research into scheduling strategies has primarily focused on two approaches: cyclic and non-cyclic scheduling. Cyclic scheduling is based on predictable work sequences, making it easier to model using techniques like Petri nets1. It works well when process times are stable, reducing scheduling complexity by imposing a repeating structure. However, cyclic scheduling struggles in dynamic production environments, often leading to suboptimal performance because of its lack of flexibility3.

Non-cyclic scheduling offers greater flexibility by dynamically adjusting tasks based on the system’s current state. This adaptability improves throughput in environments with high variability, making it well-suited for modern semiconductor fabs, where complexity continues to increase. However, non-cyclic scheduling is NP-hard, requiring significant computational resources because of the vast number of possible sequences and states that must be considered in real-time3.

In today’s semiconductor fabs, while various optimization techniques are employed, rule-based heuristic methodologies remain a highly prevalent approach for many cluster tool scheduling tasks. These methods are typically based on domain expertise and developed through simulations and experience1. While rule-based scheduling is relatively simple to implement, it has several significant limitations. First, it demands substantial customization for each fab environment, requiring considerable time and cost investments when introducing new equipment or processes. Second, heuristic methods are often suboptimal, particularly in complex or dynamic scenarios because they rely on predefined rules that may not adapt well to real-time variability or unexpected changes. Third, these approaches lack the flexibility to handle new problems or disruptions, limiting their effectiveness in rapidly evolving manufacturing environments. Finally, the dependence on expert knowledge complicates knowledge transfer and can lead to inconsistencies in scheduling practices across different fabs.

To overcome the limitations of traditional scheduling, recent research has focused on using artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) techniques, particularly DRL, to optimize cluster tool scheduling. In DRL, agents learn through interaction with the environment and receive rewards as feedback. DRL agents learn to maximize long-term rewards, enabling more effective scheduling decisions3,4. In the context of cluster tool scheduling, DRL has the potential to significantly improve performance by continuously learning and adapting to changing conditions within the cluster tool. DRL-based systems are inherently more flexible than rule-based methods because they can adjust to new process configurations, tool conditions, or unexpected events in real-time.

The application of DRL to semiconductor cluster tools is still in its early stages and faces several challenges. One of the key issues is accurately modeling the complex behavior of cluster tools in simulations. Previous studies have primarily used simulators that focus only on the interactions between VTM robot and process chambers, without fully capturing the entire cluster tool structure, including components like ATM robot3–5. As a result, many existing simulations fail to accurately represent the complexity of real-world semiconductor cluster tools.

Moreover, there has been little research on evaluating the generalization performance of DRL agents across different cluster tool configurations and process parameters. Most studies train DRL models in specific, fixed environments, with little consideration of how these models perform when applied to new, altered process parameters. In real-world fabs, where equipment configurations and process parameters can vary significantly, it is essential to assess how well DRL agents can adapt to these changes.

The purpose of this study is to implement a realistic simulator that replicates a typical cluster tool system used in semiconductor fabs and to evaluate whether DRL agents can effectively learn in this environment. By comparing the performance of DRL-based scheduling with traditional rule-based methods, the research aims to assess the efficiency of DRL agents. Furthermore, this study investigates how well DRL agents, trained in specific simulated environments, generalize to new conditions where process parameters or cluster tool configurations have changed, demonstrating the potential adaptability and practical applicability of DRL-based scheduling in real-world semiconductor cluster tools.

Literature review

Cluster tools have become essential equipment in semiconductor manufacturing over the past few decades because of their ability to integrate multiple process modules and improve production efficiency. As the semiconductor industry has advanced, the scheduling and control of cluster tools has emerged as a critical research area to maximize throughput and meet increasingly stringent manufacturing requirements. Various approaches and methodologies have been developed in this field.

Early research on cluster tool scheduling in semiconductor manufacturing focused on understanding basic operational characteristics and developing foundational methodologies. Perkinson et al.6 analyzed the relationship between processing time, transfer time, and maximum throughput of single-arm cluster tools using timing diagrams. This contributed significantly to understanding the basic operational characteristics of cluster tools. Venkatesh et al.7 extended this analysis to dual-arm cluster tools, providing insights into more complex systems. Kim et al.8 introduced the crucial concept of time window constraints in cluster tool scheduling, proposing a systematic method for determining schedulable process time ranges. This approach allowed for more precise scheduling and improved efficiency. Yi et al.9 developed a decomposition method to calculate the fundamental period of individual cluster tools and derive the fundamental period of the entire system, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of multi-cluster tool environments. Lee1 and Pan et al.2 provided a comprehensive review of scheduling theory and methodologies for semiconductor manufacturing cluster tools, focusing particularly on cyclic scheduling approaches. This review synthesized existing knowledge and highlighted key areas for future research. Chan et al.10 proposed a polynomial-time algorithm for two-cluster tools and an exponential-time algorithm for k-cluster tools, addressing the computational complexity of scheduling problems in multi-cluster environments. These initial studies laid the groundwork for more advanced scheduling techniques and provided crucial insights into the operational dynamics of cluster tools, setting the stage for future developments in the field.

Building upon this foundation, researchers extensively used Petri nets and related models to address increasingly complex scheduling problems. Zuberek11 used timed marked graphs to model the steady-state of single-arm and dual-arm cluster tools and calculated system cycle times. This demonstrated that Petri net-based approaches could effectively represent the concurrency and constraints of cluster tools. H. Y. Lee and Lee12 formulated the scheduling problem of single-arm cluster tools with wafer revisiting as mixed-integer programming using the process-oriented Petri net (POPN) model, addressing the complexity of reentrant flows. Wu and Zhou13,14 analyzed schedulability conditions considering time window constraints and activity time variations, incorporating more realistic operational conditions into the models. Jung and Lee15 proposed an efficient mixed-integer programming model based on timed Petri nets to solve complex cluster tool scheduling problems, improving computational efficiency. Wu et al.16 proposed the resource-oriented Petri net (ROPN) model to eliminate infeasible robot activities and derive optimal schedules, further refining the application of Petri nets to cluster tool scheduling. Kim et al.17 proposed a cluster tool scheduling method that includes cleaning operations, addressing an important practical consideration in semiconductor manufacturing. Lee et al.18,19 developed methods for scheduling cluster tools for concurrent processing of two wafer types, introducing the efficient concurrent backward sequence approach. Wu et al.20 proposed a three-wafer cyclic schedule based on swap strategy, which was later improved to a one-wafer cyclic schedule by Qiao et al.21, demonstrating ongoing refinements in cyclic scheduling approaches. Zhu et al.22 presented a method to derive optimal one-wafer cyclic schedules using colored-ROPN, further advancing the application of Petri net variants. Kim et al.23 developed a mathematical model and a branch-and-bound algorithm to optimize robot operations during transient periods, addressing non-steady-state conditions. Lee and Kim24,25 analyzed lot completion times for single-arm and dual-arm cluster tools, providing insights into overall system performance. Yu et al.26 proposed a cluster tool scheduling method that includes process chamber cleaning operations, further integrating practical considerations into scheduling models. Roh et al.27 examined worst-case wafer delays in K-cyclic schedules and developed optimization methods to minimize delay variability, providing critical insights into quality control in semiconductor cluster tools. Yan et al.28 proposed a noncyclic scheduling method for multi-cluster tools with residency constraints using Pareto optimization, offering a dynamic programming algorithm that enhanced scheduling efficiency and addressed the complexities of multi-robot coordination. Tliba et al.29 proposed a digital twin-driven dynamic scheduling approach for hybrid flow shops, integrating mixed integer linear programming (MILP) optimization with real-time simulation. Their method dynamically reschedules production in response to internal and external disruptions, enhancing adaptability in uncertain manufacturing environments. Wang et al.30 introduced an optimal cyclic scheduling strategy for dual-arm cluster tools under wafer residency time constraints, developing a polynomial-complexity algorithm to minimize production cycle times while ensuring the necessary number of processing modules and robot wait times. Li and Qian31 proposed a K-unit cyclic scheduling approach for single-arm multi-cluster tools connected by one-space buffer modules, addressing scheduling beyond the traditional schedulability assumption. They developed a MILP model to optimize cycle time while considering residency time constraints, demonstrating that K-unit cyclic scheduling significantly improves productivity compared to conventional 1-unit cycles. Research has also explored other advanced decision-making frameworks, such as the game theory models systematically reviewed by Renna32 for their potential in addressing multi-objective problems in production planning, scheduling, and cloud manufacturing.

In recent years, the focus has shifted towards leveraging reinforcement learning (RL) techniques to address the dynamic and complex nature of cluster tool scheduling. This shift was inspired by breakthroughs in RL, such as the DQN proposed by Mnih et al.33 and the asynchronous advantage actor-critic (A3C) algorithm34, which demonstrated excellent performance in complex decision-making problems. Even earlier, foundational work in multi-agent RL, such as the independent Q-learning (IQL) approach, provided a basis for applying learning agents to cooperative tasks35. Various attempts have been made to apply RL in semiconductor manufacturing to broader production scheduling problems. Cao et al.36 explored solving scheduling problems in semiconductor test facilities using a cuckoo search algorithm combined with RL and surrogate modeling. Hu et al.37 applied DRL and graph convolutional networks to Petri net-based dynamic scheduling to improve the scheduling performance of flexible manufacturing systems. Hong et al.38 introduced the application of RL to optimize overhead hoist transport (OHT) systems in semiconductor fabs, focusing on Q-learning-based vehicle allocation and route guidance, improving the efficiency of material handling systems. Waschneck et al.39 applied cooperative DQN agents to frontend-of-line production scheduling, showing they could autonomously optimize for various targets and outperform standard heuristics. The direct application of DRL to cluster tool scheduling began to emerge more recently. Hong and Lee4 was among the first to propose a scheduling method for cluster tools that considered condition-based process chamber cleaning using multi-agent reinforcement learning. This study demonstrated that DRL could determine the optimal timing of cleaning operations in the dynamic environments typical of cluster tools. C. Lee and Lee5 approached the semiconductor equipment scheduling problem using DQN, constructing a simulation model considering real production environments and evaluating the performance of DQN agents in various situations. They showed that pre-trained models could quickly adapt to new environments and proposed methods to effectively respond to exceptional situations such as equipment failures. Suerich and McIlroy40 proposed an AI approach for real-time cluster tool scheduling, introducing a method to generate optimal schedules through DRL by constructing an experimentable digital twin (EDT) to simulate various scenarios. Kim and Lee41 proposed a method that combines DRL and look-ahead search to solve the scheduling problem of dual-gripper robot cells, demonstrating superior performance compared to existing heuristic methods. Kim et al.42 introduced a practical RL-based approach for adaptive scheduling in photolithography, focusing on the mass production of active-matrix organic light emitting diode (AMOLED) displays. Their method employed DQN to optimize the scheduling of photolithography machines, demonstrating superior performance in reducing setup times and improving lot assignments in comparison to traditional rule-based systems. Qin and Lu43 introduced a knowledge graph-enhanced multi-agent reinforcement learning (KGMARL) approach for adaptive scheduling in smart manufacturing. Their method integrates interoperable communication via knowledge graphs with reinforcement learning, leveraging historical job allocation data to improve scheduling policies and accelerate learning. Experimental results show that KGMARL outperforms individual reinforcement learning methods and heuristic rules in dynamic manufacturing environments. Most recently, Kim and Lee3 proposed a method combining DRL and adaptive search to solve the scheduling problem of single-arm cluster tools considering concurrent processing of two wafer types. These recent DRL-based approaches provide flexible and effective solutions to cluster tool scheduling problems with complex constraints and uncertainties. However, most existing studies have focused on solving optimization problems between process chambers and VTM robots in ideal vacuum environments. In actual semiconductor manufacturing sites, various variables and constraints exist, and realistic simulation and optimization considering all of these are necessary.

Building on recent research trends, this study focuses on constructing a realistic simulation of a cluster tool system to analyze the performance and generalization potential of a DQN agent. The simulation considers both atmospheric and vacuum environments, as well as robot movement times. This is the first attempt to investigate the generalization ability of DRL agents trained under varying equipment configurations and process parameters. Unlike previous studies, which mainly presented solutions optimized for simplified environments or specific situations, this approach aims to contribute to the development of scheduling methods that can flexibly adapt to the diverse conditions encountered in real semiconductor cluster tools.

Approach

This study examines a scenario where wafers are processed in a single process chamber (PM) and then ejected, mirroring the operation of most cluster equipment. This study develops simulators that represent the scheduling environment between a VTM robot and PMs, as well as the layout of typical field equipment featuring a VTM and an ATM robot, as illustrated in Figs. 1 and 2. The simulator used in this study is implemented using Python 3.11, OpenAI Gym framework and PyTorch for training reinforcement learning agents. All training and evaluations were conducted on a desktop computer equipped with an Intel Core i5-13500 CPU, an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 Ti GPU, and 64 GB of RAM. As a reference for computational cost, training for 100,000 episodes (as used in the long-term experiments) required approximately 1.5 days (around 36 h) per environment. It employs a parameterized and modular structure, enabling flexible modification of cluster tool configurations and scheduling scenarios.

Part 2: VTM and ATM robot environment and agent

In the Part 2 environment, as shown in Fig. 2, this section implements the complete cluster equipment commonly used in semiconductor manufacturing, including modules operating in the atmospheric environment, rather than limiting the scope to scheduling VTM robots and PMs. In addition to the modules existing in the VTM robot environment, an ATM robot, load port (LP), and aligner are added as additional modules. In this environment, modules are distinguished as being either in a vacuum state or an atmospheric state. The ATM robot is assumed to be in the same dual-arm robot form as the VTM robot. The LP is assumed to be a location where a lot, which is a collection of wafers, is loaded by OHT or workers. In Part 2, the LP performs the entry and exit roles of the LL from Part 1, and it consists of LP_in where wafers are input and LP_out where processed wafers are output. Moreover, the aligner ensures proper alignment of individual wafers. Because wafers may become misaligned during transportation by OHT or workers, the aligner adjusts them to an ideal state to ensure consistent process quality.

Wafer flow: As shown in Fig. 2, the flow of wafers in the Part 2 environment is more complex compared to that in Part 1. When wafers are supplied at the LP_in, the ATM robot picks up the wafers and transfer them to the aligner. Once aligned, the ATM robot moves the wafers to LL_entry, which is at atmospheric pressure at this stage. It takes a certain amount of time for the LL_entry to transition to a vacuum environment. After the transition to vacuum is complete, the VTM robot transfers the wafer to the PM for processing. After processing, the VTM robot moves the wafer to LL_exit. At this point, the LL_exit, which is still in a vacuum state, transitions back to an atmospheric environment over . Once the wafer in LL_exit has completed its transition to an atmospheric state, the ATM robot transfers it to LP_out, completing the process for one wafer.

As in Part 1, DQN is used. In the Part 2 environment, the VTM and ATM robots are controlled independently in the actual cluster equipment. Therefore, the policies and of the VTM and ATM robots should be different. To address this, this study uses a MADQN that configures separate DQN policy agents for each robot. Here, and are trained independently using different neural networks (the DQN agent parameters are detailed in Appendix B). This study does not differentiate the observation space for each agent. The reason is that, for example, if only the status of the VTM and its related PMs is provided, the agent would be unaware of events occurring in the atmospheric environment. Because all modules in the actual cluster equipment can access all states, both the VTM and ATM robots are given the entire cluster equipment state as their observation space.

This MADQN setup employs an IQL framework to address the credit assignment challenge. A shared global reward signal is used, where the environment provides a single, scalar reward based on the combined system outcome (such as a wafer being processed or a valid action occurring) to both agents. The VTM and ATM agents then independently update their policies to maximize this identical, shared reward. This approach is effective for this fully cooperative task, as the highly coupled and sequential nature of the VTM and ATM operations implicitly encourages the agents to learn coordinated policies to optimize the shared, system-wide objective of throughput maximization. Experiments

Experimental setup and data generation

All experiments in this study are based on synthetic data generated by the developed simulator. The agent interacts with the environment, generating tuples stored in a replay memory of 100,000 and sampled in mini-batches of 32 for training. To ensure a consistent basis for comparison, each episode was initialized from an identical starting state (), defined as the cluster tool being completely empty.

Part 1 (Simple, DQN) and Part 2 (Complex, MADQN) environments differed significantly in their complexity, learning framework, and observed reward dynamics. To compare agent characteristics under these varied conditions, this study intentionally established two distinct training duration policies: ‘short-term’ () and ‘long-term’ () training.

As observed during the training process, Figs. 6 and 7 show the representative training reward curves for the Part 1 and Part 2 environments, respectively. Based on the distinct training dynamics observed in these curves, this study defined two training durations. The ‘short-term’ policy, , was trained for 1000 episodes. This study set this duration to capture the ‘initial convergence point’ observed in the simpler Part 1 environment (Fig. 6) and the ‘initial policy peak’ in the complex Part 2 environment (Fig. 7). The ‘long-term’ policy, , was trained for 100,000 episodes. This study set this upper limit to investigate two contrasting hypotheses: to test for potential overfitting in Part 1 (Fig. 6) and to allow the agent to reach its peak performance in Part 2 (Fig. 7), where rewards continued to increase even after tens of thousands of episodes. The policies ultimately used for evaluation were the specific and checkpoints that achieved the highest training reward observed within their respective 1000 and 100,000 episode windows.

In this environment, the number of wafers processed within a limited time was used as a performance indicator. If the agent effectively learned the environment, it was expected to process more wafers within the unit time (set to 1000 timesteps) compared to the rule-based schedulers. Part 2: VTM and ATM robot environment

In the Part 1, experiments were conducted with the DQN agent in a simplified environment where only vacuum modules were configured. In Part 2, this study introduced a more complex cluster equipment environment that included both atmospheric and vacuum states, more closely reflecting real-world semiconductor operations. The addition of atmospheric modules (ATM robot, aligner, and load port) significantly increased the scheduling complexity, creating a more challenging environment for the DQN agents.

The key difference in Part 2 is the use of a MADQN architecture. In real-world cluster tools, VTM and ATM robots operate separately but cooperate toward a common production goal. Accordingly, this study designed two DQN-based policy agents that are trained independently in terms of network parameters, yet share a global reward signal to encourage coordinated scheduling and resolve the credit-assignment problem. In addition, compared with Part 1, both vacuum and atmospheric module states were modeled, and operation-time variations depending on the robots’ moving distances were introduced to increase task complexity. For details of the MADQN training algorithm, please refer to Appendix C.

The results indicate that the more complex configuration in Part 2 required longer training durations to effectively learn the environment’s characteristics, a finding that contrasts directly with Part 1, where extended training led to overfitting (as seen in Table 2 and Fig. 9). This apparent contradiction highlights a key finding: environmental complexity dictates the relationship between training time and generalization. The simpler Part 1 environment has a relatively small state space, making its policy susceptible to ‘memorization’ and overfitting with prolonged training. Conversely, the significantly larger and more complex multi-agent state space in Part 2 is too vast to be simply memorized. This forces the agent to abandon memorization and instead learn a more abstract, generalizable policy, which requires a longer training duration to develop. Therefore, the results confirm that given sufficient training to learn a complex environment, DRL agents can exhibit robust generalization abilities, even under variations in process parameters, emphasizing their practical applicability.

Discussion

Limitations and future research directions

A primary limitation of this study is the scope of the scheduling problem addressed. The current DRL agent is designed to optimize a single objective: throughput maximization (i.e., the number of wafers processed). Consequently, critical real-world fab constraints and multi-objectives, such as wafer residency times (WRT), lot priorities, or on-time delivery (OTD) targets, were not included in the current framework. Integrating these complex constraints would require significant modifications, such as augmenting the state space to track wafer-specific timers and designing a multi-objective reward function to penalize WRT violations. This remains a significant and necessary avenue for future research.

Furthermore, while the current simulation environment captures operational variability in basic scenarios (as seen in Part 1), it lacks major stochastic disturbances such as unexpected equipment breakdowns or unplanned maintenance. Real-world fabs are inherently uncertain, and the exclusion of these disruptive events is another limitation of the current study. To address this, the framework can be extended to a stochastic formulation where the transition follows a probability distribution , heavily influenced by random variables . By training on , the agent can learn risk-aware policies —for example, proactively avoiding a PM module where the failure probability exceeds a certain threshold.

Expanding the DRL framework to handle more complex scheduling environments, such as those with a larger number of modules or simultaneous processing of different recipes, is a key area for future work. This would require specific, yet feasible, extensions to the proposed framework:

First, to handle an increasing number of diverse modules , the input dimension of the state vector (or observation ) serving as the input to the DQN agent would be expanded. The action space (Appendix A) would also grow linearly, . While the true state space grows exponentially , the DRL agent, as a function approximator, is designed to address this ‘curse of dimensionality’. Its neural network (Appendix B) learns an effective policy from the state vector, whose input dimension grows only linearly , allowing it to manage the increased complexity.

Second, to manage the parallel scheduling of different wafer recipes (such as wafers with different process times ), the environment’s state definition must be augmented. The state for a module holding a wafer (Eq. 1) would need to include wafer-specific information, such as its recipe type or required next step, . This extension allows the action masking mechanism (Eq. 4) to play a more critical role. The set of valid actions would be dynamically filtered based on the wafer’s recipe , ensuring the agent only considers modules compatible with that recipe. The DQN agent would then learn the optimal policy within this constrained action space, such as choosing between available parallel chambers based on other state information.

Additional analysis: hyperparameter sensitivity

To further examine the robustness of the proposed DRL framework, an additional hyperparameter sensitivity analysis was conducted independently from the main experiments. The objective was to investigate how variations in key learning parameters affect the agent’s stability and performance across different scheduling environments. Three primary hyperparameters were examined: the -decay rate controlling the exploration schedule, the reward-scaling factor, and the mini-batch size used for stochastic gradient updates. Each configuration was trained for 1000 episodes and validated by measuring the number of wafers processed during the validation phase. The full results of this analysis are detailed in Appendix E.

The sensitivity analysis revealed contrasting trends across the two environments. In the simpler single-robot configuration, the agent’s throughput remained nearly constant regardless of the tested hyperparameters, with differences within 2%, indicating high robustness of the learning process. In contrast, the dual-robot configuration exhibited much greater variability. The best combination achieved 512 processed wafers, while the poorest achieved 362 wafers, corresponding to roughly 30% performance variation.

Specifically, larger batch sizes () and high reward-scaling factors () generally led to slower convergence and reduced throughput, whereas moderate reward scales (0.5–1.0) and smaller batch sizes (32–64) produced more consistent results. These observations suggest that as system complexity and the decision space expand, the DRL agent becomes more sensitive to training dynamics governed by exploration and gradient-update parameters.

Overall, the analysis confirms that the proposed framework is robust under simple scheduling conditions but requires careful hyperparameter calibration in complex multi-robot scenarios.

Conclusion

This study examined the use of DRL to address inefficiencies in semiconductor cluster tool scheduling, with a primary focus on improving productivity in dynamic manufacturing environments. By developing a comprehensive simulation environment that models both VTM and combined VTM-ATM robot configurations, this study conducts a rigorous evaluation of DRL-based agents.

A major contribution of this study is the development of a realistic cluster tool simulation that captures the full complexity of semiconductor manufacturing, incorporating both vacuum and atmospheric modules. This advanced simulation enabled a thorough assessment of the generalization capabilities of DRL agents across various equipment configurations and process parameters. The experimental results demonstrated that both single-agent and multi-agent DQN models successfully trained and generalized within these environments, showing strong potential to outperform traditional rule-based scheduling methods in terms of productivity, flexibility, and adaptability, particularly in complex, dynamic scenarios.

For future work, we plan to refine the simulation by incorporating stochastic events, such as equipment breakdowns and maintenance activities, to better reflect real-world unpredictability. Furthermore, we aim to conduct comparative studies across different DRL models to develop and propose optimized solutions.
