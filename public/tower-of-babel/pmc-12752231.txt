Genomic characterization of an environmental Burkholderia thailandensis strain from Kerala, India reveals virulence and antimicrobial resistance signatures
Authors: Athira Santhosh, Parasmal Suresh, Sanjayan Arinarayanan, Lalitha Biswas, Pankaj Kumar Jha, Harisree Sudersanan, Raja Biswas, Arun Sachu, Hridya Vikraman, Divya Vijayakumar, Harpreet Kaur, Chiranjay Mukhopadhyay, Anil Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBurkholderia thailandensis is an environmental Gram-negative bacterium closely related to the highly pathogenic B. pseudomallei, the causative agent of melioidosis. Although B. thailandensis is generally considered avirulent, sporadic human infections and genomic features suggest potential for pathogenic adaptation. We report the whole-genome sequencing and comparative analysis of a B. thailandensis strain (BUR-TH_250524) isolated from soil in Kerala, India, to assess its taxonomic identity, genetic composition, and potential for antimicrobial resistance and virulence. Genomic DNA was extracted from a biochemically identified isolate, which was subsequently confirmed as B. thailandensis through 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Whole-genome sequencing was performed using Illumina paired-end technology. The genome was assembled de novo and functionally annotated using Bakta, KEGG, GO, CARD, and VFDB databases. Comparative analyses included ANI, variant calling, prophage prediction, and mobile genetic element profiling.ResultsThe Ragout-scaffolded draft genome (6.93 Mb) exhibited a genome fraction of 94.67%, with 280,948 undetermined bases (N’s) corresponding to unresolved gaps, that represent ~ 4.05% of the total assembly, reflecting a high overall completeness. The assembled genome comprises two chromosomes with high completeness (95.1%, BUSCO). Annotation revealed 5,647 coding sequences, including 225 virulence-related genes and 8 high-confidence antimicrobial resistance genes. Seven prophage regions and 215 mobileOG elements were identified, highlighting genomic plasticity. ANI values (≥ 99.6%) and phylogenetic analysis confirmed close relatedness to reference B. thailandensis strains, yet the isolate shared features with B. pseudomallei and B. mallei.ConclusionThis study provides the first genomic insights into an Indian B. thailandensis environmental isolate. The detection of virulence and resistance-associated determinants underscores the importance of genomic surveillance of environmental Burkholderia populations and warrants genomic surveillance and awareness of the potential for misidentification with B. pseudomallei in regions where melioidosis is endemic.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12363-6.Keywords: Burkholderia thailandensis, Whole-genome sequencing, Antimicrobial resistance, Virulence factors, Mobile genetic elements, Environmental isolate, Comparative genomics, Melioidosis

Introduction

Burkholderia thailandensis is a non-fermenting, motile, Gram-negative bacillus that inhabits soil and water environments, primarily in Southeast Asia and northern Australia [1, 2]. While closely related to the highly virulent Burkholderia pseudomallei the causative agent of melioidosis, it is generally considered non-pathogenic to humans [3–5]. Distinction between these species is subtle, with B. thailandensis uniquely capable of assimilating L-arabinose and lacking key virulence determinants such as the capsular polysaccharide synthesis operon present in B. pseudomallei [6, 7]. Despite its classification as avirulent, there is increasing recognition of B. thailandensis as an opportunistic pathogen [8]. Human infections with B. thailandensis are rare but have been documented from various parts of the world, the earliest documented case occurred before 1999 in Thailand, involving a 16-year-old boy with compound fractures from a motorcycle accident in 1997 [9], B. thailandensis H0587 was recovered from a pleural wound in Louisiana, USA and B. thailandensis TXDOH was recovered from a 2-year-old child from Texas, USA [3, 10, 11]. B. thailandensis BtAR2017 was recovered from a patient with an infected wound in Arkansas, USA, in 2017 [10]. B. thailandensis BPM was isolated from a fatal case that occurred in 2013 in a 67-year-old man who was treated at a hospital in Chongquing, China [12]. B. thailandensis 2022DZh was obtained from a patient with an infected wound in southwest China in 2022 [13]. Isolates such as B. thailandensis BPM and B. thailandensis TXDOH have demonstrated enhanced virulence in murine models and harbor virulence-associated traits, including capsular gene clusters analogous to those of B. pseudomallei [13, 14]. These cases suggest that horizontal gene transfer and selective pressure may be driving a shift in pathogenic potential within certain environmental or clinical B. thailandensis strains [15].

India lies within a geographic region where B. pseudomallei is endemic, and melioidosis is increasingly recognized though still underdiagnosed and underreported. This makes the accurate distinction between B. thailandensis and B. pseudomallei particularly significant for both clinical diagnostics and public health surveillance. Whole-genome sequencing has proven instrumental in elucidating the evolutionary and functional landscape of Burkholderia species [13, 16, 17]. However, genomic data on Indian B. thailandensis strains, from clinical or environments sources are currently not available. Given the shared ecological niches and genomic similarities among B. thailandensis, B. pseudomallei, and B. mallei, high-resolution genomic surveillance is essential for early detection of emerging virulence or resistance traits [18].

In this study, we report the genomic and functional characterization of an environmental B. thailandensis isolate (BUR-TH_250524) recovered from soil sample in Kerala, India. Through comprehensive annotation, comparative genomics, resistance and virulence profiling, we aimed to evaluate the isolate’s potential relevance to public health and contribute to the broader understanding of Burkholderia genome evolution.

This study aimed to characterize the whole genome of an environmental B. thailandensis isolate from Kerala, India and compare its genomic features with known clinical and environmental strains to assess its potential for pathogenicity and antimicrobial resistance.

Methods

Sample collection and culture conditions

A soil sample was collected in January 2024 from Thiruvanvandoor, Chengannur, Alappuzha district, Kerala, India. The isolate was cultured on Ashdown agar and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Preliminary biochemical testing indicated colistin resistance and oxidase positivity, consistent with Burkholderia species [19].

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA confirmation

Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany). DNA concentration and purity were assessed via NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher) and Qubit Fluorometer (Invitrogen). The isolate was analysed using multiplex PCR described by Koh et al., targeting three gene loci: BPSS2022 (primers 32 F/32R; 321 bp, specific to B. pseudomallei), BPSL1958 (primers 51 F/51R; 516 bp, shared by B. pseudomallei and B. mallei), and BPSS1649 (primers 71 F/71R; 709 bp, present in B. pseudomallei, B. mallei, and B. thailandensis but absent in the B. cepacia complex). PCR amplification was performed in 50 µL reactions under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of 95°C for 20 s, 59°C for 35 s, and 72°C for 30 s. PCR product was analyzed using 2% agarose gel and the species confirmation was further done by Sanger sequencing [20]. Species-level identity was confirmed by amplifying and sequencing the 16S rRNA gene using universal primers, followed by BLASTn analysis against the NCBI database.

Whole genome sequencing and assembly

A paired-end library (2 × 150 bp) was prepared using the Illumina TruSeq Nano DNA kit and sequenced on an Illumina platform. Raw reads were quality-filtered using Trimmomatic v0.39 [21]. De novo assembly was performed with SPAdes v3.15.3 [22]. Assembly scaffolding and gap filling were done using Ragout v2.3 [23] and GapCloser v1.12 [24], respectively. Assembly quality evaluation was done by QUAST v2.0 [25] and Benchmarking Universal Single-copu orthologs (BUSCO) [26] was used to analyse the Genome completeness. Species identity at the whole-genome level was assessed using ribosomal MLST (rMLST) [27] and genome-wide relatedness was assessed using Genome-to-Genome Distance Calculator (GGDC), which computes digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) values for high-resolution taxonomic delineation [28].

Genome annotation and functional classification

Genome annotation was conducted using Bakta (DB: v5.0 - Light), tool version 1.1.0 [29] via the PROKSEE platform (https://proksee.ca) [30]. Functional classification of protein-coding genes was performed using Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway mapping via OmicsBox v3.3 (Biobam, Spain) [31–33] and KAAS v2.10 [34].

AMR gene detection (CARD)

Antimicrobial resistance genes were identified using multiple tools such as Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) v6.0.0 tool from the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) [35], employing the DIAMOND algorithm for protein homology-based prediction. AMRFinderPlus (NCBI Antimicrobial Resistance Gene Finder) tool from Galaxy Version 3.12.8 + galaxy0 [36] and ResFinder v2.5.1 from Center for Genomic Epidemiology online bioinformatics tools (https://genepi.food.dtu.dk/) [37, 38].

Virulence factor prediction (VFDB)

Putative virulence genes were predicted using DIAMOND BLASTx searches against both the core and full datasets of the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB), applying a minimum coverage threshold of 80% and an e-value cut-off of 1e-05 [39, 40]. Genes were classified by virulence category and factor type. Further, to validate the completeness of Type III and Type VI secretion system–associated genes identified through VFDB annotation, the draft genome of BUR-TH_250524 was analyzed using TXSScan implemented in MacSyFinder v2.0 (TXSScan models v1.1.0) via the Galaxy.eu platform [41]. The parameters used for the analysis is listed in Supplementary file 1.

CRISPR-Cas and prophage prediction

CRISPR arrays and associated Cas proteins were predicted using CRISPRCasFinder [42] via PROKSEE. Prophage regions were identified with Phigaro p2.4.0, which integrates pVOGs for functional annotation of phage-related genes [43].

Phylogenetic and ANI analysis

Phylogenetic analysis was performed using representative Burkholderia genomes obtained from NCBI RefSeq. The 16S rRNA gene was extracted from the assembled genome and aligned with reference Burkholderia genomes, including B. thailandensis isolates (BPM: CP050020, 2022 DZh: CP141809, TXDOH: NZ ABBD01000779, TXDOH: NZ JAWE01000093, E264: CP000086 and E264: U91838), using MUSCLE implemented in MEGA X v12 [44, 45]. A maximum likelihood tree was constructed with 100 bootstrap replicates. Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) values were calculated using FastANI v1.33 with complete genomes of B. thailandensis BPM, B. thailandensis E264, B. thailandensis 2022DZh, B. thailandensis E264 ATCC, B. thailandensis TXDOH, B. pseudomallei, B. mallei, B. cepacia, and B. cenocepacia [46].

Variant calling

Genome-wide variant analysis was performed using Snippy v4.6.0 [47] implemented via the Galaxy.eu platform. The draft genome assembly of the study isolate (BUR-TH_250524) was used as the reference sequence. Each publicly available Burkholderia genome (B. thailandensis E264, B. thailandensis E264 ATCC, B. pseudomallei, B. mallei, B. cepacia, and B. cenocepacia) was individually aligned to this reference to identify single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), insertions, and deletions. Snippy employs BWA-MEM for read alignment and FreeBayes for variant calling, with default filtering thresholds: minimum mapping quality ≥ 30, base quality ≥ 20, read depth ≥ 10, and variant call frequency ≥ 0.9. The resulting VCF files were parsed and visualized using VCF Observer for interpretation of variant distribution and annotation [48].

Plasmid and other mobile genetic element analysis

Plasmid sequences in the draft genome were identified using PlasmidFinder [49] version 2.1.6 (bacterial plasmid identification) implemented via Galaxy.eu, and the results were validated with MobSuite version 3.1.9 accessed through the Solu Platform [50–52]. Mobile orthologous genetic elements (e.g., integrases, transposases, conjugation systems) were identified using mobileOG-db via PROKSEE [53], which provides functional categorization of bacterial mobile elements.

Data deposition

This Whole Genome Shotgun project has been deposited at DDBJ/ENA/GenBank under the accession JBQIHS000000000. The version described in this paper is version JBQIHS010000000. The chromosome level genome sequences of Burkholderia thailandensis strain BUR-TH_250524 are deposited under accession numbers CM125682.1 and CM125683.1. The raw paired-end read files (R1 and R2) have been submitted to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under submission ID SUB15730623 and are accessible through this record.

Results

Species confirmation and genome features

The isolate BUR-TH_250524 was recovered from a soil sample in Kerala was identified as B. pseudomallei by VITEK automated system and B. thailandensis by multiplex PCR. 16S rRNA gene sequencing confirmed its identity, showing 100% similarity to B. thailandensis sequences in the NCBI database.

Illumina paired-end sequencing produced 8.75 million raw reads, of which 5.19 million high-quality reads remained after trimming. De novo assembly yielded the draft genome comprised of two scaffolds totalling 6.93 Mb, with an average scaffold length of 3.46 Mb. The scaffold N50 was 3.99 Mb, which also represents the length of the largest scaffold, while the shortest scaffold measured 2.94 Mb. Genome completeness assessment using BUSCO v5.8.0 (burkholderiales_odb10 lineage) identified 654 complete BUSCOs (95.1%), of which 650 (94.5%) were present as single-copy and 4 (0.6%) as duplicated. No fragmented BUSCOs were detected, and 34 BUSCOs (4.9%) were classified as missing out of the 688 orthologous groups searched. Alignment-based quality metrics from QUAST further revealed a genome fraction of 94.67% with 280,948 undetermined bases (‘N’s) corresponding to unresolved gaps, representing ~ 4.05% of the assembly with a duplication ratio of 1.026 and GC content of 67.65%. These results indicate a high-quality draft genome with strong gene space completeness despite the presence of unresolved scaffold gaps (Fig. 1).

Species confirmation was further validated by rMLST analysis, which assigned the isolate to B. thailandensis with high confidence. Followed by the genome-to-genome comparison using GGDC (Formula 2) yielded a dDDH value of approximately 97%, well above the 70% species threshold, with narrow confidence intervals. These results confirm that the isolate belongs to the same species as the reference strain B. thailandensis E264 genome and therefore, is classified as B. thailandensis.

Phylogenetic and average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis

Phylogenetic relationship of the Burkholderia species was analysed using 16S rRNA sequences. The phylogram shows the top-left cluster contains B. cepacia/members of B. cepacia complex clusters together, indicating they are more similar to each other, than to other Burkholderia species. Another clade group comprised of B. pseudomallei complex strains which included B. pseudomallei, B. mallei and B. thailandensis. B. thailandensis strains are tightly clustered, often with high bootstrap, indicating those sequences are very similar and form a well-supported monophyletic group. Paraburkholderia xenovorans is separated by a long branch and sits as the outgroup — consistent with it being more distantly related. The study isolate BUR-TH_250524 is placed within the B. pseudomallei complex and clustering closely with B. thailandensis E624 and 2022DZh strains (Fig. 2). ANI comparisons revealed the genomic proximity of our environmental isolate to multiple B. thailandensis strains, including BPM (99.75%), E264 ATCC (99.63%), E264 Reference strain (99.70%), TXDOH (98.87%), and 2022DZh (99.70%). The study isolate also shows substantially moderate ANI values with B. mallei (92.79%), B. pseudomallei (92.82%), and lower values with B. cepacia (84.78%), and B. cenocepacia (84.75%). These results further confirm the close relationship of our study isolate to B. thailandensis reference strains, particularly BPM (Fig. 3) [Supplementary file 2].

Genome annotation and functional classification

The assembled genome contained 5,764 predicted features, including 5,647 protein-coding sequences (CDSs), 64 tRNAs, 5 rRNAs (16S rRNA: 2; 23S rRNA: 2; 5S rRNA: 1), 2 oriC regions, 1 tmRNA, and 45 other non-coding RNAs. Functional annotation identified key categories involved in metabolism, transport, signal transduction, and environmental adaptation.

KEGG pathway analysis assigned 2,455 genes to 22 categories, dominated by metabolism-related modules such as amino acid metabolism (307 genes), carbohydrate metabolism (288 genes), and energy metabolism (195 genes). Genetic information processing pathways included replication and repair (48 genes) and protein folding and degradation (41 genes) [Supplementary file 3].

Gene Ontology (GO) classification revealed 1,782 CDSs associated with biological processes, 1,466 with cellular components, and 2,021 with molecular functions. Prominent functions included hydrolase activity, membrane localization, and small molecule binding (Fig. 4).

Antimicrobial resistance gene prediction

Screening with the Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI v6.0.0, CARD 2024-06−01) using the DIAMOND algorithm (≥ 90% identity, ≥ 60% coverage) revealed seven high-confidence (Strict hit) resistance genes were identified using the CARD database. These included genes associated with antibiotic efflux (adeF, amrA, amrB, qacJ), β-lactamase activity (OXA-42), and vancomycin resistance (vanH, in the vanO cluster) (Table 1). Additionally, a porin gene (Omp38), known to influence membrane permeability, was identified but is not considered a direct antimicrobial resistance determinant.

Virulence-associated genes

A total of 225 virulence-associated genes were identified using the VFDB, with the majority related to secretion systems (102 genes), motility functions (47 genes), immune modulation (29 genes) (Table 2). Among the secretion system genes, majority of them belong to Type III and Type VI. To know completeness of the secretion systems TXSScan was performed and the analysis confirmed the presence of a complete Type III secretion system (T3SS), with all essential components detected (wholeness = 1.0), indicating full operon integrity. Similarly, a complete Type VI secretion system cluster I (T6SS-I) was identified, encompassing the entire core tssA–M gene set (wholeness = 1.0). In contrast, T6SS cluster II (T6SS-II) was incomplete (wholeness = 0.06) and comprised only partial iglB homologs, suggesting a degenerated or fragmented locus. These findings verify that BUR-TH_250524 retains functionally intact T3SS and T6SS-I systems, while additional T6SS variants are truncated.

The genome encoded capsule biosynthesis genes, including a single complete wcbR gene (Gene_02980) and a partial thioester reductase–like homolog (Gene_05547) showing local similarity to wcbR, suggesting possible diversification of capsule-related biosynthetic functions that may influence immune evasion under specific environmental or host conditions.

Biofilm formation, adherence factors, and metabolic virulence contributors were also detected, though no classical B. pseudomallei-specific toxin genes were present. The presence of multiple virulence elements suggests potential for adaptation to host environments [Supplementary file 4].

CRISPR-Cas system and prophage elements

Five CRISPR arrays were detected across two scaffolds, each containing 1–2 spacers. However, no Cas proteins were identified, indicating a potentially non-functional or incomplete CRISPR-Cas system. Prophage regions were identified using Phigaro, which predicted seven putative prophages across two scaffolds of the draft genome. Five regions were taxonomically assigned to the Myoviridae family, while two remained unclassified. These regions ranged from 3.5 kb to 57.7 kb in length and collectively contained multiple phage-related genes, including integrases, terminases, tail, and capsid proteins, as defined by the Prokaryotic Virus Orthologous Groups (pVOGs) database (Table 3).

Variant analysis with reference strains

Variant calling using Snippy revealed 6,106 and 6,088 total variants when BUR-TH_250524 was compared with B. thailandensis E264 (reference and ATCC strains, respectively). These included ~ 5,400 SNPs and moderate numbers of insertions and deletions. In contrast, comparisons with B. pseudomallei, B. mallei, B. cepacia, and B. cenocepacia showed a much higher variant burden (> 143,000 variants), reflecting greater evolutionary divergence [Supplementary file 5]. A comparative genomic analysis of various B. thailandensis strains and B. pseudomallei K96243 reveals differences in genomic size, GC content, gene counts and virulence factors highlighting distinct genomic features associated with environmental and clinical isolates (Table 4).

Plasmid and other mobile genetic elements

Analysis with PlasmidFinder and MobSuite 3.1.9 revealed no detectable plasmid sequences in the draft genome. Analysis using mobile OG-db identified 215 mobile element-related genes. These included 74 integration/excision-related elements, 41 associated with replication/recombination/repair, 22 transfer-associated genes, and 66 predicted phage components. These features underscore the genome’s plasticity and potential for acquiring novel traits, including virulence and resistance.

Discussion

B. thailandensis is widely considered an environmental, less virulent species that is related to B. pseudomallei and B. mallei, yet growing evidence suggests that under certain genomic configurations or environmental pressures, it can acquire features associated with pathogenicity [14, 55, 56]. In this study, we present a comprehensive genomic and functional characterization of a B. thailandensis strain (BUR-TH_250524) isolated from soil sample in Kerala, India. The assembled genome reveals multiple features of potential clinical relevance, including antibiotic resistance genes, virulence factors, mobile genetic elements, and prophage regions. Together, these findings underscore the potential for adaptive evolution in environmental B. thailandensis populations [57].

Species identification was confirmed by 16S rRNA sequencing, rMLST, digital DNA-DNA hybridization. ANI analysis revealed that with BUR-TH_250524 showing >99.75% identity to the strain B. thailandensis BPM followed by E264 and 2022DZh with 99.70%. However, phylogenetic clustering also revealed close proximity to B. thailandensis E264, BPM and 2022DZh strains. BPM, isolated from a human patient in China, exhibits higher virulence in mice than the avirulent strain E264 [13, 14]. Its pathogenicity has been linked to several unique virulence-associated genes, including components of the Type III and Type VI secretion systems and secreted effectors that enhance host invasion and immune evasion. Genomic insertions and translocations further elevate the virulence gene expression, making its profile closely resemble that of B. pseudomallei [14, 58]. The genomic proximity of our environmental isolate to these strains raises the possibility of shared ancestry or convergence towards increased pathogenic potential. The problems are further compounded by the fact that phenotypic identification methods like VITEK also mis-identified our isolate as B. pseudomallei. BUR-TH_250524 carries several AMR determinants as identified by multiple tools. AMRFinderPlus identified blaPEN-L (92.94%) and blaOXA (100%; OXA-42), while ResFinder confirmed blaOXA-42 (93.46%), a class D oxacillin-hydrolyzing β-lactamase common in B. pseudomallei. Further it encodes seven strict-hit antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes, including efflux pumps (adeF, amrA, amrB), and the β-lactamase gene OXA-42. The presence of the vanO cluster reflects the intrinsic glycopeptide non-susceptibility of Burkholderia species rather than an acquired mechanism conferring clinical resistance. While these genes are often associated with intrinsic resistance in Burkholderia species, their presence in a soil-derived B. thailandensis strain suggests that environmental reservoirs may contribute to the maintenance and spread of resistance determinants [59–61]. No perfect CARD hits were identified, and phenotypic resistance was not assessed in this study; however, the presence of multiple resistance mechanisms warrants closer surveillance [35]. Although the genome encodes several intrinsic and putative AMR determinants, that may contribute to the naturally reduced susceptibility of B. thailandensis, phenotypic validation through MIC testing will be essential to validate their functional impact. In addition, the isolate harboured 225 virulence-associated genes, including complete Type III and Type VI secretion systems, motility factors, and stress survival modules [5]. The detection of complete Type III and Type V operons in BUR-TH_250524 highlights the genetic potential for active protein translocation and interbacterial interactions typical of Burkholderia environmental isolates. The genome contained a single complete wcbR gene and an additional thioester reductase–like homolog showing partial sequence similarity that may influence surface composition and immune interactions. In B. pseudomallei, upregulation or duplication of capsule genes has been linked to complement resistance and enhanced persistence in host environments [62]. Although no canonical toxins or lethal effectors were found, the combination of capsule biosynthesis genes and secretion systems points to an underlying potential for pathogenicity, particularly in immunocompromised hosts [63].

BUR-TH_250524 displayed a high degree of genomic plasticity, with 215 mobile OG elements identified, including integrases, transposases, and genes involved in horizontal gene transfer [53]. Seven prophage regions were detected, five of which were assignable to the Myoviridae family [43]. These regions encoded structural proteins, lytic enzymes, and recombination modules, consistent with phage origin. Phage-mediated gene transfer is a known driver of rapid adaptation in Burkholderia spp. and could facilitate acquisition of accessory genes involved in antibiotic resistance or virulence [64].

Although five CRISPR arrays were identified, no Cas genes were detected, suggesting an incomplete or vestigial CRISPR-Cas system [42, 43]. The absence of a functional defence mechanism may increase permissiveness to foreign DNA uptake, including phage-derived or plasmid-borne genes. This aligns with observations in other Burkholderia species where genomic islands are frequently acquired via horizontal gene transfer in the absence of CRISPR interference [65].

Despite its environmental origin, BUR-TH_250524 exhibits several genomic features shared with clinical B. thailandensis and even B. pseudomallei. These include core virulence determinants, mobile genetic elements, and a partial capsule biosynthesis operon. While the genome size (~ 6.93 Mb) is slightly smaller than B. pseudomallei (~ 7.2 Mb), the overall organization remains conserved, and the presence of multiple hypothetical proteins suggests that additional, uncharacterized functions may contribute to adaptability or host interaction [66].

The proximity of this isolate to known clinical strains raises important questions about the ecological boundaries of pathogenicity in the B. pseudomallei complex. Environmental strains may serve as a genomic reservoir for virulence traits, particularly under selective pressures such as antibiotic use, cohabitation with animal hosts, or phage-mediated recombination. It also needs to be investigated if B. thailandensis infections are being misidentified as B. pseudomallei.

Limitations and future directions

This study was limited to in silico analysis. Phenotypic validation of antimicrobial resistance and virulence (e.g., macrophage infection assays, mouse models) will be required to assess functional significance. Additionally, transcriptomic or proteomic profiling under host-mimicking conditions would help clarify the expression potential of virulence and resistance genes. Phenotypic validation (MIC testing) will be essential to confirm the functional relevance of the AMR genes identified in this study.

Future studies should include a larger number of environmental Burkholderia isolates from India to determine whether the observed genomic features are unique to this strain or part of a broader trend. The inclusion of longitudinal surveillance data may also provide insights into the stability or evolution of these traits in soil ecosystems.

Conclusion

This study presents the whole-genome analysis of an environmental Burkholderia thailandensis isolate from Kerala, India, revealing a genomic profile that includes antimicrobial resistance determinants, multiple virulence-associated genes, prophage regions, and a high density of mobile genetic elements. Despite its environmental origin, the isolate shares several key features with clinically relevant B. thailandensis strains, including those previously implicated in human infections. The detection of a capsule biosynthesis gene (wcbR), secretion system components, and efflux pumps suggests the potential for enhanced adaptability and opportunistic pathogenicity. These findings highlight the importance of genomic monitoring of environmental and clinical Burkholderia strains, especially in regions where melioidosis is endemic or underreported. Broader surveillance efforts integrating genomic, ecological, and phenotypic data will be essential for anticipating and mitigating the emergence of pathogenic variants within environmental reservoirs.
