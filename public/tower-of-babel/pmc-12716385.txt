Dynamic control of molecular transport in MXene transistor membranes
Authors: Aaditya Pendse, Arjun V. Yennemadi, Thomas J. Ferron, Anthony van Buuren, Armin VahidMohammadi, Teng Zhang, Yury Gogotsi, Martin Z. Bazant, Aleksandr Noy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Controlled spatial confinement and surface properties of lamellar 2D nanomaterial membranes could enhance many precision separation processes. Traditionally, researchers view channel dimensions, surface properties, and permeation rates of these membranes as intrinsic properties that cannot be modulated in operando. We report that gate voltage applied to the conducting laminar MXene membrane can modulate the permeation rate of ions and neutral solutes, as well as its effective size rejection. In operando wide-angle x-ray scattering measurements reveal that these changes are not driven by electrically induced variations in the d spacing of the MXene layers. Instead, experimental data and continuum electrokinetic modeling reveal that ion transport through the MXene channels is primarily affected by Donnan equilibrium at the membrane-solution interface. We also report a strong increase in the permeation rates through the membrane under a low-frequency ac voltage gating regime that we attribute to diffusioosmotic flow oscillations induced in the membrane. Overall, MXene “transistor” membranes provide a previously unidentified approach to dynamic control of molecular separations.

INTRODUCTION

Precise ion sieving technologies are essential components of resource extraction, energy production, and environmental remediation applications (1–4) that range from large-scale desalination plants producing more than a billion liters of water per day to nanogenerators harnessing osmotic power using 0.65-nm-thick nanosheets (5, 6). Lamellar membranes based on two-dimensional (2D) materials have catalyzed research into ion transport behavior under nanoconfinement, opening new avenues for advanced ion separation technologies (7). The atomic thinness, surface hydrophilicity, load density, and abundant surface functional groups (8, 9) of 2D materials, such as graphene and its derivatives (10, 11), transition-metal dichalcogenides (12, 13), and hexagonal boron nitride (14, 15), allow these membranes to achieve competitive ion and water transport rates and selectivities compared to conventional materials (1–4, 16). Transition metal carbides and/or nitrides (MXenes), with their hydrophilic surface (17, 18), excellent thermal (19, 20) and mechanical stability, and high electrical conductivity (21, 22), have recently emerged as a promising platform for facilitating selective and rapid ion transport. Experimental investigations and computer simulations have indicated that strong nano-confinement in lamellar membranes and the resulting ion dehydration barriers remain the primary drivers of ion selectivity (12, 21).

Unfortunately, hydrophilic surfaces of these 2D membranes may cause uncontrolled swelling in aqueous environments, altering the interlayer spacing (21, 23, 24) and thus changing membrane selectivity. Researchers demonstrated that negatively charged MXene (Ti3C2Tx) membranes (25, 26) show selective transport of metal ions with varying size and charge with higher fluxes compared to their graphene-based counterparts due to increased water content in their interlayers. However, similar to many other 2D membranes, ion intercalation into MXene (Ti3C2Tx) membranes can uncontrollably alter the interlayer spacing (9). Therefore, active dynamic control over membrane properties using an external stimulus, e.g., a gating potential, can modulate membrane selectivity and enable control of the ion permeation rate (27, 28).

In this study, we present a dynamically adjusting, self-pumping MXene membrane (Fig. 1A), capable of efficiently regulating the ion permeation rate using a small gating voltage applied to the membrane. We attribute these effects to the gate voltage modulating the electrostatic equilibrium and ion concentrations in the water-filled inter layer of the MXene membrane. In operando wide-angle x-ray scattering (WAXS) measurements indicate that this modulation also results in changes in the d spacing, but those changes are too small to alter the ion permeation characteristics. In addition, we demonstrate that applying an ac gate voltage to the membrane enhances the transport rates of ions and neutral solutes and can control the effective molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of the membrane.

RESULTS

We fabricated lamellar MXene (Ti3C2Tx) nanosheet membranes by vacuum-assisted filtration (see Materials and Methods for details). The Ti3C2Tx nanosheets that we used were produced using the minimally intensive layers delamination approach, which uses manual shaking instead of sonication to avoid fragmenting the MXene flakes (29, 30), resulting in Ti3C2Tx membranes with nanosheet sizes of 3 to 5 µm. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the membrane cross section (Fig. 1B) confirm the creation of a freestanding Ti3C2Tx membrane approximately 10 µm thick with well-ordered stacking of the nanosheets. Our membranes showed an electrical conductivity of 4900±75 S cm−1, as determined by the four-probe technique, which was consistent with the previous reported results (31). WAXS on dry MXene membranes showed a strong (002) reflection near q=0.441 Å−1 corresponding to a d spacing of d(002)=14.2 Å (fig. S1). The d spacing measured with WAXS accounts for the interlayer spacing as well as the thickness of an individual MXene nanosheet. If we assume that the MXene nanosheet thickness is ≈9.8 Å (32), we can estimate the interlayer spacing as 4.4 Å (less than 2 water molecules). Wetting the membrane with 10 mM KCl resulted in the (002) reflection shifting to q=0.397 Å, indicating that the MXene interlayer spacing expanded to 5.9 Å.

Ion transport measurements

For ion transport measurements, we have sandwiched the MXene membrane between two fluid reservoirs in a PermeGear cell that allowed us to apply a controlling gate voltage to the membrane (fig. S2). Excessive anodic bias can lead to oxidation of Ti3C2Tx, potentially altering its surface chemistry and affecting ion transport behavior; therefore, to ensure the structural and chemical integrity of the membrane, we limited the applied gate voltage to the range between −0.5 and 0.5 V. Cyclic voltammetry measurements confirmed the electrochemical stability of Ti3C2Tx within this window, showing no discernible Faradaic peaks or parasitic reactions on the MXene surface (fig. S3A). To confirm the absence of redox activity on the membrane surface, we monitored the leakage current through our system. Notably, after the initial transient capacitive current spike, the gate leakage current quickly dropped below 1 μA (fig. S3B), which would contribute less than 1% to the ion fluxes typically measured in our experiments.

K+ ion permeation rate through the membrane, driven by a concentration gradient and quantified with ion exchange chromatography (IEC), was strongly dependent on the applied gate voltage (Vg) with the transport characteristic resembling that of a transistor device (Fig. 1C), although with a relatively low on/off ratio. Researchers reported higher on/off ratios in devices using potential gradient to drive ions across the transistor devices (28, 33, 34). This gating behavior was fully reversible (Fig. 1D) and we could repeatedly enhance the ion flow through the nanochannels of our “transistor” membrane at +0.5 V and restrict it using the −0.5 V gate over multiple cycles. Similar switch-on/switch-off behavior was also observed for permeation of divalent Mg2+ and trivalent Al3+ ions with a common Cl− anion. The permeation rates for Mg2+ and Al3+ were, however, lower compared to those of K+. Repeated cycling of the applied gate voltage confirmed the stability and reversibility of voltage gated ion transport, showing consistent ion blocking at negative voltages and transport at positive voltages. Our modeling suggests that this gating effect is dominated primarily by variations in the concentration of the dominant K+ ion at the dilute end of the membrane (see the “Modeling of ion transport in MXene channels under applied gate voltage” section for details).

To investigate the mechanism for this voltage-regulated ion transport in the Ti3C2Tx membrane further, we studied the transport of neutral sugar and polyethylene glycol (PEG) molecules with molecular weights ranging from 75 to 1500 Da in the presence of a KCl electrolyte (Fig. 1E). The measured solute permeation rates exhibited similar gate voltage–dependent characteristics to the ion transport, with the apparent rejection of each solute shifting in response to the applied gate voltage. This result indicates that this transistor membrane’s ability to control transport is quite universal. We observed that the effective MWCO shifted systematically with the gate voltage (Fig. 1E, inset), increasing from 200 Da at −0.5 V to 850 Da at 0.5 V, indicating a remarkable tunability of MXene transistor membranes.

When we removed the background KCl electrolyte from the system, this tunable selectivity behavior vanished and the membrane performance reverted to gate-independent behavior that was solely based on the solute size (fig. S4A). These findings strongly suggest that the ion equilibrium at or near the surface of the gated Ti3C2Tx membrane sheet is critical to the mechanism of the observed membrane tunability. This observation is also supported by another control experiment where we kept the background electrolyte present, but replaced water with a low polarity solvent (ethanol). Under these conditions, we also observed no dependence of the membrane rejection characteristics on the gate voltage (fig. S4B).

MXene interlayer spacing in transistor membranes under applied gate potential

To quantify the extent at which the MXene interlayer spacing is influenced by the applied gate voltage, we conducted in operando WAXS measurements (see the Supplementary Materials for measurement details). A 2D WAXS image of a MXene under −0.5 V bias (fig. S6A and B) shows that the (002) reflection is isolated from the background signal associated with scattering from the electrochemical cell (fig. S5A and B) and electrolyte and thus provides an ideal window into the MXene membrane spacing behavior. For these experiments, we subjected the MXene membrane to a square-wave potential that started with a prebias of 0 V and then jumped to a chosen gate voltage, Vg, before returning to the original prebias value (Fig. 2A). The cell current, monitored throughout the experiment, indicated that negligible current was flowing through the membrane when Vg < 0.5 V besides the capacitive current spikes induced by the membrane voltage changes (figs. S7 to S13).

Our WAXS results find that an applied gate voltage causes the MXene interlayer spacing to either expand or contract depending on the bias. At the start of the square wave, during the prebias portion of the gating waveform, d(002) remains at a baseline value near 16.56 Å (Fig. 2A) for all conditions measured. Notably, this spacing value is 0.76 Å greater than that of a freshly hydrated membrane due to an initial expansion of the interlayer spacing that took place during our conditioning protocol (see Materials and Methods for details and Fig. 2A for an representative experiment). Stepping the potential to Vg < −0.5 V, causes d(002) to contract toward a value of d(002)=16.27 Å after ~4 min of the equilibration. This contraction starts rapidly, with ~80% of the spacing change occurring within the first few seconds, followed by a much slower additional contraction. This kinetics, which can be described by a double exponential, is reminiscent of the two-step dynamic response observed by Sobyra et al. (35) for Li+ ion intercalation into MXenes.

At the end of the pulse, the membrane returns to 0 V and d(002) restores to near its original position, 16.58 Å, confirming that this contraction is reversible in operando. WAXS data confirm that membrane contraction is bias dependent (Fig. 2B). Positive values of Vg cause the interlayer spacing to expand as evident by the shift in the (002) reflection to lower values of q. The change in d(002), compared to the spacing prior the voltage pulse, follows an approximate linear relationship with respect to the applied Vg (Fig. 2C). The direction of these changes in the interlayer spacing aligns with our experimental observations of modified ion flux under similar applied bias voltages (Fig. 1C), where, at positive gate voltages, an expansion of the interlayer spacing correlates with the enhanced ion flux, while a contraction of the interlayer spacing seen at negative gate voltages is consistent with reduced permeation. The observed relative shift in d spacing, particularly in the presence of an electrolyte, can be attributed to gate voltage–induced osmotic swelling as the charged MXene sheets accumulate counterions in the membrane sheet interlayers.

Even though the qualitative trends align with our transport data, the magnitude of the observed changes in d spacing (∼0.5Å) remains far too small to cause the transport gating behavior observed in our MXene membranes. Specifically, the substantial dependence of the MWCO of the neutral solutes [hydrodynamic radius (Rh) ranging from 0.3 to 1.2 nm (36)] on the applied gate voltage cannot be explained by these rather minor changes in the d spacing. This observation suggests that ionic equilibrium at and in the vicinity of the MXene surfaces influences membrane’s mass transport characteristics much stronger than the alterations in its d spacing.

Surface charge of the MXene membranes

Streaming current measurements (fig. S14 and Fig. 3C; see also the Supplementary Materials for details) reinforce this conclusion. When we applied hydrostatic pressure in the range of 0 to 2 kPa to the MXene membrane, we observed the expected linear increase of the measured ion current with the applied pressure. Notably, the streaming current values varied strongly as a function of applied gate voltage, again indicating that ion equilibrium at the MXene surface was the major driving force for the observed gating behavior in our membranes.

Modeling of ion transport in MXene channels under applied gate voltage

To test our hypothesis, and to gain a better understanding of ionic equilibrium at the MXene surface and how it influences ion transport under an applied gate voltage, we constructed a simple continuum physical model (Fig. 3A and fig. S15) describing the steady transport of a symmetric monovalent electrolyte through the conductive MXene membrane under a dc voltage gating. We follow an approach similar to the work of Zhang et al. (37) where the membrane is modeled as identical nanopores in parallel, spanning the entire length of the membrane, and associated with a porosity-to-tortuosity ratio, ϵ/τ. As MXene sheets are rigid and flat on the scale of a single pore, we assume a rectangular geometry for the pores. Briefly, the model analyzes electrokinetics in a single such nanopore and takes into account the three major physical phenomena: electrostatics, mass transport, and fluid flow (see the Supplementary Materials for a detailed description of the model). The pore is connected to a reservoir at each end, and Donnan equilibrium exists between the reservoirs and solution in the pore (Fig. 3A). The strong asymmetry observed in ion flux about Vg = 0 suggests an additional force “pulling” cations into the pore. This force could occur as a consequence of the highly electronegative surface groups as well as image charge forces. We describe these effects by introducing a phenomenological parameter μatt that accounts for the excess chemical potential of cations when they are inside the pore, in a manner similar to the modified Donnan model of Biesheuvel et al. (38).

Last, our model assumes cross-sectional electroneutrality within the membrane’s nanopores, where the surface charge is balanced by the net ionic charge at every cross section. The total surface charge (X) on a conductive MXene sheet is then written as the sum of the constant intrinsic surface charge (Xc) due to the ionization of terminal groups like -OH and -O- on the MXenes, and electronic charge induced by the applied gate voltageX=Xc+Cs(Vg−ϕ)(1)where the electronic charge is written as a Stern layer capacitance (Cs) multiplying the potential difference across the layer. Because of the high polarizability of the MXene surface terminations, we further assume a slowly increasing linear form of the Stern layer capacitanceCsCs0=1+Vg−ϕΔV0(2)where Cs0 and ΔV0 are positive constants. This form also accounts for the higher compressibility of anions, which are attracted at more positive gate voltages (39).

For modeling diffusion-driven transport, we assume that the two reservoirs are at the same hydrostatic pressure, that one reservoir is at the concentration C0 = 10 mM and the other is at the concentration C0/106, and that no current is flowing through the pore (open-circuit condition, fig. S15). To describe the streaming current experiments, the model assumes that the reservoirs contain the same electrolyte concentration C0 and are at the same electric potential (short-circuit condition) but that one of the reservoirs is under a higher applied pressure head, ph0. In both systems, charge conservation enforces that the two reservoirs are always bulk-electroneutral. Overall, the model contains five fitting parameters, μatt, representing the attractive excess chemical potential term; Xc, the dimensionless chemical surface charge; Cs0, the Stern layer capacitance when ϕ = Vg; ΔV0, the voltage scale in the Stern layer capacitance function; and ϵ/τ, the porosity-to-tortuosity ratio.

A global fit of our model to the experimentally measured datasets on gate voltage–controlled diffusion and streaming current measurements (Fig. 3, B and C) produced excellent agreement between the model prediction and the data at the rather reasonable fitting parameter values given by μatt=4.26 kBT, Xc=−1.72 mC/m2, Cs0=0.25 μF/cm2, ΔV0=42.7 kBT/e, and ϵ/τ=0.11. Furthermore, additional parameter sweep studies (fig. S16) show that the model is mostly sensitive to the variables that describe variations in surface charge. Lower values of the chemical surface charge density Xc result in an increased permeation rate and decreased streaming current, and a larger capacitance Cs0 increases the sensitivity of the permeation and streaming current to the applied gate voltage. While the effect of the surface charge on the streaming current is intuitive, the predicted increase in permeation with the reduction in surface charge (e.g., at higher applied gate voltage values) is not.

To rationalize this behavior, we first quantify the contributions of each transport mechanism to overall flux (Fig. 3D). Estimates (see the Supplementary Materials for details) show that, by a large margin, diffusion remains the dominant mechanism for ion transport under all applied gate voltages. Furthermore, the model estimates that the presence of these electrostatic effects increases the diffusion by a factor of 6 to 9, again demonstrating that electrostatically induced mechanisms dominate transport in our membranes. Because the model does not contain any physical mechanism for changes in ion diffusivity, diffusion enhancement must arise from changes in the concentration gradient inside the pore. Further analysis reveals a complicated effect of the applied gate voltage on the ion concentration gradient inside the pore. Specifically, the Donnan equilibrium at the pore ends has a very unequal effect on the total ion concentration (Fig. 3E and fig. S17). In the pores close to the feed, the applied gate voltage produces relatively weak modulations of the total ion concentration. In contrast, in the pores close to the permeate, strong negative surface charge induced by a negative gate voltage (Vg = −0.5 V) attracts a large concentration of cations, which reduces drastically (by 94%) at positive gate voltage values (Vg = +0.5 V). This effect produces steeper internal concentration gradients at higher gate voltages and faster diffusion despite the lower surface charge.

The model predicts that concentration gradients can also induce diffusioosmotic transport (40–42). Donnan equilibrium at the concentrated end of the nanopore generates a large cation concentration at the end. Because the total pressure, which is a sum of hydrostatic and osmotic pressure, must balance that of the feed reservoir, this results in an increase in the local hydrostatic pressure in the pores at the concentrated end, inducing fluid flow. Though the contribution of advection to the total ion flux is small (~6%), it is not negligible (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, the induced diffusioosmotic velocity increases with increasing gate voltage (Fig. 3F), again due to Donnan equilibrium steepening the concentration gradient and, hence, the pressure gradient. This trend (42) could also explain the observed decrease in the rejection of neutral sugars at high gate voltage in the presence of background KCl, where the diffusioosmotic flow will “drag” the neutral molecules across the membrane and thus decrease the size discrimination characteristics of the membrane. The MXene membrane achieves its sharpest rejection profile at −0.5 V where the advection is minimized. In the absence of the electrolyte, advection is not induced, and rejection is determined solely by the shape and size of each sugar/PEG molecule. Last, we note that the observed gate voltage dependence of large neutral molecule rejection, together with the presence of advective transport within the membrane, parallels the trends reported in recent literature (31, 43–45).

Mass transport under oscillating applied gate voltage

While dc gating effectively modulates ion transport by tuning the surface charge of the MXene membrane, it primarily influences steady-state ion distribution and selectivity. To evaluate our membrane’s potential for dynamic modulation of solute transport, we have explored the ion and neutral solute transport under oscillating applied gate voltage. For these experiments, instead of using a constant gate potential, we have applied a low-frequency (0 to 300 Hz) periodic sinusoidal gate voltage centered at 0 V to the membrane. Just as with the dc-gated transport experiments, we kept the applied gate voltage within the −0.5 V to +0.5 V range for all the ac-gated experiments to avoid electrochemical degradation of MXene sheets. Unexpectedly, we have observed that the membrane exhibited self-pumping behavior where such ac gating produced a strong increase in the K+ ion flux (Fig. 4A). As the frequency of the ac gating signal increased, the permeation rate also increased until it saturated after ~100 Hz frequency. We have also observed similar self-pumping behavior for Li+ ion transport (Fig. 4B).

Frequency-dependent ion selectivity values, measured for transport of binary ion mixtures (see Materials and Methods for details), indicate that the membrane exhibits reduced selectivity between monovalent ions and di- and trivalent ions at low frequencies, which slowly climbs to ~80% of the bulk values at high frequency, perhaps reflecting the relative lack of discrimination between ions of different sizes for diffusioosmotic transport (Fig. 4C). For the monovalent ions, relative K+/Li+ selectivity reaches the bulk value at 50 Hz and lastly exceeds the intrinsic bulk selectivity values at 100 Hz.

We have also observed a similar ac gating–induced enhancement behavior for transport of neutral solvents (Fig. 4D). Neutral molecule diffusion was consistently enhanced under the ac drive in the presence of 1 mM KCl, LiCl, or MgCl2 as background electrolyte, with MgCl2 providing the strongest enhancement (Fig. 4D, inset). For all three background electrolytes, the heaviest neutral molecule showed the largest enhancement with the applied ac drive.

The observed diffusion enhancement is reminiscent of the increase in the effective diffusion coefficient predicted by a model developed by Marbach et al. (46), where “wiggling” of the channel wall size induced the collective motion of fluid in the membrane and enhanced ion transport asDe=D01−a022R021−3(D≈/D0)21+(D≈/D0)2(3)where R0 represents the effective nanochannel spacing, a0 is the amplitude of the surface fluctuations, D0 is the ion diffusion coefficient at dc bias (Vg = 0 V), D≈/D0 represents a Peclet-like number (Pe) and defined by Pe=l2/2D0T is the diffusion associated with the external stimuli (applied voltage bias), and D≈ is the effective diffusion constant in presence of surface wiggling.

Could the small variations of the MXene channel spacing observed in the WAXS results be responsible for this transport enhancement? The mathematical form of the Marbach-Bocquet model can fit our data (Fig. 4, A and B), but a closer investigation of the fit parameters reveals notable differences. The model fit indicates that the observed transport enhancement requires a rather large amplitude of the effective changes in the channel size (a0/R0≈1, i.e., almost 100% of the channel size), whereas WAXS data indicate that these variations in d spacing are extremely small, on the order of ~0.5 Å or 5%. Moreover, time-resolved WAXS data indicate that the voltage-gated expansion/contraction of the interlayer spacing is a slow process even relative to the low drive frequencies used in our experiments. Thus, we conclude that physical modulation of the interlayer spacing cannot cause the observed self-pumping behavior.

Instead, we look at whether our dc electrokinetic model can explain the observed increase in permeation under ac gating. Using a pseudo–steady-state approximation, our model predicts an 8% increase in permeation when time-averaged over a full period of the applied sinusoidal ac voltage, substantially below the near 200% increase observed for K+ and 100% increase for Li+. This discrepancy suggests that transient effects play a dominant role in the system. From Fig. 4 (A and B), we observe that the characteristic frequency of these transients is around 10 to 100 Hz.

One such transient phenomenon is the charging of electric double layers by counterions, characterized by the RC charging timescale (1/fRC). At ac frequencies much smaller than fRC, counterions have enough time to transport into or out of the interlayers and fully balance the surface charge set up by the gate voltage at every point along the membrane axis. However, at ac frequencies much larger than fRC, the charging process cannot keep up with changes in the gate voltage, potentially leading to altered concentration gradients and unbalanced surface charges downstream of the feed.

We can estimate this timescale by separately determining the ionic resistance and the electric double layer capacitance. If we consider the case of ions transporting from the feed reservoir to the middle of the membrane, the effective path length is L2×τ and the effective area of ion transport is A×ϵ, where L is the membrane thickness and A is the cross-sectional area of the membrane. The ionic resistance is then L/2σIAϵ/τ, where σI=2e2c0DkBT is the ionic conductivity. Assuming the MXene sheets cover most of the membrane’s cross section, the effective surface area at which double layers form (up to the middle) is approximately given by (2A)×Nl/2, where Nl∼L/d(002)∼7000 is the total number of layers in the membrane, and the factor of 2 comes from each interlayer forming two separate double layers. We can then estimate the total electric double layer capacitance to be on the order of Cs0ANl. We can then combine these estimates to obtain the RC-charging timescale, 1/fRC, as1fRC=L/2σIAϵ/τ×Cs0ANl=Cs0LNl2σIϵ/τ∼5.3 ms(4)which corresponds to a characteristic frequency of fRC∼190 Hz. This crude estimate of the RC-charging frequency is close to the 10- to 100-Hz frequency range where we observed the saturation of the ion flux enhancement under the ac gating of the MXene membrane, suggesting that double layer charging across the membrane could indeed be the dominant transient underlying this effect. It is possible, then, that the self-pumping behavior arises from a coupling of the double layer charging process to diffusioosmosis within the nanochannels, in a manner akin to ac electroosmotic pumping (47, 48). This could enhance the advective component of flux, explaining not only the enhanced permeation of ions, but also that of the neutral sugars. We note that accurately capturing these phenomena would require a more detailed time-resolved analysis of electric double layer charging and coupled electrokinetic transport across the membrane. While this analysis is beyond the scope of our work, the large enhancement in molecular permeation under ac gating is a compelling observation that argues for the need to develop and validate such models.

DISCUSSION

We show that application of gate voltage to conductive lamellar MXene membranes can induce strong variation in membrane permeability for ions and neutral solutes. The gating potential induces a complex ion equilibrium in narrow MXene channels that strongly alters MXene surface charge, local ion concentrations, and ion gradients. In operando WAXS studies revealed only minimal MXene d spacing changes that cannot account for the observed transport modulation; instead, we show that these phenomena are caused largely by the strong modulations of the MXene surface charge induced by the applied gate voltage and ion gradients induced by the Donnan equilibrium at the pore entrances. The applied gate voltage also has a strong effect on the neural solute transport, resulting in a tunable MWCO of the MXene membrane, which our model attributes to the effects of induced diffusioosmotic flow. When the membrane was subjected to an oscillating ac gate voltage, we also observed a strong enhancement of transport efficiency akin to a self-pumping behavior, which we attributed to collective diffusioosmotic fluid motion induced by the oscillating gate voltage. Our findings establish these electrically gated transistor membranes as a promising approach for real-time regulation of ion permeation rates. Enhanced permeation efficiency delivered by these self-pumping membranes could hold substantial potential for various applications, including drug delivery and dialysis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Exfoliated MXene concentrate solutions were provided by Lim et al. (49). Potassium chloride (KCl, ≥99%), lithium chloride (LiCl, ≥99%), magnesium chloride (MgCl2, ≥99%), and aluminum chloride (AlCl3, ≥99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Neutral solutes, including glycine (≥99%), arabinose (≥99%), glucose (≥99%), sucrose (≥99%), raffinose (≥99%), and PEG (molecular weight 1000, 1500, and 2000 Da), were also obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. All chemicals were used without further purification. Aqueous solutions were prepared with Millipore Direct-Q 3 deionized (DI) water (18.2 MΩ cm−1) and reagent grade ethanol (≥99.5%). The polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane filter with a 0.45-μm pore size was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich.

Fabrication of MXene membranes

The MXene concentrate (1 mg/ml) was diluted 10-fold with DI water and sonicated in an ice bath under vacuum to achieve a stable suspension with a concentration of 0.1 mg/ml. The ice bath and vacuum prevent the oxidation of the MXene nanosheets due to the high temperatures during sonication. The resulting suspension was vacuum filtered through a PTFE membrane with a 0.45-μm pore size and allowed to dry at room temperature for 10 to 12 hours. The resulting sheet exhibited a metallic luster and could be easily peeled from the PTFE filter support.

Ion permeation under applied gate voltage

The ion permeation tests were conducted using a PermeGear side-by-side diffusion cell with an effective membrane area of 0.52 cm2 (fig. S2). Before testing, the MXene membrane was soaked in the respective salt solution for 2 hours to allow the membrane to hydrate and equilibrate. For a typical single-ion permeation test, 10 ml of 0.01 M salt solution and 10 ml of a 0.0197 M PEG-2000 solution (0.0298 M for MgCl2 and 0.0398 M for AgCl3) in DI water [PEG-2000, dh ≈ 3 nm (36) was added to balance osmotic pressure between the two compartments] were placed into the feed and permeate compartments of the cell. Both cell compartments were also magnetically stirred to minimize concentration polarization effects.

Gate voltage was applied to the membrane with a platinum electrode connected to the MXene membrane with conductive carbon glue (PELCO, Ted Pella Inc., USA) and electrically insulated with marine epoxy. A three-electrode setup with Ag/AgCl pellet electrodes was used, with the reference and counter electrodes positioned in the feed reservoir. A bias voltage was applied using an electrochemical workstation (Autolab PGSTAT128N, Metrohm, USA). For ac-driven experiments, the electrochemical workstation was replaced with a function generator (Keysight 33220A, Agilent, USA) to apply an ac gate voltage at varying frequencies ranging from 2 to 300 Hz.

To quantify ion diffusion, we collected aliquots from the permeate compartment and analyzed them with the IEC system (Dionex Aquion, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). All experiments were conducted at room temperature (~25°C).

The permeation rate, Pion (mol m−2 hour−1), was calculated using the equationPion=Jion⋅Atmem(5)where Jion (mol m−3 hour−1) is the ion flux per unit time measured experimentally, A (m2) is the effective membrane area, and tmem (m) is the membrane thickness.

Neutral solute transport and rejection measurements

Neutral solute rejection was evaluated using a PermGear side-by-side diffusion cell, the same setup used in previous experiments. The feed and permeate solutions were continuously stirred to reduce concentration polarization near the membrane surface. The feed concentration of the neutral solute solutions was 0.01 M, with a 0.01 M solution of PEG-2000 used on the permeate side to match the osmotic pressure of the feed. To assess the effect of the background electrolyte, 0.001 M KCl was added to the neutral solute solution, and a 0.012 M solution of PEG-2000 was used on the permeate side to balance the osmotic pressure. This setup ensured that only diffusion effects were studied, eliminating any contributions from water flow due to osmotic pressure gradients. Further, to investigate the effect of the solvent on neutral ion transport, the same cell and ion concentrations were used, but with ethanol replacing water as the solvent.

A typical experiment was conducted for 24 hours, and the concentrations of feed and permeate were analyzed by collecting aliquots and analyzing them with a high-performance liquid chromatography system equipped with a Phenomenex BioSep SEC-S3000 column, a mobile phase of 70% water with 30% acetonitrile (v/v), and a Waters 2414 RI detector. The flow rate was set to 0.7 ml/min. RNS was calculated asRNS=(Cf−CpCf)×100%(6)where Cf and Cp are the concentrations of the feed and permeate, respectively.

Wide-angle x-ray scattering

WAXS measurements were performed at Beamline 7.3.3 at the Advanced Light Source. The photon energy was fixed at 10 keV, and all data were collected on a Pilatus 2M area detector with an exposure time of 1 s and an angle of incidence of 0.25°. The detector was positioned ≈28.5 cm away from the center of the sample and calibrated with a silver behenate standard. The d spacing was calculated as d(002)=2∗π/q∗, where q* is the peak position off the (002) reflection. The position is determined by modeling the data with a Lorentzian peak shape and a linear background to account for scattering from the electrolyte and in operando cell. All data were reduced and analyzed in Igor Pro (v.9.0.1.2) using Nika (v.1.85) (50).

Experiments were conducted in open air using a custom in operando electrochemical cell (fig. S6). The cell consists of an open electrolyte reservoir that contacts the MXene from below, keeping it away from the incident x-rays and reducing electrolyte scattering. A polyamide window was fixed to the top surface of the MXene to prevent electrolyte evaporation during experiments. Measurements without this window resulted in sample oxidation, which was found to greatly influence the interlayer spacing of the MXene. A counter (platinum mesh) and reference (Ag/AgCl, Koslow) electrode were positioned within the reservoir via two fill ports on either side of the sample. A platinum wire is connected to the MXene with a conductive adhesive and acts as the working electrode. The counter electrode was positioned ≈3 mm below the MXene within the electrolyte. The MXene was epoxied to the cell to prevent leaks and electrical shorts during operation.

WAXS measurements were conducted in both ex situ and in operando conditions. All membranes were first mounted into the electrochemical cell and measured ex situ without any electrolyte; these results area labeled as “Dry” in all figures. The reservoir is then filled with electrolyte (KCl, 10 mM) and electrodes are added to the system. The sample is then measured following this initial hydration. Before any in operando characterization, the MXene is conditioned by applying a fixed potential of −0.5 V until the (002) peak position has stabilized for ≈5 min. At this point, the potential is returned to 0 V and held at this condition for an additional ≈20 min (see fig. S11 for conditioning results). In operando WAXS was conducted by applying a square-wave potential to the MXene using a GAMRY Reference 620 potentiostat. Each experiment consisted of a prestep voltage at 0 V (held for 30 s) before the potential was stepped to a fixed bias (held for 1200 s) before returning to 0 V (held again for up to 1200 s). WAXS measurements began during the prestep voltage and were collected periodically throughout the measurement period. The x-ray beam was laterally offset between each experiment to reduce radiation damage. Processed in operando data are given in figs. S12 to S17 for each experiment.

Streaming current measurements

For these measurements, the membrane was placed into the measurements cell where both cis and trans compartments were filled with 10 mM KCl. The cell was sealed except for one port that was connected to a graduated tube filled with water to create the required pressure head. Ion current was measured with Ag/AgCl electrodes inserted into the cis and trans compartments and recorded with a Keithley 6485 picoammeter. Gate voltage was applied to the membrane as described in previous sections.
