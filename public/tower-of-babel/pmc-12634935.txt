Preliminary screening of pomegranate-derived compounds for antimicrobial and anti-virulence effects against cariogenic streptococci
Authors: Nurhayati Mohamad Zain, Indah Mohd Amin, Fathilah Abdul Razak, Mohamed Ibrahim Abu Hassan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a biofilm-mediated disease, dental caries is primarily attributed to the activity of Streptococcus mutans and Streptococcus sobrinus, key contributors to enamel mineral loss under acidic conditions. Current broad-spectrum antimicrobials disrupt the oral microbiota and carry undesirable side effects, prompting interest in targeted, microbiome-friendly alternatives. This study evaluated the antimicrobial, anti-cariogenic, and cytotoxic properties of pomegranate (Punica granatum) derived compounds, corilagin, ellagic acid, gallocatechin, kaempferol-7-O-glucoside, punicalagin, punicalin, and rutin against cariogenic S. mutans and S. sobrinus, and the commensal S. gordonii. Antibacterial activity was assessed using disc diffusion, minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) methods. Anti-virulence effects were evaluated through glycolytic pH drop and cell surface hydrophobicity assays. Cytotoxicity was determined using the brine shrimp lethality assay. Punicalagin, punicalin, and ellagic acid showed strong, selective bactericidal activity against S. mutans and S. sobrinus, with low MICs and MBC/MIC ratios, while sparing S. gordonii. These compounds significantly suppressed acid production, maintaining pH above the critical demineralisation threshold, and reduced surface hydrophobicity in cariogenic strains without affecting the commensal. Most compounds exhibited low toxicity (LC₅₀ > 500 µg/mL), indicating a favourable safety profile. Overall, punicalagin and punicalin demonstrated dual antimicrobial and anti-virulence activity with selective targeting of cariogenic pathogens. These findings support the potential inclusion of punicalagin and punicalin in oral care formulations aimed at preventing dental caries while preserving beneficial oral microbiota. As this is a preliminary screening study, the results should be interpreted cautiously, and further biofilm and host-cell assays are needed to confirm translational potential.Keywords: Anticaries, Oral streptococci, Pomegranate-derived compound

Introduction

Dental caries ranks among the most widespread chronic conditions worldwide, impacting more than a billion individuals and playing a major role in global oral health inequalities. Untreated caries leads to pain, infection, tooth loss, school absenteeism, and reduced quality of life, especially among vulnerable populations such as children, the elderly, and individuals with limited access to dental care (Eid et al. 2020; Lima et al. 2018).

Streptococcus mutans (S.mutans) and Streptococcus sobrinus (S.sobrinus), belonging to the mutans streptococci group, are recognised as key contributors to dental caries because of their ability to produce acid, tolerate acidic environments and synthesise glucans. These traits enable them to colonise the tooth surface, form biofilms, and drive enamel demineralisation. Importantly, the co-presence of S. mutans and S. sobrinus has been linked to more severe caries outcomes. For example, children harbouring both species show higher DMFT scores than those with S. mutans alone (Korona-Glowniak et al. 2022; Okada et al. 2012). Despite preventive advances, including fluoride-based therapies and oral hygiene measures, current interventions often fall short in completely preventing caries.

The use of broad-spectrum antimicrobials, such as chlorhexidine, while effective, is associated with side effects including tooth staining, mucosal irritation, altered taste, and disruption of the commensal oral flora (Boulares et al. 2025; James et al. 2017). This may result in microbial dysbiosis and reduced ecological resilience of the oral microbiome. According to the ecological plaque hypothesis, disease occurs when environmental changes in the oral cavity promote the overgrowth of pathogenic bacteria. This has led to growing interest in strategies that modulate the oral ecosystem rather than eradicate bacteria indiscriminately.

Natural products, particularly those derived from medicinal plants, are being explored as selective antimicrobial agents with the potential to suppress oral pathogens while preserving beneficial microbes (Tzimas et al. 2024). Pomegranate (Punica granatum) has attracted attention for its broad pharmacological properties, including antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anticancer activities (Sweidan et al. 2023; Chaves et al. 2020). While crude pomegranate extracts have shown effectiveness across various applications, the results have been inconsistent due to variations in composition influenced by factors such as cultivar, extraction method, and solvent polarity (Man et al. 2022; Young et al. 2017). Modern drug discovery increasingly prioritizes purified phytocompounds over complex plant extracts due to their superior selectivity, consistent potency, and suitability for high-throughput screening (Nemmani 2021). Isolated phytochemicals not only serve as therapeutic agents themselves but also act as valuable chemical leads for the synthesis of bioactive analogs. The enduring pharmaceutical relevance of phytochemicals is underscored by data showing that nearly 50% of all approved drugs within the 25 years leading up to 2007 were derived from plant-based natural products or their synthetic counterparts (Alam et al. 2022).

The present study conducted a preliminary screening of seven purified pomegranate-derived compounds—corilagin, ellagic acid, gallocatechin, kaempferol-7-O-glucoside, punicalagin, punicalin, and rutin for their antimicrobial, cytotoxic, and anti-virulence properties. The compounds were tested against the cariogenic streptococci S. mutans and S. sobrinus, alongside the commensal Streptococcus gordonii. The evaluation included antibacterial activity, cytotoxicity using the brine shrimp lethality assay, and inhibition of two key virulence traits, glycolytic acid production and cell surface hydrophobicity. This approach provides preliminary insights into the potential of pomegranate-derived agents as microbiome-friendly oral therapeutics for targeted caries prevention.

Materials and methods

Phytocompounds

The compounds used in this study corilagin (CG), ellagic acid (EA), gallocatechin (GC), kaempferol-7-O-glucoside (KPG), punicalagin (PUG), punicalin (PUN) and rutin (RT) were selected based on their identification as major bioactive constituents of pomegranate peel, as reported by Man et al. (2022). To ensure consistency, purity, and traceability of the test materials, all compounds were procured from a reputable commercial supplier, BOC Sciences (Shirley, NY, USA).

Bacterial strain

The bacterial strain, Streptococcus mutans (ATCC 700610), Streptococcus sobrinus (ATCC 33478) and Streptococcus gordonii (ATCC 51656) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Streptococcus mutans (S.mutans) and Streptococcus sobrinus (S.sobrinus) were selected as the primary cariogenic pathogens due to their well-established role in dental plaque formation, acid production, and enamel demineralisation. In contrast, S. gordonii represents a common commensal species in the oral cavity that contributes to microbial homeostasis and early colonisation without significant cariogenic potential. The inclusion of both groups enables the assessment of compound selectivity, allowing differentiation between broad-spectrum antimicrobial effects and targeted inhibition of pathogenic species.

Disc diffusion assay

The antibacterial activity of the phytocompounds was assessed using the disc diffusion assay following the CLSI guidelines (CLSI 2012) with minor modifications. A volume of 25 µL from each compound stock solution (20 mg/mL) was applied onto sterile filter paper discs (6 mm diameter), corresponding to 0.5 mg per disc. A standardised bacterial suspension, approximately 10⁸ CFU/mL, was evenly spread on brain heart infusion (BHI) agar plates using sterile cotton swabs. After allowing the surface to dry for 5 min, the treated discs were placed on the agar. Sterile distilled water and 0.12% chlorhexidine (CHX) discs served as negative and positive controls, respectively. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 16–18 h. Following incubation, inhibition zones were recorded in millimetres with a digital calliper under inverted plates. All experiments were conducted in triplicate and repeated on three separate occasions to confirm reproducibility.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

The MICs of the compounds were determined using the broth microdilution method, as outlined in CLSI guidelines (CLSI 2018), with minor adjustments. A bacterial inoculum of ~ 10⁸ CFU/mL was diluted 1:20 in sterile BHI broth to achieve a concentration of 5 × 10⁶ CFU/mL. Serial twofold dilutions of each compound (starting from 12,800 µg/mL) were prepared in 96-well microtiter plates to obtain final concentrations ranging from 12.5 to 6400 µg/mL. Each well received 90 µL of the diluted compound and 10 µL of bacterial suspension, resulting in a final concentration of ~ 5 × 105 CFU/mL per well. Each test was conducted in triplicate. Growth control wells (bacteria + broth), sterility controls (broth only), and compound blanks (compound + broth without bacteria) were included to verify viability, sterility, and exclude background absorbance. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, bacterial growth was quantified by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) using a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite 200 PRO). The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration at which the OD600 differed by less than 0.05 compared to the corresponding blank control. To determine MBC, 10 μL from wells showing no visible turbidity were streaked onto BHI agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 36–48 h. The MBC was defined as the lowest concentration that completely prevented colony formation, indicating ≥ 99.9% bacterial killing.

Brine shrimp lethality assay (BSLA)

The cytotoxicity of the pomegranate-derived compounds was assessed using the brine shrimp (Artemia salina) lethality assay, a widely accepted preliminary screening tool for evaluating general bioactivity and toxicity. Although not directly representative of mammalian systems, this assay provides a simple, rapid, and cost-effective method for identifying potentially harmful compounds before more advanced cytotoxicity models. Its use is especially valuable in natural product research where high-throughput, early-stage screening of multiple compounds is needed to inform further safety testing relevant to oral applications.

The assay was performed with minor modifications based on the method by Sarah et al. (2017). Artificial seawater was prepared by dissolving 27 g of marine salt in 3 L of distilled water. Brine shrimp eggs were incubated at room temperature for 48 h under continuous aeration and illumination. The hatched nauplii were then attracted to a light source, collected and transferred into fresh seawater. Stock solution of the test compounds (20,000 μg/mL) was prepared in distilled water containing 1% DMSO, followed by serial twofold dilutions to achieve concentrations ranging from 12.5 to 6400 μg/mL. For each concentration, 20 nauplii were exposed to 1 mL of the compound solution combined with 1 mL of seawater. After 24 h of incubation at room temperature, mortality was assessed by counting the dead nauplii. Negative (seawater) and solvent (1% DMSO) controls were included. Each concentration was tested in triplicate. The mortality percentage was calculated, and LC₅₀ values were estimated by nonlinear regression (variable slope) using GraphPad Prism v10. Toxicity was categorised using Meyer’s and Clarkson’s criteria: LC₅₀ > 1000 µg/mL (non-toxic), 500–1000 µg/mL (low toxicity), 100–500 µg/mL (moderate toxicity) and < 100 µg/mL (high toxicity).

Glycolytic pH drop assay

The effect of pomegranate-derived compounds on the acidogenic activity of S. mutans was assessed using a glycolytic pH drop assay, with modifications from the method of He et al. (2019). Bacterial cultures at the mid-logarithmic phase were collected, rinsed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in a saline solution containing 50 mM potassium chloride and 1 mM magnesium chloride. Each compound was evaluated at its previously established minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), ensuring a standardised sub-inhibitory level relevant for assessing anti-virulence activity. This strategy allowed the bacteria to remain viable while enabling measurement of reductions in pathogenic traits. Chlorhexidine (0.12%) and 1% DMSO were included as positive and negative controls, respectively. The suspension was supplemented with glucose to a final concentration of 1% (w/v), and its initial pH was adjusted to 7.2–7.4 using 0.2 M potassium hydroxide (KOH). Measurements were taken at 15-min intervals for 120 min with a calibrated pH meter. The acidogenic response was expressed as the overall decrease in pH during the assay.

Cell surface hydrophobicity (CSH) assay

The influence of the test compounds on the cell surface hydrophobicity of S. mutans and S. gordonii was evaluated using the microbial adhesion to hydrocarbon (MATH) method, as described by Rosenberg (2006), with modifications. To assess anti-adhesive properties independent of bactericidal effects, all compounds were tested at their respective MICs, which represent biologically relevant sub-inhibitory concentrations. This allows the evaluation of virulence modulation under non-lethal exposure conditions. Untreated cells were used as the negative control (NC), whereas a 1% DMSO vehicle control (VC) was included to verify that the solvent did not influence cell surface characteristics. Bacterial suspensions were exposed to the test compounds at 37 °C for 1 h. Following exposure, the cells were harvested by centrifugation (5000 × g, 5 min, 4 °C), rinsed twice with sterile distilled water, and resuspended in PUM buffer (potassium phosphate, urea, and magnesium sulfate; pH 7.1) to achieve an optical density of 0.8 at 600 nm (OD₀).For each measurement, 3 mL of the bacterial suspension was combined with 0.2 mL of hexadecane, vortexed for 60 s, and allowed to separate for 15 min. Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out in at least three independent replicates. Results are expressed as mean values ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10). For comparison among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied to determine significant differences in both disc diffusion and cell surface hydrophobicity assays. A threshold of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and distinct superscript letters were used to indicate differences between groups.

For the brine shrimp lethality assay, the lethal concentration (LC₅₀) values were calculated using nonlinear regression analysis with a variable slope model. Goodness of fit was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R2) to confirm the reliability of dose–response relationships.

Results

Disc diffusion assay

All pomegranate-derived compounds exhibited measurable antibacterial activity against S. gordonii, S. mutans, and S. sobrinus, while no inhibition was observed with the negative control (Table 1). Among them, PUG demonstrated the most potent effect, with inhibition zones comparable to CHX, 0.12%, particularly against S. mutans (18.5 ± 2.0 mm) and S. sobrinus (19.0 ± 1.1 mm). EA followed closely, showing consistent activity across all species. In contrast, GC and KPG showed significantly lower inhibition (p < 0.05). These results indicate that different compounds exert distinct effects on oral streptococcal species, reflecting species- and compound-specific antimicrobial activities.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

The antimicrobial activity of selected pomegranate-derived compounds was assessed against S. gordonii, S. mutans, and S. sobrinus using MIC, MBC, and MBC/MIC ratios (Table 2). PUG, PUN and EA exhibited the most potent and selective bactericidal activity against cariogenic S. mutans and S. sobrinus, with low MIC values (100 µg/mL) and favourable MBC/MIC ratios (2), while showing higher MICs and MBCs against S. gordonii. CG displayed moderate activity, with a higher MBC/MIC ratio (8) against S. gordonii, indicating reduced bactericidal efficacy. KPG and RT showed weaker overall activity with higher MIC values and MBC/MIC ratios of 2–4 across all species. CHX demonstrated potent but non-selective antimicrobial activity across all tested species. It showed identical or similar MIC and MBC values for both cariogenic (S. mutans, S. sobrinus) and commensal (S. gordonii) bacteria, with MBC/MIC ratios ranging from 1 to 2. These findings highlight CHX's broad-spectrum action.

Brine shrimp lethality assay (BSLA)

The brine shrimp lethality assay results are summarised in Table 3. Most of the tested pomegranate-derived compounds demonstrated low toxicity, with LC₅₀ values exceeding 500 µg/mL and strong dose–response correlations (R2 > 0.98), indicating a favourable safety profile. PUN (941.2 µg/mL) and KPG (998.9 µg/mL) exhibited the highest LC₅₀ values. In contrast, EA was the only compound classified under moderate toxicity, with an LC₅₀ of 159.2 µg/mL, according to Clarkson’s toxicity criteria. All other compounds were categorised as low toxicity. Notably, the LC₅₀ values for all compounds were substantially higher than their respective MIC values, indicating a wide therapeutic margin and supporting their potential for safe antimicrobial application.

Glycolytic pH drop

The glycolytic pH drop assay (Fig. 1) demonstrated clear differences in the acidogenic potential of S. mutans and S. sobrinus following treatment with the tested compounds over 120 min period. In the untreated control (1% DMSO), both species exhibited a rapid and sustained decrease in pH, dropping below the critical demineralisation threshold (pH 5.5) within 30 min. By the end of the experiment, the final pH reached 3.5 ± 0.2 for S. mutans and 4.0 ± 0.3 for S. sobrinus, indicating high glycolytic activity. In contrast, selected compounds notably suppressed acid production. For S. mutans PUG, PUN, and CHX, the final pH values were maintained above 5.5 at 120 min, indicating effective suppression of acid production. For S. sobrinus, PUG, PUN, GT, and CHX achieved similar suppression, suggesting broader inhibitory effects in this species. To further assess early acidogenic activity, the initial rate of pH drop during the first 15 min was calculated for each treatment (Table 4). The control group exhibited the highest pH drop rates, 0.0467 pH units/min for S. mutans and 0.0600 pH units/min for S. sobrinus. Among the tested compounds, PUG most effectively reduced initial acid production, with corresponding rates of 0.025 and 0.013 pH units/min, respectively. These results indicate that specific pomegranate-derived compounds can attenuate the acidogenic activity of cariogenic bacteria in a time-dependent manner.

Cell surface hydrophobicity (CSH) assay

The hydrophobicity index of S. mutans, S. sobrinus, and S. gordonii was significantly influenced by treatment with the tested compounds (Fig. 2a–c). For S. mutans (Fig. 2a), untreated control cells showed the highest hydrophobicity (~ 75%). Treatment with CHX 0.12% significantly reduced hydrophobicity (p < 0.05) to below 40%, showing the most substantial reduction among all treatments. Among the compounds, PUN, PUG, and CG caused significant decreases in hydrophobicity compared to the control, with PUG and PUN showing the lowest values among natural compounds. EA and RT also showed moderate but statistically significant reductions (p < 0.05). In contrast, KPG and NC (DMSO control) maintained high hydrophobicity levels similar to the untreated control (denoted by shared superscript ‘a’), indicating minimal effect. For S. sobrinus (Fig. 2b), a similar pattern was observed. The control group maintained a high hydrophobicity index (~ 55%), whereas CHX significantly decreased the hydrophobicity (p < 0.05). Natural compounds, such as PUN, PUG, and CG, caused significant reductions (p < 0.05) compared to the untreated group, with PUN showing one of the most potent effects. Interestingly, KPG, EA, and RT retained hydrophobicity values statistically comparable to those of the control, suggesting less pronounced anti-hydrophobicity activity against S. sobrinus. For S. gordonii (Fig. 2c), the baseline hydrophobicity was lower (~ 35%) compared to the mutans group. CHX 0.12% again showed the most drastic reduction (p < 0.05). Among the natural compounds, only EA significantly reduced hydrophobicity (p < 0.05), while PUN, PUG, CG, and KPG did not produce a statistically significant effect, maintaining values comparable to the control. This suggests a degree of selectivity, where some compounds reduced hydrophobicity in cariogenic strains while sparing the commensal S. gordonii.

Discussion

This study demonstrated the selective antimicrobial and anti-virulence effects of purified pomegranate-derived phytocompounds against cariogenic mutans streptococci, while sparing the beneficial oral commensal S. gordonii. These findings align with the ecological approach to caries prevention, which favours microbiome-preserving strategies over broad-spectrum eradication.

The inclusion of both S. mutans and S. sobrinus was guided by increasing evidence of their synergistic role in cariogenesis. While S. mutans is well-established as a key etiological agent, S. sobrinus is often associated with increased caries severity, particularly in early childhood caries (ECC) and severe ECC. Studies have shown that individuals harboring both species exhibited significantly higher DMFT scores than those colonised by S.mutans alone (Korona-Glowniak et al. 2022; Veena and Nagarathna 2020; Gotouda et al. 2017). Thus evaluating both pathogens provides a more comprehensive and clinically relevant assessment of compound efficacy. To assess selectivity, S. gordonii was included as a representative oral commensal. Streptococcus gordonii an early coloniser of dental surfaces, supports oral microbial balance by competing with S. mutans for binding sites and nutrients while also generating alkaline by-products and antimicrobial subtances (Ye et al. 2025).

The cytotoxicity profiles of the tested compounds were first evaluated using the brine shrimp lethality assay (BSLA), a widely accepted screening tool in natural product research. Most compounds exhibited low toxicity, with LC₅₀ values exceeding 500 µg/mL. Notably, PUN and KPG demonstrated the highest LC₅₀ values, while EA was the only compound classified under moderate toxicity (LC₅₀ = 159.2 µg/mL). Nevertheless, this moderate cytotoxicity observed in vitro contrasts with findings form in vivo model. Oral administration of EA at doses up to 2000 mg/kg showed no sign of toxicity in animal (Hurtado-Nuñez et al. 2022). Likewise, Sprague–Dawley rats receiving a punicalagin-rich diet (~ 4.8 g/kg/day) for 37 days exhibited no harmful effects (Cerdá et al. 2003). Collectively, these results indicate that although BSLA provides useful preliminary safety information, in vitro cytotoxicity outcomes may not fully predict in vivo tolerance, highlighting the importance of confirming safety through mammalian-based cytocompatibility assays in future work.

In addition to their antimicrobial selectivity, PUG and PUN significantly suppressed acidogenic activity in S.mutans and S.sobrinus as shown by the glycolytic pH drop assay in Sect. 3.4. The glycolytic pH drop assay reflects the capacity of cariogenic bacteria to ferment dietary sugars into organic acids, primarily lactic acid, leading to a rapid decrease in environmental pH and promoting enamel demineralisation. S. mutans and S. sobrinus can persist and continue glycolytic activity at low-pH environments, which gives them an advantage over oral bacteria that are less tolerant to low pH. This adaptation is considered a key factor in caries progression (Nascimento et al. 2004). Marsh (2017) has also noted that oral microbial dysbiosis often arised from ecological changes, such as sustained acidity or inflammation, which favour the growth of acidogenic, aciduric, or proteolytic species. In the present study, treatment with PUG and PUN significantly reduced the glycolytic pH drop in both S. mutans and S.sobrinus, showing effective suppression of acid production. Lower acidogenicity implies interference with important virulence mechanisms and could help promote a shift towards a less cariogenic microbial balance. These findings are consistent with the ecological plaque hypothesis, which stresses modifying environmental conditions to influence microbial composition rather than relying solely on broad-spectrum antimicrobials (Rajasekaran et al. 2024).

The cell surface hydrophobicity (CSH) as demonstrated in Sect. 3.5 plays an important role in the initial adhesion of oral bacteria to tooth surfaces, which is the first step in biofilm formation. According to Spatafora et al. (2024), the adhesion to oral surfaces involves specific adhesins and non-specific forces such as hydrophobic interactions and van der Waals forces. Consequently, reducing the hydrophobicity index (HI) of bacterial cells can weaken their ability to adhere, coaggregate with other species, and establish biofilms. Previous studies have shown that several plant-derived compounds are capable of lowering the HI of S. mutans, thereby reducing its prevalence within treated groups (Karnjana et al. 2023; Hassan et al., 2015). However, little is known about how S. sobrinus responds to such interventions, and direct comparisons with S. mutans remain scarce. In the present study, treatment with pomegranate-derived compounds significantly reduced the HI of S. mutans and S. sobrinus. In contrast, S. gordonii, a beneficial early coloniser that contributes to microbial homeostasis, exhibited no significant changes in HI after treatment. This selective action suggests that the tested compounds can target pathogenic species while preserving commensal adhesion, an attribute that is crucial for maintaining a balanced oral microbiome.

PUG and PUN consistently outperformed other pomegranate-derived phytocompounds in their antimicrobial and anti-virulence activities against S. mutans and S. sobrinus. Their enhanced efficacy is likely attributed to their unique molecular structures and physicochemical properties. PUG (MW: 1084.7 g/mol) and PUN (MW: 782.5 g/mol) are large, highly hydroxylated ellagitannins containing multiple galloyl moieties. These features enable strong interactions with bacterial surface proteins, glucosyltransferases, and membrane components, contributing to their broad antimicrobial potential. As hydrolysable tannins, PUG and PUN exert antibacterial effects through multiple, well-documented mechanisms. Tannins exert their antibacterial effects through multiple mechanisms, such as sequestering iron, disrupting cell membrane integrity, interfering with cell wall synthesis, and inhibiting fatty acid biosynthesis. Additionally, they can suppress the expression of key virulence factors, including those involved in biofilm development, enzymatic activity, adhesion, motility, and toxin production by acting as quorum-sensing antagonists (Farha et al. 2020). In contrast, EA, although structurally related as a degradation product of ellagitannins, exhibits low absorption primarily due to its poor solubility (less than 10 µg/mL) and low lipophilicity, which together limit its membrane permeability and stability in aqueous environments (Nyamba et al. 2021). The CG (molecular weight: 634.45 g/mol), while also an ellagitannin, is structurally smaller and shows moderate activity, possibly due to fewer reactive hydroxyl groups available for hydrogen bonding. Flavonoids such as GC, KPG, and RT are known for their antioxidant properties but generally possess less antimicrobial potency, which may be attributed to their limited structural complexity and fewer functional groups relevant to disrupting bacterial virulence mechanisms. These findings suggest that larger, highly polar, and polyphenol-rich compounds may exert stronger selective pressure on cariogenic bacteria.

Despite promising results, this work should be interpreted as a preliminary screening study. Only three bacterial species were evaluated, and virulence testing was restricted to acidogenicity and cell surface hydrophobicity. While these assays provide valuable first insights, other key virulence traits such as biofilm formation, extracellular polysaccharide production, and acidurance were not included. Moreover, the use of planktonic monospecies cultures does not fully capture the complex dynamics of multispecies oral biofilms. All tested strains, as described in Sect. 2.2, were laboratory reference strains, which may not reflect the diversity and resistance profiles of clinical isolates.

The cytotoxicity assessment was also limited to the brine shrimp lethality assay as described in Sect. 2.5, which, although widely used for preliminary toxicity screening, does not predict mammalian cytocompatibility. Importantly, no host-cell assays were conducted to evaluate the compatibility of the compounds with oral epithelial or keratinocyte cells. These limitations highlight the need for further studies incorporating dual- or multispecies biofilm models, clinical isolates, and host cell cytocompatibility assays to fully validate the selective anti-virulence potential of punicalagin and punicalin. In addition, integrating these compounds into prototype oral care formulations such as varnishes, rinses, or gels and testing their stability and performance under simulated oral conditions will be critical steps toward translational application.

Conclusion

This study highlights the selective antimicrobial and anti-virulence potential of purified pomegranate-derived compounds against cariogenic Streptococcus species. Punicalagin (PUG) and punicalin (PUN) demonstrated the most promising activity, combining selective bactericidal effects with inhibition of acidogenicity and surface hydrophobicity, two key traits in caries development. Their ability to suppress pathogenic species while sparing beneficial commensals underscores their relevance to microbiome-friendly and ecological approaches in oral health management. Overall, these findings provide an essential first step toward the translational development of phytochemical-based therapeutics in preventive dentistry. In particular, their incorporation into oral care formulations such as mouth rinse, gels or toothpastes represents a promising direction for future development, offering a strong foundation for more advanced studies and future formulation work.
