Decreased renal function predicts severe cytokine release syndrome after CAR‐T‐cell therapy for large B‐cell lymphoma
Authors: Yasuyuki Arai, Tomoyasu Jo, Takayuki Sato, Masatoshi Sakurai, Daisuke Kaji, Toshio Kitawaki, Kazuyuki Shimada, Tatsu Shimoyama, Satoshi Yoshihara, Shinichi Makita, Nobuharu Fujii, Go Yamamoto, Keisuke Kataoka, Emiko Sakaida, Hideki Goto, Yasuhiro Nakashima, Akiyo Yoshida, Yoshihiro Umezawa, Haryoon Kim, Motohiro Kato, Yoshiko Atsuta, Koji Kato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCytokine release syndrome (CRS) remains a major toxicity of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cell therapy in large B‐cell lymphoma (LBCL), and robust pre‐infusion predictors are needed for risk‐adapted management. We retrospectively analysed LBCL patients in the Japanese nationwide registry who underwent CD19 CAR‐T‐cell therapy between 2019 and 2024. Among 900 patients (median age 62 years), cumulative incidences of CRS within 30 days after infusion were 75.0% for any grade, 20.8% for grade ≥ 2 and 14.0% for grade ≥ 3. In multivariable analysis, lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 1.108 per 10 mL/min per 1.73 m2 decrease; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.015–1.209; p = 0.022), higher ferritin (aHR 1.006 per 100 ng/mL; 95% CI 1.001–1.010; p = 0.016), C‐reactive protein (CRP) (aHR 1.142 per mg/dL; 95% CI 1.091–1.195; p < 0.001) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) (aHR 1.073 per 100 U/L; 95% CI 1.008–1.142; p = 0.028) independently predicted grade ≥ 2 CRS. We then built a four‐factor CRS pre‐infusion risk evaluation model, cytokine release syndrome‐pre‐infusion risk evaluation (CRS‐PRE), that stratified grade ≥ 2 CRS risk into low, intermediate and high groups with incidences of 2.8%, 26.0% and 50.0% respectively. Decreased eGFR, a surrogate of host renal reserve, with elevated ferritin, CRP and LDH emerged as predictors of high‐grade CRS. The CRS‐PRE may facilitate risk‐adapted monitoring and intervention in clinical practice.Keywords: chimeric antigen receptor T‐cell therapy, cytokine release syndrome, estimated glomerular filtration rate, large B‐cell lymphoma

INTRODUCTION

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cell therapy targeting CD19 has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) large B‐cell lymphoma (LBCL), as shown in pivotal trials and real‐world studies. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Its use has rapidly expanded in clinical practice, offering a curative option for patients with limited treatment choices. However, CAR‐T‐cell therapy is associated with immune‐related toxicities, which can significantly impact clinical outcomes and treatment feasibility.

Among these, cytokine release syndrome (CRS) is the most frequent and clinically significant acute‐phase complication. It arises from rapid immune activation and excessive pro‐inflammatory cytokine release, particularly interleukin‐6 (IL‐6). 6 , 7 While most cases are mild and manageable with supportive care, high‐grade CRS can result in hypotension, hypoxia and multi‐organ dysfunction, posing a major threat to treatment safety. 6 Even when not lethal, high‐grade CRS may increase risk of prolonged cytopenias and functional decline, complicating post‐infusion care. 8 , 9 Accordingly, appropriate risk evaluation is crucial to enable timely intervention and risk‐adapted management strategies.

Several studies have explored predictors of CRS, including tumour burden and cytokine kinetics. 10 , 11 However, models based on post‐infusion cytokine levels are difficult to apply in real‐world settings, as they require specialized assays. In addition, host‐related factors, such as renal function, have been implicated in CRS susceptibility. 12 Impaired renal function may worsen CRS through reduced ability to regulate intravascular fluid balance and haemodynamic stability during systemic inflammation. 13 Furthermore, decreased estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) has been associated with endothelial dysfunction, which is increasingly recognized as a key component in the pathophysiology of severe CRS. 14 , 15 However, the clinical significance of renal function in predicting CRS remains heterogeneous across studies and insufficiently characterized. 10 , 16 , 17 , 18 These limitations highlight the need to identify robust and readily available pre‐infusion parameters to predict CRS risk.

In this study, using a Japanese nationwide registry, we focused on patients with LBCL treated with CD19 CAR‐T‐cell therapy to identify simple, early pre‐infusion parameters, including renal function, associated with grade ≥ 2 CRS. Our findings may facilitate practical risk prediction and contribute to safer management of CAR‐T‐cell therapy in clinical practice.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Patients

Data on patients with R/R LBCL who received tisagenlecleucel (tisa‐cel), axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi‐cel) or lisocabtagene maraleucel (liso‐cel) between January 2019 and September 2024 were obtained through a Japanese nationwide registry, FormsNet3, provided by the Japanese Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (JSTCT) and the Japanese Data Center for Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation (JDCHCT). 19 For the present analysis, we included only patients treated in the post‐marketing clinical setting, and patients who received CAR‐T‐cell therapy within clinical trials were excluded. Patients with indolent non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (iNHL) without transformation, those with unknown lymphoma type at infusion and those with unspecified CAR‐T product type were excluded from the analysis. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient via their respective institutions. The study was planned by the Cellular Therapy Working Group of the JSTCT, approved by data management committees of FormsNet3 and by the institutional review board of Kyoto University. It was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study end‐points and definitions

The primary analysis outcome of this study was the cumulative incidence of high‐grade CRS. High‐grade CRS was defined as grade ≥ 2 CRS. CRS and immune effector cell‐associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) were diagnosed and graded according to criteria from the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT). 20 For each patient, the highest CRS or ICANS grade observed after CAR‐T‐cell infusion was used for analysis. Baseline patient characteristics were defined as clinical status at the time of CAR‐T‐cell infusion and therefore reflect patient characteristics after completion of bridging therapy. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the equation developed by the Japanese Society of Nephrology. 21 Details are described in the Supplemental Methods.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared using Fisher's exact test; overall significance for variables with more than two levels was assessed using global Wald tests; and trends for ordinal variables were evaluated by modelling them as continuous predictors. Continuous variables were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared using two‐tailed, unpaired Student's t‐test. Univariate and multivariate analyses for CRS, including any grade, grade ≥ 2 and grade ≥ 3 events, were performed using Fine–Gray hazard models, considering death without CRS as a competing event. In the multivariate analysis, all baseline variables listed in Table 1 were considered candidate predictors and evaluated by a stepwise selection procedure with forward inclusion and backward elimination. The final model was selected based on the lowest Bayesian information criterion (BIC). A risk‐scoring system for grade ≥ 2 CRS, including cut‐off selection, was developed using a training‐validation cohort approach. Details are provided in the Supplemental Methods. All tests were two‐sided and p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed with Stata, version 19 (Stata Corp., College Station, TX).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

Of 976 patients with non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) receiving CD19 CAR‐T‐cell therapy between January 2019 and September 2024, 76 were excluded for iNHL without transformation (n = 62), unknown lymphoma type (n = 13) or unknown CAR‐T product (n = 1) (Figure S1). Consequently, 900 patients with a median age of 62 (IQR, 54–69) were analysed (Table 1). Among them, 736 (81.8%) had de novo LBCL, and 164 (18.2%) had LBCL transformed from iNHL. Seven hundred and twenty‐five patients (80.6%) had received three or more prior lines of therapy before CAR‐T‐cell therapy, and 206 (22.9%) had previously undergone high‐dose chemotherapy with autologous haematopoietic stem cell transplantation. For bridging, 423 patients (47.0%) received systemic chemotherapy alone, 24 (2.7%) received radiation therapy alone and 61 (6.8%) received both. Disease status at infusion was complete response (CR) in 206 (22.9%), partial response (PR) in 271 (30.1%) and stable/progressive disease or relapse (SD/PD/Rel) in 423 (47.0%). At infusion, central nervous system (CNS) involvement was present in 23 (2.6%), and non‐CNS extranodal involvement was observed in 293 (32.6%). CAR‐T products were tisa‐cel (n = 568, 63.1%), liso‐cel (n = 226, 25.1%) and axi‐cel (n = 106, 11.8%). Lymphodepletion consisted of fludarabine and cyclophosphamide in 86.9% of patients, while bendamustine‐based regimens were used in 3.6%. The median interval from leukapheresis to infusion was 60 days (IQR, 50–75).

For laboratory data before lymphodepletion (Table 1), median ferritin concentration was 442 ng/mL (IQR, 202–988), median CRP concentration was 0.20 mg/dL (IQR, 0.10–0.70) and median LDH level was 235 (IQR, 192–306). Median creatinine concentration was 0.74 mg/dL (IQR, 0.60–0.92), and median eGFR was 73.4 mL/min/1.73 m2 (IQR, 60.3–89.8).

Post‐CAR‐T outcomes

Among the 831 patients evaluable for treatment response, the best overall response included CR in 536 patients (64.5%) and PR in 102 patients (12.3%) (Table S1). At a median follow‐up of 6.3 months (IQR, 3.4–12.5) after infusion, 1‐year overall survival (OS), progression‐free survival (PFS), cumulative incidence of progression and NRM were 67.9%, 50.2%, 42.8% and 6.9% respectively (Figure S2). The leading causes of death were disease progression (n = 209, 77.7%), infections (n = 12, 4.5%), organ failures (n = 9, 3.4%), secondary primary malignancies (n = 8, 3.0%) and other causes (Table S1).

CRS after CAR‐T‐cell therapy

CRS of any grade occurred in 678 patients (75.3%), with grade 1 in 487 (54.1%), grade ≥ 2 in 189 (21.0%) and grade ≥ 3 in 126 (14.0%). ICANS occurred in 81 patients (9.0%), with grade ≥ 2 in 46 (5.1%) and grade ≥ 3 in 36 (4.0%) (Table S1). The cumulative incidence of CRS by day 30 post‐infusion was 75.0% for any grade, 20.8% for grade ≥ 2 and 14.0% for grade ≥ 3 (Figure 1), which was similar to the crude frequency because early non‐CRS mortality was rare and no CRS events occurred beyond day 30. Deaths attributed to CRS occurred in two patients (0.7%).

Risk factors for grade ≥ 2 CRS

To evaluate pre‐infusion clinical and laboratory parameters associated with grade ≥ 2 CRS, univariate analyses were performed (Table S2). Age at infusion was associated with higher risk of grade ≥ 2 CRS (hazard ratio [HR] 1.014 per year increase, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.003–1.027). Comparisons using tisagenlecleucel (tisa‐cel) as the reference showed that axi‐cel had a higher point estimate for grade ≥ 2 CRS (HR 1.621, 95% CI 1.152–2.282), whereas liso‐cel had a lower point estimate (HR 0.433, 95% CI 0.279–0.672). For bridging therapy, although patients who received both chemotherapy and radiation therapy showed higher point estimates for grade ≥ 2 CRS (HR 1.744, 95% CI 1.052–2.892), compared with patients who did not receive bridging therapy, the global test across bridging therapy categories was not statistically significant. Patients with SD/PD or relapsed disease had a higher incidence of grade ≥ 2 CRS compared with those in CR/PR (HR 1.563, 95% CI 1.067–2.291).

Among pre‐infusion laboratory parameters, higher white blood cell (WBC) counts (HR 1.029 per 109/L increase, 95% CI 1.010–1.048) and neutrophil counts (HR 1.039 per 109/L increase, 95% CI 1.014–1.064) were associated with an increased risk of grade ≥ 2 CRS. Platelet concentrate transfusion was also associated with grade ≥ 2 CRS (HR 1.715, 95% CI 1.149–2.559). Elevated serum ferritin (HR 1.013 per 100 ng/mL increase, 95% CI 1.009–1.017), CRP (HR 1.130 per mg/dL increase, 95% CI 1.080–1.182) and LDH (HR 1.037 per 100 U/L increase, 95% CI 1.023–1.051) were associated with an increased risk of grade ≥ 2 CRS. Many of these factors were also commonly associated with the development of any grade or grade ≥ 3 CRS (Table S3).

Multivariate analysis was performed by including all clinical and laboratory factors and applying a stepwise variable selection method based on the BIC. As a result, decreased eGFR (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.108 per 10 mL/min/1.73 m2 decrease; 95% CI, 1.015–1.209; p = 0.022) as well as elevated ferritin (aHR, 1.006 per 100 ng/mL; 95% CI, 1.001–1.010; p = 0.016), CRP (aHR, 1.142 per mg/dL; 95% CI, 1.091–1.195; p < 0.001) and LDH (aHR, 1.073 per 100 U/L; 95% CI, 1.008–1.142; p = 0.028) before infusion were independently associated with grade ≥ 2 CRS (Table 2). Of these factors, elevated CRP and LDH were significantly associated with any grade and grade ≥ 3 CRS, whereas associations of ferritin and eGFR with any grade and grade ≥ 3 CRS were not statistically significant (Table S4).

Development of a CRS risk prediction model: CRS‐PRE (cytokine release syndrome‐pre‐infusion risk evaluation)

The four variables identified in the multivariate analysis for grade ≥ 2 CRS, eGFR, serum ferritin, CRP and LDH are routinely available before CAR‐T‐cell infusion. Therefore, we constructed a predictive model for high‐grade CRS using these markers.

Using the training cohort, cut‐off values for eGFR (66 mL/min/1.73 m2), ferritin (980 ng/mL), CRP (0.25 mg/dL) and LDH (290 U/L) were determined by ROC curve analysis. For eGFR, restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a non‐linear association between eGFR, which showed an approximately normal distribution, and the hazard of grade ≥ 2 CRS, with an inflection point near the ROC‐derived cut‐off (Figure S3A–C). Patients who developed high‐grade CRS showed significantly lower eGFR (Figure S3D).

Based on the multivariate findings in the training cohort, we developed the CRS‐PRE (cytokine release syndrome‐pre‐infusion risk evaluation) system by assigning weights to each variable (Table S5). One point was assigned for eGFR <66 mL/min/1.73 m2, ferritin ≥980 ng/mL, CRP ≥0.25 mg/dL and LDH ≥290 U/L. Patients were then classified into three CRS‐PRE risk categories according to their total score: low risk (score 0), intermediate risk (score 1–2) and high risk (score 3–4).

In the validation cohort, when stratified by these cut‐offs, the cumulative incidence of grade≥2 and grade≥3 CRS differed significantly among groups (Figure 2A–D, and Figure S4A–D). The CRS‐PRE risk groups demonstrated significantly different cumulative incidences of grade ≥ 2 CRS (2.8%, 26.0% and 50.0% for low‐, intermediate‐ and high‐risk groups, respectively) and grade ≥ 3 CRS (0.0%, 16.4% and 35.0%, respectively). In addition, patients in the high‐risk CRS‐PRE group also exhibited a higher cumulative incidence of any‐grade CRS compared with the other groups (Figure 3A–C). Using the high‐risk group as the threshold yielded high specificity (89.9%) for predicting grade ≥ 2 CRS, whereas combining intermediate‐ and high‐risk groups provided high sensitivity (96.8%) (Table S6).

DISCUSSION

In this nationwide registry‐based analysis of 900 patients with LBCL who received CD19 CAR‐T‐cell therapy, we identified four readily available pre‐infusion parameters associated with high‐grade CRS: elevated serum ferritin, CRP and LDH, together with reduced eGFR. Based on these factors, we developed a simple pre‐infusion risk model, CRS‐PRE, that separated three risk groups with distinct risk of high‐grade CRS. To our knowledge, this is among the largest analyses to establish a clinically applicable CRS prediction model using routinely measurable parameters.

In our cohort, CRS occurred in 75.3%, with grade ≥2 and ≥3 events in 21.0% and 14.0%, respectively, consistent with pivotal trials and real‐world studies. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 In our dataset, the product distribution (tisa‐cel 63%, liso‐cel 25%, axi‐cel 12%) with a relatively smaller share of axi‐cel likely contributes to the mid‐range any‐grade incidence and the moderate proportion of higher grade events. Overall, these findings suggest that the burden of CRS in routine Japanese practice aligns with prior literature. In this study, we defined high‐grade CRS as grade ≥ 2 CRS, as it often requires clinical intervention and has clinical impact.

A novel finding is the association between reduced eGFR and grade ≥ 2 CRS. Prior reports on the relationship between renal function and CRS have been inconsistent. The endothelial activation and stress index (EASIX), calculated from creatinine, platelet count and LDH, was originally developed as a surrogate marker of endothelial stress in the setting of allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation and has also been reported to be useful for CRS risk prediction. 22 , 23 , 24 However, another study has suggested that the contribution of creatinine to CRS prediction is limited and that a modified score replacing creatinine with CRP would be useful. 12 Although serum creatinine is a marker of renal function, it is substantially influenced by age, sex and muscle volume. 25 Therefore, eGFR, which incorporates these factors, may better reflect renal function and its link to CRS risk.

In our study, eGFR was not significant in univariate analysis but remained in the final multivariate model. This suggests that correlations with age, sex and other clinical factors may have masked its association. Biologically, impaired renal function could reduce cytokine clearance and compromise fluid volume regulation, leading to fluid retention and blood pressure lability. 26 , 27 Together, these changes provide a plausible mechanism by which renal dysfunction contributes to the development of more severe CRS. The effect of eGFR on the risk of grade ≥ 2 CRS appeared non‐linear, indicating that the choice of an appropriate threshold is important. This may also suggest that the optimal threshold could vary across different CAR‐T products. In our cohort, the number of patients receiving liso‐cel or axi‐cel was limited, precluding a robust evaluation of product‐specific thresholds. In addition, because eGFR was calculated using a Japanese population–specific equation, the distribution of eGFR and the optimal threshold may differ in non‐Japanese cohorts. Further studies are warranted.

In this study, inflammatory markers, ferritin and CRP, were also independently associated with grade ≥ 2 CRS, consistent with prior reports linking pre‐infusion inflammation to CRS severity. 10 , 18 These markers have been implicated in vascular endothelial dysfunction, a key component of CRS pathophysiology. 10 , 28 LDH, a surrogate marker of tumour burden, was similarly retained in the final model, suggesting that it captures disease‐related factors relevant to CRS risk.

Several factors, such as CAR‐T product type and disease status at infusion, were associated with CRS in univariate analysis, but did not remain significant in the final multivariate model. Regarding CAR‐T product type, prior studies have reported that axi‐cel is associated with a higher CRS risk. 3 , 29 , 30 In Japan, tisa‐cel was the first product approved for LBCL in 2019, followed by axi‐cel and liso‐cel in 2021; thus, the proportion of patients receiving axi‐cel in our cohort remained relatively small. 31 Moreover, early corticosteroid intervention for CRS has become more common in recent years, and prophylactic corticosteroid use has also been implemented in some settings. Patient characteristics and management strategies may therefore differ across products. Such temporal changes in CRS management may also influence the observed associations and should be considered when interpreting models derived from earlier cohorts. Therefore, the impact of CAR‐T product type on CRS incidence warrants further investigation. Although disease status at infusion did not remain significant in multivariate analysis, LDH is likely collinear with disease activity.

To translate findings in the multivariate analysis into clinical practice, we developed a novel risk stratification system for grade ≥ 2 CRS, called CRS‐PRE. CRS‐PRE uses four routine pre‐infusion parameters to stratify patients into low (2.8%), intermediate (26.0%) and high (50.0%) risk for grade ≥ 2 CRS; for grade ≥ 3 CRS, the corresponding incidences were 0.0%, 16.4% and 35.0% respectively. This simple model can support risk‐adapted monitoring and pre‐emptive care, such as intensified early observation and lower thresholds for tocilizumab or corticosteroids. In selected high‐risk patients, prophylactic corticosteroids, as explored in ZUMA‐1 cohort 6, may warrant prospective evaluation. 32 Because these inputs are universally measured before lymphodepletion, CRS‐PRE is readily applicable in routine practice.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective registry design could introduce unmeasured confounding and heterogeneity in supportive care practices. Second, the number of patients with certain histological subtypes, including high‐grade B‐cell lymphoma (HGBCL) and primary mediastinal B‐cell lymphoma (PMBCL), was relatively small, limiting our ability to evaluate subtype‐specific associations between renal function and CRS risk. Third, the median follow‐up duration was relatively short. Although the present study primarily focused on short‐term outcomes after CAR‐T‐cell therapy, further evaluation with longer follow‐up will be necessary. Fourth, the relationship between the predictive model and ICANS requires further investigation. Finally, external validation and prospective impact analyses are needed to assess generalizability.

In summary, this nationwide analysis identified low eGFR, along with elevated ferritin, CRP and LDH levels, as independent pre‐infusion predictors of high‐grade CRS in patients receiving CD19 CAR‐T‐cell therapy. We developed CRS‐PRE, a simple scoring system using these parameters, which effectively stratifies patients according to their risk of severe CRS. Incorporation of this model into clinical decision‐making may improve patient safety and support individualized management strategies for CAR‐T‐cell therapy.
