ABSTRACT
Icotrokinra (ICOTYDE™) is a targeted oral peptide interleukin (IL)-23 receptor (IL-23R) antagonist being developed by Johnson & Johnson, and Protagonist Therapeutics, Inc., for the treatment of immune-mediated inflammatory diseases. In March 2026, the US FDA approved icotrokinra for the treatment of moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis (PsO) in adults and paediatric patients ≥ 12 years of age and who weigh ≥ 40 kg who are candidates for systemic therapy or phototherapy. Icotrokinra is also under review for this indication in the EU and in Canada. Introduction

The interleukin (IL)-23/IL-23 receptor (IL-23R) signaling pathway has a central role in the pathogenesis of immune-mediated inflammatory diseases, such as psoriasis (PsO), psoriatic arthritis (PsA), ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD) [1–3]. These disorders are managed with biologics that target key cytokines and oral small molecules that target intracellular signaling pathways involved in inflammation [1, 3]. While biologics achieve high efficacy and durable disease control, significant limitations include parenteral administration, high production costs, the need for cold-chain logistics, and an increased risk of infection and injection-related adverse reactions. While oral small molecule therapies are more convenient, they are less effective than biologics and have tolerability concerns [1, 3]. Consequently, there is a need for effective oral treatment options that are targeted, effective and well tolerated [1, 3].

Icotrokinra (ICOTYDE™), a targeted oral peptide IL-23R antagonist, is being developed by Johnson & Johnson and Protagonist Therapeutics, Inc., for the treatment of immune-mediated inflammatory diseases [4–6]. In March 2026, the US FDA approved icotrokinra for the treatment of moderate-to-severe plaque PsO in adults and paediatric patients ≥ 12 years of age and who weigh ≥ 40 kg who are candidates for systemic therapy or phototherapy [5, 7]. The recommended dosage of icotrokinra in the USA is 200 mg orally once daily. Icotrokinra should be administered on an empty stomach with water upon waking and food should not be eaten until at least 30 mins after taking icotrokinra [5]. Prior to starting treatment, consider evaluating patients for tuberculosis infection based on clinical judgement and complete all age-appropriate vaccinations. Avoid treatment with icotrokinra in patients with clinically important active infections until the infection resolves or is adequately treated. If such an infection develops, discontinue icotrokinra until the infection resolves. Patients with moderate or severe renal impairment (eGFR < 60 mL/min) should be monitored for potential adverse reactions when receiving icotrokinra [5].

Icotrokinra is under regulatory review for use in moderate-to-severe plaque PsO in the EU [8] and Canada [9].

Company Agreements

Icotrokinra was jointly discovered and is being developed under a license and collaboration agreement between Protagonist and Johnson & Johnson. Johnson & Johnson retains exclusive worldwide rights to develop icotrokinra in Phase 2 clinical trials and beyond, and to commercialize compounds derived from research in a broad range of indications [4]. In October 2020, Protagonist and Johnson & Johnson expanded their original 2017 co-development and commercialization agreement to include additional IL-23R antagonists (one of which was icotrokinra) with applications in multiple indications [10, 11]. This collaboration agreement was amended in July 2021, resulting in Johnson & Johnson solely conducting all future studies beyond those that are currently ongoing with the current IL-23 antagonists, including icotrokinra [12].

Scientific Summary

Pharmacodynamics

Icotrokinra is a chemically synthesized cyclic peptide that selectively binds to the IL-23R with high affinity (ΚD 7.1 pM), and potently blocks IL-23 signaling (IC50 5.6 pM) and downstream production of inflammatory cytokines, without affecting IL-12 signaling [2, 5]. In vitro and ex vivo, icotrokinra inhibited IL-23 induced interferon-γ (IFNγ) production in natural killer cells (IC50 18.4 pM), and in blood from healthy donors (median IC50 11 pM) and from patients with PsO (median IC50 9 pM) [2]. In a rat model of IL-23 induced skin inflammation, icotrokinra inhibited IL-23 induced skin thickening and IL17A, IL17F and IL22 gene induction [2]. In a rat model of trinitrobenzene sulfonic acid (TNBS)‑induced colitis, icotrokinra attenuated TNBS induced weight loss and reduced colon weight/length ratios, which is an indicator of tissue oedema. In blood from rats administered icotrokinra, a dose-dependent inhibition of ex vivo IL-23-stimulated IL-17A production was evident [2]. Dose-dependent IL-23-induced IL-22 expression was seen in human colon explants exposed to icotrokinra in vitro, and significant inhibition of IL-23-induced IL-22 expression was seen in colon and rectum biopsies from healthy subjects administered icotrokinra compared with those administered placebo in a phase 1 trial [13].

In the phase 3 ICONIC-LEAD trial in patients with moderate-to-severe plaque PsO (NCT06095115), icotrokinra significantly (p < 0.05) reduced serum levels of proinflammatory cytokines IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-19, IL-22 and β-defensin-2 (BD-2) from baseline pretreatment levels at week 16, and achieved a decrease from baseline in the expression of mRNA related to the IL-23/Th17 pathway and PsO, including the molecular targets IL17A, IL17F, IL19, IL22, IL23A and DEFB4A, in lesional skin biopsies up to 24 weeks post treatment [5, 14].

In the 16-week phase 2 FRONTIER 1 trial (NCT05223868) and the FRONTIER 2 long-term extension study (through week 52) [NCT05364554] in patients with moderate-to-severe plaque PsO [15], treatment with icotrokinra was associated with rapid, dose-related reductions from baseline in serum levels of IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-22 and BD-2 that were evident from week 4, continued through week 52 and were greatest with icotrokinra 100 mg twice daily; reductions in these markers were greater for all icotrokinra doses relative to placebo [15]. Consistent with serum responses, administration of icotrokinra 100 mg twice daily, but not placebo, significantly reduced BD-2 levels in lesional skin at week 16 to levels seen in non-lesional skin, and reduced expression of PsO-associated genes and downregulated disease-relevant proteins in lesional skin at week 16 [15].While icotrokinra selectively inhibited IL-23–driven psoriatic inflammation, broader modulation of circulating proteins, including serum IL-23 levels, was not observed. [15].

In the phase 2b ANTHEM-UC trial in patients with moderately-to-severely active UC (NCT06049017), treatment with once daily icotrokinra 100mg, 200 mg and 400 mg, but not placebo, reduced serum levels of C-reactive protein, faecal calprotectin, IL-22, IL-17A and IL-17F relative to baseline, and reduced expression of gene sets associated with IL-23-driven inflammation in tissue at week 12 relative to baseline [16].

Pharmacokinetics

The pharmacokinetics of oral icotrokinra are dose proportional across a 10–1000 mg range in healthy subjects [2, 5]. After administration of a single dose of icotrokinra 200 mg, mean Cmax is 3.62 ng/mL, median Tmax is 2 h and AUC∞ is 44.8 ng·h/mL. Accumulation after administration of repeated doses was up to 1.6-fold for Cmax and up to 1.5-fold for AUC. Icotrokinra steady state is reached in ~ 3 days. When administered with a high-fat meal, icotrokinra Cmax decreased by 59% and AUC decreased by 43%. Icotrokinra is 52% bound to plasma protein and the blood to plasma partition ratio is 0.53. At steady state, icotrokinra distributes widely into tissues (Vd 92,800 L) [5].

Icotrokinra is a peptide and it is metabolized by peptide catabolism into smaller peptides. Icotrokinra median t1/2 is 12 h CL/F is 6550 L/h. After oral administration of icotrokinra to healthy subjects, ~ 37–81% of a dose was recovered in faeces within 24 h as the unchanged drug, and 0.001% of the dose was recovered in urine as the unchanged drug [5, 17]. Icotrokinra AUC is increased 2.47-fold in patients with moderate (eGFR ≥ 30 to < 60 mL/min) renal impairment and 2.78-fold in severe (eGFR ≥ 15 to < 30 mL/min) renal impairment. Mild renal impairment (eGFR ≥ 60 to < 90 mL/min) had no clinically significant effect on the pharmacokinetics of icotrokinra [5]. Therapeutic Trials

Plaque Psoriasis

Icotrokinra was effective for treating plaque PsO in the global, phase 3, placebo- and deucravacitinib-controlled ICONIC ADVANCE 1 (NCT06143878) trial in adults with moderate-to-severe disease [18]. At week 16, significantly more recipients of icotrokinra 200 mg once daily (n = 311) than placebo (n = 156) recipients achieved an Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA) 0/1 response [68% vs 11%; treatment difference 58% (95% CI 50–64); p < 0.0001] and at least 90% improvement in Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI 90) [55% vs 4%; treatment difference 51% (95% CI 44–57); p < 0.0001] (co-primary endpoints). Icotrokinra met all multiplicity-adjusted key secondary endpoints, showing superiority versus placebo (evaluated at weeks 4, 8, and 16) and oral deucravacitinib 6 mg once daily (evaluated at weeks 16 and 24) [18].

Icotrokinra was effective for treating plaque PsO in the global, phase 3, placebo- and deucravacitinib-controlled ICONIC ADVANCE 2 (NCT06220604) trial in adults with moderate-to-severe disease [18]. At week 16, significantly more recipients of icotrokinra 200 mg once daily (n = 322) than placebo (n = 82) recipients achieved an IGA 0/1 response [70% vs 9%; treatment difference 62% (95% CI 53–69); p < 0.0001] and PASI 90 [57% vs 1%; treatment difference 56% (95% CI 48–62); p < 0.0001] (co-primary endpoints). Icotrokinra met all multiplicity-adjusted key secondary endpoints, showing superiority versus placebo (evaluated at weeks 4, 8 and 16) and oral deucravacitinib 6 mg once daily (evaluated at weeks 16 and 24) [18]. ICONIC‑ADVANCE 1 and ICONIC-ADVANCE 2 are companion trials in design [18]. Eligible patients were ≥ 18 years of age with moderate-to-severe plaque PsO [defined as body-surface-area (BSA) involvement ≥ 10%, PASI ≥ 12, and IGA ≥ 3 (where 3 is moderate and 4 is severe)] diagnosed for at least 26 weeks at screening who were candidates for phototherapy or systemic treatment. IGA 0/1 response was defined as IGA score of 0 (cleared) or 1 (minimal) with a ≥ 2-grade improvement from baseline. At baseline, mean age was 46.7 years in ICONIC‑ADVANCE 1 and 46.1 years in ICONIC‑ADVANCE 2; mean PsO disease duration was 17.3 and 17.6 years; mean total PASI scores were 20.1 and 19.8; mean BSA involvement was 26.1% and 25.8%, respectively [18].

Icotrokinra improved plaque PsO in the global, phase 3, placebo-controlled ICONIC-LEAD (NCT06095115) trial in adults and adolescents ≥12 years of age with moderate-to-severe disease [19]. At week 16, significantly more recipients of icotrokinra 200 mg once daily (n = 456) than placebo (n = 228) recipients achieved IGA 0/1 response [65% vs 8%; treatment difference 56% (95% CI 50–62); p < 0.001] and PASI 90 [50% vs 4%; treatment difference 45% (95% CI 40–50); p < 0.001] (co-primary endpoints). At week 16 in the adolescent subgroup, an IGA 0/1 response occurred in 84% of icotrokinra recipients (n = 44) and in 27% of placebo recipients (n = 22) and a PASI 90 response occurred in 70% and 14% of those in the respective treatment groups [19]. Eligible patients had been diagnosed with moderate-to-severe plaque PsO for at least 26 weeks before screening and were candidates for phototherapy or systemic treatment. Of the 684 participants who underwent randomization, 66 (10%) were adolescents (44 in the icotrokinra group and 22 in the placebo group). At baseline, mean age was 42.6 years; mean PsO disease duration was 17.1 years; mean PASI total score was 19.9; mean BSA involvement was 25.5% [19].

Icotrokinra was effective in improving PsO involving high-impact sites in the global, phase 3, placebo-controlled ICONIC-TOTAL (NCT06095102) trial in adults and adolescents ≥12 years of age with moderate-to-severe disease [20]. At week 16, significantly more recipients of icotrokinra 200 mg once daily (n = 208) than placebo (n = 103) recipients achieved IGA 0/1 response [56.7% vs 5.8%; treatment difference 51.1% (95% CI 42.1–58.8); p < 0.001]. Significantly more icotrokinra than placebo recipients achieved absence/clear or minimal/almost clear responses in the scalp [65.9% vs 10.6%; treatment difference 55.5% (95% CI 44.8–64.4); p < 0.001] and genitalia [76.5% vs 21.4%; treatment difference 55.4% (95% CI 39.1–68.0); p < 0.001], but not in the hands and feet (41.7% vs 26.1%; treatment difference 16.7%) [20]. Eligible patients (n = 311) were diagnosed with moderate-to-severe plaque PsO for ≥ 26 weeks and were candidates for phototherapy or systemic treatment; had a minimum BSA involvement ≥ 1%; an IGA score ≥ 2 (where 2 is mild, 3 is moderate and 4 is severe) for overall skin and had failed to respond to ≥ 1 topical therapy. Patients were also required to have at least moderate PsO involving the high impact sites of scalp (n = 252), genitalia (n = 140), and/or hands and feet (n = 71). At baseline, mean age was 45 years; mean PsO disease duration was 16.3 years; mean BSA involvement was 16% [20].

Icotrokinra was an effective treatment in patients with moderate-to-severe plaque PsO in the 16-week, phase 2 placebo-controlled, dose-finding FRONTIER 1 (NCT05223868) trial [21]. At week 16, significantly more icotrokinra 25 mg once daily (n = 43), 25 mg twice daily (n = 41), 50 mg once daily (n = 43), 100 mg once daily (n = 43) and 100 mg twice daily (n = 42) recipients achieved a PASI 75 response (primary endpoint) compared with placebo (n = 43) recipients (37%, 51%, 58%, 65% and 79% vs 9%; p < 0.001 for all comparisons) and a significant dose-response signal was evident [21]. At baseline in FRONTIER 1, the mean PASI score was 19.1, the mean duration of PsO was 18.2 years, and 78% of the patients had previously received systemic treatments [21].

Patients completing FRONTIER 1 could enrol in the FRONTIER 2 (NCT05364554) extension study in which icotrokinra recipients continued to receive the dose assigned in FRONTIER 1 in a blinded manner through week 52, and placebo recipients crossed over to receive icotrokinra 100 mg once daily from week 16–52 of FRONTIER 2 [22]. Response rates in the icotrokinra groups at week 16 were maintained through week 52. At week 52, a PASI 75 response was seen in 49%, 58%, 70%, 65% and 76% of those in the 25 mg once daily (n = 35), 25 mg twice daily (n = 40), 50 mg once daily (n = 39), 100 mg once daily (n = 40) and 100 mg twice daily (n = 38) groups, respectively. At week 52, a PASI 75 response was seen in 66% of placebo recipients who crossed over to icotrokinra 100 mg once daily (n = 35) [15, 22].

Ulcerative Colitis

In the phase 2b ANTHEM-UC (NCT06049017) dose-finding trial in patients with moderately-to severely active UC [23], a clinical response at week 12 (primary endpoint) was achieved in significantly more once daily icotrokinra 400 mg (n = 63), icotrokinra 200 mg (n = 62) and icotrokinra 100 mg (n = 64) than placebo (n = 63) recipients (63.5%, 58.1% and 54.7% vs 27.0%; all p < 0.001 vs placebo). At week 12, significantly more icotrokinra 400 mg than placebo recipients achieved clinical remission (30.2% vs 11.1%; p = 0.006), symptomatic remission (46.0% vs 19.0%; p < 0.001) and endoscopic improvement (36.5% vs 14.3%; p = 0.002) [23]. Eligible patients had a diagnosis of UC ≥ 12 weeks duration prior to screening, a modified Mayo score (mMS) of 5–9 inclusive, a Mayo endoscopic subscore ≥ 2 and inadequate response/intolerance to a broad range of therapies, or corticosteroid dependence. Clinical response was a decrease from baseline in the mMS score by ≥ 30% and ≥ 2 points, with either a ≥ 1 point decrease from baseline in the rectal bleeding subscore or a rectal bleeding subscore of 0 or 1. Adverse Events

The most common adverse reactions reported more frequently with icotrokinra 200 mg once daily (n = 1296) than placebo (n = 568) between weeks 0–16 of treatment in four phase 3 trials (ICONIC-ADVANCE 1, ICONIC-ADVANCE 2, ICONIC-LEAD and ICONIC-TOTAL) in adult and paediatric patients ≥ 12 years of age with moderate-to-severe plaque PsO were headache (4.1% vs 3.3%), nausea (1.2% vs 0.5%), cough (1.2% vs 0.2%), fungal infection (1.1% vs 0%) and fatigue (1.0% vs 0.5%) [5]. Adverse reactions occurring in < 1% of icotrokinra recipients and more frequently than with placebo through week 16 were gastritis, abdominal discomfort, and one fatal case involving upper gastrointestinal bleeding in a patient with underlying risk factors. A relationship of this event to icotrokinra is not established [5]. The adverse reactions profile of icotrokinra 200 mg once daily in paediatric patients ≥ 12 years of age (n = 72) in ICONIC-LEAD and ICONIC-TOTAL was consistent with that in the overall population [5].

Through week 52 in ICONIC-ADVANCE 1, ICONIC-ADVANCE 2, ICONIC-LEAD and ICONIC-TOTAL, 9.6% of icotrokinra recipients (199/2083) developed anti-drug antibodies (ADAs); no neutralizing antibodies were detected [5]. ADAs had no clinically relevant effects on icotrokinra pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, efficacy or safety during the 52 weeks of treatment [5].

Ongoing Clinical Trials

Ongoing trials of icotrokinra in the phase 3 ICONIC clinical development program include the ICONIC-ADVANCE 1, ICONIC-ADVANCE 2, ICONIC-LEAD, ICONIC-TOTAL and ICONIC-ASCEND (NCT06934226) trials in moderate-to-severe plaque PsO; the ICONIC-PsA 1 (NCT06878404) and ICONIC-PsA 2 (NCT06807424) trials in PsA; the ICONIC-UC (NCT07196748) trial in UC; and the ICONIC-CD (NCT07196722) trial in CD.

Current Status

Icotrokinra received its first approval on 17 March 2026 for the treatment of moderate-to-severe plaque PsO in adults and paediatric patients ≥ 12 years of age and who weigh ≥ 40 kg who are candidates for systemic therapy or phototherapy in the USA [5, 7].
