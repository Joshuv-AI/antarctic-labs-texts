Design, optimization and characterization of mucoadhesive buccal film as a novel delivery system for doxazosin mesylate
Authors: Nidhal Khazaal Maraie, Ali N. Wannas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Doxazosin mesylate is a selective α1-adrenergic receptor antagonist with low oral bioavailability due to poor aqueous solubility and extensive first-pass hepatic metabolism. This study aimed to design and optimize a doxazosin mucoadhesive buccal film to overcome hepatic metabolism and enable rapid drug onset. The buccal films were prepared by solvent casting, using a Plackett–Burman design to screen formulation variables affecting ex vivo mucoadhesive force, followed by a 22 full factorial design to optimize in vitro drug release by varying the concentrations of HPMC, the main film-forming polymer, and glycerin, a plasticizer. The prepared films were characterized for physico-mechanical properties, surface pH, mucoadhesive force, swelling properties, and drug release. In addition, the optimized formula was further characterized by scanning electron microscopy and ex vivo permeation study. The screening study showed that mucoadhesive strength ranged from 28 to 41 g, with the polymer type, polymer concentration, plasticizer type, and plasticizer concentration identified as the most influential variables. The optimized formula (FF3), containing 66 w/w% HPMC K15M and 21 w/w% glycerin, achieved 100% drug release within 15 min with acceptable film properties, in addition, SEM analysis showed a relatively uniform and compact surface with slight undulations, furthermore the ex vivo permeation reached 91% within 15 min, corresponding to a cumulative permeated amount of 1820 µg, a cumulative amount per unit area of 2275 µg/cm2, and an estimated steady-state flux of 12750 µg/cm2/h. It was concluded that the developed buccal film is a promising system for improving doxazosin bioavailability and providing rapid therapeutic onset.

Introduction

Oral route is still the most accepted route by most patients, and is associated with high compliance; however, it is associated with poor drug bioavailability owing to the extensive first-pass metabolism and enzymatic degradation by digestive enzymes. These limitations are particularly important for drugs that need a rapid onset of action or may cause dose-related adverse effects (Sabra et al. 2024; Mahanur et al. 2022). Buccal drug delivery has been recognized as a better route to overcome these challenges. Since it allows direct access to systemic circulation through the jugular vein, it overcomes the bioavailability limitations associated with first-pass metabolism and improves the bioavailability of many drugs. In addition, buccal delivery offers several advantages, including ease of administration, rapid onset of action, and improved patient compliance, especially in patients with swallowing difficulties. This approach may be particularly useful in antihypertensive therapy, where rapid systemic absorption and avoidance of first-pass metabolism may improve the therapeutic response and reduce the variability in drug exposure (Southward et al. 2025).

Buccal film has considerable advantages over different buccal dosage forms owing to improved patient compliance, resistance to the mechanical forces of the oral environment, and flexibility without affecting speech or mastication (Halasagi et al. 2025; Solanki and Parmar 2025). Moreover, the use of mucoadhesive film polymers is associated with enhanced drug residence time at the absorption site, thereby improving drug permeation and therapeutic efficacy, allowing a rapid or controlled release drug profile in accordance with the need. Formulation of buccal films for poorly water soluble drugs is still a challenge, especially for Biopharmaceutics Classification System (BCS) Class II drugs, which have low solubility and high permeability, in which, the dissolution is considered as the rate-limiting step for absorption, which requires formulation strategies to enhance solubility and dissolution. Several approaches have been used, including the incorporation of hydrophilic polymers, surfactants, and plasticizers to enhance drug release and bioavailability in buccal delivery systems (Albani et al. 2025; Mahmud et al. 2025).

Hydrophilic polymers such as hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) are widely utilized in the formulation of buccal films due to their ability to form hydrated matrices that facilitate rapid wetting and uniform drug dispersion, in addition surfactants such as Tween 80 reduce interfacial tension and improve drug wettability, which may enhance the dissolution performance, moreover plasticizers such as glycerol and propylene glycol have an important role in improving film flexibility and mechanical strength, while also enhancing the film forming ability of the polymeric system and ensuring uniform drug distribution within the matrix (Ahmed et al. 2020; Mady et al. 2021).

Doxazosin mesylate, a selective α1 adrenergic receptor antagonist commonly associated with many side effects, had a major formulation development challenge and is still used for the treatment of hypertension and benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) (Yadaf et al. 2023). It is a BCS Class II drug, and oral administration of the drug is associated with extensive first-pass metabolism and variable bioavailability of approximately 65%, which may require frequent dosing to maintain therapeutic plasma concentration (Al Ashmawy et al., 2021). The commercially available dosage forms of doxazosin mesylate are mainly immediate and extended release tablets, which do not completely overcome pharmacokinetic limitations and may be associated with dose related adverse effects and delayed onset of action (Al Zahraa et al. 2023).

Several formulation strategies have been studied to improve the bioavailability of doxazosin, including nanosuspensions, self-nanoemulsifying systems, and fast-disintegrating tablets (Al Ashmawy et al. 2024; Al Zahraa et al. 2021; Yadav et al. 2024). Buccal delivery has also been investigated for some BCS class II antihypertensive drugs such as carvedilol, which has been formulated as a buccal or mucoadhesive system to improve bioavailability (Thimmasetty et al. 2008; Hamed et al. 2016). However, studies concerning buccal or mucoadhesive delivery systems for doxazosin mesylate are limited, and a systematic approach for the design and optimization of such systems has not been extensively studied. In addition, there is still a need to develop a dosage form that provides rapid drug release with adequate mucoadhesion and mechanical integrity.

In the development of mucoadhesive buccal films, the proper selection of formulation components plays an important role, including film-forming polymer, plasticizers to improve film flexibility and decrease brittleness, and surfactant to enhance drug solubility and permeation (Shaikh et al. 2011; Irfan et al. 2020). The appropriate combination and optimization of these components are essential to achieve a balance between mechanical strength, mucoadhesion, and drug release performance (Kianfar et al. 2014). To our knowledge, no previous study has systematically developed and optimized a mucoadhesive buccal film of doxazosin mesylate using combined experimental design approaches, including Plackett–Burman screening and factorial optimization, to enhance mucoadhesion and obtain rapid drug release.

Thus, the aim of the present study is to develop and optimize a mucoadhesive buccal film of doxazosin mesylate by focusing on identifying the key formulation variables affecting mucoadhesive strength and drug release, followed by optimization of the formulation to improve bioavailability and provide a rapid therapeutic onset while reducing dose-related side effects for the treatment of hypertension and benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH).

Materials and methods

Materials

Doxazosin mesylate (DOXM) was purchased from Baoji Guokang Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (China). Hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC K15M and HPMC E5) and ethyl cellulose were purchased from Provizer Pharma (India). Sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (Na CMC) and sodium alginate were obtained from SDI (Iraq). Propylene glycol and glycerin were sourced from Panreac AAG (Spain) and GCC (UK). Dibutyl phthalate was purchased from Fluka Chemika (Switzerland). All chemicals and reagents used in this study were of analytical grade.

Assessment of key formulation variables by Plackett–Burman design

A Plackett–Burman design was applied using Minitab® software (version 21, Minitab Inc., USA), was applied as a preliminary screening tool to assess the effect of formulation components on the ex vivo mucoadhesive performance of the fabricated buccal films. This design strategy enables the rapid identification of critical factors while minimizing the number of experimental runs (Paun et al. 2024; Gregor et al. 2024). As summarized in Table 1, twelve distinct formulations were prepared to examine the influence of seven independent variables, including polymer grade (HPMC K15M versus HPMC E5), polymer content (66% versus 70%), type of plasticizer (glycerin versus propylene glycol), plasticizer amount (13% versus 21%), citric acid level (2% versus 6%), stevia level (2% versus 6%), and Tween 80 concentration (3% versus 7%). The selected formulation variables and their investigated ranges were chosen according to the literature review, the solvent casting method adopted in the present study, and preliminary formulation trials performed to identify suitable experimental limits and practically feasible compositions for buccal film preparation. Preparation of mucoadhesive films

The mucoadhesive buccal films were prepared using the solvent casting technique (Salehi and Boddohi 2017). In brief, the required amount of polymer (HPMC K15M or HPMC E5) was dispersed in about 20–30% of the total volume of 50 mL hot distilled water (80–90 °C). The rest of the volume of cold distilled water was added, and the mixture was stirred continuously at 400 rpm until a clear and homogeneous solution was formed. Plasticizers (glycerin or propylene glycol) and Tween 80 were added. Doxazosin mesylate (2.42 mg, which is equivalent to 2 mg base), citric acid, and stevia were dissolved in a small amount of distilled water and added to the polymeric solution. Then the solution was stirred for 24 h to ensure homogeneity and then degassed to eliminate air bubbles. The prepared drug-polymer solution was then cast onto the preformed backing layer in the same Petri dish (4 × 6 cm) to obtain a uniform film thickness. Drying was carried out in a hot air oven at 40 °C overnight until complete evaporation of the solvent, then the dried films were carefully peeled off, inspected for any defects, and cut into uniform patches of 2 × 2 cm, each containing approximately 2 mg of doxazosin mesylate. The patches were then packaged in aluminum foil and stored in a desiccator until further use (Maslii et al. 2025).

Optimization drug release (Full factorial design)

After the initial screening, a 22 full factorial design was employed to optimize the in vitro drug release profile. The design included two important factors at two levels: the concentration of HPMC K15M (66% and 70%) and the concentration of glycerin (13% and 21%), These factors were selected since the screening study showed that the polymer and plasticizer related variables were the most influential factors affecting mucoadhesive performance, and they were also considered the most relevant variables for the subsequent optimization of drug release and overall film performance. Since only two key factors were advanced to the optimization stage and the investigated ranges had already been narrowed based on preliminary trials and screening results, a 22 full factorial design was considered sufficient to evaluate the main effects and their interaction within the selected experimental domain, resulting in a total of four experimental runs (Table 2), in which the cumulative percentage of drug released at 15 min was selected as the response variable for optimization. The experimental design and statistical analysis were carried out using Minitab® software (version 21, Minitab Inc., USA), in which regression analysis and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were applied to evaluate the significance of the main effects and their interaction on the response. The optimized formulation was selected on the basis of maximizing drug release while maintaining acceptable physicomechanical properties and film integrity (Gohel and Amin 1998). Characterization and evaluation

Weight and thickness variation

Three samples of each formulation were randomly chosen. Film weight was determined using a digital analytical balance. Film thickness was measured at the center and at four peripheral points using a digital Vernier caliper, and the mean thickness was calculated (Bose et al. 2020).

Surface pH

The surface pH of the buccal films was evaluated to ensure mucosal compatibility, as highly acidic or alkaline pH can cause irritation. Each film was pre-moistened with 1 ml of distilled water at room temperature for 1 h. The pH was determined by touching the electrode to the film surface, allowing 1 min for equilibration. Measurements were performed in triplicate, and the mean values were reported (Bose et al. 2020).

Folding endurance

Folding endurance was determined by repeatedly folding the film at the same location until it either fractured. The total number of folds sustained at that point was recorded as the film's folding endurance. Three films from each formula were chosen at random for this test (Pradeep et al. 2025).

Content uniformity

To ensure uniform distribution of doxazosin, the film was placed in a beaker containing 100 mL of phosphate buffer pH 6.8, stirred with a magnetic bead at 37 °C and 300 rpm for 4 h. Then, the solution was filtered with 0.45 µm filter, and the drug content of the filtrate was checked with a UV–visible spectrophotometer at λ max (245nm) (Maharjane et al. 2021). The test was repeated three times for each formula.

Swelling index

The swelling index was measured by putting a pre-weighed film (W₁) on a glass slide in a Petri dish with 20 mL of phosphate buffer solution (pH 6.8). At fixed time intervals (over 60 min), the film was removed, and the surface water was carefully wiped off, and the weight of the swollen film (W₂) was measured. All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3). In vitro drug release

Drug release studies were performed using a USP dissolution apparatus type II (paddle method) at 37 ± 0.5 °C and 50 rpm. The film was fixed to a glass slide with the backing layer in contact with the glass surface and submerged in 250 mL of phosphate buffer solution (pH 6.8) with 1% sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS). Samples (5 mL) were removed at predetermined time intervals (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 15, 30, 60, 120, 180, and 240 min) and replaced with fresh medium. Drug concentration was determined spectrophotometrically at 245 nm. A calibration curve was constructed in the concentration range of 2–10 µg/mL using the same dissolution medium. Ex vivo mucoadhesive strength

Mucoadhesive strength was measured using a modified physical balance method. Fresh chicken pouch mucosa was used as the model membrane. The mucosal tissue was carefully rinsed with phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) to remove any adhering debris, then mounted at the bottom of a Petri dish with the mucosal surface facing upward using cyanoacrylate adhesive, the phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) was added to the Petri dish to keep the mucosal surface hydrated throughout the experiment, after that the buccal film (2 × 2 cm) was attached to the lower surface of a glass stopper using cyanoacrylate adhesive. The stopper was then connected to one arm of a modified physical balance, and the glass stopper carrying the film was gently brought into contact with the mucosal surface and kept under preload force for 10 min to allow sufficient mucoadhesive interaction. The force required to detach the film from the mucosal surface was determined by adding water weight to the other side of the balance until detachment occurred. The water weight (g) required for detachment was recorded as the mucoadhesive strength (Ghareeb and Mohammad 2013). Ex vivo residence time

The ex vivo residence time was determined by attaching the buccal film (2 × 2 cm), premoistened with one drop of phosphate buffer solution (pH 6.8), to the mucosal surface of excised chicken pouch mucosa, with the backing layer facing the dissolution medium, then by gentle finger pressure for 60 s to ensure proper adhesion. The beaker was filled with 500 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C, and the system was stirred at 150 rpm to simulate the dynamic conditions of the oral cavity. The ex vivo residence time was defined as the time required for complete detachment of the film from the mucosal surface. All measurements were carried out in triplicate (n = 3), and the mean residence time was recorded (Ghareeb and Mohammad 2013).

Ex vivo permeation study

A Franz diffusion cell with a receptor compartment volume of 10 mL and an effective diffusion area of 0.8 cm2 was used. The fresh chicken buccal mucosal tissue was rinsed with phosphate buffer and placed between the donor and receptor compartments, where the receptor chamber was filled with phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.4) containing 1% SLS to maintain sink conditions throughout the experiment, and the system was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C with continuous stirring at 50 rpm, then, the film was applied to the mucosa of the donor compartment, and a few drops of phosphate buffer were added to maintain hydration, and at predetermined time intervals for 4 h, 1 mL samples were withdrawn from the receptor compartment and immediately replaced with equal volume of fresh medium to maintain constant volume and sink condition. Drug concentration was determined spectrophotometrically at λ max 245 nm using the previously constructed calibration curve (El-Nahas et al. 2017; Navamanisubramanian et al. 2018). The cumulative amount of drug permeated per unit area (µg/cm2) was calculated, and the steady-state flux (J, µg/cm2/h) was obtained from the slope of the linear portion of the permeation profile. All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to assess the morphology of the film; the sample of the chosen formula was fixed on circular brass stubs using double backed adhesive tape, sputter coated for 30 s with gold palladium under an argon environment, and then examined under the SEM to assess surface uniformity and drug distribution (Abdella et al. 2022).

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis for drug compatibility

FT-IR spectroscopy was used to investigate possible interactions between the drug and the polymer, with the pure drug (formula FF3), the physical mixture, and the polymer analyzed separately. Each sample was powdered and placed in the designated area of the device for spectral analysis over the range of 400–4000 cm⁻1 (Anwar et al. 2024).

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent measurements (n = 3). Statistical analysis of the experimental designs, including the Plackett–Burman screening and the 22 full factorial design, was performed using Minitab® software (version 21, Minitab Inc., USA), in which the regression analysis and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to assess the significance of the formulation variables and model fitness. The remaining comparative experimental data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 26, IBM Corp., USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, was applied where appropriate. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Achieving a balance between adhesion, mechanical strength, and drug release rates in the formulation of a mucoadhesive buccal film for poorly soluble drugs such as doxazosin mesylate is challenging. The results of the Plackett–Burman screening study demonstrated that polymer type, polymer concentration, plasticizer type, and plasticizer concentration were the most significant factors influencing mucoadhesive strength, with HPMC K15M showing greater mucoadhesive strength than HPMC E5. This can be attributed to the higher molecular weight and viscosity of K15M, which enables greater chain entanglement with mucin glycoproteins when the polymer matrix comes into contact with water (Peppas and Buri 1985). Moreover, the increased concentration of the polymer to 70% resulted in a more compact matrix with a higher concentration of hydrogen-bonding sites, thus enhancing the adhesive force (Smart 2005). Plasticizer type and concentration also significantly influenced mucoadhesive strength, in which Formulations containing glycerin exhibited higher adhesion than those containing propylene glycol, which may be attributed to glycerin greater hydrophilicity and its ability to enhance polymer hydration and flexibility, in addition, increasing plasticizer concentration further improved mucoadhesion, likely due to increased surface contact with the mucosal tissue and enhanced polymer chain mobility. These findings are similar to previous studies that have reported that both polymer characteristics and plasticizer properties play a crucial role in modulating mucoadhesive performance by affecting polymer flexibility, hydration, and interactions with mucin (Perioli et al. 2004). In contrast, citric acid, stevia, and Tween 80 did not significantly affect mucoadhesive strength, suggesting that their primary role is in auxiliary functions, such as taste modification and solubilization, rather than adhesion.

However, although a high concentration of the polymer is desirable for adhesion, the full factorial analysis showed that it retards drug release. The slower release rate in 70% HPMC formulations (FF2, FF4) can be attributed to the presence of a strongly viscous gel barrier, which increases the diffusional path length of the drug (Peppas and Sahlin 1989). On the other hand, glycerin exhibited a positive effect on drug release, due to the hydrophilic plasticizer properties, which caused a substantial decrease in the glass transition temperature of the polymer, thus increasing the flexibility of the chains, which explains the high folding endurance of the 21% glycerin formulations (Eslami et al. 2023). At the same time, the hygroscopic properties of glycerin caused a rapid increase in water uptake (swelling), which facilitated the rapid formation of channels in the matrix, resulting in the fast release. These findings are consistent with previous studies that reported that increasing polymer concentration decreases drug release, whereas higher plasticizer levels enhance release by promoting matrix hydration and flexibility (Semalty et al. 2008). A balance between strong mucoadhesion and rapid drug release was achieved by selecting a formulation composition that provides sufficient adhesive performance without forming an excessively dense polymeric matrix, resulting in the FF3 formula, which provides adequate mucoadhesion and rapid release. The formula containing 66% HPMC and 21% glycerin (FF3) was selected as the optimized formulation, as it provided the highest drug release while maintaining acceptable physicomechanical and mucoadhesive properties.

The physicomechanical properties of the prepared films support their suitability for buccal application, in which the film uniformity is essential to ensure dose consistency and reproducible performance. Appropriate mechanical properties are required to allow handling and application without cracking or deformation, and a suitable surface pH is particularly important to minimize the risk of mucosal irritation, thereby improving patient acceptability (Muzib and Kumari 2011). The improvement in folding endurance with increasing glycerin content can be attributed to the reduced intermolecular forces within the polymer matrix and increased polymer chain mobility, leading to greater flexibility and reduced brittleness, which is a desirable characteristic for buccal films intended to remain intact during handling and administration. Similar effects of plasticizers on the flexibility and mechanical performance of polymeric films have been reported previously in buccal and related film systems (Perioli et al. 2004; Kittipongpatana et al. 2022). These findings indicated that the selected FF3 formula provided films with appropriate flexibility, mechanical integrity, and mucosal compatibility, all of which are essential attributes for effective buccal drug delivery.

The ethyl cellulose backing layer is a water-insoluble polymer that also contributes to the functional performance of the buccal film, reducing drug loss from the non-mucosal surface and favoring unidirectional release toward the buccal mucosa; it provides structural support and improves handling during application, so it was selected for its established suitability in buccal bilayer systems, compatibility with the solvent-casting technique, and availability. Similar use of ethyl cellulose as a backing layer in buccal drug-delivery systems has been reported previously (Remunan-Lopez et al. 1998; Abruzzo et al. 2017).

The swelling behavior of buccal films is a critical determinant of in vivo performance, as hydration promotes intimate contact with the mucosal surface and facilitates drug diffusion by partially relaxing the polymer matrix. The higher swelling tendency observed for the selected FF3 formula can be attributed to the combined effects of a lower polymer concentration and a higher glycerin content, which permits faster penetration of the aqueous medium, whereas glycerin, as a hydrophilic plasticizer, enhances water uptake and polymer chain mobility, thereby promoting matrix expansion. This behavior is consistent with previous studies on buccal mucoadhesive systems, which have shown that swelling is strongly dependent on formulation composition and plays an important role in both mucoadhesion and drug release performance (Perioli et al. 2004; Semalty et al. 2008; Muzib and Kumari 2011).

The SEM image of the optimized film showed a compact, continuous matrix with slight surface undulations, with no obvious cracks, large pores, or discrete drug crystals observed, indicating satisfactory film formation and suggesting uniform distribution of the incorporated components. The minor surface irregularities are common in solvent-cast films and may result from solvent evaporation and matrix reorganization during drying. Similar findings have been described in previous buccal film studies, where continuous surfaces with limited roughness were considered acceptable and indicative of good film quality (Salehi and Boddohi 2017; Abdella et al. 2022).

The short residence time of the optimized formula may be related to the low concentration of HPMC K15M, which decreases the surface viscosity of the matrix and enhances the displacement of the hydrated film, as well as a high percentage of glycerin (21%), which may increase water uptake and enhance drug dissolution. It has been reported that mucoadhesion and residence behavior are affected by the hydration and dissolution of the dosage form (Smart 2005; Mishra and Ramteke 2011). A similar finding was also reported with buccal films containing duloxetine hydrochloride (El Sharawy et al. 2017).

The rapid permeation of doxazosin mesylate from FF3 suggests that early hydration of the film was sufficient to promote drug dissolution and subsequent transport across the buccal membrane. Although the extent of swelling in the initial period was lower than the cumulative permeation, these parameters reflect different processes, as swelling describes water uptake by the polymeric matrix, whereas permeation reflects membrane transport of the dissolved drug. Thus, a moderate degree of swelling may still be adequate to relax the matrix, facilitate drug release, and support rapid permeation. This interpretation is consistent with previous buccal film studies showing that swelling contributes to release and residence behavior, while permeation is additionally influenced by formulation composition and membrane transport (Lodhi et al. 2013; Salehi and Boddohi 2017; Semalty et al. 2008). Although ex vivo permeation data cannot be directly equated with the in vivo onset of action, the rapid transport observed in the present study suggests that buccal delivery may shorten the absorption phase and support a faster therapeutic response.

The FTIR spectrum of doxazosin mesylate showed characteristic bands at 1637 cm⁻1 corresponding mainly to C = O stretching vibrations, 1592 cm⁻1 attributed to aromatic C = C stretching, and 1332 cm⁻1 assigned to S = O stretching of the mesylate group. The slight broadening and overlapping of peaks observed in the optimized formulation may be attributed to intermolecular interactions, such as hydrogen bonding, and to the high polymer content, which can mask or reduce peak intensity. These findings are consistent with previously reported studies on polymeric film systems, where physical interactions rather than chemical incompatibility are commonly observed (Anwar et al. 2024).

Conclusion

The study successfully used a Plackett–Burman design and a factorial design to develop and optimize a mucoadhesive buccal film of doxazosin mesylate, identifying the key formulation factors influencing bioadhesion and drug release. The optimized formula achieved a suitable balance between mucoadhesive performance, rapid drug release, and acceptable film characteristics. In addition, the rapid ex vivo permeation behavior of the optimized film suggests that buccal delivery of doxazosin mesylate may provide a fast onset of action through rapid transmucosal absorption. This delivery system may also help to overcome first-pass hepatic metabolism and may reduce dose-related side effects. Accordingly, the developed mucoadhesive buccal film can be considered a promising alternative dosage form for improving the therapeutic performance of doxazosin mesylate.
