Suboptimal lipid reduction and follow-up in patients with transient ischaemic attack: a need for a systematic, guideline-based approach to optimising lipid-lowering therapy
Authors: Yan Shi, Fahad Mir, Isuru Induruwa, Kayvan Khadjooi
Published: 2024

Introduction

Lipid management in patients with transient ischaemic attack (TIA) is critical to minimise the risk of future TIA and recurrent ischaemic stroke. National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines stipulate that part of secondary prevention after TIA should be initiation of a high-intensity statin (generally atorvastatin 40 mg or 80 mg), with a target of reducing non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) levels by more than 40% from baseline. Further research advises that absolute values of <3.8 mmol/L for total cholesterol (TC) and <1.8 mmol/L for low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels should be targeted.1 A full lipid profile (TC, HDL-C and non-HDL-C) should be measured at diagnosis and 3 months after commencing treatment to assess target success, with appropriate management adjustments such as increasing statin dose, discussing adherence or using a statin alternative if targets are not met.

Methods

Electronic clinic records were reviewed retrospectively using a data extraction template reflecting current national guidelines. Our study population included patients who attended the outpatient neurovascular clinic ('TIA clinic’) at Cambridge University Hospitals between 1 January and 30 June 2020. We excluded patients with alternative diagnoses to TIA and those admitted to hospital from the clinic. We collected demographic data, comorbidities and statin prescription in the clinic. Baseline and repeat lipid profile values, as well as lipid management advice included in GP letters sent from the clinic, were recorded.

Results

67 of the included 168 patients were already taking a statin (Table 1). Of these patients, 23 (34.3%) were on atorvastatin 40 mg or 80 mg daily. At the point of diagnosis, 35 out of 133 (26.3%) patients who were either not prescribed a statin or not taking a high-intensity statin were started on atorvastatin 40 mg or 80 mg.

Baseline full lipid profiles were collected in 127/168 (75.6%) patients. Out of this group of patients, only 49 (38.6%) had a follow-up check by 6 months. More specifically, only 26 (20.5%) met the gold-standard timeframe of 3 months.

Of the patients who had a repeat check by 6 months, only 14 (28.6%) achieved TC <3.8 mmol/L or 18 (36.7%) LDL-C <1.8 mmol/L. Only 11 (22.45%) achieved the NICE-recommended >40% reduction in non-HDL-C, with an average reduction of 57.2% among those patients. Out of all 49 patients, the average was a 10.7% reduction.

Review of clinic letters revealed that 98 (58.0%) contained no lipid modification advice, only 18 (10.7%) recommended a target range and <3% mentioned a time point for repeat check (Table 2). No patient received any dietary advice on lipid management and none of the patients with inadequate lipid modification on statin therapy had been changed to a statin alternative by the time of data collection.

Conclusion

Appropriate management of hyperlipidaemia after TIA needs to be carefully considered in every patient. Better lipid modification advice from secondary care is imperative, including advice on statin dose and timeframe for repeat lipid-profile check. Stricter adherence to guidelines could be implemented through a standardised care bundle by a dedicated secondary prevention service for ischaemic stroke and TIA patients.
