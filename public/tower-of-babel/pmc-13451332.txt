Developing interactive learning interfaces for teaching AI in health professions education using generative AI tools
Authors: Peter Washington, Anoop Muniyappa, Chen Liang, Kushith Reddy Hanmandla, Harshil Shah, Pierre Martin, Rishi Kadakia, Christian Burke, Sandrijn Van Schaik, Christy Boscardin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealth professions education (HPE) must now equip students to use artificial intelligence (AI) tools safely. We offer an instructional design strategy for HPE educators aiming to teach fundamental AI concepts to non-technical audiences: using generative AI to rapidly create interactive hands-on learning experiences, such as web-based simulations, dynamic visualizations, and case studies, that enable learners to explore AI concepts relevant to HPE.Subject terms: Health care, Mathematics and computing, Scientific community, Social sciences

The push for AI literacy in healthcare

“AI literacy” is a critical skill for healthcare professionals, essential for tasks that are becoming more common, such as interpreting diagnostic algorithms, working with clinical decision support, and communicating with AI developers1. The Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) recently (October 2025) put together a list of “AI Competencies for Medical Educators”2. The first category is “Understanding AI (What is AI and how does it work?)”, including topics such as understanding AI fundamentals, describing the statistical properties of AI and varied AI approaches to data, and describing the strengths and weaknesses of various AI systems2. While these competencies define what learners should know, they do not prescribe how educators can effectively teach such abstract and technical material. Generative AI now offers a promising pathway to bridge this gap by enabling rapid creation of interactive, hands-on learning experiences that make these AI principles digestible and attainable by health profession students who typically do not have a computer science background.

The potential of generative AI to create interactive educational experiences for AI literacy in HPE

Recent literature has rapidly cataloged the expanding uses of generative AI in medical education, including curriculum development, personalized learning materials, simulated patients, assessment support, and educational resource generation3–5. These reviews consistently emphasize both the promise of these tools and recurring risks, including hallucinated or inaccurate content, algorithmic bias, academic integrity concerns, privacy, inequitable access, overreliance, and the need for faculty oversight. In contrast to these prior works focusing on leveraging AI for traditional medical education, we discuss here the potential of leveraging AI to teach health AI concepts given the growing push for AI literacy in HPE. Teaching health AI concepts is distinct from much of traditional HPE: rather than the recall of clinical facts or the mastery of procedural skills, it requires quantitative, engineering-oriented reasoning about concepts such as probabilistic model outputs, statistical performance metrics, and how a model’s behavior shifts as its settings are changed. Concepts such as the way an interpretable model’s predicted risk changes as its inputs are varied and the movement of a model’s operating point along an ROC curve as its decision threshold is adjusted can be naturally conveyed through interactive web and mobile interfaces.

With only brief natural language prompts, large language models (LLMs) can now generate fully functional web-based simulations, dynamic visualizations, and data-driven exploratory tools that bring complex AI concepts to life for learners. Modern commercial LLMs like ChatGPT and Claude can easily be leveraged to create manipulable website interfaces such as sliders, toggles, or simulated clinical environments that respond in real time to user input, allowing learners to experiment with clinically oriented AI concepts without requiring any programming expertise. New specialized no-code AI tools for generating such interfaces are increasingly available.

Our suggested approach of teaching AI in HPE using interactive educational interfaces is grounded in experiential learning theory, which holds that learning is the process by which knowledge is created through the transformation of experience. Kolb’s experiential learning model6 involves a four-stage cyclical process: (1) a concrete experience, (2) reflective observation about the experience, (3) abstract conceptualization to learn from the experience, and (4) active experimentation to enable learners to try out what was learned. The central claim of experiential learning theory, and the reason it suits the teaching of quantitative material such as AI for health and healthcare, is that durable understanding of quantitative and engineering processes can often arise from the repeated process of acting, observing the consequences, and revising one’s mental model. In HPE, experiential learning has also been understood as a socially mediated process, in that experience alone is not sufficient because learners benefit from educator support, opportunities for participation, and guided reflection that help transform activity into transferable understanding7.

Recent trends in health professional education highlight the value of this style of technology-enhanced learning. Health professions educators are already experimenting with AI-driven virtual patients, adaptive quizzes, and even conversational agents like ChatGPT to support learning in fields from anatomy to clinical reasoning8,9. Furthermore, experiential learning theory is widely used across HPE to structure hands-on, reflective curricula, including clinical reasoning instruction and virtual simulation-based teaching that maps each simulation stage onto a cycle phase10,11. Similarly, engineering and AI education has been previously enhanced with experiential learning12, though we are not aware of published applications to the teaching of AI within HPE.

Understanding the role of educator facilitation through an AI-generated sensitivity-specificity simulation tool

To illustrate both the power of interactive, AI-generated learning experiences and the educator’s essential role in making them effective, we demonstrate an AI-generated web interface that teaches a fundamental lesson for clinicians: prediction models, including but not limited to AI models, can be made more sensitive or more specific depending on the chosen decision threshold, and there is a fundamental trade-off between sensitivity (i.e., the proportion of truly diseased patients the model correctly flags) and specificity (i.e., the proportion of truly healthy patients it correctly clears). Clinicians already learn the sensitivity–specificity tradeoff for interpreting diagnostic tests.

Using a simple ChatGPT 5 prompt (full prompt is in Supplementary Materials), we created a tool to simulate a binary clinical prediction model that predicts the risk of a disease based on test results (Fig. 1). Learners can adjust the model’s decision threshold via a slider and observe how metrics change: as sensitivity increases (catching more true positives), specificity falls (resulting in more false alarms), and vice versa. This directly ties to real-world decisions, such as whether to tune a sepsis alert system to catch every possible case (at the cost of many false alerts) or to only flag the most likely cases to avoid clinician alert fatigue. We note that this visualization was inspired by prior work13.

Interactive healthcare AI educational experience developed using a simple paragraph-long ChatGPT prompt.

This visualization teaches learners about the tradeoffs between sensitivity and specificity. This also teaches how AI-based risk models operate under the hood: the model computes a continuous probability score, and the chosen threshold determines whether that score triggers an automated alert or remains silent. Pedagogically, the value of the slider is not the metric itself but the experience of discovering the tradeoff: by moving the threshold and watching false alarms rise as missed cases fall, the learner enacts the Reflective Observation and Active Experimentation stages of Kolb’s cycle, which a static ROC figure does not afford. The interactive demo lets learners see how the AI’s internal decision logic changes as the threshold moves. A full HTML demo is provided in the Supplementary Material.

Our proposed instructional design strategy consists of the following cycle: (1) a concrete experience such as a class discussion of a case study, (2) reflective observation through interacting with the generated interface, (3) abstract conceptualization that occurs as the learner starts to connect changes in the interface with educational concepts, and (4) active experimentation, where the learner tests their newfound conceptualization on the interface.

In the concrete experience stage (stage 1), learners reason individually about a specific clinical case posed in the classroom, before anyone opens the simulator. For example, the educator might describe a 35-year-old patient with sudden shortness of breath and pleuritic chest pain who is being evaluated for a pulmonary embolism. The educator could ask learners to weigh, for this one patient, whether it is worse to miss the diagnosis (a false negative) or to raise a false alarm that sends the patient for an unnecessary CT scan (a false positive).

In the reflective observation stage (stage 2), learners then turn to the AI-generated simulator to see what the sensitivity-specificity trade-off looks like in general, moving the decision threshold slider and watching how sensitivity and specificity respond. The goal of this stage is for learners to notice that specificity falls as sensitivity rises, and vice versa.

In the abstract conceptualization stage (stage 3), learners put that observation into words as a general concept: a single threshold parameter governs the trade-off, no setting optimizes both metrics at once, and this principle holds well beyond the specific patient and beyond this particular pair of metrics.

Finally, in the active experimentation stage (stage 4), learners return to the simulator to validate the concept they have just formed, generating a concrete prediction (“raising the threshold should reduce false positives”), and immediately testing it, then feeding the result into the next turn of the cycle.

Crucially, the educator would facilitate all steps of the process except for arguably the final stage, where the learner is free to experiment (though the educator may facilitate that stage as well in some instantiations of this paradigm). This need for facilitation is consistent with broader experiential learning scholarship in medical education, which emphasizes that authentic or simulated experience becomes educationally meaningful through context, participation, interaction, and support rather than through exposure alone7.

Limitations and risks of generative AI-based interactive educational interfaces

The strategy we advocate is not universally appropriate, and we discuss the limitations and risks that should be considered. We note that prior literature reviews and viewpoints on AI for medical education have discussed limitations that also apply to our discussed use case. For example, in a scoping review of 131 studies published between 2023 and 2024, Lin et al. mapped generative AI’s rapidly expanding use across educational resources, methods, and assessment, while cataloging its recurrent failure modes (factually inaccurate or hallucinated content and the erosion of learners’ critical thinking through overreliance)3. Earlier reviews reached similar conclusions: Preiksaitis and Rose found the literature to be largely speculative and in need of rigorous empirical study5, and Abd-alrazaq et al. cataloged the opportunities and open challenges of using LLMs in this setting, which include the promise of LLM-assisted curriculum development, personalized learning materials, and student assessment, weighed against risks of algorithmic bias, overreliance, plagiarism, misinformation, inequity, privacy, and copyright14.

Building upon this prior literature, we separate the risks of using generative AI to create interactive educational interfaces for teaching AI in HPE into 3 categories: (1) content correctness risks, (2) interface risks relating to cognitive load, and (3) burden on educators. We discuss each of these risks specifically within the context of using generative AI to create interactive educational interfaces to teach AI literacy in HPE.

Content correctness risks

Generative AI can often “hallucinate” (i.e., generate incorrect content) when generating interactive content, and this phenomenon has recently been reported in AI-generated medical education content15. During the development of the example websites provided in the Supplementary Material, we encountered several hallucinations both in terms of the data points used to power the examples as well as the educational concepts themselves (e.g., incorrect formulas). These hallucinations required several iterations of asking the interface to correct prior errors. For example, in Fig. 3, we provide a real example generated by ChatGPT 5.2 when prompted to create an interactive decision tree visualization by an undergraduate student. When prompted to illustrate a decision tree for heart disease prediction, all bottom nodes of the tree except for one pointed to “no heart disease”. This is not how decision trees work; in reality, all penultimate nodes should point to either “heart disease” or “no heart disease”. This simple example demonstrates that, at least at this juncture, learners should not create these educational experiences by themselves and domain experts should thoroughly review and stress-test all educational material.

B Similarly, the decision boundary did not appear to make a difference in the background shading on either side of the line, which can confuse learners. A correct interface would shade the background blue on one side of the line and red on the other side of the line.

While this issue could be corrected through additional prompting, doing so reliably requires domain expertise to notice the error in the first place. Several failures, including incorrect simulation logic, plausible-looking but wrong data points, and misleading visualizations, are subtle enough that a non-expert educator or unsupervised learner would be unlikely to detect them.

Because of the content correctness risk, we heavily advocate for deploying these interactive educational experiences only within classroom settings within an active learning environment. Contextualization by educators is essential. Interactive demos should be accompanied by instructor-facilitated discussion of clinical and technical relevance. For example, after experimenting with the sensitivity-specificity tool, an instructor might present a real-world case of adjusting an alert system threshold in a hospital and discuss the outcomes. Similarly, the fairness demo could segue into a conversation about bias observed in a particular clinical algorithm and how a health system addressed it.

Interface and cognitive load risks

Beyond the correctness of a concept itself, the interface through which a tool is delivered can either enhance or impede learning, regardless of the underlying educational content. When prototyping educational experiences, we frequently encountered a failure mode of excess, in which the interface presented far more than the learning objective requires. Without explicit specification for a minimal interface, the AI-generated interfaces usually defaulted to visual clutter. An example of this is shown in Fig. 4. This is a direct liability under cognitive load theory16,17, which distinguishes three demands on a learner’s limited working memory: (1) the intrinsic load imposed by the difficulty of the material itself, (2) the extraneous load imposed by how that material is presented, and (3) the germane load devoted to the productive work of building and consolidating mental models. The failure mode of excessive visual clutter and unnecessary generated information that generative AI is prone to risks exacerbating extraneous load, which is unproductive and should be reduced as far as possible. Intrinsic load, by contrast, is to be calibrated by the educator rather than eliminated. Germane load is actively desirable, and a central reason to strip out the extraneous load that generative AI naturally adds is to free working memory that can then be redirected toward this productive processing.

Example of extraneous load created by generative AI-based interface development.

This interface, designed to teach the concept of algorithmic fairness metrics, presents lots of visual clutter and extraneous information that is not relevant to the task at hand, making it difficult for learners to focus on the core concept.

Burden on educators

A further limitation is the burden that the creation of interactive educational experiences can place on educators. Every failure mode described above must be detected and corrected by a knowledgeable human before a tool is fit for the classroom. The effort is also recurring: it must be repeated for each module, grow with the size of a curriculum, and, as our own experience attests, often spans several rounds of prompting and review before an artifact is simultaneously correct, usable, and pedagogically focused.

While generative AI technology lowers the access barrier to creating educational technologies, what it does not lower and may even raise is the demand for expert judgment to specify a tool precisely, verify its correctness, and refine it iteratively. Thus, we emphasize that our approach, when deployed safely, will not necessarily reduce overall educator burden but rather redistribute it towards creating tools that can facilitate experiential learning in the classroom.

Design principles for generating interactive educational experiences with AI

We created several samples of AI-generated educational web interfaces. A description of all interfaces, the AI prompt used to create them, their alignment with AAMC competencies, and a fully functional demo of each interface is provided in the Supplementary Materials. Based on the experience creating these interfaces and the first author’s piloting of the use of these tools across some of his lectures in HPE settings, we offer a list of practical design guidelines for educators who seek to develop active learning experiences using generative AI, ordered chronologically in the curriculum development process. Importantly, the design principles below are experience-informed rather than empirically validated:

Specificity is crucial when prompt engineering. Just like any other prompt engineering exercise, providing exact requirements is essential. State the learning objective, the exact interaction flow, hard constraints, and if needed, what not to include.

Explicitly prompt the AI to generate a minimalistic user interface. Oftentimes, various generative AI tools would default to creating overly crowded user interfaces that, while comprehensive and technically functional, were not amenable to learning due to their complexity, as corroborated by cognitive load theory. Using qualifiers such as “simple and elegant website (HTML file only)”, oftentimes repeatedly throughout the prompt, was in our experience necessary to limit the generation of components that were not prompted for or overly cluttered.

Create “unit tests”. A common practice in software engineering is to create “unit tests”, or small, predefined checks that verify whether specific components of a system behave as expected under known conditions. We recommend adopting this practice when generating interactive educational tools. Examples of salient unit tests may include: manually verifying that performance metrics change correctly when thresholds are adjusted, that fairness calculations produce expected values in edge cases, or that logical branches in visualizations correspond to the intended model behavior before deploying the tool in a classroom setting.

Always verify the emitted educational concepts. Generated interfaces can hallucinate educational concepts, for example by generating slightly incorrect facts and formulas. A common approach for ensuring that LLM output is constrained to a certain knowledge base is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), a technique that enables a set of documents to be referenced or “retrieved” by the LLM as it generates its outputs. However, we caution educators that even if RAG is used, the generative AI tool may still hallucinate. This aligns with broader digital pedagogy scholarship, which positions generative AI not simply as a content generator but as a collaborator in learning design, with educators retaining responsibility for aligning tools with learning objectives, assessment, and institutional values18.

We reiterate that these are experience-informed design principles rather than empirical findings. They reflect the qualitative experience of the authors in building these interfaces and of the first author in using them informally in the classroom and in supervising students who generated their own interfaces. Establishing whether they improve learning outcomes will require prospective evaluation, which we hope to motivate others to complete.
