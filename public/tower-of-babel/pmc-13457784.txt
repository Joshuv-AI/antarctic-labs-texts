Prediction model for hypertrophied liver volume followed by hemihepatectomy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jiwon Yu, Sunghae Park, Gyu-Seong Choi, Jongman Kim, Namkee Oh, Bogeun Kim, Jinsoo Rhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to develop a statistical model for predicting hypertrophied future liver remnant volume (HFLRV) in patients with right hemihepatectomy (RHH).MethodsPatients who underwent RHH at Samsung Medical Center between January 2022 and March 2024 were included in this study. Liver volume was calculated from abdomen CT by using a deep learning-based volumetric tool.ResultsA total of 98 patients were included. There were 58 and 40 patients whose liver regeneration index was less than 0.8 and 0.8 or more, respectively. The prediction model, which focused on identifying variables associated with HFLRV, included age at diagnosis (P = 0.14), sex (P = 0.01), both preoperative hemi-liver volumes (P < 0.001), and international normalized ratio (P < 0.05). This model demonstrated adjusted R2 of 0.60, correlation coefficient (r) of 0.78, and P-value of <0.001.ConclusionThis study developed a linear prediction model for HFLRV and determined which factors can be used to predict HFLRV before RHH.

INTRODUCTION

Major liver resections are performed with an increasing number for malignant liver diseases such as hepatocellular carcinoma as well as for benign liver diseases [123]. In cases of liver malignancies, surgical resection is the first choice of treatment due to the remarkable improvement of long-term survival of patients [4567]. Due to advances in diagnostic imaging and surgical techniques, liver resections can now be safely performed with a low postoperative mortality in high-volume centers [78910]. Studies have consistently shown that for successful surgical resections, a sufficient future liver remnant volume (FLRV), which is defined as a percentage of remaining functional liver volume compared with preoperative functional liver volume, is the most important predictor of perioperative mortality [6789111213141516].

While previous studies had focused on the factors associated with the recovery of liver volume in healthy living liver donors after hepatectomy [17], there remains limited understanding of liver regeneration in patients undergoing hepatectomy for various pathological conditions. Unlike living donor hepatectomy, which involves a relatively homogeneous population, hepatectomy is often performed in patients with heterogeneous liver diseases, such as liver cirrhosis with chronic hepatitis B or C, benign hepatic tumors, and hepatocellular carcinoma. These underlying conditions may significantly affect the liver’s regenerative capacity after major liver resection.

Therefore, this study aims to identify key clinical factors that can predict hypertrophied FLRV (HFLRV) prior to major liver resection by analyzing a diverse patient cohort. In particular, we specifically focus on patients undergoing right hemihepatectomy (RHH), as this procedure involves the resection of a larger portion of the liver compared to left-sided resections, resulting in a smaller HFLRV and a higher risk of post-hepatectomy liver failure (PHLF) [18], a significant cause of morbidity and mortality after major liver resections [71619].

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center (No. SMC 2024-11-138-002) and was granted a waiver of the need for informed consent since the study was designed as a retrospective study.

Definition of liver regeneration index

The extent of liver regeneration was quantified by calculating the growth rate of the remnant liver after hepatectomy, defined as the ratio of HFLRV 6 months after RHH to preoperative total liver volume. This value was termed the liver regeneration index (LRI) and calculated using the following formula: Liver regeneration index(LRI)=Hypertrophied future liver remnant volume(HFLRV)Preoperative total liver volume

The mean LRI of the entire study population was 0.80 ± 0.15, and based on this average, a cutoff value of 0.8 was used to classify patients into 2 subgroups for further analysis (Table 1).

Study population

A total of 100 patients who underwent RHH at Samsung Medical Center between January 2022 and March 2024 were included in this study. Patients who experienced multiple recurrent hepatocellular carcinomas after liver resection or had insufficient postoperative clinical data were excluded. Based on previous findings that the liver typically regenerates to more than 75% of its original volume after RHH [2021], patients were divided into 2 subgroups using a LRI threshold of 0.8. Patients with a regeneration index of less than 0.8 were classified into subgroup 1, while those with a regeneration index of 0.8 or higher were classified into subgroup 2.

Volumetric assessment

HFLRV and preoperative liver volume were evaluated using automated segmentation system developed by the authors and published in previous studies [2223] (Fig. 1). LIVERAIZ 3D Maker (LIVERAIZ Inc., Seoul, Korea) utilizes a nnU-Net–based deep learning model trained on liver MRI and CT images, specifically optimized for hepatic segmentation and volumetric analysis, enabling manual editing after auto-segmentation. The model demonstrated high performance with strong dice similarity coefficient and R2 values, and was validated by experts, indicating its potential as a reliable alternative to manual volumetric assessment [2223].

Data collection

Demographic data including age at diagnosis, sex, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI) were collected. Preoperative clinical lab data such as serum albumin, total bilirubin, international normalized ratio (INR), platelet count, and the presence of liver cirrhosis were also reviewed. Preoperative liver volume and HFLRV were measured using MRI and CT. In particular, postoperative images collected approximately 6 months after hepatectomy were analyzed, based on previous studies which reported that liver regeneration reaches its maximum size between 6 months and 1 year after hepatectomy [2021].

Data analysis

Both univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses were performed to build a model predicting the HFLRV following RHH. Univariate linear regression was performed for each clinical variable independently. Subsequently, all variables were included in multivariate regression analysis using backward selection as a variable selection method, and internal validation was conducted by bootstrapping. The explanatory power and statistical significance of the identified model were assessed by adjusted R2 and P-value. The statistical significance of the regression coefficient for each variable was also assessed by P-value.

Baseline characteristics were summarized as mean and standard deviation for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentage for categorical variables. Comparisons between the 2 subgroups were performed using either the 2 sample t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables to identify the interaction effect between those groups on each clinical factor.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software ver. 3.6.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

RESULTS

The flow diagram of the study population is shown in Fig. 2. Among 100 patients who underwent RHH during the study period, 2 patients were excluded from the analysis: one due to multiple recurrent hepatocellular carcinomas following RHH, and the other due to the absence of follow-up INR data. As a result, 98 patients were included in the final analysis. Patients were categorized into 2 subgroups according to their LRI, using 0.8 as the cutoff value. Among the 98 patients included in the final analysis, 58 patients had an LRI of less than 0.8 and were assigned to subgroup 1, while the remaining 40 patients with a LRI of 0.8 or higher were assigned to subgroup 2.

An overview of liver volumetric parameters is presented in Table 1. Prior to surgery, the mean volume of the left hemi-liver was 509.1 ± 159.9 mL, and the mean volume of the right hemi-liver was 768.7 ± 214.7 mL. The mean HFLRV after RHH, assessed on postoperative imaging at 6 months after RHH, was 1,003.1 ± 225.5 mL. The mean LRI (defined as the ratio of HFLRV to preoperative total liver volume) was 0.80 ± 0.15.

Baseline characteristics of study population and comparing patients from the 2 subgroups

Table 2 shows the baseline characteristics of the study population. There were no statistically significant differences between the 2 groups in terms of age, sex, height, weight, or BMI. Preoperative laboratory values, including serum albumin, total bilirubin, and platelet count also showed no significant differences. However, mean INR, prothrombin time was significantly lower in subgroup 2 while the absolute difference was small (1.01 ± 0.09 vs. 0.98 ± 0.06, P = 0.03). Furthermore, the proportion of patients with INR lower than 0.98 preoperatively was significantly lower in subgroup 1 (n = 13, 22.4%) compared to subgroup 2 (n = 20, 50.0%, P = 0.005).

The presence of liver cirrhosis was comparable between the 2 groups (20.7% vs. 22.5%, P = 0.83). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in preoperative liver volume measurements of the left lobe (median [interquartile range]: 493.1 [408.8–597.0] mL vs. 483.9 [391.5–581.3] mL, P = 0.64) or the right lobe (774.5 [650.4–909.1] mL vs. 733.0 [643.8–888.7] mL, P = 0.22). However, the mean ratio of HFLRV to preoperative left hemi-liver volume showed significant differences between 2 groups (91% vs. 137%, P < 0.001).

Prediction model for liver regeneration volume after right hemihepatectomy

Univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses were used to identify clinical factors associated with HFLRV following RHH (Table 3). In the univariate linear regression analyses, variables showed statistically significant associations with HFLRV, including patient’s age at diagnosis (P < 0.05), sex (P < 0.001), height (P < 0.001), weight (P < 0.001), BMI (P < 0.001), preoperative right hemi-liver volume (P < 0.001), preoperative left hemi-liver volume (P < 0.001), the volume of preoperative left medial section (P < 0.001), the volume of preoperative left lateral section (P = 0.001), and the volume of preoperative spigelian (P < 0.001).

Considering collinearity between variables, only selected variables including age, sex, height, weight, BMI, albumin, total bilirubin, INR, platelet, cirrhosis, preoperative left and right hemi-liver volumes, preoperative left medial section, and preoperative left lateral section were used when building the model. According to the multivariate linear regression analysis, the model included the following variables: patient’s age at diagnosis (P = 0.14), sex (P = 0.11), preoperative left hemi-liver volume (P < 0.001), preoperative right hemi-liver volume (P < 0.001), and INR (P < 0.05). Among these, INR and both preoperative hemi-liver volumes remained statistically significant predictors. The final formula of prediction model for predicting HFLRV after RHH was as follows:

Predicted HFLRV after RHH = −2.2 × age at diagnosis −65.8 × sex + 0.9 × preoperative left hemi-liver volume + 0.5 × preoperative right hemi-liver volume −601.3 × INR + 932.1

The predictive performance of the model was visualized in a scatter plot comparing predicted and actual HFLRV after RHH (Fig. 3). The model demonstrated good explanatory power with adjusted R2 of 0.60, correlation coefficient (r) of 0.78, and P-value of <0.001.

DISCUSSION

As PHLF remains one of the main causes of morbidity and mortality after hepatectomy [71619], predicting regenerated liver volume, especially FLRV, is important to assess surgical feasibility and patient’s safety [7891112]. Schindl et al. [24] reported that PHLF incidence increased from 1% with resection of 1-2 segments to about 30% after removal of 5 or more segments. Other studies reported that 80% of PHLF related deaths occur after resection of more than 50% liver volume [13141516]. However, most previous studies on liver regeneration mainly focused on recovery of liver volume after hepatectomy for healthy living liver donors [172526].

Therefore, this study was designed to predict the regenerated liver volume after major liver resection, such as RHH, in a heterogeneous patient population with various liver diseases. Using clinical and anatomical data, univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses were conducted to construct a prediction model. According to univariate analysis, age at diagnosis, sex, and preoperative right hemi-liver volume were significantly associated with HFLRV after RHH. Among these variables, according to the prediction model preoperative right and left hemi-liver volume, and INR were identified as statistically significant regression predictors of HFLRV while other variables such as age at diagnosis, and sex were not. The final prediction model demonstrated a correlation coefficient of 0.78 and adjusted R2 of 0.60, indicating moderate-to-strong predictive power.

The interesting finding of this study is the impact of INR level on liver regeneration. When the patients were divided into 2 groups with a cutoff of 0.80 LRI, all other variables except INR were similar between the 2 groups. This can be interpreted that INR can be a surrogate of the liver’s regenerative capacity, which is well-known, but has again been emphasized by this study. The factors included in the multivariate linear regression model also show the importance of INR. Age, sex, and the volumes of right and left hemi-liver are factors that are less related to the functional reserve of the liver. Of course, there can be an argument that when the liver becomes fibrotic and cirrhotic, the left hemi-liver tends to get bigger while the right hemi-liver volume decreases. However, these findings are rather an exceptional finding which cannot be applied in a general fashion. INR, on the other hand, has traditionally been considered as the hallmark of liver’s ability to produce biosynthetic molecules. While the patient group included in the study had a very low level of INR, the result shows that even small differences in preoperative INR can affect the liver’s regeneration. The β coefficient of INR was −601.3 (95% confidence interval, −1,018.8 to −183.8; P < 0.05) in the final model, which means that an INR difference of 0.1 can result in 60 cm3 difference in liver volume.

Another important point the authors wanted to emphasize in this study was that the study used a deep learning model as an automatic volumetric assessment tool. According to our previous published study, volumetric assessment of 3-dimensional liver volume using a well-made deep learning model shows higher accuracy and lower variation compared to conventional methods measuring liver volume with 2-dimensional images manually based on conventional volumetry software [27]. These automated algorithms can be extended as a tool that can be usable for general medical staff to automatically calculate the HFLRV before RHH if adequate medical DICOM images are provided.

By using the final prediction model, clinicians can easily calculate and predict the HFLRV before conducting RHH with information of patient’s age, sex, INR, and preoperative right and left hemi-liver volume. Furthermore, preoperative right and left hemi-liver volume can be automatically calculated by a deep learning-based liver volumetry tool, which can reduce the workload of clinicians and increase the accuracy of the measurement.

However, this study has several limitations. First, since this is a retrospective study from a single center, the study population was too small, and has not yet undergone external validation, which limits its generalizability. In addition, as the predictive power was improved with the limited number of study population, further external validation is required to prove its generalizability. Second, the study population was inevitably heterogeneous as this study dealt with liver diseases with various etiologies. In this context, as the HFLRV was measured 6 months after an operation, during this period, each patient would undergo different therapeutic courses, which increases the heterogeneity of the study population. Even though this heterogeneous patient cohort could increase its generalizability to real-world clinical practice, an externally validated study with larger, multicenter cohorts, including patients with known PHLF would be still needed.

This study developed a linear prediction model for hypertrophied FLRV before performing RHH and determined clinical factors that can be used to predict the HFLRV, emphasizing the importance of INR among other variables. The variables in the final prediction model are routinely obtained, so that if a deep learning-based automatic liver volume calculator is introduced in a clinical setting, these prediction models can provide practical advantages in terms of increasing accuracy and reducing workload as an automatic volumetric tool automates and accelerates the work process. Further studies to improve and optimize this prediction model will permit major liver resections to be done with decreased morbidity and mortality and increased safety.
