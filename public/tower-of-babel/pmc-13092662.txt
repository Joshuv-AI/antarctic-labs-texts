Ct-derived basal septal thickness predicts Post-TAVR pacemaker implantation in patients with preexisting right bundle branch block
Authors: Lieyou Li, Feng Hu, Linyan Chen, Junhua Lin, Lin Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Transcatheter aortic valve replacement, Permanent pacemaker, Interventricular septal thickness, Right bundle branch block

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has become a minimally invasive interventional procedure mainly used to treat aortic valve stenosis[1], [2]. Despite procedural advances, post-TAVR conduction disturbances remain frequent, often necessitating permanent pacemaker implantation (PPI). Documented rates of PPI following the procedure span 3.4% to 25.9%, with this intervention linked to elevated morbidity and reduced long-term survival[3], [4], [5]. The known risk factors of conduction disturbances include pre-existing right bundle branch block (RBBB), shorter membranous septum length, deeper valve implantation, and the use of self-expanding valves[6], [7], [8], [9], [10].

The anatomical substrate for these disturbances lies in the close proximity between the atrioventricular conduction axis and the aortic annulus—the TAVR prosthesis landing zone[8], [11]. This conduction axis traverses the muscular part at the base of the interventricular septum (IVS). The mechanical compression of such region during the valve expansion process is the key mechanism of injury. Recent study indicated that greater muscular IVS thickness may serve as a protective anatomical barrier, protecting the underlying left bundle branch (LBB) from compression and reducing the incidence of new-onset left bundle branch block (LBBB)[12]. From a physiological perspective, complete atrioventricular block (AVB) typically occurs only if the injury affects the His bundle proximal to its bifurcation or concurrently damages both left and right bundle branches. If the compression is limited to the left bundle branch, and the right bundle branch still functions normally, the right bundle branch can maintain atrioventricular conduction, thereby preventing complete AVB from occurring.

In patients with pre-existing RBBB, the cardiac conduction system depends entirely on left bundle branch. This functional anatomy renders them particularly susceptible to complete AVB from any new left bundle branch injury incurred during TAVR. We therefore hypothesize that a thicker basal muscular IVS may alleviate mechanical compression on left bundle branch, thereby reducing the risk of new left bundle branch injury and subsequent complete AVB, and potentially avoiding the use of PPI. To test this, the current study aimed to evaluate whether pre-procedural CT-derived basal IVS thickness predicts the risk of PPI after TAVR specifically in patients with pre-existing RBBB.

Methods

Patient selection

Between 2018 and 2025, a total of 426 patients with severe AS who underwent pre-TAVR computed tomography (CT) evaluation were initially considered. The detailed exclusion criteria and patient screening process are outlined in Fig. 1. This study focused exclusively on patients with preexisting RBBB, as they rely entirely on the left bundle branch for atrioventricular conduction, making them uniquely vulnerable to complete heart block following left bundle branch injury. Sixty-six patients were included in the final analysis. Ethical approval for the study protocol was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital (Approval No.2025KY957), with the research conforming to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to being enrolled in the study.

Flowchart of Patient Selection and Group Stratification.

CT Image acquisition

Image datasets were collected using a dual-source computed tomography instrument (SOMATOM Force, Siemens Healthineers, Munich, Germany). All participants received intravenous administration of a nonionic contrast medium (Iomeprol, Iomeron® 400, 400 mg iodine/mL, Bracco Imaging SpA, Milan, Italy) via the antecubital vein at a flow rate of 4–5 mL/s, followed by a 40-mL saline chase using a dual-barrel power injector. The total volume of contrast medium was individualized based on each patient's body weight and estimated scan duration. Tube voltage and current were dynamically regulated by the automatic exposure control system according to the patient's body habitus to optimize image quality while minimizing radiation dose. Cardiac CT angiography (CCTA) images were obtained with a prospective electrocardiogram (ECG)-triggered acquisition protocol, with the scanning window spanning 35%–75% of the R to R interval. For the third-generation dual-source CT system, CCTA images were reconstructed with the following parameters: collimation = 96 × 0.6 mm, slice thickness = 0.75 mm, slice interval = 0.5 mm, and gantry rotation time = 250 ms. The reference tube current and voltage were set to 320 mAs and 100 kVp, respectively.

CT image analysis

The enhanced CT datasets were transferred to a dedicated CT workstation (CardioVerse TAVR Aortic Valve Intelligent Imaging Analysis System) for multiplanar reconstruction. Image reconstruction was performed at 10% intervals across the RR-cycle, with the peak systolic phase selected for subsequent analysis.

Membranous septum (MS) length was measured on standard non-reformatted coronal views during systole, according to the protocol of Hamdan et al[8]. The measurement was defined as the linear distance from the basal aortic annulus (right/non-coronary cusp junction) to the anatomic transition zone where the MS meets the muscular IVS. The basal interventricular septum was defined as the muscular portion of the septum immediately below the membranous septum, and its thickness was measured perpendicular to the septum at 2, 4, 6, and 8 mm below the inferior edge of the membranous septum on coronal views (Fig. 2). Aortic annular dimensions, left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) parameters, and calcium scores were also assessed. The LVOT area was measured at 5 mm below the aortic annulus (AA), and the LVOT area/AA area ratio was calculated and stratified using a cutoff of 1.0. Annular eccentricity and LVOT eccentricity were defined as (1 − [minimum diameter / maximum diameter]) × 100. Additional computed parameters included the ratio of prosthesis size to LVOT diameter and the oversizing index (OI). For self-expandable prostheses, OI was calculated as: OI = ([nominal valve perimeter-measured AA perimeter]/nominal valve perimeter) × 100. All measurements were performed independently by two experienced observers blinded to clinical outcomes. Inter- and intra-observer reproducibility were evaluated using Bland-Altman analysis and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs).

TAVR procedure and Post-Procedural management

All TAVR procedures were performed under general anesthesia or conscious sedation. The prosthetic valve type and size were selected based on pre-procedural measurements of the aortic annulus and left ventricular outflow tract, following the manufacturer's recommendations. All TAVR procedures were performed using the cusp-overlap technique to achieve a higher implant position. The target implantation depth was aimed to be ≤ 3 mm below the non-coronary cusp to minimize conduction disturbances. Implantation depth was assessed fluoroscopically using the two-cusp (cusp-overlap) view with isolation of the non-coronary cusp, which has been shown to minimize conduction disturbances. This projection was pre-validated by CT to ensure optimal alignment for assessment of the membranous septum and conduction system. Specifically, implantation depth was quantified as the mean distance between the native aortic annulus plane (encompassing both the non-coronary/right coronary cusp aspect and the left coronary cusp aspect) and the nearest proximal margin of the implanted prosthetic valve.

Continuous telemetry monitoring was maintained for at least 72 h post-TAVR. Twelve-lead ECGs were obtained daily and in case of symptoms. Indications for PPI followed current guideline recommendations, with all decisions reviewed by a heart team including electrophysiologists.

Study Endpoint

The indication for pacemaker implantation was assessed in consultation with the electrophysiology team, guided by recommendations from the Journal of the American College of Cardiology Scientific Expert Panel and ESC Guidelines[5], [13]. Permanent pacing was performed in patients who developed new high-grade atrioventricular block or new conduction disturbances in the setting of preexisting right bundle branch block, according to standard indications. All patients underwent prolonged in-hospital telemetry monitoring to assess dynamic rhythm changes, and each case was reviewed in a multidisciplinary team meeting. All patients were followed up at 30 days with electrocardiogram and clinical assessment. No delayed PPI beyond discharge was recorded in this cohort.

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables, data are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range. Differences between groups were analyzed using the Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages, with group comparisons performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of PPI. Variables with P < 0.20 in univariate analysis or of clinical relevance were included in the multivariate model. Collinearity among predictors was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF). All variables included in the multivariable model had VIF < 2.5, indicating no significant multicollinearity. The optimal cutoff for IVS thickness was determined using Youden's index. Predictive performance was evaluated using ROC curves and area under the curve (AUC). Net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) was calculated to assess the incremental value of IVS thickness. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided P < 0.05. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 23) and Stata MP 18.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

66 Consecutive patients with pre-existing right bundle branch block who underwent TAVR were included. The mean age was 73.17 ± 7.28 years, and 71.2% were male. 28 patients (42.4%) required permanent pacemaker implantation (PPI) within one month post-procedure

Baseline demographic characteristics, cardiovascular risk factors, surgical risk scores (STS score), past medical history, baseline electrocardiogram, and preoperative echocardiographic parameters were largely comparable between patients with and without subsequent PPI (Table 1). However, patients who required PPI had a significantly lower rate of smoking history (10.71% vs. 39.47%, P = 0.010) and lower levels of pro-B-type natriuretic peptide [1550.00 (460.75, 2682.50) pg/mL vs. 3406.07 (824.78, 8308.77) pg/mL, P = 0.006].

Procedural characteristics

All procedures were performed using the cusp-overlap technique, which has been associated with lower pacemaker rates. Despite this uniform approach, PPI was still required in 42.4% of patients, underscoring the dominant role of anatomical factors in this high-risk cohort. All patients received self-expanding valves. Valve size selection was based on manufacturer recommendations incorporating aortic annulus and LVOT measurements, with a median size of 26.0 mm (IQR 24.0, 27.0). Procedural aspects, including predilatation, post-dilatation, and anesthesia approach, were similar between groups. Notably, the PPI group exhibited a deeper valve implantation depth (5.85 ± 1.86 mm vs. 4.74 ± 2.58 mm, P = 0.046) and a significantly lower membranous septum length to implantation depth difference (MSL-ID) (−1.73 ± 2.02 mm vs. 1.25 ± 2.71 mm, P < 0.001) (Table 1).

CT-derived anatomical measurements

Computed tomography analysis revealed that the PPI group had a significantly shorter membranous septum length (MSL) (4.15 ± 1.00 mm vs. 4.81 ± 1.17 mm, P = 0.019). Crucially, the basal muscular interventricular septum was significantly thinner in the PPI group at all four measured levels below the MS (4.13 ± 0.17 mm vs. 4.42 ± 0.26 mm, 5.99 ± 0.21 mm vs. 6.44 ± 0.59 mm, 6.90 ± 0.55 mm vs. 7.28 ± 0.66 mm, and 9.33 ± 0.50 mm vs. 9.86 ± 0.76 mm for 2, 4, 6 and 8 mm below MS, respectively; all P < 0.05) (Table 2, Fig. 3). No significant differences were observed in aortic annulus dimensions, LVOT parameters, eccentricity indices, or aortic valve calcification scores between groups.

Predictors of permanent pacemaker implantation

The indication for PPI in all 28 cases was new-onset complete atrioventricular block, occurring at a mean of 3.88 ± 1.28 days post-TAVR.

Univariate logistic regression identified IVS thickness at every measured level as a significant predictor of PPI (Table 3). A 1-mm increase in IVS thickness at 2 mm below the MS was associated with a markedly reduced risk of PPI (OR 0.003, 95% CI: 0.001–0.059, P < 0.01). Significant inverse associations were consistently observed at 4 mm (OR 0.10, 95% CI: 0.02–0.43, P < 0.01), 6 mm (OR 0.36, 95% CI: 0.15–0.85, P = 0.02), and 8 mm (OR 0.28, 95% CI: 0.12–0.67, P < 0.01) below the MS.

For the primary outcome prediction, the optimal cutoff value for IVS thickness at 2 mm below the MS was 4.35 mm (Youden's index = 0.508), yielding an OR of 0.056 (95% CI: 0.012–0.27, P < 0.001) and an AUC of 0.818. The performance of this cutoff was characterized by a sensitivity of 92.9%, a specificity of 42.1%, a positive predictive value of 8.3%, and a negative predictive value of 61.9%.

Multivariable analysis, adjusting for age, implantation depth, and MSL, confirmed that a thinner IVS (dichotomized at < 4.35 mm at 2 mm below the MS) (adjusted OR 0.05, 95% CI: 0.01–0.32, P = 0.002) and a lower MSL-ID (adjusted OR 0.57, 95% CI: 0.40–0.81, P = 0.002) were independent predictors of PPI (Table 4). Furthermore, a significant interaction was observed between IVS thickness and MSL-ID (interaction OR 0.881, 95% CI 0.815–0.952, P < 0.001), indicating that the protective effect of greater IVS thickness diminishes with greater implantation depth. The predictive model incorporating IVS thickness demonstrated excellent discrimination (AUC 0.91, 95% CI: 0.84–0.98), superior to the model without it (AUC 0.82, 95% CI: 0.71–0.92; Fig. 4), with a NRI of 1.083 (95% CI: 0.690–1.476, P < 0.001) and an IDI of 0.208 (95% CI: 0.121–0.295, P < 0.001).

Reproducibility of IVS measurements

Inter-observer and intra-observer variability for muscular interventricular septal thickness measurements were as follows: At 2 mm below the membranous septum, the values were 0.010 ± 0.061 (−0.11 to 0.130) and −0.027 ± 0.064 (−0.153 to 0.098), respectively; at 4 mm below, −0.019 ± 0.047 (−0.112 to 0.074) and 0.0163 ± 0.055 (−0.092 to 0.125), respectively; at 6 mm below, 0.013 ± 0.050 (−0.086 to 0.111) and −0.0178 ± 0.028 (−0.072 to 0.037), respectively; and at 8 mm below, 0.025 ± 0.063 (−0.098 to 0.149) and 0.020 ± 0.066 (−0.109 to 0.150), respectively.

Inter-observer and intra-observer intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were as follows: At 2 mm below the membranous septum, 0.98 and 0.97, respectively; at 4 mm below, 0.98 and 0.96, respectively; at 6 mm below, 0.99 and 0.99, respectively; and at 8 mm below, 0.98 and 0.97, respectively. ICCs for IVS measurements were excellent (>0.90).

Clinical outcomes at 1-Year follow-up

At 30-day follow-up, no deaths or strokes occurred. Two patients in the PPI group experienced transient heart failure symptoms, which resolved with medical therapy. At 1-year follow-up, no significant differences were observed between patients with and without PPI in terms of clinical outcomes, including all-cause mortality, cardiovascular death, heart failure rehospitalization, stroke, major bleeding, or myocardial infarction (all p ≥ 0.05, shown in Table S1 in supplementary material).

Discussion

This study identifies preprocedural CT-derived basal IVS thickness as a independent predictor of PPI following TAVR in patients with preexisting RBBB. Our principal finding is that patients requiring PPI exhibited significantly thinner IVS across all measured levels below the MS. After adjusting for established risk factors—including implantation depth and MSL-IVS thickness remained a robust predictor. Incorporating this parameter into a multivariate model substantially improved discrimination for PPI risk(AUC = 0.91), underscoring its incremental clinical utility.

Our findings strongly support the hypothesis that the basal muscular portion of the IVS serves as a protective anatomical cushion for the underlying cardiac conduction system. The atrioventricular conduction axis courses through the superior aspect of the IVS, directly beneath the membranous septum. A thicker septal myocardium likely attenuates the radial mechanical forces transmitted from the expanding TAVR prosthesis, thereby mitigating direct trauma or ischemic insult to the vulnerable conduction fibers. This concept is grounded in known anatomical variability. Prior anatomical studies, such as those by Kawashima and Sasaki, have categorized the course of the atrioventricular bundle, with a “naked” or subendocardial subtype (lacking overlying myocardium) present in a significant minority of individuals12. It is plausible that patients with a thinner basal IVS are more likely to possess such an exposed conduction pathway, rendering it exceptionally susceptible to compression during valve deployment. This anatomical predisposition, coupled with the functional vulnerability imposed by preexisting RBBB, creates a “perfect storm” for the development of complete heart block following even minor procedural injury to the left bundle branch. In addition, in patients with aortic stenosis, the etiology of septal hypertrophy extends beyond increased afterload and may include undiagnosed conditions such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or cardiac amyloidosis. In these pathological states, the myocardial composition of IVS differs—for example, featuring increased fibrosis or altered conduction tissue distribution. These differences may subsequently modify the response to the mechanical stress exerted by the valve prosthesis. However, the precise impact of these distinct etiologies and pathological substrates on post-TAVR outcomes remains unclear due to a lack of targeted evidence.

Our results directly corroborate and importantly extend the recent work by Schamroth Pravda et al., which first identified IVS thickness as a predictor of conduction disturbances in a broad TAVR cohort[14]. While that study established a cutoff of < 4 mm at 2 mm below the MS as a significant risk factor, our investigation refines this metric for the specific high-risk subgroup of patients with preexisting RBBB, deriving an optimal cutoff of 4.35 mm with excellent predictive performance (AUC 0.91). In our analysis, although the specificity of the 4.35 mm cutoff is modest (42.1%), its high sensitivity (92.9%) supports its use as a screening tool to identify patients at risk. In combination with other predictors (e.g., MSL-ID), it may guide monitoring intensity and implant strategy. This result underscores the condition-specific value of septal thickness measurement in procedural risk assessment.

Although some reports link basal or sigmoid septal morphology to higher pacemaker implantation rates, conclusions are inconsistent due to methodological limitations: lack of standardized measurement points, significant echocardiographic variability, undefined diagnostic thresholds, and limited sample sizes[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]. Therefore, interventricular septum (IVS) thickness should not be used alone to predict conduction disturbances. It must be integrated with established predictors—including implant depth, left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) encroachment by septal bulge, and septal-aortic angle—for comprehensive risk assessment. Current analysis reinforces the critical role of the interaction between native anatomy and procedural technique. The independent predictive value of both IVS thickness and the MSL-ID parameter underscores that PPI risk is not determined by a single factor but by a confluence of inherent anatomical susceptibility (short MS, thin IVS) and procedural characteristics (deep implantation). The significant interaction we observed between IVS thickness and MSL-ID quantitatively demonstrates that the protective effect of a thick septum can be overcome by excessively deep valve placement.

The integration of basal IVS thickness into pre-procedural risk assessment offers tangible clinical benefits. First, it provides an easily obtainable and highly reproducible measurement from CT scans already routinely performed for TAVR planning. Second, it enables improved risk stratification. Identifying patients with high-risk anatomy (e.g., IVS thickness < 4.35 mm combined with a short MSL) should prompt heightened vigilance and tailored management strategies. These may include deliberate attempts to achieve a higher implant position, cautious oversizing, consideration of balloon-expandable valves in select cases, and pre-emptive consultation with electrophysiology colleagues. Third, such high-risk patients may warrant prolonged post-procedural monitoring.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be considered. The retrospective, single-center nature and modest sample size may affect generalizability, necessitating future prospective, multicenter validation. Furthermore, without detailed imaging or histopathological data on the basal septal conduction axis, the underlying mechanism remains unverified. Moreover, as only self-expanding valves were used, the predictive role of IVS thickness for balloon-expandable valves is unclear and requires dedicated study. Finally, while measurement reproducibility was excellent, accurate assessment of IVS thickness requires careful attention to the imaging plane and cardiac phase, underscoring the need for standardized protocols to facilitate widespread clinical adoption. Regarding statistical considerations, the extremely low odds ratio (OR = 0.003) for IVS thickness as a continuous variable reflects its narrow measurement range and should be interpreted with caution. We therefore used a dichotomized cutoff (≥4.35 mm) in the final model to improve clinical interpretability and model stability. Furthermore, with only 28 events, the multivariable model may be susceptible to overfitting. Although we limited the number of predictors to maintain an events-per-variable ratio > 10, internal validation (e.g., bootstrap) was not performed and warrants investigation in future larger cohorts.

Conclusion

In patients with preexisting RBBB undergoing TAVR, preprocedural CT-derived basal IVS thickness is an independent predictor of the need for PPI. A thinner IVS likely reflects diminished anatomical protection for the conduction system, increasing its vulnerability to TAVR-induced injury.
