Characterization of John domains via weak tangents
Christina Karafyllia

Abstract
. We characterize simply connected John domains in the plane with the aid of weak tangents of the boundary. Specifically, we prove that a bounded simply connected domain D D is a John domain if and only if, for every weak tangent Y Y of ∂ D \partial D , every connected component of the complement of Y Y that “originates” from D D is a John domain, not necessarily with uniform constants. Our main theorem improves a result of Kinneberg [ 10 ] , who obtains a necessary condition for a John domain in terms of weak tangents but not a sufficient one. We also establish several properties of weak tangents of John domains.

1.
Introduction

The object of this paper is to characterize simply connected planar John domains by studying weak tangents of the boundary. Intuitively, this means that we zoom into the boundary of the domain at certain points, along with a sequence of scales tending to zero. More precisely, let
X,Y
be two closed sets in the complex plane
\C
. We say that
Y
is a
weak tangent
of
X
if there is a sequence of points
p_{n}\in X
and scales
\lambda_{n}>0
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, with
\lambda_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
such that if

X_{n}=\frac{X-p_{n}}{\lambda_{n}}

then the sequence of sets
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
Y
. We explain the precise notion of convergence in Section
2
. Note that one can also define weak tangents of arbitrary metric spaces but, for our purposes, we restrict to planar sets. Weak tangents play a significant role in the study of fractal metric spaces since they describe the infinitesimal structure of such spaces.

Often, one can extract information about the global geometry of a space by studying its infinitesimal structure. For example, weak tangents of the Sierpiński carpet have been studied by Bonk and Merenkov
[
3
]
in connection with the problem of finding all quasisymmetric automorphisms of the carpet. Moreover, weak tangents have been studied by Hakobyan and Li
[
8
]
in connection with the problem of quasisymmetrically embedding a metric space into Euclidean space.

We focus on the problem of characterizing a metric space by studying its weak tangents. A first motivating result is that a Jordan curve in the plane is a quasicircle if and only if its weak tangents are connected
[
10
, Theorem 5.3]
. For higher dimensional objects similar results are false in general. For instance, Wu
[
18
, Theorem 1.0.10]
shows that for each
n\geq 2
there is a metric space
X
homeomorphic to the
n
-sphere whose weak tangents are isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, but
X
is not a quasisphere (see also
[
12
, Corollary 4.5]
).

Bonk and Kleiner
[
2
]
provide a positive result for topological
n
-spheres
X
that arise as the boundary at infinity of a Gromov hyperbolic finitely generated group
G
. Specifically, a consequence of
[
2
, Lemma 5.2]
is that such a boundary
X
is a quasisphere if and only if every weak tangent of
X
is quasisymmetric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Such a characterization was also obtained by Wu
[
18
, Theorem 1.0.9]
for visual spheres of expanding Thurston maps (see also
[
12
, Theorem 1.5]
).

In
[
16
, Corollary 3.8]
, Väisälä studies uniform domains, a class of domains that contains quasicircles, and among other results he gives a characterization of uniform domains in terms of weak tangents. In this paper we study the weak tangents of a more general class of domains known as John domains. John domains were introduced by F. John
[
9
]
and studied by Martio–Sarvas
[
14
]
. Roughly speaking, a planar domain is a John domain if any two points of the domain can be connected by a curve that is not too close to the boundary. In other words, John domains are not allowed to have outward cusps, but they can have inward cusps.

John domains arise naturally in distortion problems of conformal and quasiconformal mappings, and in complex dynamics. For example, in
[
6
]
Carleson, Jones and Yoccoz give a classification of polynomials whose Fatou components are John domains. In
[
13
]
, Lin and Rhode characterize the laminations of the disk that arise from conformal mappings onto planar dendrites whose complement is a John domain.
Simply connected John domains can be considered as one-sided quasidisks. In fact, Nakki–Väisälä
[
15
, Theorem 9.3]
prove that a Jordan curve in the plane is a quasicircle if and only if both connected components of its complement are John domains.

By their original definition John domains are bounded. However, later, Nakki–Väisälä
[
15
]
extended the definition to allow for unbounded domains. In this paper we adopt their definition from
[
15
]
:

Definition 1.1
.

Let
D\subset\C
be a domain and let
A\geq 1
. We say that
D
is an
A
-John domain
if for every pair of distinct points
a,b\in D
there exists a curve
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to D
such that
\gamma(0)=a
,
\gamma(1)=b
and

\min\{\ell(\gamma|_{[0,t]}),\ell(\gamma|_{[t,1]})\}\leq A\dist(\gamma(t),\partial D),

for all
t\in[0,1]
. In this case
\gamma
is called an
A
-John curve
connecting
a
to
b
. We say that
D
is a John domain if it is an
A
-John domain for some
A\geq 1
.

Kinneberg
[
10
, Proposition 1.5]
proved that if
D
is a bounded simply connected John domain, then the weak tangents of
\partial D
have a uniformly bounded number of connected components. It is easy to see that the converse is not true. For example, one can construct a Jordan region
D
with an outward cusp (like a water droplet), which is therefore not a John domain, but which has the property that every weak tangent of
\partial D
has at most two connected components. Note, however, that the complement of this Jordan region is a John domain. One can easily generalize this construction and allow for both inward and outward cusps while keeping the property that the weak tangents have at most two connected components. Thus, there seems to be no characterization of John domains that takes into account only the geometry of weak tangents
Y
of
\partial D
, but one has to study as well the connected components of
\C\setminus Y
.

For this reason, given a domain
D\subset\C
and a weak tangent
Y
of
\partial D
, we wish to distinguish between connected components of
\C\setminus Y
that “originate” from
D
and connected components that do not have this property. If
Y
is a weak tangent of
\partial D
,
there is a sequence of points
p_{n}\in\partial D
and scales
\lambda_{n}>0
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, with
\lambda_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
such that

\left\{\frac{\partial D-p_{n}}{\lambda_{n}}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

converges to
Y
in the local Hausdorff sense, which we define in Section
2
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we set

D_{n}=\frac{D-p_{n}}{\lambda_{n}}.

We say that a connected component
U
of
\C\setminus Y
is a
D
-component
if there exists
z\in U
such that
z\in D_{n}
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Note that the weak tangent
Y
and the
D
-components of
\mathbb{C}\setminus Y
depend on the sequences
\lambda_{n}
and
p_{n}
. Our main result gives a necessary and sufficient condition for a bounded simply connected domain
D\subset\C
to be a John domain by studying the geometry of
D
-components.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
D\subset\C
be a bounded simply connected domain. The following are equivalent.

(1)

D
is a John domain.

(2)

There exists
A\geq 1
such that for every weak tangent
Y
of
\partial D
, every
D
-component of
\C\setminus Y
is an
A
-John domain.

(3)

For every weak tangent
Y
of
\partial D
, every
D
-component of
\C\setminus Y
is a John domain.

Remark 1.3
.

In (3) we do not require that
D
-components are John domains with a uniform constant. Moreover, if
D
is an
A
-John domain in (1) then we actually prove that (2) holds for the same constant
A
. However, the implication from (2) to (1) is not quantitative. For example, a domain
D
bounded by a smooth Jordan curve is always a John domain, but not necessarily with a small constant
A
, while the weak tangents of
\partial D
are actual tangent lines.

Remark 1.4
.

The theorem is not true for unbounded domains. Actually, the implication from (1) to (3) (or (2)) holds even if
D
is unbounded but the converse does not. For example, consider the domain

D=\C\setminus((-\infty,-1]\cup[1,\infty)),

which is not a John domain. Yet, every weak tangent
Y
of
\partial D
is either a line or a ray and the connected components of
\C\setminus Y
are John domains.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
is given in Section
3
and the most involved implication is that (3) implies (1). In Section
4
, we establish some properties of weak tangents of John domains.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
D\subset\C
be a bounded simply connected John domain and let
Y
be a weak tangent of
\partial D
. The following statements are true.

(1)

Every
D
-component of
\C\setminus Y
is unbounded and simply connected.

(2)

Every connected component of
Y
is unbounded.

(3)

Y
is equal to the union of the boundaries of
D
-components of
\C\setminus Y
.

(4)

The number of
D
-components of
\C\setminus Y
is bounded, quantitatively.

(5)

The number of connected components of
Y
is bounded, quantitatively.

If
D
is an
A
-John domain, in (4) and (5) the term “quantitatively” means that the relevant constant depends only on
A
. Properties (1)–(5) are established in Lemma
4.1
, Lemma
4.2
, Lemma
4.4
, Lemma
4.3
and Corollary
4.6
, respectively. We remark that (5) was also obtained by Kinneberg
[
10
, Theorem 5.6]
through the notion of the discrete UWS property (uniformly well-spread discrete cut points). Here, it follows from Theorem
1.2
and properties (1)–(4). We remark that, given a bounded simply connected domain
D\subset\C
and a weak tangent
Y
of
\partial D
, none of conditions (1)–(5) imply that
D
is a John domain. This can be easily seen by considering, for example, a Jordan region with an outward cusp (like a water droplet). Note that all of our proofs rely on planar techniques. An interesting question is whether any of the preceding results remain valid for John domains in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Convergence of metric spaces

A
pointed metric space

(Y,d,p)
is a metric space
(Y,d)
along with a distinguished base-point
p\in Y
. For
R>0
and
a\in Y
, we set
B_{d}(a,R)=\{y\in Y:d(y,a)<R\}
. When it is clear which metric is used, we denote the ball simply by
B(a,R)
. We give the following definition according to
[
5
, Section 8]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A sequence of pointed metric spaces
\{(Y_{n},d_{n},p_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to a pointed metric space
(Y,d,p)
in the
(pointed) Gromov–Hausdorff sense
if the following holds: For every
\varepsilon>0
and
R>0
, there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
n\geq N
, there is a mapping
f_{n}\colon B_{d_{n}}(p_{n},R)\to Y
for which

(1)

f_{n}(p_{n})=p
,

(2)

|d_{n}(x,y)-d(f_{n}(x),f_{n}(y))|<\varepsilon
for each pair
x,y\in B_{d_{n}}(p_{n},R)
and

(3)

B_{d}(p,R-\varepsilon)\subset N_{\varepsilon}(f_{n}(B_{d_{n}}(p_{n},R)))
.

Recall that the Hausdorff distance between two sets
E,F
is defined to be the infimum of
r>0
such that
E
is contained in the open
r
-neighborhood
N_{r}(F)
of
F
and
F
is contained in
N_{r}(E)
.
For our purposes we will need the following modification of Hausdorff convergence of subsets of a metric space.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
(Z,d)
be a metric space. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
Y_{n},Y\subset Z
be closed sets. We say that the sequence
\{Y_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in the
local Hausdorff sense
to the set
Y
if for each point
z_{0}\in Z
and for each
R,\varepsilon>0
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
n\geq N
we have

\displaystyle Y_{n}\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y\cap B(z_{0},R))\,\,\,\textrm{and}\,\,\,Y\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y_{n}\cap B(z_{0},R)).

Remark 2.3
.

The definition of local Hausdorff convergence does not require that
Y_{n}\cap B(z_{0},R)
converges in the Hausdorff sense to
Y\cap B(z_{0},R)
and it is important to consider instead balls of radius
R-\varepsilon
. For example, if
Y_{n}=\{1/n\}\subset\mathbb{R}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
Y=\{0\}\subset\mathbb{R}
, then
Y_{n}\to Y
in the (local) Hausdorff sense. However,
B(1,1)=(0,2)
and
Y\cap(0,2)=\emptyset
, so
Y_{n}\cap(0,2)
does not converge to
Y\cap(0,2)
in the Hausdorff sense.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(Z,d)
be a metric space. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
Y_{n},Y\subset Z
be closed sets and let
z_{n},z\in Z
. Suppose that
\{Y_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in the local Hausdorff sense to
Y
and
d(z_{n},z)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Then
\dist(z_{n},Y_{n})\to\dist(z,Y)
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let
R=\dist(z,Y)\geq 0
and
\varepsilon>0
. We have

\emptyset\neq Y\cap B(z,R+\varepsilon)\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y_{n}\cap B(z,R+2\varepsilon))\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y_{n}\cap B(z_{n},R+3\varepsilon))

for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This shows that
Y_{n}\cap B(z_{n},R+3\varepsilon)\neq\emptyset
and thus

(2.1)

\dist(z_{n},Y_{n})<R+3\varepsilon

for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, if
R=0
, then
\dist(z_{n},Y_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Now, suppose that
R>0
and
0<\varepsilon<R/2
. We have
Y\cap B(z,R)=\emptyset
and hence, for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

Y_{n}\cap B(z_{n},R-2\varepsilon)\subset Y_{n}\cap B(z,R-\varepsilon)\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y\cap B(z,R))=\emptyset.

Therefore,
\dist(z_{n},Y_{n})\geq R-2\varepsilon
for all large enough
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This in combination with (
2.1
) implies that
\dist(z_{n},Y_{n})\to\dist(z,Y)
as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
(Z,d)
be a proper metric space. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
Y_{n},Y\subset Z
be closed sets. The following are equivalent.

(1)

\{Y_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
Y
in the local Hausdorff sense.

(2)

There exists a point
z_{0}\in Z
such that for each
R,\varepsilon>0
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
n\geq N
we have

\displaystyle Y_{n}\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y\cap B(z_{0},R))\,\,\,\textrm{and}\,\,\,Y\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y_{n}\cap B(z_{0},R)).

(3)

For each
y\in Y
there exists a sequence
y_{n}\in Y_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, that converges to
y
and if
y\in Z
is a limit point of a sequence
y_{n}\in Y_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then
y\in Y
.

Proof.

Suppose (2) is true. Let
y\in Y
. Let
R>2
such that
y\in B(z_{0},R/2)
. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
N_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

y\in Y\cap B(z_{0},R-1/k)\subset N_{1/k}(Y_{n}\cap B(z_{0},R))

for all
n\geq N_{k}
. This implies that there exists a sequence
y_{n}\in Y_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, that converges to
y
. Next, let
y_{n}\in Y_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, be a sequence that has a subsequence
y_{k_{n}}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, converging to a point
y\in Z
. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be arbitrary and fix
R>4
such that
y\in B(z_{0},R/2)
. For all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
d(y_{k_{n}},y)<\varepsilon
, so
y_{k_{n}}\in B(z_{0},R/2+\varepsilon)\subset B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)
. By assumption,
y_{k_{n}}\in N_{\varepsilon}(Y\cap B(z_{0},R))
for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which implies that
y\in N_{2\varepsilon}(Y\cap B(z_{0},R))
. Since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, the fact that
Y
is closed implies that
y\in Y
. Thus, we have verified (3).

Now suppose that (3) is true and suppose that (1) fails. That is, there exist
z_{0}\in Z
,
R>0
, and
\varepsilon>0
such that
Y_{n}\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)\not\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y\cap B(z_{0},R))
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
or
Y\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)\not\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y_{n}\cap B(z_{0},R))
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In the first case we find a sequence
y_{k_{n}}\in Y_{n}\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)
such that
\dist(y_{k_{n}},Y\cap B(z_{0},R))\geq\varepsilon
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
Z
is proper, after passing to a further subsequence, we may assume that
y_{k_{n}}
converges to a point
y\in\overline{B}(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)
. By the assumption in (3), we have
y\in Y
. Thus,
y\in Y\cap B(z_{0},R)
, a contradiction to the condition
\dist(y_{k_{n}},Y\cap B(z_{0},R))\geq\varepsilon
. In the second case there exists a point
y\in Y\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)
such that
\dist(y,Y_{n}\cap B(z_{0},R))\geq\varepsilon
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By the assumption in (3) there exists a sequence
y_{n}\in Y_{n}
converging to
y
. In particular, since
y\in B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)
we have
y_{n}\in Y_{n}\cap B(z_{0},R)
for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This contradicts the condition that
\dist(y,Y_{n}\cap B(z_{0},R))\geq\varepsilon
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
. So, we conclude that (1) is true.
∎

Hausdorff convergence implies local Hausdorff convergence but the converse is not always true. However, if
Z
is a compact metric space the two notions agree, as a consequence of part (3) of the above proposition.

Corollary 2.6
.

If
(Z,d)
is a compact metric space, then local Hausdorff convergence and Hausdorff convergence of subsets of
Z
are equivalent.

We show that in proper metric spaces local Hausdorff limits exist.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
(Z,d)
be a proper metric space. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
Y_{n}\subset Z
be closed sets. Then there exists a subsequence
\{Y_{k_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that converges to a closed set
Y\subset Z
in the local Hausdorff sense.

Proof.

Let
z_{0}\in Z
. By Blaschke’s theorem
[
5
, Theorem 7.3.8]
and a diagonal argument, there exists a subsequence
\{Y_{k_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for each
M\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a compact set
Y^{M}\subset\overline{B}(z_{0},M)
with the property that
Y_{k_{n}}\cap\overline{B}(z_{0},M)
converges to
Y^{M}
in the Hausdorff sense. It follows that
Y^{M}\subset Y^{M+1}
for each
M\in\mathbb{N}
and also
Y^{M}\cap B(z_{0},M)=Y^{N}\cap B(z_{0},M)
for all
N\geq M
. This shows that the set
Y=\bigcup_{M=1}^{\infty}Y^{M}
is a closed subset of
Z
.

Next, we show that the sequence
Y_{k_{n}}
converges to
Y
in the local Hausdorff sense. Let
R,\varepsilon>0
. Let
M\in\mathbb{N}
,
M>R
. We have

Y_{k_{n}}\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)\subset Y_{k_{n}}\cap\overline{B}(z_{0},M)\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y^{M})\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y)

for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This implies that

(2.2)

\displaystyle Y_{k_{n}}\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y)\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon).

Now, we show that

(2.3)

\displaystyle N_{\varepsilon}(Y)\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y\cap B(z_{0},R)).

Let
y\in N_{\varepsilon}(Y)\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)
. Then
\dist(y,Y)<\varepsilon
and
d(y,z_{0})<R-\varepsilon
. There is a point
y_{0}\in Y
such that
d(y,y_{0})<\varepsilon
and thus
d(y_{0},z_{0})<R
. So, we have
y_{0}\in Y\cap B(z_{0},R)
and
\dist(y,Y\cap B(z_{0},R))\leq d(y,y_{0})<\varepsilon
, which proves (
2.3
). By (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) we infer that, for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

Y_{k_{n}}\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y\cap B(z_{0},R)).

In addition, we have

Y\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)\subset Y\cap B(z_{0},M)=Y^{M}\cap B(z_{0},M)\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y_{k_{n}}\cap\overline{B}(z_{0},M))\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y_{k_{n}})

for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This implies that

Y\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y_{k_{n}})\cap B(z_{0},R-\varepsilon)\subset N_{\varepsilon}(Y_{k_{n}}\cap B(z_{0},R)).

By Proposition
2.5
(2) we derive that the sequence
Y_{k_{n}}
converges to
Y
in the local Hausdorff sense. (Note that the subsequence
Y_{k_{n}}
depends on the point
z_{0}
so we cannot argue that
Y_{k_{n}}
converges to
Y
directly by using the definition of local Hausdorff convergence.)
∎

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
(Z,d)
be a proper metric space. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
Y_{n}\subset Z
be closed sets all of which contain a point
z_{0}\in Z
. Then the following statements are true.

(1)

Let
Y\subset Z
be a closed set such that
z_{0}\in Y
. Suppose that
\{Y_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
Y
in the local Hausdorff sense. Then the sequence
\{(Y_{n},d,z_{0})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in the Gromov–Hausdorff sense to
(Y,d,z_{0})
.

(2)

If
\{(Y_{n},d,z_{0})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in the Gromov–Hausdorff sense to a complete pointed metric space
(X,\rho,q)
, then there is a subsequence
\{(Y_{k_{n}},d,z_{0})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and a closed set
Y\subset Z
with
z_{0}\in Y
such that the sequence
\{Y_{k_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in the local Hausdorff sense to
Y
and
(X,\rho,q)
is isometric to
(Y,d,z_{0})
.

Proof.

First, we prove (1). We fix
\varepsilon>0
and
R>0
. By assumption, there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
n\geq N
,
Y\cap{B}(z_{0},R-\varepsilon/2)\subset N_{\varepsilon/2}(Y_{n}\cap{B}(z_{0},R))
and
Y_{n}\cap B(z_{0},R)\subset N_{\varepsilon/2}(Y\cap B(z_{0},R+\varepsilon/2))\subset N_{\varepsilon/2}(Y)
. Let
n\geq N
. If
x\in Y_{n}\cap B(z_{0},R)
, then there is a point
y\in Y
such that
d(x,y)<\varepsilon/2
. Let
f\colon Y_{n}\cap B(z_{0},R)\to Y
be a mapping satisfying

and

for every

. For any pair

Finally, let

. Since

, there is a point

such that

. This in combination with

gives

. Therefore,

. This completes the proof of (1).

Now we prove (2). By Lemma
2.7
, there exists a subsequence
\{Y_{k_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that converges to a closed set

in the local Hausdorff sense. Note that

. By (1), the sequence

converges to
(Y,d,z_{0})
in the Gromov–Hausdorff sense. Finally,
[

, Theorem 8.1.7]
implies that
(Y,d,z_{0})
is isometric to
(X,\rho,q)
.
∎

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
D\subset\C
be a domain and
Y
be a weak tangent of
\partial D
. Let

be a
D
-component of
\C\setminus Y
and

. Then there exists a subsequence
\{D_{k_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{D_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that satisfies the following:

(1)

For every compact set
K\subset U
we have
K\subset D_{k_{n}}
for all sufficiently large

.

(2)

\{D_{k_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
U
in the Carathéodory sense with base at

.

Proof.

First, we prove (1). Let
x\in U
be a point such that
x\in D_{n}
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there exists a subsequence
\{D_{k_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{D_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
x\in D_{k_{n}}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
K\subset U
be a compact set. Then there exists a connected compact set
K^{\prime}\subset U
such that
K\cup\{x\}\subset K^{\prime}
.

We claim that there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

for all
n\geq N
. Otherwise,
K^{\prime}\cap\partial D_{k_{n}}\neq\emptyset
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, there is a sequence of points
y_{n}\in K^{\prime}\cap\partial D_{k_{n}}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, that has a subsequence converging to a point

. By Proposition
2.5
,

. This contradicts the fact that

. Therefore, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
K^{\prime}\cap\partial D_{k_{n}}=\emptyset
for all
n\geq N
. It follows that
K^{\prime}
lies in a connected component of
\C\setminus\partial D_{k_{n}}
. Since
x\in K^{\prime}\cap D_{k_{n}}
, we conclude that

for all

.

Now, we prove (2). By (1) we infer that the

-kernel of
\{D_{k_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, which we denote by

, is non-empty and contains

. For the reverse inclusion, we claim that

. Let

. Then there exists

such that

. By the definition of the

-kernel, there is

such that

for every

and hence

for every

. Hence

cannot be a limit point of

. By Proposition

,

, so

. Since

. Since

, we derive that

.

The same argument applies to show that the

-kernel of every subsequence of

is

, so the conclusion regarding Cathéodory convergence follows.

3.
Main theorem

A
curve
in a metric space
X
is a continuous mapping
\gamma
from a compact interval into
X
. The
trace
of a curve
\gamma
is its image and it is denoted by
|\gamma|
. We define
\ell(\gamma)
to be the length of
\gamma
and
\diam(\gamma)
to be the diameter of
|\gamma|
. In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
. For this purpose we split the proof into two propositions.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
D\subset\C
be a simply connected domain and
A\geq 1
. If
D
is an
A
-John domain, then for every weak tangent
Y
of
\partial D
, every
D
-component of
\C\setminus Y
is an
A
-John domain.

Proof.

Let
Y
be a weak tangent of
\partial D
and let
U
be a
D
-component of
\C\setminus Y
. We consider a subsequence
\{D_{k_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{D_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that satisfies the conclusions of Lemma
2.10
. So, if
z,w\in U
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
z,w\in D_{k_{n}}
for every
n\geq N
. For each
n\geq N
, there is a conformal mapping
f_{n}
from the unit disk
\D
onto
D_{k_{n}}
such that
f_{n}(0)=z
and
f^{\prime}_{n}(0)>0
. Lemma
2.10
(2) implies that

converges to

in the Carathéodory sense with base at

. Thus, applying Carathéodory’s kernel convergence theorem
[
7
, Theorem II.5.1, p. 55]
, we infer that
\{f_{n}\}_{n\geq N}
converges locally uniformly in
\D
to a conformal mapping
f
. Moreover,
f(\D)=U
and
\{f^{-1}_{n}\}_{n\geq N}
converges locally uniformly in
U
to
f^{-1}
. Therefore, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}^{-1}(w)=f^{-1}(w)\in\D.

Let
w_{n}=f^{-1}_{n}(w)
and let
\gamma_{n}(t)=tw_{n}
,
0\leq t\leq 1
, be a parametrization of the line segment joining

to
w_{n}
. We deduce that
\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n\geq N}
converges uniformly to

,

, which parametrizes the line segment joining

to
f^{-1}(w)
. Also,

converges uniformly to

. We set

and
\Gamma=f\circ\gamma\colon[0,1]\to U
.

By assumption,
D
is an
A
-John domain and thus, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
D_{n}
is an
A
-John domain as well. By
[
15
, Theorem 5.2]
the curve
\Gamma_{n}
, which is actually the hyperbolic geodesic connecting

and
w
in
D_{k_{n}}
, is an
A
-John curve for every
n\geq N
. Since
\{f_{n}^{\prime}\}_{n\geq N}
converges locally uniformly in
\D
to
f^{\prime}
and by Lemma
2.4
, we derive that, for every
t\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\min\{\ell(\Gamma|_{[0,t]}),\ell(\Gamma|_{[t,1]})\}

\displaystyle=\min\{\lim_{n\to\infty}\ell(\Gamma_{n}|_{[0,t]}),\lim_{n\to\infty}\ell(\Gamma_{n}|_{[t,1]})\}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\min\{\ell(\Gamma_{n}|_{[0,t]}),\ell(\Gamma_{n}|_{[t,1]})\}

\displaystyle\leq A\lim_{n\to\infty}\dist(\Gamma_{n}(t),\partial D_{k_{n}})

\displaystyle=A\dist(\Gamma(t),Y)=A\dist(\Gamma(t),\partial U).

So,
\Gamma\colon[0,1]\to U
is an
A
-John curve joining
z
to
w
. Since

were arbitrary, we infer that
U

is an
A
-John domain.
∎

.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let

be a bounded simply connected domain. If, for any weak tangent

of

, every

-component of

Proof.

Suppose that
D
is not a John domain. Then, by
[
15
, Theorem 2.14]
, for every

there are two distinct points

such that for every curve
\gamma_{n}\colon[0,1]\to{D}
with
\gamma_{n}(0)=z_{n}
and
\gamma_{n}(1)=w_{n}

\displaystyle\min\{\diam(\gamma_{n}|_{[0,t]}),\diam(\gamma_{n}|_{[t,1]})\}\geq n\dist(\gamma_{n}(t),\partial D)

. Let
\gamma_{n}
be a quasi-hyperbolic geodesic joining

to
w_{n}
in

.

We fix

. By Lemma
3.2
there exists a subcurve

of

and a point

\displaystyle\min\{\diam\tilde{\gamma}_{n}({u_{n},y_{n}}),\diam\tilde{\gamma}_{n}({y_{n},v_{n}})\}\geq(n/3)\lambda_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad N_{\lambda_{n}/2}(|\tilde{\gamma}_{n}|)\subset D.

\displaystyle\dist(y^{\prime}_{n},\partial D_{n})=\frac{\dist(y_{n},\partial D)}{\lambda_{n}}=1=|y^{\prime}_{n}|.

\displaystyle\min\{\diam\Gamma_{n}({Z_{n},y^{\prime}_{n}}),\diam\Gamma_{n}({y^{\prime}_{n},W_{n}})\}\geq n/3

\displaystyle N_{1/2}(|\Gamma_{n}|)\subset D_{n}.

.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\geq 2
, to be specified later. For every

, by (

y_{n}^{\prime}\in B(0,m)
and by (

\Gamma_{n}({Z_{n},y^{\prime}_{n}}),\Gamma_{n}({y^{\prime}_{n},W_{n}})
intersect
\partial B(0,m)
on at least one point, respectively. We denote by
\Gamma^{-}_{n,m}
a subcurve of
\Gamma_{n}({Z_{n},y^{\prime}_{n}})
that lies in
\overline{B}(0,m)
and joins
y^{\prime}_{n}
to
\partial B(0,m)
and we denote by
\Gamma^{+}_{n,m}
a subcurve of
\Gamma_{n}({y^{\prime}_{n},W_{n}})
that lies in
\overline{B}(0,m)
and joins
y^{\prime}_{n}
to
\partial B(0,m)
. Therefore,
|\Gamma^{-}_{n,m}|
and
|\Gamma^{+}_{n,m}|
are compact and connected and they are contained in
\overline{B}(0,m)\cap D_{n}
. By Blaschke’s theorem
[
5
, Theorem 7.3.8]
, there is a subsequence of
|\Gamma_{n,m}^{\pm}|
,
{n>6m}
, that converges in the Hausdorff sense to a compact and connected set
\Gamma_{m}^{\pm}
. We denote the subsequence by
|\Gamma_{k_{n},m}^{\pm}|
,
n\geq 1
, and observe that it depends on
m
. The sets
\Gamma^{+}_{m},\Gamma^{-}_{m}
are compact and connected, they contain
y^{\prime}
, they intersect
\partial B(0,m)
and they are contained in
\overline{B}(0,m)
.

Now, we prove that
\Gamma^{-}_{m}\cup\Gamma^{+}_{m}\subset U
. Let

. Then there is a sequence
x_{n}\in|\Gamma^{+}_{{k_{n}},m}|
,

, that converges to

\lim_{n\to\infty}\dist(x_{n},\partial D_{k_{n}})=\dist(x,Y)

and by (

\dist(x_{n},\partial D_{k_{n}})\geq 1/2
. Hence,
\dist(x,Y)\geq 1/2
for every

. The same argument applies to
\Gamma^{-}_{m}
. Since
\Gamma^{-}_{m}\cup\Gamma^{+}_{m}
is compact, connected, and contains
y^{\prime}
, we deduce that
\Gamma^{-}_{m}\cup\Gamma^{+}_{m}
lies in
U
.

Our goal is to show that
U
is not a John domain. For this purpose we need to prove the following claim.

Claim:
There is a uniform constant
C_{1}\geq 2
that does not depend on
m
such that, if
m>C_{1}
, then for any
p\in\Gamma^{-}_{m}\setminus\overline{B}(0,C_{1})
and

, any curve connecting

to
q
in

B(0,C_{1})
. An immediate consequence is that
\partial U\cap\overline{B}(0,C_{1})\neq\emptyset
.

To prove this claim, recall that
\Gamma_{n}
is a quasi-hyperbolic geodesic between
Z_{n}
and
W_{n}
in
D_{n}
and
y^{\prime}_{n}\in|\Gamma_{n}|
. Corollary
3.4
and (
3.6
) imply that there is a uniform constant
C_{1}\geq 2
such that, for every
a_{n},b_{n}\in|\Gamma_{n}|
with
y^{\prime}_{n}\in|\Gamma_{n}(a_{n},b_{n})|
, the ball
B(0,C_{1})
intersects any curve connecting
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
in
D_{n}
.

Now, take
m>C_{1}
. Suppose there are
p\in\Gamma^{-}_{m}\setminus\overline{B}(0,C_{1})
and
q\in\Gamma^{+}_{m}\setminus\overline{B}(0,C_{1})
such that there is a curve
\beta
joining
p
to
q
and lying in
U\setminus B(0,C_{1})
. Then there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
B(p,\varepsilon),B(q,\varepsilon)\subset U\setminus\overline{B}(0,C_{1})
. Moreover, there are sequences
\{p_{n}\}
and
\{q_{n}\}
with
p_{n}\in|\Gamma^{-}_{k_{n},m}|
and
q_{n}\in|\Gamma^{+}_{k_{n},m}|

p_{n}\to p
and
q_{n}\to q
as

. Therefore, there is
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p_{n}\in B(p,\varepsilon)
and
q_{n}\in B(q,\varepsilon)
for every
n\geq N_{1}
. For each
n\geq N_{1}
, we take the curve
\beta_{n}
which is the concatenation of the line segment joining
p_{n}
to
p
, the curve
\beta
joining
p
to
q
, and the line segment joining
q_{n}
to
q
. By construction,

for all

. Since
U
is a

-component, there exists
N_{2}\geq N_{1}
such that
|\beta_{n}|\subset D_{k_{n}}
for every

. So,
\beta_{n}
connects
p_{n}
to
q_{n}
and
|\beta_{n}|\subset D_{k_{n}}\setminus B(0,C_{1})
for every

. This leads to contradiction and thus our claim is true.

Now, we are able to prove that

is not a John domain. On the contrary, assume that

is an
A
-John domain for some

. Let
m>(2A+1)C_{1}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
m>C_{1}
, we can find
p\in(\Gamma^{-}_{m}\cap\partial B(0,m))\setminus\overline{B}(0,C_{1})
and

. By
[

, Theorem 2.14]
there is a curve
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to U
such that
\gamma(0)=p
,
\gamma(1)=q
and

(3.9)

\min\{\diam(\gamma|_{[0,t]}),\diam(\gamma|_{[t,1]})\}\leq A\dist(\gamma(t),\partial U),

for all

and

of

Now, Theorem

4.
Further properties of weak tangents

In this section we prove Theorem
1.5
, which gives several geometric properties of weak tangents of simply connected John domains that are interesting in their own right. Each part of the theorem is proved in a separate lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
D\subset\C
be a simply connected John domain. For every weak tangent
Y
of
\partial D
, every
D
-component of
\C\setminus Y
is unbounded and simply connected.

The idea of the lemma is that if there exists a bounded
D
-component, then the domain
D
must have narrow passages, which is prohibited by the John domain condition. The last claim of the lemma about simple connectivity is more general and does not rely on the John property.

Proof.

Suppose that
D
is an
A
-John domain for some
A\geq 1
. Let
Y
be a weak tangent of
\partial D
and let
U
be a
D
-component of
\C\setminus Y
. By Lemma
2.10
there exists a subsequence
\{D_{k_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{D_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for every compact set
K\subset U
we have
K\subset D_{k_{n}}
for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
z\in U
. There is
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
z\in D_{k_{n}}
for all
n\geq N_{1}
. Let
R>0
. By (
2.4
),
\diam\partial D_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
and hence there is
N_{2}\geq N_{1}
such that
D_{k_{n}}\not\subset\overline{B}(z,2R)
for every
n\geq N_{2}
. So, for every
n\geq N_{2}
, there is
w_{n}\in D_{k_{n}}\setminus\overline{B}(z,2R)
. Since
D_{k_{n}}
is an
A
-John domain, we derive that, for every
n\geq N_{2}
, there is an
A
-John curve
\Gamma_{n}\colon[0,1]\to D_{k_{n}}
with
\Gamma_{n}(0)=z
and
\Gamma_{n}(1)=w_{n}
. For each
n\geq N_{2}
, let
E_{n}=\Gamma_{n}([0,t_{n}])
where
t_{n}=\min\{t:\Gamma_{n}(t)\in\partial B(z,R)\}
. So,
E_{n}
is a compact and connected set that is contained in
\overline{B}(z,R)
and it contains
z
and a point on
\partial B(z,R)
. By Blaschke’s theorem
[
5
, Theorem 7.3.8]
, there is a subsequence
\{E_{m_{n}}\}_{n\geq 1}
of
\{E_{n}\}_{n\geq N_{2}}
that converges in the Hausdorff sense to a compact and connected set
E\subset\overline{B}(z,R)
such that
z\in E
and
E\cap\partial B(z,R)\neq\emptyset
. We claim that
E\subset U
. Since
E
is connected and
z\in E\cap U
, it suffices to prove that
\dist(E,Y)>0
.

To show this, we choose
x\in E
such that
\dist(E,Y)=\dist(x,Y)
. If
x=z
this is trivial, so we suppose that
x\neq z
. There is a sequence
x_{n}\in E_{m_{n}}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, that converges to
x
. So, there is
t_{n}^{\prime}\in[0,1]
such that
x_{n}=\Gamma_{m_{n}}(t_{n}^{\prime})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By Lemma
2.4
we have

\displaystyle\dist(x,Y)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\dist(x_{n},\partial D_{k_{n}})\geq\frac{1}{A}\lim_{n\to\infty}\min\{\ell(\Gamma_{m_{n}}|_{[0,t_{n}^{\prime}]}),\ell(\Gamma_{m_{n}}|_{[t_{n}^{\prime},1]})\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{A}\lim_{n\to\infty}\min\{|z-x_{n}|,|x_{n}-w_{n}|\}=\frac{1}{A}|z-x|>0,

where we used the fact that
|z-x_{n}|\leq R\leq|x_{n}-w_{n}|
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. So, the claim has been proved. Since
E\subset U
, we have
\diam U\geq\diam E\geq R
. Letting
R\to\infty
we get
\diam U=\infty
and hence
U
is unbounded.

Finally, we prove that
U
is simply connected. Suppose that
U
is not simply connected. Then there is a Jordan region
V
such that
\partial V\subset U
and
V
contains a connected component
C_{0}
of
\partial U
. Since
\partial V
is compact, we have
\partial V\subset D_{k_{n}}
for all large enough
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By assumption,
D_{k_{n}}
is simply connected and thus
C_{0}\subset V\subset\overline{V}\subset D_{k_{n}}
for all large enough
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\zeta\in C_{0}
. Then
\dist(\zeta,\partial D_{k_{n}})\geq\dist(\zeta,\partial V)\geq\dist(C_{0},\partial V)
for all large enough
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, applying Lemma
2.4
, we deduce that

\dist(\zeta,Y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\dist(\zeta,\partial D_{k_{n}})\geq\dist(C_{0},\partial V)>0.

Since
\zeta\in C_{0}
was arbitrary, we have
\dist(C_{0},Y)>0
. This contradicts the fact that
C_{0}\subset Y
. So,
U
is simply connected.
∎

We prove a general property of weak tangents of the boundary of a bounded simply connected domain.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
D\subset\C
be a bounded simply connected domain. For every weak tangent
Y
of
\partial D
, every connected component of
Y
is unbounded.

Proof.

Let
Y
be a weak tangent of
\partial D
. Suppose that
Y
has one bounded connected component
Y_{0}
. First, suppose that
Y
has another connected component
Y_{1}
. By Zoretti’s theorem
[
17
, Corollary VI.3.11, p. 109]
there is a Jordan region
V
such that
\partial V\cap Y=\emptyset
,
Y_{0}\subset V
, and
Y_{1}\subset\C\setminus\overline{V}
. Let
z\in Y_{0}
and
w\in Y_{1}
. By Proposition
2.5
there are sequences
z_{n},w_{n}\in\partial D_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
z_{n}\to z
and
w_{n}\to w
as
n\to\infty
. So, there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for every
n\geq N
,
z_{n}\in V
and
w_{n}\in\C\setminus\overline{V}
. By assumption,
\partial D_{n}
is connected for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. So, for every
n\geq N
there is
x_{n}\in\partial D_{n}\cap\partial V
. Then there is a subsequence of
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq N}
that converges to a point
x\in\partial V
. Proposition
2.5
implies that
x\in Y
, which leads to contradiction, because
\partial V\cap Y=\emptyset
.

Now, suppose that
Y=Y_{0}
. Since
Y
is bounded, there is
R>0
such that
Y\subset B(0,R)
. By (
2.4
) there is
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for every
n\geq N_{1}
,
\diam\partial D_{n}>2R
and hence
\partial D_{n}\not\subset\overline{B}(0,R)
. So, for each
n\geq N_{1}
, there exists
z_{n}\in\partial D_{n}\setminus\overline{B}(0,R)
. Now, let
y\in Y
. By Proposition
2.5
there is a sequence
y_{n}\in\partial D_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, that converges to
y
. So, there is
N_{2}\geq N_{1}
such that
y_{n}\in B(0,R)
for all
n\geq N_{2}
. Since
\partial D_{n}
is connected, for every
n\geq N_{2}
there is
x_{n}\in\partial D_{n}\cap\partial B(0,R)
. By compactness there is a subsequence of
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq N_{2}}
converging to a point
x\in\partial B(0,R)
. Also, by Proposition
2.5
,
x\in Y
, which contradicts the fact that
Y\cap\partial B(0,R)=\emptyset
.
∎

The following lemma, which is an application of our main theorem, gives a uniform bound for the number of
D
-components in the case that
D
is a simply connected John domain.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
A\geq 1
. Let
D\subset\C
be a simply connected
A
-John domain. For every weak tangent
Y
of
\partial D
, the number of
D
-components of
\C\setminus Y
is bounded by
4\pi A
.

Proof.

Let
Y
be a weak tangent of
\partial D
. Suppose that there is at least one
D
-component of
\C\setminus Y
. Let
U_{1},U_{2},\dots,U_{N}
be distinct
D
-components of
\C\setminus Y
, where
N\geq 1
. By Proposition
3.1
these connected components are
A
-John domains and by Lemma
4.1
they are unbounded. Thus, there is
R>0
such that
\partial B(0,R)
intersects
U_{1},U_{2},\dots,U_{N}
and, for each
i=1,2,\dots,N
, there exist
z_{i}\in U_{i}\cap B(0,R)
and
w_{i}\in U_{i}\setminus\overline{B}(0,3R)
. Then there is a curve
\gamma_{i}\colon[0,1]\to U_{i}
such that
\gamma_{i}(0)=z_{i}
,
\gamma_{i}(1)=w_{i}
and for every
t\in[0,1]
,

\min\{\ell(\gamma_{i}|_{[0,t]}),\ell(\gamma_{i}|_{[t,1]})\}\leq A\dist(\gamma_{i}(t),\partial U_{i}).

We choose
t_{i}\in(0,1)
such that
\gamma_{i}(t_{i})\in\partial B(0,2R)
. So, we have

R\leq A\dist(\gamma_{i}(t_{i}),\partial U_{i})\leq 2AR\theta^{i}(2R),

where
2R\theta^{i}(2R)
is the length of the smallest arc on
\partial B(0,2R)
joining
\gamma_{i}(t_{i})
to
\partial U_{i}
. Now, we take the sum for all
i=1,2,\dots,N
and we get

NR\leq 2AR\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta^{i}(2R)\leq 4AR\pi.

So, we derive that
N\leq 4\pi A
.
∎

Based on the above lemmas, we establish a result that describes a weak tangent
Y
of
\partial D
in terms of the boundaries of
D
-components.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
D\subset\C
be a bounded simply connected John domain. For every weak tangent
Y
of
\partial D
and for every
z\in Y
there exists a
D
-component
U
such that
z\in\partial U
. In particular,
Y
is equal to the union of the boundaries of
D
-components of
\C\setminus Y
.

Proof.

Suppose that
D
is an
A
-John domain for some
A\geq 1
. Let
Y
be a weak tangent of
\partial D
. Let
z\in Y
and
\varepsilon>0
. By Lemma
4.2
,
Y
is unbounded and thus there is
w\in Y\setminus B(z,2\varepsilon)
. By Proposition
2.5
, there are sequences
z_{n},w_{n}\in\partial D_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
z_{n}\to z
and
w_{n}\to w
as
n\to\infty
. So, there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for every
n\geq N
,
z_{n}\in B(z,\varepsilon)
and
w_{n}\in B(w,\varepsilon)
. For each
n\geq N
, by
[
15
, Theorem 6.5]
there is a curve
\gamma_{n}\colon[0,1]\to\overline{D}_{n}
with
\gamma_{n}(0)=z_{n}
,
\gamma_{n}(1)=w_{n}
,
\gamma_{n}((0,1))\subset D_{n}
and

\min\{\ell(\gamma_{n}|_{[0,t]}),\ell(\gamma_{n}|_{[t,1]})\}\leq A\dist(\gamma_{n}(t),\partial D_{n}),

for every
t\in[0,1]
. Take
t_{n}\in(0,1)
such that
\zeta_{n}:=\gamma_{n}(t_{n})\in\partial B(z,\varepsilon)\cap D_{n}
. Then

\min\{|z_{n}-\zeta_{n}|,|\zeta_{n}-w_{n}|\}\leq A\dist(\zeta_{n},\partial D_{n}).

Moreover, there is a subsequence
\{\zeta_{k_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{\zeta_{n}\}_{n\geq N}
that converges to a point
\zeta({\varepsilon})\in\partial B(z,\varepsilon)
. So, taking limits in the above relation as
n\to\infty
, by Lemma
2.4
we infer that

(4.1)

\varepsilon=|z-\zeta({\varepsilon})|\leq A\dist(\zeta({\varepsilon}),Y).

This implies that
\zeta({\varepsilon})\notin Y
. So,
\zeta({\varepsilon})
belongs to a connected component
U_{\varepsilon}
of
\C\setminus Y
.

We now show that
U_{\varepsilon}
is a
D
-component. Lemma
2.4
and (
4.1
) give

\lim_{n\to\infty}\dist(\zeta(\varepsilon),\partial D_{k_{n}})=\dist(\zeta({\varepsilon}),Y)\geq\varepsilon/A

and thus
\overline{B}(\zeta(\varepsilon),\varepsilon/(2A))\cap\partial D_{k_{n}}=\emptyset
for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover,
\zeta_{k_{n}}\in B(\zeta(\varepsilon),\varepsilon/(2A))\cap D_{k_{n}}
for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence,
\overline{B}(\zeta(\varepsilon),\varepsilon/(2A))\subset D_{k_{n}}
and thus
\zeta(\varepsilon)\in D_{k_{n}}
for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This proves that
U_{\varepsilon}
is a
D
-component.

We apply the above argument for
\varepsilon=1/n
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and we get a sequence of points
\zeta({1/n})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
|z-\zeta(1/n)|=1/n
and
\zeta(1/n)\in U_{1/n}
, where
U_{1/n}
is a
D
-component of
\C\setminus Y
. By Lemma
4.3
the number of
D
-components is uniformly bounded and thus we derive that
\zeta(1/n)
belongs to a
D
-component
U_{0}
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\zeta(1/n)\to z
as
n\to\infty
and
z\in Y\subset\C\setminus U_{0}
, we have
z\in\partial U_{0}
.
∎

The following lemma is independent of all our previous results and asserts that the boundary of a simply connected John domain has a uniformly bounded number of connected components. The idea is that if this number is not bounded, then the domain must have narrow passages or bottlenecks, which is prohibited by the John domain condition.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
D\subset\C
be a simply connected
A
-John domain for some
A\geq 1
. Then the number of connected components of
\partial D
is bounded by
4\pi A
.

Proof.

If
\partial D
is connected then the conclusion is immediate. So, assume that
\partial D
has at least two connected components. Let
C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{N}
be distinct connected components of
\partial D
, where
N\geq 2
. Note that since
D
is simply connected,
D
and
C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{N}
are all unbounded. We take

d=\min\{\dist(C_{i},C_{j}):i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}\,\,\,{\rm and}\,\,\,i\neq j\}

and
\varepsilon=d/3
. So,
N_{\varepsilon}(C_{i})\cap N_{\varepsilon}(C_{j})=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
. There is
R>0
such that
\partial B(0,R)
intersects
C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{N}
and
D\cap B(0,R)\neq\emptyset
. Let
z\in D\cap B(0,R)
. For each
i=1,2,\dots,N
we take
z_{i}\in(D\cap N_{\varepsilon}(C_{i}))\setminus\overline{B}(0,3R)
. Then there is a curve
\gamma_{i}\colon[0,1]\to D
such that
\gamma_{i}(0)=z
,
\gamma_{i}(1)=z_{i}
and for every
t\in[0,1]
,

\min\{\ell(\gamma_{i}|_{[0,t]}),\ell(\gamma_{i}|_{[t,1]})\}\leq A\dist(\gamma_{i}(t),\partial D).

We choose
t_{i}\in(0,1)
such that
\gamma_{i}(t_{i})\in\partial B(0,2R)
. So, we have

R\leq A\dist(\gamma_{i}(t_{i}),C_{i})\leq 2AR\theta^{i}(2R),

where
2R\theta^{i}(2R)
is the length of the smallest arc on
\partial B(0,2R)
joining
\gamma_{i}(t_{i})
to
C_{i}
. Taking the sum for
i=1,2,\dots,N
, we get
NR\leq 4AR\pi
and thus
N\leq 4\pi A
.
∎

Our last result, which is a consequence of our main theorem and the previous lemmas, gives an alternative proof of Theorem 5.6 in
[

]
.

Corollary 4.6

Let
A\geq 1
. Let
D\subset\C
be a bounded simply connected
A
-John domain. Then the number of connected components of every weak tangent
Y
of
\partial D
is bounded by
16\pi^{2}A^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{N}
be distinct connected components of
Y
, where
N\geq 1
. By Lemma
4.4
every connected component of
Y
contains a connected component of the boundary of a
D
-component of
\C\setminus Y
. Thus there is a mapping from the set
B=\{Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{N}\}
into the set
E
of
D
-components of
\C\setminus Y
. By Lemma
4.1
and Theorem
1.2
, every
D
-component of
\C\setminus Y
is a simply connected
A
-John domain. So, Lemma
4.5
implies that the boundary of each
D
-component has at most
4\pi A
connected components. We conclude that each element of
E
has at most
4\pi A
pre-images in
B
. By Lemma
4.3
,
E
has at most
4\pi A
elements. Since
N
is equal to the sum of the numbers of pre-images of the elements in
E
, we derive that
N\leq(4\pi A)^{2}
.
∎
