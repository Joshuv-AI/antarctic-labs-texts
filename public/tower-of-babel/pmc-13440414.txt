A metabolite morphogen coordinates multicellular development in Bacillus subtilis
Authors: Taylor B. Updegrove, Vani Pande, Fukang She, Michael C. Gilmore, Vivek Anantharaman, David M. Stevenson, Daniel Amador-Noguez, L. Aravind, Felipe Cava, Jiji Chen, Jue D. Wang, Kumaran S. Ramamurthi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The transition from unicellular to multicellular growth requires diversification of cellular functions within genetically identical populations. In Bacillus subtilis, biofilm formation is historically viewed as a developmental precursor to sporulation along a linear pathway. Here, we show that biofilm formation and sporulation instead diverge along a branched pathway. A subpopulation that first initiates sporulation catabolizes lipoteichoic acid through the sequential action of the enzymes ShfP (Sporulation heterogeneity factor Poison) and PhoA (alkaline phosphatase A), leading to the release of millimolar concentrations of glycerol. This glycerol impedes sporulation by disrupting cell wall synthesis and cytoplasmic pH, necessitating counteraction by another protein, ShfA (Sporulation heterogeneity factor Antidote). The extracellular glycerol, however, acts as a morphogen that directs neighboring cells to initiate biofilm formation, which we directly visualize in developing populations of cells. Thus, B. subtilis multicellularity emerges through a branched developmental program in which sporulating cells generate the cue that creates the biofilm-producing lineage via cell-cell communication through repurposing of a canonical intracellular metabolite.

INTRODUCTION

Bacteria use diverse strategies to survive nutrient limitation and other environmental stresses during stationary phase (1–3). Two extreme and interrelated strategies broadly conserved in the phylum Bacillota (formerly Firmicutes) and particularly well studied in the bacterium Bacillus subtilis include biofilm formation and endospore formation (sporulation) (4–8). B. subtilis biofilms are multicellular structures of differentiated cells that are encased in a self-produced extracellular matrix composed of polysaccharide, matrix proteins, and extracellular DNA (eDNA) that acts as a physical barrier to protect the encased cells from antimicrobial agents and biocides (9–13). B. subtilis biofilm formation is widely studied for both its beneficial impact on plant growth arising from colonization on root systems (14, 15) and as a model for understanding disruptive bacterial biofilm formation in stainless steel tanks and pipes (16, 17).

Biofilm formation results in a highly differentiated population of genetically identical cells that perform distinct functions, offering a tractable system for studying the transition from unicellular bacterial growth to cooperative multicellular groups (17). Within these populations, some cells produce the extracellular biofilm matrix, while other cells differentiate into endospores (“spores”), which are dormant cells that withstand environmental insults such as high heat, predation, radiation, and caustic chemicals, independent of being encased in a biofilm (18–20). The differentiation into either a biofilm matrix-producing cell or a spore-forming cell is governed by the same stationary phase transcriptional master regulator Spo0A (21), whose activation by phosphorylation dictates cell type specification (22). Low levels of Spo0A phosphorylation in stationary phase activate the expression of genes required for sliding motility (23). Intermediate levels of Spo0A phosphorylation result in expression of genes required for biofilm matrix production (24). High levels of Spo0A phosphorylation activate sporulation-specific genes and repress sliding motility and biofilm production (25). This requirement for increasing levels of Spo0A activation led to an intuitive linear model (Fig. 1A, left) in which individual cells in a population would start off as a sliding cell in stationary phase and, as environmental conditions gradually deteriorated, a subset of those cells would transform to a more marked lifestyle change by triggering the formation of biofilms and then, as a final strategy, a subset of biofilm-forming cells would initiate the most marked change by triggering spore formation, which would commit the cell to dormancy (26–28).

Despite this shared Spo0A-dependent regulation, biofilm formation and sporulation have been historically studied separately because medium conditions that promote robust biofilm formation [the widely used Minimal Salts glycerol glutamate (MSgg) medium, for example] do not support robust sporulation levels, and media in which cells sporulate robustly [the widely used Difco sporulation medium (DSM), for example] suppress biofilm formation. One notable example of this medium effect is the inclusion of glycerol in MSgg, which stimulates biofilm formation through activation of the histidine kinase KinD and subsequent Spo0A phosphorylation (29). Although the physiological relevance of extracellular glycerol for biofilm formation was initially unclear, a later study (30) reported that sporulating B. subtilis cells produce millimolar quantities of glycerol via the phosphoesterase ShfP (Sporulation heterogeneity factor Poison; YvnB). This pathway increases population heterogeneity by inhibiting sporulation in neighboring cells, thereby enhancing a bet-hedging strategy that benefits the population as a whole (31–33) by maintaining a reserve of nonsporulating cells (30). The released glycerol impeded sporulation in the very cells that released it, which necessitated an undefined counteraction conferred by the integral membrane protein ShfA (Sporulation heterogeneity factor Antidote; YabQ) (30). However, these studies were conducted in a domesticated, biofilm-defective laboratory strain of B. subtilis, so the effect of this pathway on biofilm formation remained unresolved.

Here, we show that in an undomesticated strain of B. subtilis, the ShfA-ShfP pathway couples sporulation to biofilm formation through cell-cell signaling. Glycerol released by sporulating cells impedes sporulation in the cells that produce it due to the accumulation of phosphorylated glycolytic intermediates that lowers cytoplasmic pH and blocks cortex assembly. The same extracellular glycerol conversely functions as a morphogen that triggers biofilm formation in neighboring cells. These findings reveal that B. subtilis development proceeds through a branched pathway (Fig. 1A, right) rather than a linear sequence, with sporulating cells generating the signal that directs other cells to adopt a biofilm-producing fate. In this way, an intracellular metabolite is repurposed as an extracellular cue that organizes multicellular development.

RESULTS

Glycerol inhibits a late step of sporulation in B. subtilis

Sporulation initiates when elevated levels of phosphorylated Spo0A drive the asymmetric division of a progenitor cell, producing a large mother cell and a smaller forespore (Fig. 1B) (19, 20). The mother cell then engulfs the forespore to generate a topologically isolated forespore compartment that is encased in two membranes. Between these membranes, the mother cell constructs a cell wall “cortex” that is composed of peptidoglycan and lipoteichoic acid (LTA). After the forespore achieves dormancy, the mother cell lyses, releasing the now mature spore into the environment. Two sporulation proteins, ShfA and ShfP, localize to distinct cellular compartments. ShfA, a protein with three transmembrane segments, is produced in the mother cell and embeds in the outer forespore membrane (Fig. 1B, light blue) (30, 34, 35). ShfP, a calcineurin-like phosphoesterase synthesized in the forespore, is translocated to the intermembrane space surrounding the forespore (Fig. 1B, red) (30), where it is responsible for the generation and release of ∼1.3 mM glycerol into the growth medium (30). We previously showed that this glycerol delays sporulation in neighboring cells by repressing Spo0A activation via KinD (Fig. 1B, blue) (30), independently of the cannibalism pathway (Fig. 1B) (33).

To distinguish whether external glycerol affects sporulation or subsequent germination, we added glycerol to sporulating cultures before or after heat treatment at 80°C, which kills nonsporulating cells. Glycerol addition during sporulation, but not after heat treatment, inhibited recovery of heat-resistant spores (fig. S1A), indicating that glycerol specifically interferes with sporulation.

Because cells lacking ShfA fail to assemble the cortex (30, 34), we reasoned that this sporulation-inhibitory activity of glycerol might disrupt a late stage of sporulation downstream of Spo0A activation. To test this, we used a constitutively active variant of Spo0A (Sad67) that bypasses Spo0A phosphorylation requirements and drives synchronous sporulation even in nutrient-rich medium (36). Deletion of shfA and shfP accelerated entry into sporulation (30), and the sad67-induced entry into sporulation was largely unaffected by deletion of shfA and shfP (fig. S1B). Deletion of shfA caused a sporulation defect that was suppressed by deletion of shfP (Fig. 1C, lanes 1 to 3) (30). However, replacing the wild-type (WT) spo0A allele with spo0Asad67 did not rescue the ΔshfA defect (Fig. 1C, lanes 4 to 6), indicating that the Sad67 variant bypasses the ShfP/glycerol-mediated inhibition of entry into sporulation in cells that have not entered sporulation, but not the late-stage inhibition of sporulation by glycerol. In addition, Spo0Asad67 did not alleviate the sporulation inhibitory effect of externally added glycerol to cells lacking both ShfA and ShfP (Fig. 1D, lanes 1 to 3), which was nonetheless corrected by the addition of just ShfA (Fig. 1D, lanes 4 to 6). Together, these results indicate that extracellular glycerol inhibits sporulation at two levels: first by repressing Spo0A activation (30) and second by blocking cortex formation through an effect that is counteracted by ShfA.

Uptake and metabolism of glycerol generated from LTA catabolism blocks sporulation

Production of extracellular glycerol requires the phosphoesterase activity of ShfP, which localizes to the intermembrane space of the forespore envelope (30). However, the identity of the ShfP substrate was unknown. Because LTA is a polymer of glycerol 1-phosphate (G1P) (37) and resides in the forespore intermembrane space, we hypothesized that ShfP catabolizes LTA to generate glycerol. A “teichoicase” activity had been previously detected in cell extracts of sporulating B. subtilis, but the responsible enzyme was not identified (38, 39). Cells lacking ShfP failed to produce extracellular glycerol (30), and cells lacking the antidote ShfA also lacked detectable extracellular glycerol, likely due to sporulation arrest before cortex assembly and consequent loss of ShfP production (Fig. 2A, lanes 1 to 4). Deleting ltaSA, which encodes an LTA synthase (40, 41), reduced extracellular glycerol (Fig. 2A, lane 5), whereas deletion of glpQ, which degrades wall teichoic acid [WTA; a polymer of the enantiomer glycerol 3-phosphate (G3P) (37, 42)] had no effect (Fig. 2A, lane 6). These results indicate that ShfP likely generates extracellular glycerol by degrading LTA.

To determine whether extracellular glycerol or an intracellular metabolic intermediate is responsible for the inhibition of sporulation, we examined the glycerol utilization pathway (Fig. 2, B and C). Glycerol enters cells through passive diffusion or the GlpF transporter (43) and is phosphorylated by GlpK to produce the membrane-impermeable G3P, which is converted by GlpD to dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) that is then fed into glycolysis and/or gluconeogenesis (44, 45). In ΔshfA ΔshfP cells to which we added glycerol externally, deleting glpF partially suppressed glycerol-induced sporulation defects, while deleting glpK or glpD fully suppressed them (Fig. 2D). Thus, intracellular metabolism of glycerol, rather than glycerol itself, underlies the sporulation block, and DHAP or a downstream glycolytic intermediate likely causes the late-stage block in sporulation.

We next examined whether glycerol produced endogenously by ShfP has a similar sporulation-inhibitory effect. In cells producing ShfP (but not ShfA), deletion of glpF, glpK, or glpD individually failed to suppress the sporulation defect of ΔshfA mutants (Fig. 2E). However, combined deletion of glpF and glpK restored sporulation (Fig. 2E). Deletion of the transcription antiterminator glpP, which globally disrupts glycerol metabolism (Fig. 2C) (46, 47), or ltaSA, which abrogates LTA synthesis, also suppressed the ΔshfA defect (Fig. 2E). B. subtilis encodes three other LTA synthases: ltaS, yqgS, and yvgJ (40). Deleting ltaS or yqgS only partially suppressed the ΔshfA defect, and deleting yvgJ failed to suppress the sporulation defect of ΔshfA (Fig. 2E), suggesting that LtaSA may be the major LTA synthase for cortex-associated LTA during late sporulation. These results suggest that LTA-derived glycerol is metabolized to an intermediate that impedes sporulation and that ShfA functions to counteract this inhibitory effect.

The ShfP phosphoesterase is predicted to hydrolyze LTA to produce G1P, which would require an additional phosphatase to generate free glycerol (39). Comparative domain analysis revealed that the calcineurin-like phosphoesterase domain of ShfP homologs across bacteria is typically fused to domains involved in cell-cell adhesion, binding of extracellular matrix components such as polysaccharides and eDNA, as well as additional enzymatic domains (30), including catalytic domains of the alkaline phosphatase superfamily (Fig. 3A). Systematic investigation of the domain architectures of the secreted alkaline phosphatase superfamily members revealed that these domains are also fused to several noncatalytic domains known or predicted to be involved in adhesion, extracellular matrix, and eDNA binding. Furthermore, these alkaline phosphatase domains are also fused to other phosphoesterase and predicted phosphosugar-binding domains, such as the glycerophosphoryl diester phosphodiesterase and the synaptojanin-like phosphoesterase domain. This suggested that an alkaline phosphatase might catalyze the conversion of G1P to glycerol.

B. subtilis encodes two secreted alkaline phosphatases, PhoA and PhoB, and a related enzyme PhoD [which acts on WTA (48)]. Deletion of phoA or phoB partially suppressed the sporulation-inhibitory effect of glycerol and partially rescued the ΔshfA sporulation defect (Fig. 2E and fig. S2A), but deleting both phoA and phoB restored sporulation to levels similar to deletion of ltaSA (Fig. 2E). Thus, PhoA and, to a lesser extent, PhoB likely convert ShfP-generated G1P into glycerol. phoA lies near the glycerol metabolism operon (fig. S2B). Consistent with this model, examination of the subcellular localization of PhoA fused to green fluorescent protein (GFP) revealed a uniform localization of the enzyme at early time points after induction of sporulation (Fig. 3B, panels 1 to 3), followed by increased production and specific localization around the forespore and retention on mature spores (Fig. 3B, panels 4 to 7). This localization depended on the spore coat scaffold protein SpoIVA (49) (Fig. 3C), but not on ShfP, ShfA, or SpoVR (a sporulation protein whose gene abuts phoA; fig. S2, B and C). Similarly, the localization of ShfP did not depend on the presence of PhoA (fig. S2D). We therefore identify PhoA as a spore coat–associated alkaline phosphatase that partners with ShfP to produce extracellular glycerol from LTA degradation (Fig. 2B).

Deletion of shfA results in buildup of glycolytic intermediates that lowers intracellular pH

To identify the metabolic intermediates responsible for the sporulation-inhibitory activity of glycerol during sporulation, we performed liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS)–based metabolomic profiling of WT and ΔshfA, ΔshfP, and ΔshfA ΔshfP cells. Most metabolites were unchanged across strains (fig. S3), but several glycolytic intermediates (glucose 6-phosphate, fructose 6-phosphate, fructose 1,6-bisphosphate, and pyruvate) and malate and the nucleotide guanosine diphosphate accumulated specifically in the absence of ShfA (Fig. 4A). These metabolites returned to approximately WT levels in ΔshfA ΔshfP cells, correlating with suppression of the sporulation defect (Fig. 1C). Previous studies showed that excess phosphorylated intermediates can inhibit sporulation, likely due to their acidic character rather than structural specificity (50). We therefore hypothesized that the accumulation of these metabolites lowers cytoplasmic pH, disrupting enzymes essential for cortex synthesis.

We recently reported that the forespore surface resides in a nanoenvironment of reduced pH relative to the mother cell cytosol and that this acidification necessitates the SpoVK chaperone to stimulate the glycosyltransferase MurG, which synthesizes the peptidoglycan precursor lipid II (51). We reasoned that further acidification in ΔshfA cells might inhibit MurG and block cortex assembly. To test this, we used the pH-sensitive fluorescent reporter IpHluorin expressed either in the mother cell or fused to the forespore surface marker SpoVM (51). As expected, pH declined over time in both compartments, but ΔshfA cells exhibited a significantly greater drop in pH (Fig. 4, B to C) (51–53). This hyperacidification coincided with accumulation of uridine 5′-diphosphate (UDP)–N-acetylmuramic acid (MurNAc) (which inhibits the peptidoglycan biosynthetic enzyme MurAA) (54) and its derivative UDP-MurNAc pentapeptide (UDP-M5) (“Park’s nucleotide”) (Fig. 4D), consistent with impaired MurG activity. The MurAA-inhibiting activity of UDP-MurNAc likely explains the failure of cortex assembly in the shfA deletion mutant (34). Accumulation of fructose 6-phosphate (Fig. 4A), a precursor for UDP-MurNAc synthesis (55), is further consistent with this interpretation. Deletion of shfP partially restored normal metabolite levels (Fig. 4D) and suppressed the ΔshfA phenotype, thereby linking glycerol metabolism to peptidoglycan precursor accumulation.

Because MurG is central to the coordination of cortex and coat assembly, we asked whether restoring MurG activity by overproducing SpoVK, the chaperone that activates MurG under acidic conditions, could bypass the ΔshfA defect (4, 51, 56). Introduction of an additional chromosomal copy of spoVK rescued sporulation in ΔshfA cells (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, overexpression of murG largely corrected the sporulation-inhibitory effect of externally added glycerol during sporulation in ΔshfA ΔshfP cells (Fig. 4F). Last, deletion of shfP or phoA (or both), which blocks glycerol production, partially suppressed the severe sporulation defect of ΔspoVK (Fig. 4G). These data indicate that the requirement of SpoVK during sporulation arises partly from the need to counteract the acidifying effects of glycerol metabolism.

Together, these findings define the basis of how glycerol secreted by sporulating cells impedes sporulation in the very cells that release glycerol. ShfP- and PhoA-generated glycerol enters central metabolism to yield phosphorylated glycolytic intermediates that lower cytoplasmic pH. The resulting acid stress impairs MurG activity, which promotes the accumulation of inhibitory peptidoglycan precursors that block peptidoglycan synthesis and prevents cortex synthesis to arrest sporulation. This block is normally mitigated by ShfA, which protects sporulating cells by somehow buffering against the metabolic and structural consequences of self-produced glycerol (Fig. 4H).

Extracellular glycerol produced by ShfP during sporulation is required for biofilm formation

We next asked whether glycerol released via the ShfA-ShfP pathway provides a developmental benefit to the population. Although glycerol impedes sporulation in the glycerol-producing cells, it may serve as a signal that coordinates alternative fates within the population. We previously showed that glycerol delays sporulation in neighboring cells via the glycerol-sensing kinase KinD (29, 30). KinD not only inhibits Spo0A activation but also promotes entry into the biofilm pathway (29, 57), suggesting that extracellular glycerol might couple these developmental branches. Biofilm formation in B. subtilis is typically assayed in glycerol-containing MSgg medium (8, 26), where glycerol activates KinD via its CACHE domain (29, 58). We hypothesized instead that glycerol is a biologically relevant signal generated endogenously by sporulating cells. To test this, we used the undomesticated, biofilm-competent B. subtilis strain NCIB3610 [verifying that the isolate that we used harbored a functional swrA gene that is necessary for biofilm formation but is also subject to phase variation that can inactivate the gene (59, 60)] and evaluated growth in medium that support both sporulation and biofilm formation in the absence of exogenous glycerol. Among several media tested, lysogeny broth (10/10/10 formulation: 10 g of tryptone, 10 g of yeast extract, 10 g of NaCl/liter; hereafter simply “LB”) supported both processes, whereas DSM promoted sporulation but not biofilm formation and MSgg, which contains glycerol, severely reduced sporulation (Fig. 5, A, C, D, and H). In LB medium, deletion of shfA in the undomesticated strain caused a ∼103-fold reduction in sporulation, and deletion of shfP produced a milder (∼10-fold) defect; deleting shfP largely suppressed the sporulation defect of a ΔshfA strain (Fig. 5B), confirming that the ShfA-ShfP pathway functions similarly in the undomesticated strain as it does in the domesticated laboratory strain.

We next examined whether the ShfA-ShfP pathway contributes to biofilm formation. On LB agar, the undomesticated NCIB3610 strain formed characteristic wrinkled colonies with irregular peripheries after 48 hours, whereas mutants lacking epsA-O [which encodes the polysaccharide matrix operon (11)] produced smooth, flat colonies (Fig. 5C) (61). Deleting shfP, ltaSA, or sigG [the sigma factor governing shfP expression in the forespore (30)], all of which block glycerol release, similarly reduced colony wrinkling, as did deletion of shfA. Biofilm formation was also abolished in the absence of kinD, demonstrating that glycerol signaling through KinD is required for matrix production (62). On glycerol-containing MSgg agar, WT colonies displayed exaggerated wrinkling, and glycerol-deficient mutants permitted (albeit reduced) biofilm formation, consistent with the inclusion of glycerol in this medium (Fig. 5D). In contrast, on glycerol-free MSg medium, only the WT strain and the ΔshfP strain complemented with shfP grew appreciably, whereas all the other mutants failed to exhibit appreciable growth (Fig. 5F). Across multiple other media, we observed two distinct phenotypic outcomes. On LB made with the 10/5/10 formulation (10 g of tryptone, 5 g of yeast extract, 10 g of NaCl/liter), all strains produced smooth, flat colonies with reduced biofilm formation, but the colonies of glycerol-producing strains displayed a larger radius (Fig. 5E) (63). On TS (tryptic soy) agar, glycerol-producing strains exhibited robust motility behavior after a short incubation period (likely sliding motility; Fig. 5G) (64, 65) and exhibited a three-dimensional biofilm phenotype, whereas glycerol-deficient strains did not. No biofilm formation was detected on DSM, which favors sporulation but not biofilm development (Fig. 5H) (8). In liquid LB culture, the pattern was recapitulated: The WT strain and shfP-complemented strain formed robust pellicles at the air-liquid interface (evidenced by a thick, textured mat of bacterial growth at the liquid surface; Fig. 5I, arrows, and magnified images shown in fig. S4), whereas mutants lacking shfP, shfA, ltaSA, sigG, or kinD failed to form pellicles (evidenced by thin, wispy bacterial growth on the liquid surface; Fig. 5I and fig. S4) (66).

Together, these results demonstrate that biofilm formation in B. subtilis depends on the sporulation-derived ShfA-ShfP pathway and its glycerol product. The data are consistent with a model in which glycerol produced by sporulating cells acts as an extracellular signal sensed by KinD to trigger biofilm formation in neighboring cells, thereby establishing a developmental feedback loop in which one subpopulation directs another to build a multicellular structure.

Activation of the biofilm pathway is genetically, temporally, and spatially dependent on a subset of sporulating cells

To define the temporal and genetic relationship between sporulation and biofilm formation, we examined gene expression from two biofilm matrix promoters, eps and tapA [TapA is an extracellular biofilm matrix protein; the tapA operon also includes tasA, encoding the major biofilm fiber protein (67–69)], using lacZ reporter fusions in the WT strain and key mutants. Both promoters were activated in WT cells grown in LB medium but not in ΔshfP and ΔltaSA mutants (Fig. 6, A and B). Supplementing the medium with glycerol (normally absent in LB) restored promoter activity in ΔshfP cells, indicating that ShfP-generated glycerol drives biofilm gene expression. This activation required the glycerol-sensing kinase KinD, as evidenced by the failure of the ΔshfP ΔkinD strain to activate either promoter even in the presence of externally added glycerol (Fig. 6, A and B). Furthermore, deletion of the late sporulation sigma factor σG, which is responsible for shfP expression, abolished eps and tapA activation, similar to loss of the master regulator Spo0A (8, 21, 29). Thus, activation of biofilm genes requires both the late sporulation machinery and the KinD-dependent sensing of sporulation-derived glycerol.

To investigate this coupling directly, we monitored activation of the tapA promoter during pellicle formation using a fluorescent reporter. In cultures of the WT strain grown in the absence of exogenously added glycerol, PtapA activity appeared after ∼30 hours and peaked at ∼40 hours (Fig. 6C, blue). In contrast, ΔshfP cells failed to activate PtapA under these conditions (Fig. 6C), similar to the level of PtapA activation observed in LB 10/5/10 medium (Fig. 6C, black and gray), which does not promote robust biofilm formation (Fig. 5E). We therefore conclude that the activation of biofilm genes is genetically dependent on the late sporulation protein ShfP and the production of glycerol from LTA.

Because sporulation and biofilm formation are governed by high and low levels of phosphorylated Spo0A, respectively, these observations suggest that a subpopulation of cells with elevated Spo0A activity initiates sporulation, releasing glycerol that triggers biofilm formation in neighboring cells with lower levels of phosphorylated Spo0A. To test this, we simultaneously tracked sporulation and biofilm gene expression in pellicle-forming cultures using dual fluorescent reporters: GFP under the sporulation-specific PspoIIE promoter and mScarlet3 under biofilm-specific PtapA. Pellicles were disrupted at various time points, and the liberated cells were examined using fluorescence microscopy, which revealed a defined temporal sequence. Sporulation-specific gene expression appeared first (∼9% of cells at 8 hours), followed by biofilm gene activation at ∼26 hours [in cells mostly exhibiting chaining morphology (70)], which peaked around 40 to 50 hours as pellicles matured (Fig. 6D). As biofilms dispersed (90 to 160 hours), the fraction of biofilm-producing cells declined, while the sporulating subpopulation increased.

Together, these data indicate that sporulation precedes and instructs biofilm formation through the production of extracellular glycerol. While the increase in the number of sporulating cells detected after 90 hours (coincident with pellicle disruption) is consistent with the notion that cells previously activated for biofilm production can later initiate sporulation, the data as a whole indicate that the ShfP-PhoA pathway, activated late in sporulation, initially generates a metabolic signal sensed by KinD that redirects other cells toward biofilm matrix production.

To test the spatial dependence of biofilm-forming cells on sporulating cells that produce glycerol, we examined whether ΔshfP cells could be induced to form biofilms on solid medium when placed physically adjacent to WT cells. We therefore spotted, on LB 10/10/10 agar, ΔshfP cells harboring mScarlet3 under control of PtapA and surrounded this colony with spots of eight WT colonies (Fig. 7A) at a distance of ∼1 cm. We also spotted, at four distal locations near the periphery of the plate, ΔshfP PtapA-mScarlet3 ∼ 2.5 cm from the nearest WT colony. After 15 hours at 37°C, we observed activation of PtapA only in the ΔshfP colony that was surrounded by WT colonies but not in the colonies spotted further away at the periphery of the plate (Fig. 7C). In addition, the PtapA activation appeared preferentially at the periphery of the ΔshfP colony, closest to the surrounding WT colonies, rather than the center of the colony. As a control, ΔshfP PtapA-mScarlet3 cell colonies that were instead surrounded by eight ΔshfP colonies (Fig. 7B) failed to activate PtapA (Fig. 7D). We next monitored the morphology of the ΔshfP colony at the center of the plate to see if the surrounding WT colonies could eventually induce biofilm formation in the ΔshfP colony (to slow down this process, we shifted the plates to 22°C). At early time points, the WT colonies developed characteristic biofilm architectures, but the central ΔshfP colony did not (Fig. 7, E and G; inset). However, by 39 to 87 hours, the edges of the central ΔshfP colony developed raised ridges and more complex architecture [Fig. 7, I and K; coincident to where the Ptap-mScarlet3 was activated at the early time point (Fig. 7C)], whereas the ΔshfP colonies located at the periphery of the plate, further away from the WT colonies, failed to display similar architecture. This delay in biofilm formation is likely due to the time required for sufficient quantities of glycerol to diffuse from the neighboring WT colonies. In contrast, when the central ΔshfP colony was surrounded by ΔshfP cells, the central colony failed to develop any biofilm characteristics (Fig. 7, F, H, J, and L). These observations are therefore consistent with a model in which activation of biofilm formation occurs in a subset of cells that are physically close to another subset of cells that release glycerol.

On LB medium, which supports both sporulation and biofilm formation, we observed robust PspoIIE activation throughout the biofilm, both at the center and periphery of the colony (Fig. 8B, green, and movies S1 and S2). Distinct subpopulations of PtapA-positive cells were interspersed among the sporulating cells (Fig. 8, B and C; magenta), confirming that biofilm gene expression arises in a separate lineage. xz projections revealed that sporulating and biofilm-forming cells were distributed throughout the vertical depth of the biofilm, indicating that the two developmental programs occur alongside each other within a mixed population of differentiating cells.

In contrast, biofilms grown on glycerol-rich MSgg agar exhibited strong PtapA activation but weak PspoIIE expression (Fig. 8D, green; movies S3 and S4), consistent with the inhibitory effect of exogenous glycerol on sporulation (Fig. 5A). The few sporulating cells that did appear localized near the biofilm-air interface, distant from the glycerol-containing medium, as reported previously (8, 26). Thus, externally supplied glycerol suppresses sporulation while promoting biofilm gene expression, whereas in the absence of exogenous glycerol, sporulation-derived glycerol initiates biofilm formation.

The imaging data therefore reveal that sporulating and biofilm-forming cells occupy overlapping but distinct niches within the biofilm. In medium lacking added glycerol, a subset of cells must first enter sporulation to release glycerol, which then triggers biofilm formation in neighboring cells. The resulting biofilm therefore arises from a heterogeneous but interdependent population of differentiating cells in which a metabolite signal coordinates developmental specialization.

Together, we conclude that B. subtilis development proceeds through a branched, metabolically coupled program in which sporulating cells shape the fate of their neighbors (Fig. 1, right). The sequential actions of ShfP and PhoA degrade LTA to release glycerol, a metabolite that is both inhibitory to the producing cells and instructive to others. This extracellular glycerol acts as a morphogen sensed by KinD, directing a distinct subpopulation to initiate biofilm formation. Temporal and spatial analyses show that sporulation precedes and promotes biofilm development, establishing a developmental hierarchy in which metabolite-based communication coordinates multicellular organization.

DISCUSSION

Stationary phase responses in B. subtilis are traditionally viewed as a sequential progression of stress responses (motility, biofilm formation, and ultimately sporulation) driven by increasing levels of the phosphorylated master regulator Spo0A (11, 27, 28). This linear model posits that cells gradually escalate their developmental commitment, as environmental conditions deteriorate, culminating in spore formation (5, 11).

Our findings redefine this model by showing that, when B. subtilis is grown in medium that permits multiple stationary phase behaviors, B. subtilis development proceeds through a branched, metabolically coupled pathway in which sporulating cells play an instructive role in shaping population behavior. We previously described a population-level “altruistic” pathway in which sporulating cells release millimolar quantities of glycerol to delay sporulation in neighboring cells, thereby enhancing population heterogeneity (30), independent of the antagonistic “cannibalism” pathway (33, 72–74). Here, we demonstrate that glycerol release has a second, morphogen-like function, wherein it directs distinct developmental fates among genetically identical cells.

First, we show that sporulating cells generate extracellular glycerol by catabolizing LTA through the sequential actions of the phosphoesterase ShfP and the phosphatase PhoA. While this glycerol has the capacity to impede sporulation in the glycerol producers due to the accumulation of phosphorylated glycolytic intermediates that acidify the cytoplasm and block peptidoglycan synthesis, sporulating cells mitigate this inhibitory effect through the counteracting protein ShfA. LTA production is required for robust sporulation (75, 76). However, deleting shfP, which blocks the degradation of LTA during sporulation, did not result in a sporulation defect, suggesting that cell wall remodeling via the construction, then degradation, of LTA plays two distinct roles: initially enabling spore morphogenesis (76) and later producing a diffusible developmental cue.

Second, we show that the extracellular glycerol acts as a signaling molecule by triggering biofilm gene expression in neighboring cells and, under some conditions, sliding motility (Fig. 5G) (29, 30). Thus, instead of a unidirectional developmental cascade, stationary-phase behaviors arise from branching and feedback between subpopulations wherein a set of cells skips over behaviors such as sliding motility and biofilm formation and directly initiates sporulation early, likely at the very onset of stationary phase. These sporulating cells then generate a metabolic signal that both delays sporulation in some neighbors (30) and induces biofilm formation in others. Consistent with this branched model, mutants defective in sporulation fail to form biofilms, which is inconsistent with a linear model in which biofilm formation is a prerequisite for spore formation. The discrepancy with prior studies likely reflects the use of media that repress sporulation (LB supplemented with glucose or MSgg, which contains glycerol) (8, 21, 77) while stimulating biofilm formation, masking this interdependence. Using media that support both behaviors, we uncover a temporal sequence in which sporulating cells appear first, followed by a separate subpopulation of biofilm producers, and lastly a resurgence of sporulation as the biofilm disperses. Of course, further studies that employ the use of other growth conditions will reveal whether this sequence of events also occurs in more native settings such as on the roots of plants where B. subtilis naturally forms biofilms (57).

The shfA gene is restricted to Bacillota and is found primarily in species reported to sporulate (30, 78). In contrast, shfP is seen in most bacterial lineages and is widely distributed outside Bacillota. However, in Bacillota, most species that harbor a ShfA ortholog also harbor a ShfP ortholog. PhoA and related phosphatases are likewise seen in most bacterial lineages and show considerable overlap with the presence of ShfP, and in some cases, both proteins are even fused into a single polypeptide (Fig. 3A). This coconservation of ShfP and PhoA suggests that the synthesis, then subsequent catabolism, of LTA is a mechanism by which cells may transport glycerol from the cytosol to the extracellular milieu. Although we were unable to find studies that directly examined the levels of LTA in mature spores, indirect evidence suggests that mature spores do not harbor substantial amounts of LTA (79), consistent with a model in which the high levels of extracellular glycerol detected during sporulation requires the degradation of most of the LTA that is synthesized around the developing forespore. Although cells devoid of LTA are defective in sporulation (76, 80), the block in sporulation in these cells occurs early, suggesting that LTA likely stabilizes the cell envelope of the progenitor cell/sporangium during engulfment when extensive membrane remodeling takes place. In contrast, the continued production of LTA later during sporulation (at the forespore surface) may be required only to produce extracellular glycerol as a signaling molecule.

The use of a diffusible molecule to direct multiple developmental decisions to build a multicellular structure of differentiated cells mirrors the use of morphogens during embryonic development that triggers distinct developmental responses in neighboring cells in a concentration-dependent manner (81). While most morphogens are proteins, small molecules such as retinoic acid can also play this role. Glycerol, although a simple metabolite, fulfills key morphogen criteria, including production by a specialized subpopulation, diffusion through the extracellular milieu, and concentration-dependent effects on neighboring cells (30). Comparative genomic analyses suggest that ShfP and PhoA homologs are widespread across bacterial taxa and are often fused to domains that interact with extracellular matrix components and coupled with other enzymatic domains involved in phosphor-carbohydrate processing (Fig. 3A). These architectures imply that the capacity to liberate small molecules from biopolymers with phosphosugar units such as teichoic acid and eDNA and to use them as signals may be a common feature of bacterial multicellularity. A recent study reported that inclusion of glycerol in the medium of Streptomyces triggered “exploratory growth” behavior in addition to exospore formation (82). Because Streptomyces harbors teichoic acid in its cell wall (83) and the phylum Actinomycetota contains several Streptomyces spp. that harbor a ShfP homolog (30), this raises the tempting possibility that some Streptomyces species can also produce extracellular glycerol as a self-generated signaling molecule to modulate its behavior during development.

Glycerol-mediated communication differs from quorum sensing, which coordinates uniform responses across a population in response to cell density (84, 85). Instead, cell-cell communication using glycerol drives heterogeneity by partitioning cells into distinct developmental programs, enabling cooperative division of labor. This discovery raises the broader possibility that central metabolites that are typically considered intracellular may also serve extracellular signaling roles. For example, lactate produced by pathogenic or commensal bacteria can modulate the inflammatory properties of the innate immune response in a mammalian host (86). More recently, reports revealed that Staphylococcus aureus releases micromolar quantities of citrate into the culture media (87) and Enterococcus faecalis exports ornithine (88, 89). While these extracellular metabolites can be exploited as a nutrient by cocultured bacterial species, a potential self-serving function for the extracellular metabolites by the S. aureus or E. faecalis cells that release them is yet to be determined.

Our findings therefore suggest that B. subtilis multicellularity emerges from a branched developmental network in which sporulating cells generate other lineages through the release of a simple metabolite. More broadly, we propose that the evolution of bacterial multicellularity may have been driven not only by regulatory innovations but also by the repurposing of core metabolic pathways into systems of communication.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Strain construction and growth media

Strains used in this study (table S1) are otherwise isogenic derivates of the WT laboratory strain B. subtilis PY79 (90) or the undomesticated B. subtilis strain NCIB3610 obtained from L. Sonenshein (strain 1969, a.k.a. RL1765) (8), as indicated. Genes of interest were amplified by polymerase chain reaction to include either their native or heterologous promoter, as indicated, and cloned using Gibson Assembly kit (NEB) into integration vectors pDG1662 (for insertion into the amyE locus), pDP150 (for insertion into the thrC locus), or pSac-Cm (for insertion into the sacA locus) (91, 92). All plasmids were integrated into the B. subtilis PY79 chromosome by double recombination events at the specified ectopic locus. For integration in the NCIB3610 strain background, constructs were transduced using the SPP1 bacteriophage, as described previously (93). Strain TU335 was adapted from strain AHB98 (94); strain TU341 was adapted from strain FC5 (95). Plasmid construction was verified by DNA sequencing before chromosomal integration.

Cells were grown for routine manipulation on LB 10/10/10 (10 g of tryptone, 10 g of yeast extract, 10 g of NaCl/liter; KD Medical) in the presence of appropriate antibiotics as required for single colony isolation. Where indicated, for sporulation or biofilm formation assays, cells were grown in DSM, LB 10/5/10 (10 g of tryptone, 5 g of yeast extract, 10 g of NaCl/liter), tryptic soy broth [17 g of casein peptone (pancreatic), 3 g of soya peptone (papain digest), 5 g of NaCl, 2.5 g of dipotassium hydrogen phosphate, and 2.5 g of glucose per liter at pH 7.3], or MSgg [5mM potassium phosphate (at pH 7.0), 100 mM morpholinepropanesulfonic acid (pH 7.0), 2 mM MgCl2, 700 μM CaCl2, 50 μM MnCl2, 50 μM FeCl3, 1 μM ZnCl2, 2 μM thiamine, 0.5% glycerol, 0.5% glutamate, 50 μg ml−1 tryptophan, 50 μg ml−1 phenylalanine, of 50 μg ml−1 threonine] (62).

Sporulation efficiency assay

Sporulation efficiency (resistance to wet heat relative to WT) was assessed as previously described (96). Briefly, cells were grown in ∼2 ml of indicated medium for >24 hours at 37°C. Cultures were then subjected to 80°C for 30 min to kill nonsporulating cells and defective spores. Surviving cells were enumerated by serial dilution in sterile water, plating on LB agar, incubation at 37°C overnight, counting colony-forming units per milliliter, and reported relative to colony-forming units per milliliter obtained for a culture of WT grown in parallel.

Biofilm formation and imaging

Biofilm formation was assessed as previously described (29). Briefly, for biofilms on solid media, strains of the NCIB3610 strain background were grown on LB 10/10/10 plates overnight at 37°C. One colony was picked to inoculate 2 ml of LB 10/10/10 and grown for 4 hours at 37°C in a roller drum. A total of 2 μl of each strain was deposited on 1.5% agar plates made with the indicated medium. TS agar plates were incubated at 22°C for 12 hours; all other plates were incubated at 37°C for ∼15 hours. Plates were imaged using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System. For Fig. 7, 2 μl of the indicated strains that was grown in LB 10/10/10 at 37°C for 5 hours was spotted on LB 10/10/10 plates in the indicated arrangement. The plates were grown for ∼15 hours at 37°C, then further incubated at 22°C for an additional 24 to 72 hours, and imaged using Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System using Cy3 setting for mScarlet3 signal and white light. To image biofilms using iSIM, individual biofilms forming colonies were excised from an agar plate, excess agar was gently excised using a scalpel, and the colony was placed on a lysine-coated glass bottom 35-mm-diameter, high-precision 1.5 dish (Mattek, no. P35G-0.170-14-C) with the air-interface side of the biofilm facing the coverslip. iSIM imaging was conducted on a Nikon Ti body base integrated with a VT-iSIM (VisiTech International) module. Z-stacks of biofilm samples were acquired using a 60× oil immersion objective lens (Nikon NA 1.49) and sCMOS camera (ORCA-Quest, Hamamatsu). Lasers, cameras, stages, triggering, and microscopy are all controlled through Nikon NIS-Elements Imaging software. A 488-nm laser was used to excite GFP with a 525/50 band-pass filter. A 561-nm laser was used to excite mScarlet with a 605/52 band-pass filter. Images were displayed with Imaris (Bitplane 10.2.0) and ImageJ. For pellicle formation, 9 μl of cultures grown at 37°C for ∼5 hours in LB 10/10/10 was used to inoculate 9 ml of LB 10/10/10 in a Costar 6-Well Flat Bottom Ultra-Low Attachment Surface plate (Corning) and incubated at 32°C for the indicated times. Pellicles were imaged using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System.

Epifluorescence microscopy

Epifluorescence microscopy of B. subtilis strains was performed as previously described (97). Briefly, cells were induced to sporulate using the resuspension method in Sterlini-Mandelstam medium (98). Harvested cells were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline [containing FM4-64 (1 μg ml−1), where indicated, to visualize membranes] and placed on a glass bottom dish (Mattek) under a 1% agarose pad. Cells were visualized using a DeltaVision Core microscope system equipped with an environmental chamber at 22°C; images were captured with a Photometrics CoolSNAP HQ2 camera. Seventeen planes were acquired every 0.2 μm, and the data were deconvolved using the softWoRx software (GE Healthcare). Control experiments with strains that did not harbor a gfp or mScarlet3 fusion indicated that the level of fluorescence was well above the limited background fluorescence of the cells.

Supernatant harvesting

To collect conditioned cell-free supernatants, PY79 or isogenic mutant cultures were grown overnight in casein-hydrolysate (CH) medium at 22°C, shaking at 250 rpm. Cultures were then diluted 1:20 [to optical density at 600 nm (OD600) ∼ 0.1] in CH medium and grown at 37°C for 2 hours of shaking at 250 rpm. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in an equal volume of resuspension medium (supplemented with threonine at 80 μg ml−1 if the thrC locus disrupted) and grown at 37°C for 6 hours. Cultures were then centrifuged for 7 min at 7000g; the supernatants were collected and filtered through a 3000 Dalton MWCO Amicon Ultra-4 (Millipore) filter, and aliquots were stored at −80°C. To measure glycerol concentration, cell-free supernatants from the indicated strains were harvested after 6 hours of growth at 37°C in resuspension medium and quantified using the Free Glycerol Assay Kit (Abcam, Fluorometric, High Sensitivity, Ab174092) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Peptidoglycan accumulation

Quantification of Park’s nucleotide was performed as described previously (51, 56). Briefly, cells were induced to sporulate by resuspension in A + B medium, harvested at t = 5.5 hours, washed thrice with ice cold 0.9% NaCl, resuspended in a final volume of 100 μl 0.9% NaCl, and boiled for 5 min to extract soluble peptidoglycan precursors. Insoluble material was pelleted by centrifugation at 21,000g for 5 min, and the resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-μm pore-size filter. Detection and quantification of Park’s nucleotide were performed by LC-MS analysis using a UPLC system (Waters) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (130-Å pore size, 1.7-μm particle size, 2.1 mm × 150 mm; Waters) coupled to a Xevo G2-XS QTof mass spectrometer (Waters). Chromatographic separation of the soluble fraction was achieved using a linear gradient from 0.1% formic acid in water to 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile over 18 min at 45°C. The QTof instrument was operated in positive ionization mode, and detection of UDP-M5 was performed in the untargeted MSe mode. The MS parameters were set as follows: capillary voltage, 3 kV; source temperature, 120°C; desolvation temperature, 350°C; sample cone voltage, 40 V; cone gas flow, 100 liters hour−1; and desolvation gas flow, 500 liters hour−1. Data acquisition and processing was performed using the UNIFI software (Waters). To quantify the Park’s nucleotide, its calculated [M + 2H]2+ ion of mass/charge ratio of 597.68 was extracted from the total ion chromatogram, and the corresponding peak in the resulting extracted ion chromatogram was integrated to give a peak area.

LC-MS quantification of metabolites

Cells from strains PY79, CW202, TD517, and TD507 were grown in CH medium to OD600 ∼ 0.5 and induced to sporulate via the resuspension method as described above. Metabolite extraction was performed as described previously (99). Briefly, 10 ml of culture was sampled and harvested by filtration through polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane (Sartorius) at time points before and after resuspension in A + B medium. Pellets on the PTFE membranes were soaked in 3 ml of extraction solvent mix [on ice 50:50 (v/v) chloroform/water] and then vortexed to quench metabolism and extract metabolites. Cell extracts were centrifuged at 5000g for 10 min to remove the organic phase and then centrifuged at 20,000g for 10 min to remove cell debris. Samples were frozen at 80°C if not analyzed immediately. Samples were analyzed using LC-MS, and the metabolites were quantified as described previously (99, 100) using an high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)–MS system consisting of a Vanquish UHPLC system linked to electrospray ionization (negative mode) to a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated in full-scan mode to detect targeted metabolites based on their accurate masses. LC was performed on an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (1.7 mm, 2.1 × 100 mm; Waters). Total run time was 30 min with a flow rate of 0.2 ml min−1, using solvent A [97:3 (v/v) water/methanol, 10 mM tributylamine, and 10 mM acetic acid] and acetonitrile as solvent B. The gradient was as follows: 0 min, 5% B; 2.5 min, 5% B; 19 min, 100% B; 23.5 min 100% B; 24 min, 5% B; 30 min, 5% B. Quantification of metabolites from raw LC-MS data was performed using the MAVEN software (101). Normalized ion count was defined and calculated as the ion count per OD600 per unit volume (5 ml) of the culture.

Intracellular pH measurements

The intracellular pH measurements of the indicated strains were carried out as previously described (51). Briefly, strains SC765, SC767, TU317, and TU318 were induced to sporulate by the resuspension method. A total of 200 μl of each culture was then placed into individual wells of black-walled 96-well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and placed in a Synergy H1 plate reader (BioTek) and grown at 37°C, with continuous shaking. Fluorescence signal (emission at 510 nm, with excitation at 390 nm and 470 nm) was measured every 15 min, and the 390/470 ratios were calculated. To convert the 390/470 ratios to pH values, we constructed a calibration curve as described previously (52). Briefly, strain SC777, which produces isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG)–inducible Iphluorin, was grown in casein-hydrolysate (CH) medium with 1 mM IPTG and then induced to sporulate by resuspension in A + B medium containing 10 mM tris at pH 7.5, 1 μM nigericin, and 1 μM valinomycin (to equilibrate external and internal pH) that was adjusted to different pH values ranging from 5 to 8.5 using 5 N NaOH or 6 N HCl. Fluorescence signals of cells grown at different pH values were measured as described above, and 390/470 ratios were calculated and plotted as a function of pH. The resulting plot was fit with a linear curve with equation y = 0.7868x – 2.638, where y represents 390/470 ratio and x represents pH.

β-Galactosidase activity

β-galactosidase assays were conducted as previously described (29, 102). Briefly, 2-ml cultures of NCIB3610 in LB 10/10/10 were inoculated and incubated at 37°C for 5.5 hours, after which the OD600 was measured for each culture. One milliliter of each culture was centrifuged, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 1 ml of Z buffer (40 mM NaH2PO4, 60 mM Na2HPO4, 1 mM MgSO4, 10 mM KCl, and 38 mM β-mercaptoethanol). A total of 20 μl of lysozyme (10 mg ml−1) was added, and cultures were incubated at 37°C for 15 min. A total of 200 μl of ONPG [2-nitrophenyl β-d-galactopyranoside; 4 mg ml−1 (Sigma-Aldrich)] was added to each culture, and the color change was monitored, upon which the reaction within each culture were stopped via the addition of 500 μl of 1 M Na2CO3; taking note of the time in minutes between adding the ONPG and stopping the reaction. A420 of each reaction was measured, and the resulting Miller units were calculated and plotted using the following equation: 1000 × A420/[time (min) × volume (ml) × A600].

Fluorescent reporter assay

At least three independent overnight cultures of the reporter strains TU365 and TU366 and nonfluorescent control strain NCIB3610 were grown overnight in LB 10/5/10 medium. The overnight cultures were then diluted in 200 μl of LB 10/10/10 or 10/5/10 medium to OD600 = 0.1 in a 96-well (black wall, clear bottom) microplate (PerkinElmer). The plate was then incubated at 32°C without shaking in a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader to record mScarlet3 fluorescence, using the 588 excitation and 633 emission settings, and OD600 measurements every 1 hour. The recorded fluorescence readings for strains TU365 and TU366 grown in each medium type were normalized to that of strain NCIB3610 at each time point and plotted.

Computational analysis of ShfP and PhoA

Homologs of ShfP and PhoA were identified using sequence profile searches performed with the PSI-BLAST program. The searches were run against a custom database of 13,709 complete prokaryotic proteomes. Profile-profile searches were conducted using HHpred program with multiple alignments to derive the query hidden Markov model. They were run against 1) PDB; 2) Pfam; 3) an in-house collection of profiles; the significance of the hits was assessed using the probability percentage and P value of the HHpred hits. Structure prediction was performed using AlphaFold3. Domain architectures were obtained using a combination of profile searches against Pfam and in-house profiles. They were visually rendered (Fig. 3A) using the TikZ system for LaTeX. The membrane topology was established using deep learning with the DeepTMHMM program (103) and visualized using MembraneFold (104).
