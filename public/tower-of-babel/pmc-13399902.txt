Healthcare utilization trends for adolescents and young adults with moderate and severe congenital heart disease
Authors: Victoria H. Yatsenko, Sarah A. Mathena, Jennifer G. Andrews, Brent J. Barber, Andrew W. Hoyer, Kenneth A. Fox, Omar Meziab, Claudia E. Guerrero, Scott E. Klewer, Michael D. Seckeler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Improved congenital heart disease (CHD) care allows >90% of patients to live into adulthood. Young adult CHD patients experience challenges managing their cardiac conditions and other comorbidities that may increase healthcare utilization. There are limited data on hospitalizations of adolescents and young adults with CHD, specifically during the transition from pediatric to adult care. We hypothesized increased healthcare utilization for CHD patients transitioning from adolescent to adult care.Materials and Methods:Retrospective review of the Vizient® Clinical Data Base (national, administrative database) for admissions from October 2019 to December 2023, aged 10–29 years with International Classification of Diseases-10 codes for moderate or severe CHD, stratified into age categories. Data included demographics, hospital outcomes, insurance status, admission through the emergency department (ED), and costs. Comparisons were made between the age groups.Results:There were 16,284 admissions during the study period. As age increased, the proportion of female admissions rose, with a higher prevalence of white admissions and a lower prevalence of Hispanic admissions. There was a decline in public insurance coverage, while rates of uninsured status and ED admissions increased with age. Mortality rates were highest at 15–19 years and 25–29 years.Conclusions:Despite increased admissions from the ED, complications, and mortality, healthcare costs declined between the younger and older age groups. There were higher rates of uninsured status and ED admissions for hospitalized young adults with CHD transitioning from pediatric to adult care. However, overall hospital costs decreased. More studies are needed to guide improvements in care during this at-risk time.

INTRODUCTION

Between 2000 and 2010, the prevalence of adults with CHD grew by 57%.[1] Young adult congenital heart disease (CHD) patients are challenged by managing their own care, leading to loss to follow-up and poor adherence to clinical guidelines. They also face increased risk for other comorbidities that may increase healthcare resource utilization, including cardiac complications such as arrhythmia, endocarditis, need for reintervention, pulmonary hypertension, and heart failure. CHD patients require closer follow-up than those without CHD due to increased risks for cerebrovascular disease, diabetes, and other systemic complications, including neurocognitive disorders, liver and kidney dysfunction, and cancer. These comorbidities highlight the importance of optimizing, not only the quantity, but also the quality of life for this complex patient population, as well as the potential for increased economic burden associated with it.[1] This also emphasizes the importance of training cardiologists specialized in congenital heart care.[2]

Given the increasing size and complexity of the population of patients with CHD aging into adolescence and young adulthood, there is a need for contemporary national data that demonstrates current healthcare utilization patterns in comparison to patterns previously reported by Lu et al.[3] This study analyzed inpatient admissions and costs among patients with CHD from adolescence to adulthood (10–29 years old) in California and included all the severity of CHD, including simple shunts. They found that young adults used fewer medical resources with less costly admissions.[3]

The goal of this study was to use a national administrative database to report contemporary outcomes for hospitalized adolescents and young adults with CHD and to compare them to the previous report to assess potential improvements in outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We conducted a retrospective review using the Vizient® Clinical Data Base, a national administrative database for admissions from October 2019 to December 2023. Inclusion criteria included individuals aged 10–29 years of any sex, race, or ethnicity with International Classification of Disease-10 codes for moderate or severe CHD as defined by the 2018 Adult Congenital Heart Disease guidelines.[45] The study was approved by the University of Arizona Institutional Review Board (STUDY00004654).

Data collected included demographics (age, sex, and race/ethnicity), hospital outcomes (length of stay [LOS], complication rates, in-hospital mortality, and costs), and insurance status (public, private, uninsured, other, and unknown). Frequent admission through the emergency department (ED) has been reported as a potential indicator of limited quality of, or access to, regular outpatient care.[6] To assess this, we also queried to quantify the proportion of admissions through the ED using a predefined flag in the Vizient database, similar to Lu et al.’s 2014 study.[3]

Admissions were stratified into age categories (10–14, 15–19, 20–24, and 25–29 years) to allow for comparisons with reported findings by Lu et al. Age strata comparisons were made using analysis of variance for normally distributed data and χ2 for categorical data; the Bonferroni test was used for post hoc analysis. P < 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS, version 29 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, USA).

RESULTS

There were 16,284 admissions during the study period; demographics, hospital outcomes, and costs are shown in Table 1. With increasing age, there was an increase in female admissions, an increasing prevalence of white admissions, and a decreasing prevalence of Hispanic admissions. There was a decrease in public insurance and an increase in uninsured status and ED admissions with increasing age. Complications increased between 10–14 and 25–29 years old. The mortality rate was highest at 15–19 years and 25–29 years. Despite increased ED visits, complications, and mortality, costs decreased between the younger age groups and the older age groups.

DISCUSSION

This review of a national administrative database presents the latest findings for hospitalized adolescents and young adults with moderate and severe CHD. This expands Lu et al.’s work, which presented only data from California, to a national level and also updates healthcare utilization for the current era.

Current guidelines recommend specialized care for adults with CHD.[7] There have been major improvements, with a 34.5% decline in CHD mortality between 1990 and 2017, but niche populations such as adolescents and young adults are often overlooked.[7] Lapses in care during the transition of care from pediatric to adult subspecialists are associated with increases in morbidity and mortality.[8] A previous study found 37.6% of 16–17-year olds were lost to follow-up,[9] and our current study found a peak mortality rate between 15 and 19-year old. We also found a second peak in mortality at ages 25–29, another key transition point with the loss of dependent status. Patients who are lost to follow-up are 3.1 times more likely to need urgent cardiac intervention.[10] Patients who maintain regular care report receiving education about their diagnoses, and those who do transition successfully have superior follow-up.[9] In addition, as the adult population with CHD increases, more adult CHD cardiologists will need to be trained to manage this rapidly growing population.[2]

Socioeconomic costs of CHD are high, both regarding quality of life and resource utilization.[1] Patients have lifelong complex medical needs that reduce employability and increase healthcare utilization, along with high medical costs and gaps in insurance coverages.[111213] We found costs decreased as age increased, similar to what has been previously reported.[3] This observation has been attributed to the reduced need for cardiac procedures (catheterizations and operations) as individuals with CHD progress through pubertal growth and into young adulthood, and while this may be the case for our study population as well, we were unable to provide specific data in our dataset to directly support this.[3] In addition, our data showed an increasing proportion of uninsured patients with age, which has not changed since previous studies.[8] Conflicting data exists on the lack of insurance coverage associated with loss to follow-up.[89] With no increase in costs and no definitive correlation between insurance and follow-up care, insurance, or lack thereof, may not be the problem during this period of young adulthood. In addition, our data show an increase in ED admissions with age. Previous data suggest these admissions occurred regardless of insurance status.[3] Interestingly, while apparently high, the rates of ED admissions that we identified were much lower than those reported by Lu et al. for CHD patients (20.6%–53.9%) and the general population (40.8%–61.8%).[3] ED admissions are undesirable and are felt to reflect fragmented care with a lack of coordination and lost opportunities for preventative healthcare maintenance.[614]

The current study examined national data to understand healthcare utilization among adolescents and young adults with CHD, similar to work done previously using data from California.[315] Despite concerted efforts to improve patient and family education on the need for lifelong care in CHD, our data show similar trends to a decade ago. We did find increased insurance coverage among the older age groups (20–29 years), possibly attributed to the Affordable Care Act and the ability to stay on parental insurance until age 26 years old.[13] The better coverage/access to care among those age groups could explain the decreased ED admissions over the past decade. There continues to be decreases in insurance coverage and increased ED admissions for adolescents during the transition to adulthood, leaving the question of how we can further improve care during this at-risk time to close that gap.

Like all retrospective database studies, there are potential limitations to the conclusions we can draw. There is a chance for erroneous entry of diagnostic codes. In addition, because of the deidentified nature of the data, we were unable to track patterns of care for specific individuals over time. We were also unable to account for potential social confounders such as patient and parent education level, employment status, or family support systems, as these are not included in the national data. Despite these limitations, this is one of the largest contemporary datasets of this complex medical population and provides important insights to stimulate and guide future, more detailed efforts.

CONCLUSIONS

We have provided a contemporary review of outcomes for hospitalized adolescents and young adults with CHD during the transition to adult care. Our findings remain similar to those from more than a decade ago, with minimal changes in healthcare utilization trends. This highlights the importance of continued work to understand and care for this growing population, as well as the need to grow and maintain a workforce to provide their care.
