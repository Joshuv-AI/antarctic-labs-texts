Neuroimmune Crosstalk in Psoriasis: Mechanisms and Therapeutic Implications
Authors: Hanlin Gao, Yi Fang, Yue Zhang, Tianyi Xie, Zhi Chen, Gang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory skin disorder characterized by keratinocyte hyperproliferation, dermal vascular remodeling, and dense immune cell infiltration. While the conventional immunopathological model emphasizes the IL-23/Th17 axis and aberrant T-cell responses, growing evidence highlights the central role of neuroimmune crosstalk in the initiation, amplification, and persistence of disease. This review systematically dissects the cellular and molecular mechanisms underpinning neuroimmune interactions in psoriasis, focusing on the dynamic interplay between peripheral nerve fibers, keratinocytes, and immune cells. Key neuropeptides—such as calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), substance P (SP), nerve growth factor (NGF), and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP)/PACAP—emerge as critical mediators that activate proinflammatory signaling cascades and perpetuate a positive feedback loop involving IL-23, IL-17, and other cytokines. Concurrently, neurotransmitters including norepinephrine (NE), acetylcholine (ACh), and dopamine (DA) modulate dendritic cell activation, Th17 polarization, and epidermal inflammation via adrenergic, cholinergic, and dopaminergic pathways. Importantly, both central and peripheral nervous systems are implicated in neuroinflammatory sensitization, with IL-17 A, IL-1β, and TNF-α disrupting neuronal homeostasis and contributing to pruritus, pain, and stress-induced relapse. We further summarize emerging therapeutic strategies targeting the neuroimmune axis—such as TRPV1 antagonists, botulinum neurotoxins, NK1R inhibitors, and vagus nerve stimulation—which offer promising avenues for personalized and mechanism-based interventions. By reframing psoriasis as a neuroimmune disorder, this review provides new conceptual insights into disease heterogeneity and points toward innovative treatment paradigms.Keywords: Psoriasis, Neuroimmune crosstalk, Neuropeptides, TRPV1, IL-17, Pruritus

Introduction

Psoriasis is a common chronic inflammatory skin disorder affecting approximately 2–3% of the global population [1].It is clinically characterized by hyperproliferation of keratinocytes (KCs), epidermal thickening, dermal vascular dilation, and massive infiltration of immune cells [2–4].The classical pathogenic paradigm primarily involves dendritic cell (DC) activation, polarization of Th1/Th17 cells, and sustained activation of the IL-23/IL-17 axis, collectively contributing to a chronic inflammatory microenvironment [5–8](Fig. 1).However, recent studies have highlighted a previously underappreciated role of the nervous system in both the onset and relapse of psoriasis. Neuropeptides and neurotransmitters released from cutaneous nerve endings can directly modulate skin immune homeostasis [9, 10].For instance, neurogenic factors such as Substance P (SP), calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), and nerve growth factor (NGF) have been implicated in inflammatory cascades, promoting T cell recruitment and keratinocyte activation [11–14].

Pathogenesis of psoriasis. (1) In the pathogenesis of psoriasis, keratinocytes, upon external stimulation, release self-derived nucleotides that form complexes with the antimicrobial peptide LL-37. These complexes activate plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) to produce interferon-α (IFN-α) and interferon-β (IFN-β), or directly promote the maturation and activation of myeloid dendritic cells (mDCs). Activated DCs subsequently secrete interleukin-12 (IL-12) and interleukin-23 (IL-23), which induce and activate specific T helper cell subsets, including Th1, Th17, and Th22 cells. These T cells secrete various proinflammatory cytokines, such as IFN-γ, IL-17, IL-22, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), collectively driving a cascade of inflammatory responses. This ultimately results in excessive proliferation of epidermal keratinocytes, epidermal thickening, erythema, and both dermal vascular dilation and neovascularization—hallmarks of the clinical and histopathological features of psoriasis

Notably, increased nerve fiber density and sensory nerve remodeling are frequently observed in psoriatic lesions, correlating positively with local inflammation. This suggests that the nervous system may act not only as a responder but also as a potential initiator of psoriatic pathology [15, 16]. Concurrently, neurotransmitters such as norepinephrine, released through sympathetic nervous system and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activation, can modulate immune cell functions and aggravate inflammatory responses [17, 18]. In addition, environmental triggers such as psychological stress, pain, and cold exposure frequently precipitate psoriasis flares via neural pathways, further supporting the central role of neuroimmune crosstalk in disease progression [19]. Although preliminary evidence has revealed this cross-regulatory mechanism, its precise molecular underpinnings and clinical implications remain to be fully elucidated [20].

Therefore, a comprehensive investigation of the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying neuroimmune interactions in psoriasis, including the identification of key regulatory mediators and potential therapeutic targets, is of critical importance for advancing translational research in this field. This review focuses on the interplay between the nervous and immune systems in psoriasis, emphasizing neuronal–immune signaling, the contribution of neurogenic inflammation to lesion development, and emerging neuro-targeted therapeutic strategies. Our aim is to provide a theoretical framework and practical insights for future personalized and mechanism-driven approaches to psoriasis treatment.

Neuroimmune Interface in Skin Physiology

As the largest sensory organ of the human body, the skin harbors a highly intricate neuroimmune network. Its functions extend beyond serving as a physical barrier to include critical roles in sensory transmission and immune surveillance [21, 22]. The cutaneous nervous system comprises diverse types of nerve fibers and ganglia, primarily including sensory fibers (C-fibers and Aδ-fibers) and the autonomic nervous system (sympathetic and parasympathetic branches) [23–25]. These fibers are widely distributed throughout the epidermis and dermis, with a particularly dense network of nerve endings forming in the dermal papillary layer [26, 27]. These nerve endings release a variety of neurotransmitters and neuropeptides. Sensory fibers, in particular, secrete neurogenic factors such as CGRP, SP, and NGF [15, 28, 29]. These neural signals interact with KCs and immune cells to form a complex signaling network that regulates cutaneous homeostasis and immune responses.

Keratinocytes, the predominant cell type in the epidermis, occupy the majority of its layers and are often in direct contact or close proximity with nerve fibers [30]. In addition to serving as a physical barrier, KCs express neuropeptide receptors—such as the SP receptor NK-1R and the CGRP receptor CRLR—enabling them to sense neurogenic factors released by nearby nerve fibers [31–33]. Upon neuropeptide stimulation, KCs have been shown to secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1α, IL-6, and IL-8, thereby amplifying inflammation through activation and degranulation of immune cells such as mast cells [12]. Moreover, KCs can secrete neurotrophic factors such as NGF, which promote the proliferation and remodeling of local nerve fibers, further reinforcing the reciprocal dependence between the nervous system and keratinocytes [34].

Immune cells are also widely distributed in the skin, including dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, mast cells, and various lymphocyte subsets, which are primarily located beneath the basal layer of the epidermis and within the dermis, where they closely interact with nerve fibers. In contrast, Langerhans cells (LCs) are specifically localized within the suprabasal layers of the epidermis, where they extend dendritic processes between keratinocytes to exert their antigen-presenting function [35, 36]. DCs and LCs are frequently situated near nerve endings, facilitating rapid immune responses to neuronal cues through spatially coordinated interactions [37]. For example, SP and CGRP released from sensory fibers significantly enhance the migratory and antigen-presenting capacities of DCs, as well as promote LCs migration to regional lymph nodes, initiating antigen-specific immune responses. Mast cells, often localized near nerve fibers, respond to neurotransmitter stimulation by releasing inflammatory mediators such as histamine and proteases, thereby rapidly mediating neurogenic inflammation [38].

Thus, the spatially coordinated distribution and molecular interactions among nerve fibers, keratinocytes, and immune cells constitute a finely tuned homeostatic regulatory network in healthy skin. This network ensures rapid responses to external stimuli and provides the anatomical and physiological foundation for understanding neuroimmune dysregulation under pathological conditions. A deeper understanding of the architecture and function of this intricate network is essential for elucidating the pathogenesis of psoriasis and other inflammatory dermatoses(Fig. 2).

Schematic diagram of the cutaneous neuroimmune regulatory circuit. (2) The skin harbors a complex neuroimmune regulatory circuit in which immune cells secrete cytokines, chemokines, and lipid mediators that activate receptors and ion channels on sensory neurons, eliciting sensory responses such as itch and pain. Conversely, neuropeptides released from peripheral nerve endings—such as substance P (SP), calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP)—can modulate immune cell activation, migration, and cytokine release, thereby shaping the local inflammatory milieu. These neurogenic factors can also act directly on cutaneous and vascular tissues, triggering inflammation, enhancing vascular permeability, and resulting in tissue edema. Pathogens can directly stimulate nociceptors or indirectly activate peripheral nerves by inducing immune cells to release proinflammatory mediators, thus initiating a positive feedback amplification loop. Activation of pruriceptors leads to scratching behavior, which damages keratinocytes and stimulates the release of thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), thereby further amplifying the inflammatory response. In terms of immune polarization, nociceptors primarily mediate Th1- and Th17-type immune responses, while pruriceptors tend to promote Th2-skewed immunity, playing pivotal regulatory roles in various inflammatory skin disorders

Neurogenic Inflammation and Immune Regulation in Psoriasis

As a prototypical immune-mediated chronic skin disease, psoriasis has long been studied primarily in the context of aberrant activation and imbalance within cytokine networks of the immune system. However, emerging evidence in recent years has redirected attention toward the interdisciplinary crosstalk between the immune and nervous systems [39–41]. In particular, the concept of neurogenic inflammation has introduced a novel theoretical framework and research paradigm for understanding the onset, progression, and recurrence of psoriasis and other chronic inflammatory diseases [42]. Substantial evidence indicates that neuropeptides and neurotransmitters released from cutaneous nerve terminals not only initiate and amplify early inflammatory responses but also precisely modulate immune cell functions and keratinocyte activity, thereby establishing a complex neuroimmune positive feedback loop [12, 43–45]. Evidence indicates that neuro–immune crosstalk in psoriasis is not mediated by a single sensory neuron type but instead involves a complex network of multiple subpopulations. TRPV1⁺ neurons release neuropeptides—particularly CGRP—that drive dermal dendritic cells to produce IL-23, thereby amplifying IL-23/IL-17 axis–mediated inflammation [46, 47]. In TRPV1-deficient mice or following denervation of TRPV1⁺ fibers, cutaneous IL-1β and other pro-inflammatory cytokines are markedly reduced, suggesting that additional neuropeptides, such as SP, may contribute to the amplification of inflammatory circuits, although their mechanisms remain unclear [48, 49]. By contrast, CGRP⁺ neurons are implicated in vasodilation, immune cell recruitment, and the regulation of cutaneous hemodynamics [50]. Moreover, the potential roles of atypical subsets, such as NPY⁺ neurons, remain under investigation [51]. Although the exact contributions of distinct sensory neuron subsets to psoriasis remain incompletely defined, accumulating evidence suggests that these subpopulations may exert non-redundant regulatory roles in disease onset and maintenance.Therefore, elucidating the cellular and molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying neurogenic inflammation in psoriasis holds significant promise for advancing fundamental research, while also opening new avenues for clinical translation and therapeutic innovation.

Neuropeptide-Mediated Inflammatory Cascade Reaction

Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide

Calcitonin gene-related peptide is a 37-amino-acid neuropeptide primarily synthesized by sensory neurons and broadly expressed throughout both the central and peripheral nervous systems [52]. As a key neuropeptide released by cutaneous sensory neurons, CGRP has garnered increasing attention for its role in the neuroimmune regulation of psoriasis [14, 53, 54]. Studies have demonstrated a marked upregulation of CGRP expression in psoriatic lesions, which strongly correlates with disease severity [55]. Peng et al. reported that CGRP promotes both mRNA and protein expression of IL-23 in skin tissue via the PDK1–Rsk signaling pathway, suggesting that it may directly activate the IL-23/IL-17 axis and contribute to the pathogenesis of psoriasis [56]. IL-23 is a pivotal proinflammatory cytokine whose elevated expression enhances the inflammatory milieu and promotes the release of IL-17 A and IL-22 by Th17 and γδT cells, thereby establishing a positive feedback loop that accelerates psoriatic lesion development [6, 57, 58].] In imiquimod (IMQ)-induced murine models of psoriasis, CGRP expression in the dorsal root ganglia is rapidly upregulated following IMQ application and is closely associated with characteristic psoriatic inflammation [49, 54, 59]. Notably, sensory denervation in these models leads to a significant reduction in IL-23 expression and alleviates psoriasiform skin inflammation, further confirming the critical role of CGRP-mediated neuroimmune interactions [49]. Moreover, topical administration of exogenous CGRP to denervated skin restores IL-23 expression and markedly enhances local inflammation, underscoring the immunostimulatory capacity of CGRP in psoriatic pathophysiology [60].

TRPV1 is a sensory neuron-specific ion channel widely expressed in the skin, primarily responsible for the transmission of pain and itch sensations [61]. TRPV1 is sensitive not only to thermal stimuli but also to a range of endogenous and exogenous activators, including capsaicin, acidic pH, and various inflammatory mediators [62]. In skin disorders such as psoriasis, TRPV1 activation is regarded as a critical mechanism underlying the initiation of neurogenic inflammation [12]. Upon activation, TRPV1 channels trigger the release of large amounts of CGRP from peripheral nerve endings, increasing vascular permeability and immune cell infiltration, thereby exacerbating itch and inflammatory responses in psoriatic lesions [61]. In an imiquimod (IMQ)-induced psoriasiform mouse model, Lee et al. demonstrated that TRPV1-expressing sensory neurons release CGRP upon IMQ stimulation, which in turn drives the IL-23/IL-17 inflammatory axis and worsens cutaneous pathology [63]. As such, the TRPV1–CGRP axis is considered a pivotal and non-negligible signaling pathway in the inflammatory pathogenesis of psoriasis. Recent studies have shown that pharmacological inhibition of TRPV1 activity, using agents such as Resolvin D3 (RvD3), significantly reduces CGRP release and effectively alleviates skin inflammation and pruritus [64]. These findings further support the therapeutic potential of targeting the TRPV1–CGRP signaling axis in psoriasis management, highlighting neuropeptides and their associated receptors as promising targets for future clinical intervention.

Substance P

Substance P is an 11-amino-acid neuropeptide primarily synthesized by sensory neurons [65]. Within the nervous system, SP is predominantly released from sensory nerve fibers and plays a key role in the transmission of pain and inflammatory signals [66]. In psoriatic lesions, both SP and its primary receptor—neurokinin-1 receptor (NK1R)—are markedly upregulated, a change that closely correlates with disease severity [67]. As a prototypical G protein–coupled receptor (GPCR), NK1R, upon binding to SP, activates intracellular signaling pathways such as phospholipase C (PLC) and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), thereby modulating keratinocyte proliferation, differentiation, and survival, and further contributing to the inflammatory response [68].

Studies have shown that Substance P initially acts on NK1R expressed on cutaneous vascular endothelial cells, activating downstream signaling cascades that induce nitric oxide (NO) release, thereby promoting vasodilation and increased vascular permeability [69]. In addition, SP directly activates mast cells through Mas-related G protein–coupled receptors, namely Mrgprb2 in mice and MRGPRX2 in humans, which mediate degranulation and neurogenic inflammation [70, 71] Concurrently, SP activates mast cells, triggering their degranulation and the release of inflammatory mediators such as histamine, which in turn induces intense pruritus—one of the major clinical burdens in psoriasis patients [72]. Moreover, SP facilitates the recruitment and activation of inflammatory cells, including neutrophils and macrophages, which subsequently release proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-17 and TNF-α, thereby amplifying local inflammation and accelerating psoriatic progression [73]. Notably, Zhang et al. demonstrated that SP enhances the expression of co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86 on the surface of DCs, promoting their maturation and antigen-presenting capacity [44]. This process significantly strengthens the immunological crosstalk between DCs and T cells, drives Th1 and Th17 differentiation, and reinforces immune activation through elevated production of IL-12 and IL-23 [44]. Given the critical role of SP in the pathogenesis of psoriasis, recent efforts have been directed toward developing specific inhibitors targeting SP and its receptor NK1R [74, 75]. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase II trial involving 204 patients with mild to moderate plaque psoriasis, daily oral administration of Serlopitant—a selective NK1R antagonist—led to a statistically significant yet modest reduction in psoriasis-associated pruritus, indicating that NK1R blockade alone may not fully account for the complex mechanisms of itch and that other mediators are likely to play major roles [75]. In addition, treatment with excimer light therapy in both active and stable psoriatic lesions was shown to effectively reduce tissue levels of SP, suggesting that its therapeutic efficacy may, in part, stem from the attenuation of neurogenic inflammation [76].

Nerve Growth Factor

Nerve growth factor is a pivotal neurotrophic factor that exerts its effects by binding to its high-affinity receptor TrkA and low-affinity receptor p75NTR, thereby activating downstream signaling cascades involved in cell survival, differentiation, and inflammatory responses [29, 77]. The TrkA receptor plays a central role in promoting neuronal survival and growth, whereas p75NTR is implicated in the regulation of both apoptosis and cell survival [78]. NGF, through TrkA-mediated signaling, activates key pathways such as PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK, thereby promoting cellular survival and growth, and contributing to the development and maintenance of the nervous system [79]. Notably, NGF expression and signaling vary under different physiological and pathological conditions, and these alterations have been associated with neurodegenerative diseases and chronic pain states [80].

In the pathophysiology of psoriasis, NGF is considered a crucial molecular bridge linking neuronal signaling with epidermal responses. In an in vitro model of NGF-induced human keratinocytes, Wang et al. demonstrated that the traditional Chinese formula Qingre Huoxue Jiedu (QHJ) significantly suppressed keratinocyte proliferation and induced apoptosis by blocking the NGF–TrkA signaling axis, while concurrently upregulating Bax/Bcl-xl and NGFR expression [81]. This dual effect not only attenuated NGF-mediated neurotrophic support but also interrupted the downstream TRPV1-mediated release of CGRP and SP, thereby dampening inflammation and pruritic feedback loops [81]. Recent evidence indicates that the skin microbiota can stimulate early NGF production in mast cells, which in turn lowers the threshold of TRPV1 sensitivity [82]. This mechanism suggests a potential link between microbial dysbiosis, mast cell activation, and neurogenic inflammation in psoriasis, highlighting how altered microbiota may contribute to itch and disease chronicity through NGF–TRPV1 signaling. Similarly, in an IMQ-induced psoriasiform mouse model, Shang et al. observed sustained upregulation of NGF, which was closely associated with heightened sensory nerve excitability, increased pruritus, and mast cell accumulation [83]. Their findings confirmed that CGRP and SP not only facilitate inflammatory cell infiltration via enhanced vasodilation and vascular permeability but also promote mast cell degranulation, leading to the release of histamine and proinflammatory mediators that aggravate erythema, edema, and itch [83]. Although the development of systemic TRPV1 antagonists has been limited by adverse effects such as core body temperature elevation [84], local inhibition of TRPV1 or upstream blockade of the NGF–TrkA axis has shown efficacy in disrupting the neuro–immune–inflammatory feedback loop. Elucidating these mechanisms will advance our understanding of psoriasis pathogenesis and provide a theoretical foundation for the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

Vasoactive Intestinal Peptide

Vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) is a 28-amino-acid neuropeptide belonging to the glucagon–secretin family [85]. It is widely distributed throughout both the central and peripheral nervous systems, with prominent expression in the hypothalamus, amygdala, and enteric nervous plexuses [86]. VIP exerts its biological effects by binding to two primary receptors, VPAC1 and VPAC2, both of which are members of the GPCR family [87, 88], VPAC1 and VPAC2 are expressed in various cell types. VPAC1 is predominantly enriched in vascular smooth muscle cells and neurons, whereas VPAC2 is broadly distributed in endothelial and immune cells [89]. Previous studies have reported significantly elevated VIP levels in psoriatic lesions compared to healthy skin, implicating VIP in psoriasis pathogenesis [90].

Pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide (PACAP), a multifunctional neuropeptide of the VIP family, also modulates neuroimmune and inflammatory responses via binding to VPAC1 and VPAC2 [91]. Studies have shown significantly elevated PACAP expression in psoriatic skin, accompanied by increased plasma VIP levels in affected individuals [92]. Furthermore, Lamana et al. reported increased serum levels of VIP and VPAC2 in psoriasis patients compared with healthy controls and identified miRNA signatures associated with VIP/VPAC2 expression, suggesting links to immune-mediated inflammatory diseases (IMIDs) [93]. In addition, PACAP has also been implicated in other Th17-driven skin disorders such as contact allergy, further supporting its broad relevance to neuroimmune regulation in cutaneous inflammation [94]. In vitro, PACAP—alongside VIP—in the presence of TGF-β, facilitates effective antigen presentation by Langerhans cells to Th17 cells and induces secretion of IL-17 A and IL-6 [95, 96]. Additionally, Kakurai et al. found via RT-PCR and Northern blotting that IFN-γ significantly upregulates VPAC1 expression in keratinocytes. VIP, acting through VPAC1, promotes keratinocyte proliferation and markedly enhances the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-8 [97] Yu et al. further demonstrated that VIP increases VEGF expression in human HaCaT keratinocytes via ERK1/2 and p38 MAPK signaling pathways, thereby promoting angiogenesis [98]. Therefore, elucidating the precise roles of VIP and PACAP and their stage-specific functions in psoriasis may provide a critical foundation for designing personalized and phase-specific therapeutic interventions.

In summary, key neuropeptides—including CGRP, Substance P, NGF, and VIP/PACAP—act synergistically within the neuroimmune-inflammatory axis of psoriasis, forming a tightly coupled positive feedback regulatory network (Fig. 3). CGRP primarily promotes T cell–mediated inflammatory cascades via activation of the IL-23/IL-17 pathway, whereas Substance P enhances local immune responses through NK1R-mediated vasodilation, mast cell degranulation, and Th1/Th17 polarization. NGF, as an upstream modulator, regulates neuronal survival and excitability and potentiates neuropeptide release via TRPV1 activation, thereby intensifying itch and inflammation. VIP and its homolog PACAP, via VPAC receptors, regulate keratinocyte function, proinflammatory cytokine production, and angiogenesis, further amplifying the pathological immune response. These neuropeptides collectively form intricate interaction networks with immune cells and keratinocytes in the cutaneous microenvironment. Their regulatory mechanisms highlight key nodes of neuroimmune imbalance in psoriasis and offer promising theoretical foundations and therapeutic targets for future interventions(Fig. 3).

Neurotransmitter Regulation of Immune Cell Activation

In recent years, psoriasis—traditionally regarded as a T cell–mediated chronic inflammatory skin disease—has been increasingly recognized as a systemic condition involving complex interactions between the nervous, immune, and endocrine systems, rather than merely local cutaneous immune dysregulation [9]. As the largest sensory organ of the body, the skin maintains intimate connections with both central and peripheral nervous systems. Its dense network of peripheral nerve endings provides the anatomical basis for localized neurotransmitter release and spatially restricted neuromodulation. Evidence suggests that neurotransmitters such as norepinephrine (NE), serotonin (5-HT), gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and acetylcholine (ACh) interact with their respective receptors on local immune cells, keratinocytes, and vascular endothelial cells, thereby modulating immune-inflammatory pathways, increasing vascular permeability, and disrupting skin barrier integrity—contributing collectively to the immunopathogenesis of psoriasis [99–103]. In the following section, we examine several representative neurotransmitters and explore their potential roles in the pathogenesis of psoriasis.

Norepinephrine

Norepinephrine (NE) is a catecholamine neurotransmitter characterized by a benzene ring substituted with two hydroxyl groups (–OH) and an amino side chain, a structure that enables high-affinity and selective binding to adrenergic receptors [104].NE is synthesized through a multistep enzymatic pathway beginning with the hydroxylation of tyrosine by tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) to form dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA). DOPA is then decarboxylated by aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase to yield dopamine, which is subsequently converted to NE by dopamine β-hydroxylase [105]. NE plays a pivotal role in numerous physiological processes, including regulation of cardiovascular function, metabolism, and immune responses [106, 107].

Elevated plasma NE levels have been observed in psoriasis patients compared to healthy individuals [108]. Increased NE levels can upregulate co-stimulatory molecules such as CD80 and CD86 on DCs via β2-adrenergic receptors (β2AR), thereby enhancing their T cell–activating potential [109]. Takenaka et al. showed that NE stimulation of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-primed DCs via β2AR significantly inhibits IL-12p70 production while promoting IL-23 secretion, thereby enhancing Th17 differentiation and IL-17 A release [110]. This process is critical in psoriasis pathogenesis, as the proinflammatory cytokines produced enhance the sensitivity of TRPV1 and TRPA1 channels, elevate intracellular Ca²⁺ levels, and trigger neuropeptide release, thereby intensifying clinical symptoms (Fig. 3). In an imiquimod-induced psoriasiform mouse model, Ca²⁺/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II gamma (CaMK2γ) was found to be highly expressed within sympathetic nerves as well as γδT cells. Rather than being secreted, CaMK2γ functions as an intracellular signaling molecule in γδT cells, where it directly regulates IL-17 production through norepinephrine (NE)-dependent activation of the sympathetic–NE–β1-adrenergic receptor–NF-κB/p38 MAPK axis [111]. Mice deficient in CaMK2γ exhibit significantly attenuated psoriatic inflammation in this model, suggesting a critical pathogenic role for CaMK2γ in psoriasis [111]. Thus, targeting CaMK2γ represents a promising therapeutic strategy in psoriasis, particularly given its central role in modulating NE release and inflammatory responses.

Acetylcholine

Acetylcholine (ACh) is synthesized primarily by choline acetyltransferase (ChAT), which catalyzes the reaction between choline and acetyl–coenzyme A to produce ACh [112]. ACh is typically released via a calcium-dependent mechanism at the presynaptic membrane, involving voltage-gated calcium channel activation that promotes synaptic vesicle fusion and subsequent ACh release into the synaptic cleft [113]. ACh is rapidly degraded by acetylcholinesterase (AChE), which hydrolyzes ACh and terminates its biological activity [114]. In certain contexts, non-neuronal cells—such as endothelial and immune cells—can also secrete ACh, suggesting its potential role in local immune regulation and inflammatory responses [115].

Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs), particularly the α7 and α9 subtypes, play crucial roles in modulating immune responses and inflammatory signaling。A case-control study revealed significantly elevated ACh levels in the plasma of psoriasis patients compared to healthy controls. Dysregulation of the non-neuronal cholinergic system in the skin, including increased expression of muscarinic ACh receptors (mAChRs) in psoriatic lesions, may contribute to disease pathogenesis [116]. In both imiquimod (IMQ)-induced psoriasiform mouse models and HaCaT cell studies, Chen et al. reported increased α7-nAChR expression in psoriatic skin, primarily localized to keratinocytes and macrophages [117].Treatment with the α7-nAChR agonist PNU-282,987 significantly alleviated erythema and scaling, suppressed pathological keratinocyte proliferation and abnormal differentiation, and markedly reduced proinflammatory cytokine levels including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [117]. Mechanistically, α7-nAChR activation significantly inhibited STAT3 pathway activation, reducing IL-6/IL-22–induced STAT3 phosphorylation, a pathway known to be associated with Th17 differentiation and keratinocyte proliferation [117]. Furthermore, α7-nAChR suppressed the NF-κB pathway by reducing levels of phosphorylated IκBα and p65, thereby inhibiting TNF-α–driven downstream gene expression and inflammatory cell migration [117]. The study by Chen et al. expands our understanding of neuroimmune interactions in psoriasis and highlights α7-nAChR as a promising dual-action therapeutic target—capable of modulating Th17-mediated inflammation and directly influencing keratinocyte phenotypes to alleviate clinical manifestations. Further exploration of this pathway may facilitate the development of novel neurotransmitter-targeted therapeutic strategies for psoriasis.

Dopamine

Dopamine is a critical neurotransmitter characterized by a phenylethylamine backbone with hydroxyl group modifications.It is biosynthesized via a two-step enzymatic process involving tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) and DOPA decarboxylase. Initially, TH converts tyrosine to L-DOPA, which serves as the rate-limiting step in dopamine biosynthesis [118].Subsequently, L-DOPA is decarboxylated by DOPA decarboxylase to form dopamine [119].TH activity is tightly regulated by several factors, including metal ions such as iron and various metabolic intermediates—a mechanism implicated in several neurological disorders [120].

Dopamine receptors belong to the G protein–coupled receptor (GPCR) superfamily and are broadly classified into D1-like receptors (DRD1 and DRD5) and D2-like receptors (DRD2, DRD3, and DRD4). Studies have shown that all dopamine receptor (DR) subtypes are expressed in both healthy individuals and psoriasis patients; however, DRD2 and DRD4 are overexpressed in psoriatic lesions, whereas DRD3 levels appear reduced compared to healthy skin [121]. Moreover, Mori et al. demonstrated that antagonism of D1 receptors significantly reduced the expression of IL-4 and IFN-γ in rat skin [122]. Another study found that DRD2 activation downregulates IL-17 expression, thereby suppressing psoriasis-associated inflammation [123]. Collectively, these findings suggest that dopamine may modulate the activation status of resting and activated T cells via its receptors. Targeting D1-, D2-, and D4-like receptors on activated T cells and inflamed psoriatic skin may offer novel therapeutic opportunities. These studies provide important mechanistic insights into the pathogenesis of psoriasis and underscore the immunomodulatory potential of dopamine signaling.

CNS Effects of Psoriatic Inflammatory Mediators

Recent studies have revealed that the central nervous system (CNS) functions not merely as a passive responder but as an active regulatory hub in systemic inflammation. Given that psoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated disease characterized by elevated levels of inflammatory mediators such as IL-17 A and TNF-α, it is reasonable to speculate that these cytokines may also contribute to altered neural activity and behavioral changes observed in psoriasis. In this context, the following section highlights emerging evidence linking psoriatic inflammatory mediators, particularly Th17-associated cytokines, to neuroinflammation and neural plasticity.

Among the inflammatory mediators implicated in psoriasis, Th17-associated cytokines—especially IL-17 A—are increasingly recognized for their capacity to modulate neuroinflammation and neural plasticity [124]. IL-17 A is secreted not only by Th17 cells migrating into the CNS from the periphery but also by resident astrocytes, microglia, and neurons [125–127]. Its receptor, IL-17RA, is predominantly expressed on neurons, where IL-17 signaling directly increases neuronal excitability and sensitization to nociceptive stimuli [128]. In a bone cancer pain model, Liu et al. demonstrated that spinal astrocytes promote mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia via sustained IL-17 A release [129]. Similarly, Sun et al. found that IL-17RA is primarily expressed on neurons and, to a lesser extent, on astrocytes. Pre- and post-injection of IL-17 neutralizing antibodies into the spinal cord markedly reduced C-fiber–associated long-term potentiation (LTP), highlighting IL-17’s pathogenic role in central sensitization [130].

In addition to IL-17, interleukin-1β (IL-1β) serves as another critical mediator linking psoriasis to CNS dysfunction [131]. In vitro studies on cutaneous neurons have shown that IL-1β impairs astrocyte-mediated neurotrophic support, reducing neuronal survival and neurite outgrowth—indicating its significant impact on axonal development and structural integrity [132]. This effect is primarily mediated by IL-1β–induced activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, which upregulates Wnt5a—a regulator of cortical neuron migration, synaptogenesis, and inflammation-induced neural plasticity [133, 134]. More importantly, IL-1β and TNF-α synergistically stimulate astrocytes to release extracellular vesicles enriched with regulatory microRNAs. These vesicles markedly downregulate key neuronal miRNAs such as miR-125α−5p and miR-16-5p, leading to decreased synaptic protein expression, impaired neuronal activity, and accelerated neurodegeneration [135, 136].This mechanism has been implicated in Alzheimer’s-like and chronic stress models, suggesting a potential link to the neurobehavioral symptoms observed in psoriasis. Additionally, IL-1β activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, promoting the release of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and glucocorticoids such as cortisol [137].

Chemokine pathways such as the CXCL1/CXCR2 axis have also been shown to regulate neuronal excitability and contribute to the maintenance of chronic pain in the CNS [138]. In psoriasis models, CCR6—a hallmark receptor on Th17 cells—binds its ligand CCL20, which is highly upregulated in lesional skin and drives the directed migration of inflammatory cells, including Th17 cells, to the site of inflammation [139]. CCR6-deficient mice fail to develop psoriasiform lesions in IL-23 injection models, indicating that CCR6 is essential for disease pathogenesis [139]. Unfortunately, despite its therapeutic potential, CCR6 remains a challenging target for small-molecule inhibition due to structural limitations and ligand accessibility [140]. To date, clinical investigations of CCR6 antagonists remain scarce, and the development of CCR6-targeted agents by biotech or pharmaceutical companies is still limited. Advances in drug delivery and antibody engineering may facilitate the development of therapeutic CCR6-targeting agents in the future.

In summary, IL-17 and other proinflammatory cytokines activate astrocytes, microglia, and neuronal receptors, disrupting neural network integrity and contributing to central sensitization and neurogenic pain. This forms the mechanistic foundation of neuroimmune–behavioral interactions in psoriasis. This mechanism suggests that the CNS may represent a promising therapeutic target for systemic treatment strategies in psoriasis(Fig. 4).

PNS Effects of Psoriatic Inflammatory Mediators

Inflammatory mediators in psoriasis can directly act on peripheral sensory neurons via multiple neuroimmune pathways, significantly enhancing neuronal excitability and modulating nociceptive transmission。In psoriasiform inflammation models, binding of IL-17 A to its receptor IL-17R rapidly induces phosphorylation of protein kinase B (Akt) and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) in dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons, triggering neuronal depolarization and hyperexcitability within minutes [127, 141].Specific deletion of TRPV4 in DRG neurons or pharmacological inhibition of ERK phosphorylation alleviates psoriasiform itch [141]. In vivo, intra-articular or intradermal injection of IL-17 A induces slow and persistent sensitization of nociceptive C-fibers to mechanical stimuli, highlighting IL-17 A’s specific role as a mediator of inflammatory pain [141]. TNF-α, which is markedly elevated both locally and systemically in psoriasis, promotes perineural infiltration of inflammatory cells via TNFR1/2 signaling, and upregulates voltage-gated sodium channels such as NaV1.3, NaV1.7, and NaV1.8, thereby amplifying neuronal depolarization and pain transmission [142–144]. IL-1β and IL-6 synergistically activate DRG neurons and surrounding glial cells, contributing to the development of neuropathic pain and persistent neuroinflammation [145]. Beyond classical cytokines, neuropeptides such as Substance P (SP) and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), which accumulate in psoriatic lesions, bind to their respective receptors on dendritic cells and macrophages, promoting the release of IL-23 and TNF-α and establishing a vicious feedback loop between nerve terminals and immune cells [146, 147]。.

n neuroimmune crosstalk, IL-23 not only sustains Th17 populations and IL-17 secretion but also regulates cutaneous inflammation and itch transmission via TRPV1⁺NaV1.8⁺ nociceptors. Ablation of these receptors significantly attenuates IL-23 expression and psoriasiform dermatitis in IMQ-induced models [46, 148, 149].Zhang et al. further demonstrated that IL-23 pretreatment reduces calcium influx in DRG neurons in response to histamine and capsaicin, and diminishes histamine-induced scratching behavior, suggesting that IL-23 modulates histaminergic itch via TRPV1 activity [49]. Moreover, Chen et al. found increased nerve fiber density in psoriatic lesions, with Tenascin-C⁺ fibroblasts secreting neurotrophic guidance factors that promote neural regrowth and chemotaxis, thereby intensifying neuroimmune interactions [59].

In psoriatic lesions, keratinocytes and infiltrating immune cells secrete CCL2, CCL5, CXCL1, and CXCL8, which play pivotal roles in leukocyte recruitment and amplification of local inflammation. Experimental studies further indicate that these chemokines can directly enhance the excitability of peripheral sensory neurons, thereby contributing to pain and itch signaling. Nevertheless, clinical evidence demonstrates that pruritus intensity in psoriasis correlates significantly only with CCL17, but not with CCL2, CCL5, or CXCL8 [150, 151]. CCL2 primarily acts through its receptor CCR2 on DRG neurons to induce membrane depolarization and enhance mechanical hyperalgesia. Under inflammatory conditions, CCL2 and CCR2 expression is markedly upregulated in the DRG, and pharmacologic CCR2 blockade alleviates mechanical pain sensitivity [152]. Notably, chemokine-driven immune cell infiltration enhances the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, which, in concert with chemokines, prolong DRG neuron sensitization and facilitate synaptic plasticity in the spinal dorsal horn [153, 154]. Clinical studies show elevated serum and lesional levels of CCL2, CCL5, and CXCL8 in psoriasis patients, with positive correlations to pruritus intensity and pain scores, suggesting that chemokine-mediated neuroimmune crosstalk is a key mechanism underlying sensory symptoms(Fig. 5) [150]。.

Neurotherapeutic Strategies

Psoriasis is a chronic and relapsing disorder that typically requires long-term management. Current therapeutic options include topical agents, combination therapies involving corticosteroids and vitamin D analogues, phototherapy, and systemic medications [155].In recent years, accumulating insights into the nervous system have positioned neuromodulation as a promising strategic direction in psoriasis therapy. Its mechanisms encompass inhibition of neuropeptide release, activation of the cholinergic anti-inflammatory reflex, and modulation of sensory nerve signaling pathways [44, 49, 156, 157].

Botulinum neurotoxins A and B (BoNT-A/B), produced by Clostridium botulinum, are potent neurotoxins that, in low doses, are widely used for neuromodulatory therapeutic purposes. Subcutaneous injection of BoNT-A/B has demonstrated significant therapeutic efficacy in both clinical and animal models of psoriasis. A systematic review of eight open-label clinical trials found that seven reported significant improvements in psoriatic lesions following BoNT-A/B injection, with effects lasting up to six months. This was accompanied by reduced release of neuropeptides such as CGRP and SP, as well as downregulation of the IL-23/Th17 axis [158]. Mechanistically, CGRP released from sensory neurons stimulates keratinocyte proliferation and IL-23 expression via the TRPV1 pathway. BoNT administration effectively interrupts this pathogenic feedback loop by inhibiting CGRP release [49, 59]. In imiquimod (IMQ)-induced psoriasiform mouse models, BTX-B injection reduced the infiltration of CD4⁺ T cells and CD11c⁺ dendritic cells, along with decreased IL-17 A/F production [159]. Additionally, in vivo studies revealed that subcutaneous BoNT-A injection significantly reduced CGRP⁺ fiber density in DRG neurons of IMQ-treated mice, restoring CGRP levels to baseline and markedly alleviating psoriasiform inflammation [59]. These findings provide robust experimental support for BoNT as a neuromodulatory intervention targeting neuroimmune crosstalk in psoriasis。.

In addition, transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS), a non-invasive neuromodulatory technique, exerts both systemic and local anti-inflammatory effects by activating the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway [160].An open-label study in patients with psoriatic arthritis reported that taVNS led to an approximately 20% reduction in systemic inflammatory markers such as CRP, along with a marked increase in cardiac vagal tone [157]。Furthermore, a case report indicated that 3 months of taVNS reduced serum cortisol levels in a psoriasis patient, suggesting potential restoration of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis function [161]。.

Molecular interventions targeting sensory neuron-associated pathways have also shown great promise. Early clinical use of capsaicin-based TRPV1 modulators in psoriasis has demonstrated substantial itch relief。TRPV1 overexpression has been positively correlated with pruritus severity [61]. Capsaicin, a TRPV1 agonist, induces channel desensitization upon repeated stimulation, thereby reducing the release of CGRP and other neuropeptides. It has been used to treat various chronic pain and pruritic conditions [162, 163]. Although capsaicin use is limited by its burning sensation, newer small-molecule TRPV1 antagonists such as PAC-14,028 (Asivatrep) have demonstrated antipruritic efficacy in clinical trials for skin diseases like atopic dermatitis, without disrupting thermoregulation [164]。.

Moreover, inflammation-resolving lipid mediator Resolvin D3 regulates the TRPV1/CGRP axis in both murine and human DRG neurons and is considered a potential therapeutic target for psoriasis [63]. The natural compound evodiamine significantly alleviates inflammation and pruritus in IMQ-induced psoriasis models by downregulating SP, NGF, CGRP, and TRPV channel expression [165]. In addition, recent studies suggest that lidocaine may exert therapeutic effects in psoriasis through multiple mechanisms. Lidocaine disrupts CGRP signaling and suppresses IL-23 production by DCs co-cultured with DRG neurons, thereby attenuating the neuroimmune axis that drives psoriatic inflammation. Moreover, as a non-selective Nav blocker, epidural administration of lidocaine has been reported in a limited number of cases to effectively alleviate severe plaque psoriasis. Collectively, these findings highlight lidocaine as a promising neurotherapeutic strategy for psoriasis [166, 167]. In summary, BoNT-A/B, taVNS, SP/NK1R antagonists, and TRPV1/CGRP modulators collectively represent promising neuromodulatory strategies for psoriasis. Future large-scale randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm their efficacy and safety, and to explore multi-target combinatorial approaches that concurrently alleviate cutaneous inflammation and improve symptom burden.

Conclusion and Future Perspectives

Psoriasis, a systemic, immune-mediated chronic inflammatory disorder, is increasingly understood within an integrated model involving dynamic interactions among the nervous, immune, and endocrine systems—moving beyond the traditional immune-centric paradigm. The emerging talk has significantly reshaped our understanding of disease onset and symptom persistence in psoriasis. This review systematically examines the roles of key neuropeptides (such as CGRP, SP, VIP/PACAP, and NGF) and neurotransmitters (including norepinephrine, acetylcholine, and dopamine) in the pathophysiology of psoriasis. We delineate a multi-tiered signaling network formed between peripheral nerve terminals, keratinocytes, and immune cells. These neural factors not only act as catalysts in initiating localized inflammatory responses but also modulate central and peripheral sensory processing to drive clinical manifestations such as pruritus and pain. Notably, the nervous system is no longer viewed as a passive responder in psoriatic inflammation, but rather as an active regulatory hub that orchestrates the dissemination of key inflammatory mediators—such as IL-17 A, IL-1β, and TNF-α—and governs neuro-sensitization.

Despite recent advances in neuroimmune research, several critical challenges remain. First, current evidence is largely derived from animal models or immunohistochemical analyses of skin tissue, which often lack the resolution to distinguish whether neuropeptides originate from neurons, keratinocytes, or immune cells—thus limiting target precision for therapeutic intervention. Second, neurogenic inflammation is influenced by external factors such as psychological stress, metabolic status, and gut-skin axis dysregulation, yet existing studies seldom integrate these upstream signals with the endogenous neuroimmune network. Mechanistic insight into how these signals converge to drive disease remains underdeveloped. Third, although early interventions—such as botulinum toxin A (BoNT-A), transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS), and TRPV1 antagonists—have shown promise in modulating neuroimmune interactions and alleviating symptoms, most studies are limited to case reports or small cohorts, lacking robust, subtype-spanning, mechanistic randomized controlled trials.

Future research should prioritize the identification and mechanistic dissection of key regulatory nodes in neuroimmune interactions, particularly the dynamic signaling of neuropeptides (e.g., CGRP, SP, NGF) and their receptors. Advances in single-cell and spatial transcriptomics may uncover subtype-specific neuroimmune heterogeneity, paving the way for personalized therapeutic approaches. Additionally, selective small-molecule inhibitors or biologics targeting TRPV1, NK1R, and VPAC receptors hold promise in overcoming the limitations of conventional therapies. Altogether, centering psoriasis treatment paradigms around the nervous system not only offers a novel mechanistic lens for understanding pathogenesis but also opens up more precise and efficacious therapeutic avenues.
