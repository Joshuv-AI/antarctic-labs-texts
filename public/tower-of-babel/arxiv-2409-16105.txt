Linear isometries on the annulus: description and spectral properties Thanks: This research is partly supported by the Bézout Labex, funded by ANR, reference ANR-10-LABX-58.
I. Chalendar, L. Oger, J. R. Partington

Abstract
. We give a complete characterisation of the linear isometries of Hol ( Ω ) \mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\Omega) , where Ω \Omega is the half-plane, the complex plane or an annulus centered at 0 and symmetric to the unit circle. Moreover, we introduce new techniques to describe the holomorphic maps on the annulus that preserve the unit circle, and we finish by proving results about the spectra of the linear isometries on the annulus.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 47B33; Secondary 30H05, 47A10

1.
Introduction

In order to study a metric linear space, and in particular its geometry, one of the main tools is an analysis of the linear isometries of this space. For spaces of holomorphic functions, there is a strong connection between isometries and weighted composition operators.

This link can be traced back to Banach himself
[
3
]
in the space
\mathcal{C}(K)
of continuous real-valued functions on a compact metric space
K
. For most well-known spaces, such as the Hardy, Bergman, Bloch spaces or the disc algebra, the linear isometries have been completely described
[
6
,
8
,
9
,
11
,
13
,
16
,
19
,
20
]
.

This paper can be considered as a sequel to
[
7
]
, where the authors characterised completely the linear isometries
V
on the space
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
of holomorphic functions on the unit disc, relative to one of the two classical distances. We showed there that
V
is of the form

V(f)(z)=\alpha f(\beta z)=:T_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(z),\quad\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{T},

where
\mathbb{T}
is the unit circle of
\mathbb{C}
. Those
V
are determined by being isometric for any two seminorms of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, defined by
\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,r}:=\sup_{z\in K_{r}}\left\lvert f(z)\right\rvert
, with
r\in[0,1)
and
K_{r}=r\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

It is natural to consider the same problem when replacing the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
with other domains, such as the half-plane
\mathbb{H}
or the whole plane
\mathbb{C}
. Those two cases are easily obtained from the results of
[
7
]
.

\star

For the half-plane
\mathbb{H}
, let us define the
Cayley transform

\tau:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{H}
by

\tau(z)=\frac{1+z}{1-z}.

The map
\tau
is biholomorphic. For all
r\in[0,1)
, denote
L_{r}=\tau(K_{r})
. We aim to find a linear isometry
V
of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{H})
for the seminorms associated with the sets
L_{r}
. To do so, note that for all
F\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{H})
, the map
f=F\circ\tau
is in
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
. Hence, for every linear isometry
V
of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{H})
,

\displaystyle\sup_{w\in K_{r}}\left\lvert f(z)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in L_{r}}\left\lvert F(z)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in L_{r}}\left\lvert(VF)(z)\right\rvert=\sup_{w\in K_{r}}\left\lvert(C_{\tau}\circ V\circ C_{\tau^{-1}})(f)(w)\right\rvert.

Therefore,
C_{\tau}\circ V\circ C_{\tau^{-1}}
is a linear isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, so we obtain that
V
is of the form
C_{\tau^{-1}}\circ T_{\alpha,\beta}\circ C_{\tau}
for some
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{T}
.

\star

For the plane
\mathbb{C}
, assume that
\left\|Tf\right\|_{\infty,\rho}=\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,\rho}
for all
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{C})
and
\rho\geq 0
.
Then, it is in particular true for all
f
polynomial. By density, for all
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
and
0\leq\rho<1
,
\left\|Tf\right\|_{\infty,\rho}=\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,\rho}
. Thus,
T
is an isometry of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{D})
, and
T=T_{\alpha,\beta}
for some
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{T}
.

We now consider a doubly-connected domain, that is an annulus.
We fix
R>1
and define
\mathbb{A}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:1/R<\left\lvert z\right\rvert<R\}
. Our main goal is to obtain a similar characterisation as in
[
7
]
.

In Sections
2
and
3
, we give preliminary results about distances, seminorms, composition operators and powers of
z
. These will be present in the proof of the Main Theorem (Section
4
), which says that if we consider annuli symmetric to the unit circle, then the only linear isometries of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
are rotations or inversions.

In
[
7
]
, we also used a description of the Blaschke products to obtain a characterisation of linear isometries for one seminorm associated to one of the
K_{r}
. In Section
5
, we describe the annulus version of Blaschke products, that is, unimodular functions on the unit circle. Results of Fatou
[
12
]
and Bourgain
[
5
]
will be in the spotlight in this section.

Finally, Section
6
is devoted to the spectral study of the linear isometries of
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
. In particular, depending on the angle of the rotation, we give examples of rotations such that the spectrum is different from the point spectrum or the unit circle.

2.
First results

This section is devoted to proving the main ingredients in the proof of the main result, that is, the description of linear isometries on the Fréchet space of analytic functions on an annulus.

The content of this section may be of independent interest.

2.1.
Algebraic characterisation of composition operators

From now on, for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we write
e_{n}
for the function defined by
e_{n}(z)=z^{n}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
L:\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})\to\mathbb{C}
be non-zero, linear, continuous and multiplicative.
Then there exists
z_{0}\in\mathbb{A}
such that for all
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
,
L(f)=f(z_{0})
.

Proof.

The proof uses the same ideas as in
[
1
, Lemma 2.2]
. For the sake of completeness, we detail them.
Note that for all
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
,

L(f)=L(fe_{0})=L(f)L(e_{0}).

Choosing
f
such that
L(f)\neq 0
, we have
L(e_{0})=1
. Denote by
z_{0}=L(e_{1})
. Thus,

Assume that
z_{0}\not\in\mathbb{A}
.
Then
g=(e_{1}-z_{0}e_{0})^{-1}\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
and

(e_{1}-z_{0}e_{0})g=e_{0}\implies 0=[L(e_{1})-z_{0}L(e_{0})]L(g)=L(e_{0})=1,

a contradiction. Hence,
z_{0}\in\mathbb{A}
.

Let
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
such that
f(z_{0})=0
. There exists
h\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
such that
f=(e_{1}-z_{0}e_{0})h
, so

L(f)=[L(e_{1})-z_{0}L(e_{0})]L(h)=[z_{0}-z_{0}]L(h)=0.\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

If
f(z_{0})\neq 0
, then the function
g=f-f(z_{0})e_{0}\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
satisfies
g(z_{0})=0
. Hence,
L(g)=0
, and
L(f)=L(f(z_{0})e_{0})=f(z_{0})
. ∎

Along the same lines as in
[
1
]
, Lemma
2.1
combined with Runge’s theorem
[
18
]
, provides an algebraic characterisation of composition operators, whose proof is omitted.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
T:\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})\to\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
be a continuous linear operator. Then
T
is a composition operator if and only if for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
Te_{n}=(Te_{1})^{n}
, where
e_{n}(z)=z^{n}
.

2.2.
From distances to seminorms

The two next results are contained in
[
7
]
.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
7
, Lemma 1.1]
)
.

Let
X
be a Fréchet space, and
(\left\|\cdot\right\|_{k})_{k\geq 1}
an associated increasing family of semi-norms.
A linear operator
T:X\to X
is isometric, considering the distance
d_{X}
defined by

d_{X}(x,y)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k}\min(1,\left\|x-y\right\|_{k}),

if and only if for all
x\in X
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\left\|Tx\right\|_{k}=\left\|x\right\|_{k}
.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
7
, Lemma 1.2]
)
.

Let
X
be a Fréchet space, and
(\left\|\cdot\right\|_{k})_{k\geq 1}
the associated increasing family of semi-norms.
A linear operator
T:X\to X
is isometric, considering the distance
d^{\prime}_{X}
defined by

d^{\prime}_{X}(x,y)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k}\frac{\left\|x-y\right\|_{k}}{1+\left\|x-y\right\|_{k}},

if and only if for all
x\in X
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\left\|Tx\right\|_{k}=\left\|x\right\|_{k}
.

In particular, we consider here the seminorms

\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,n}:=\sup_{z\in K_{n}}\left\lvert f(z)\right\rvert,\quad K_{n}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:R^{\frac{1}{n}-1}\leq\left\lvert z\right\rvert\leq R^{1-\frac{1}{n}}\}.

Clearly,
K_{1}
is the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
. In the following, denote
R_{n}=R^{1-\frac{1}{n}}
.

3.
Three useful characterisations

We first recall the famous Hadamard three-circle theorem. Its detailed proof can be found for example in
[
17
]
. However, this paper deals with regular functions on an annulus rather than a half-line. Since we work with holomorphic maps on the entire annulus, we can be more precise in the case of equality.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
, and for
r\in(1/R,R)
, let
M(r)=\sup\{\left\lvert f(z)\right\rvert:\left\lvert z\right\rvert=r\}
. Then, the map
r\mapsto\log(M(r))
is convex, i.e. for all
1/R<r_{1}<r_{2}<r_{3}<R
,

(1)

\log\left(\frac{r_{3}}{r_{1}}\right)\log(M(r_{2}))\leq\log\left(\frac{r_{3}}{r_{2}}\right)\log(M(r_{1}))+\log\left(\frac{r_{2}}{r_{1}}\right)\log(M(r_{3})).

Moreover, we have equality if and only if
f(z)=cz^{n}
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

We can use this result to show that the analytic functions on an annulus
\mathbb{A}
preserving three circles of
\mathbb{A}
are the obvious ones.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
, and
1/R<r_{1}<r_{2}<r_{3}<R
. Assume that
f(r_{j}\mathbb{T})\subset r_{j}\mathbb{T}
for all
j=1,2,3
.
Then, there exists
c\in\mathbb{T}
such that
f(z)=cz
.

Proof.

Taking the notations of Theorem
3.1
, for all
j=1,2,3
,
M(r_{j})=r_{j}
. If the map
f
is not of the form
cz^{n}
, with
c\in\mathbb{C}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, then

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{r_{3}}{r_{1}}\right)\log(r_{2})<\log\left(\frac{r_{3}}{r_{2}}\right)\log(r_{1})+\log\left(\frac{r_{2}}{r_{1}}\right)\log(r_{3})

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\log(r_{3})\log(r_{2})-\log(r_{1})\log(r_{2})<\log(r_{2})\log(r_{3})-\log(r_{2})\log(r_{1})

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle 0<0.

It follows that
f(z)=cz^{n}
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then,
f(r_{j}\mathbb{T})\subset\left\lvert c\right\rvert r_{j}^{n}\mathbb{T}
. Thus,

r_{j}=\left\lvert c\right\rvert r_{j}^{n},\quad j=1,2,3,

which means that
\left\lvert c\right\rvert=1
and
n=1
.
∎

We now present and prove the last tool we use in the proof of our main result. The ideas are very similar to the ones involved in
[
7
, Theorem 1.4]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
such that for some
r\in(1/R,R)
and
\rho>0
,

\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=r}\left\lvert f(z)\right\rvert=\rho.

Then the set
\Xi=\{\xi\in r\mathbb{T}:\left\lvert f(\xi)\right\rvert=\rho\}=f^{-1}(\rho\mathbb{T})\cap r\mathbb{T}
is either equal to
r\mathbb{T}
, or a finite set.

Proof.

Assume that
\Xi\neq r\mathbb{T}
. Since
f
is continuous on
\mathbb{A}
and
f^{-1}(\rho\mathbb{T})\neq\varnothing
, we know that
f^{-1}(\rho\mathbb{T})
is a closed subset of
\mathbb{A}
. Hence,
\Xi
is a closed subset of
r\mathbb{T}
.

Assume that
\Xi
is not finite.
Then, the set
r\mathbb{T}\backslash\Xi
is an open subset of
r\mathbb{T}
, which is a union of an infinite number of open circular arcs of
r\mathbb{T}
, and
one of the endpoints is a limit point of
\Xi
, denoted in the following as
z_{0}
.

This point satisfies
\left\lvert f(z_{0})\right\rvert=\rho
, and there exist two sequences
(u_{n})\subset\Xi
and
(v_{n})\subset r\mathbb{T}\backslash\Xi
(it is sufficient to take points from the chosen arc) which tend to
z_{0}
, such that
\left\lvert f(u_{n})\right\rvert=\rho
and
\left\lvert f(v_{n})\right\rvert\neq\rho
.

We set
\phi_{1}:r\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
and
\phi_{2}:\rho\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
two conformal maps defined by

\phi_{1}(z)=i\frac{z-z_{0}}{z+z_{0}},\qquad\phi_{2}(z)=i\frac{z-f(z_{0})}{z+f(z_{0})}.

They satisfy
\phi_{1}(z_{0})=0
and
\phi_{2}(f(z_{0}))=0
. Denote
h=\phi_{2}\circ f\circ\phi_{1}^{-1}
.

Since
\phi_{1}^{-1}
is holomorphic near
0
, as is
\phi_{2}
near
f(z_{0})
, the function
h
is holomorphic around
0
, satisfies
h(0)=0
and there exist two sequences
(a_{n}),(b_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}
(corresponding to
(u_{n})
and
(v_{n})\subset r\mathbb{T}
) such that
a_{n}\to 0
,
b_{n}\to 0
,
h(a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}
and
h(b_{n})\not\in\mathbb{R}
. However,
all the derivatives of
h
at
0
must be real, so
h(b_{n})\in\mathbb{R}
(since
b_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
). Impossible.

Finally, we must have
r\mathbb{T}\backslash\Xi=\varnothing
, i.e.,
\Xi=r\mathbb{T}
, that is
f(r\mathbb{T})\subset\rho\mathbb{T}
.
∎

4.
Main result

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
T:\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})\to\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
a linear and continuous operator such that for all
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\left\|T(f)\right\|_{\infty,n}=\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,n}=\sup_{z\in K_{n}}\left\lvert f(z)\right\rvert.

Then there exist two constants
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{T}
such that
T=T_{\alpha,\beta}
or
S_{\alpha,\beta}
, defined by

(2)

T_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(z)=\alpha f(\beta z),\quad S_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(z)=\alpha f(\beta/z).

Proof.

The steps of the proof go along the same lines as in
[
7
, Theorem 2.1]
, with important technical changes since our domain is not simply connected.
In the following, denote by
e_{k}(z)=z^{k}
and
f_{k}=Te_{k}
, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Step 1
: We begin by showing that
Te_{0}=\alpha e_{0}
, with
\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert=1
.
Indeed, since the operator
T
is isometric for the seminorms
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,2}
and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,3}
, we obtain

\left\|f_{0}\right\|_{\infty,2}=\left\|f_{0}\right\|_{\infty,3}=1.

Using the maximum modulus principle, the map
f_{0}
is constant and unimodular. Denote by
\alpha
this constant. Without loss of generality, we will assume that
\alpha=1
.

Step 2
: We show that
f_{k}
is a rotation or an inversion, for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Note that for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{\infty,n}=\left\|e_{k}\right\|_{\infty,n}=(R_{n})^{k}.

The maximum is attained at the boundary of
K_{n}
, which is
R_{n}\mathbb{T}\cup R_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{T}
. Hence,

\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert\in\{R_{n},1/R_{n}\}}\left\lvert f_{k}(z)\right\rvert=(R_{n})^{k}.

Now, remark that

(R_{n})^{k}\mathbb{T}\subset f_{k}(K_{n})=:L.

Indeed, assume that there exists
\xi=(R_{n})^{k}e^{i\theta}
such that
\xi\not\in L
, which is a compact set. Then
\delta=d(\xi,L)>0
, so
L\subset(R_{n})^{k}\overline{\mathbb{D}}\backslash D(\xi,\delta)
.
We set
g=e_{0}+e^{-i\theta}e_{k}
. Hence,
Tg=e_{0}+e^{-i\theta}f_{k}
.
Now, compare the seminorms: we have
\left\|g\right\|_{\infty,n}=1+(R_{n})^{k}
and

\left\|Tg\right\|_{\infty,n}=\sup_{z\in K_{n}}\left\lvert 1+e^{-i\theta}f_{k}(z)\right\rvert=\sup_{w\in L}\left\lvert 1+e^{-i\theta}w\right\rvert,

and since
\left\lvert w\right\rvert\leq(R_{n})^{k}
, we have the equality
\left\|Tg\right\|_{\infty,n}=1+(R_{n})^{k}
if and only if
e^{-i\theta}w=(R_{n})^{k}
, which is impossible because
w\in L
.
Thus,
T
cannot be isometric for
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,n}
, an absurdity.
Therefore,
(R_{n})^{k}\mathbb{T}\subset L
.

Assume that
\Xi_{1}=\{\xi\in R_{n}\mathbb{T}:\left\lvert f_{k}(\xi)\right\rvert=(R_{n})^{k}\}
and
\Xi_{2}=\{\xi\in R_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{T}:\left\lvert f_{k}(\xi)\right\rvert=(R_{n})^{k}\}
are both finite sets.
Since
f_{k}(\mathring{K_{n}})\subset(R_{n})^{k}\mathbb{D}
and
f_{k}(\Xi_{1}\cup\Xi_{2})
is finite, we obtain a contradiction because
(R_{n})^{k}\mathbb{T}\subset L
.
Therefore, we have two choices: either
\Xi_{1}=R_{n}\mathbb{T}
, or
\Xi_{2}=R_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{T}
. In other words, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(E_{1}):f_{k}(R_{n}\mathbb{T})\subset(R_{n})^{k}\mathbb{T}\quad\text{ or }\quad(E_{2}):f_{k}(R_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{T})\subset(R_{n})^{k}\mathbb{T}.

In particular, for
n=1
, both equations give
f_{k}(\mathbb{T})\subset\mathbb{T}
.
Moreover, there are at least two different values of
n
among
2,3
and
4
satisfying both
(E_{1})
, or both
(E_{2})
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
n=2
and
n=3
satisfy the same inclusion.

\star

If
f_{k}(R_{n}\mathbb{T})\subset(R_{n})^{k}\mathbb{T}
and
g_{k}(z)=f_{k}(z)/z^{k-1}
, then
g_{k}(R_{n}\mathbb{T})\subset R_{n}\mathbb{T}
for
n=1,2,3
.
Hence, by Corollary
3.2
,
g_{k}(z)=c_{k}z
, with
\left\lvert c_{k}\right\rvert=1
, so
f_{k}(z)=c_{k}z^{k}
.

\star

If
f_{k}(R_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{T})\subset(R_{n})^{k}\mathbb{T}
and
g_{k}(z)=z^{k+1}f_{k}(z)
, then
g_{k}(R_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{T})\subset R_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{T}
for
n=1,2,3
.
Hence, by Corollary
3.2
,
g_{k}(z)=c_{k}z
, with
\left\lvert c_{k}\right\rvert=1
, so
f_{k}(z)=c_{k}/z^{k}
.

Thus, for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
f_{k}=c_{k}e_{k}
or
c_{k}e_{-k}
, for some
c_{k}\in\mathbb{T}
.

Step 3
: Assume that
f_{1}=c_{1}e_{1}
. If
f_{k}=c_{k}e_{-k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, then for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

R_{n}+(R_{n})^{k}=\left\|e_{1}+e_{k}\right\|_{\infty,n}=\left\|f_{1}+f_{k}\right\|_{\infty,n}=\left\|c_{1}e_{1}+c_{k}e_{-k}\right\|_{\infty,n}.

Note that

\left\|c_{1}e_{1}+c_{k}e_{-k}\right\|_{\infty,n}=\sup_{z\in K_{n}}\left\lvert c_{1}z+\frac{c_{k}}{z^{k}}\right\rvert=\sup_{z\in K_{n}}\left\lvert z+\frac{c_{k}}{c_{1}}\frac{1}{z^{k}}\right\rvert.

By the maximum modulus principle, the maximum is attained at the boundary of
K_{n}
, which is
R_{n}\mathbb{T}\cup R_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{T}
. We consider two possibilities:

\star

If the maximum is attained in
R_{n}\mathbb{T}
, then

\displaystyle R_{n}+(R_{n})^{k}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=R_{n}}\left\lvert z+\frac{c_{k}}{c_{1}}\frac{1}{z^{k}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(R_{n})^{k}}\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=R_{n}}\left\lvert z^{k+1}+\frac{c_{k}}{c_{1}}\right\rvert=\frac{(R_{n})^{k+1}+1}{(R_{n})^{k}}=R_{n}+\frac{1}{(R_{n})^{k}}.

\star

If the maximum is attained in
R_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{T}
, then

\displaystyle R_{n}+(R_{n})^{k}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=1/R_{n}}\left\lvert z+\frac{c_{k}}{c_{1}}\frac{1}{z^{k}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=(R_{n})^{k}\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=1/R_{n}}\left\lvert z^{k+1}+\frac{c_{k}}{c_{1}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=(R_{n})^{k}\left(\frac{1}{(R_{n})^{k+1}}+1\right)=\frac{1}{R_{n}}+(R_{n})^{k}.

Both of the cases lead to a contradiction, since
R_{n}\neq 1
for
n\geq 2
. Hence,
f_{k}=c_{k}e_{k}
for some
\left\lvert c_{k}\right\rvert=1
. In the same way, if
f_{1}=c_{1}e_{-1}
, then
f_{k}=c_{k}e_{-k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Step 4
: In the following, we assume that
f_{1}=c_{1}e_{1}
.
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Note that

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\partial K_{n}}\left\lvert(f_{k}+f_{k+1}+f_{k+2})(z)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in\partial K_{n}}\left\lvert(c_{k}e_{k}+c_{k+1}e_{k+1}+c_{k+2}e_{k+2})(z)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in\partial K_{n}}\left\lvert c_{k}z^{k}+c_{k+1}z^{k+1}+c_{k+2}z^{k+2}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in\partial K_{n}}\left\lvert z^{k}+z^{k+1}+z^{k+2}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=(R_{n})^{k}+(R_{n})^{k+1}+(R_{n})^{k+2}.

Recall that
\partial K_{n}=R_{n}\mathbb{T}\cup R_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{T}
. Hence, for
n\geq 2
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=1/R_{n}}\left\lvert c_{k}z^{k}+c_{k+1}z^{k+1}+c_{k+2}z^{k+2}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(R_{n})^{k}}+\frac{1}{(R_{n})^{k+1}}+\frac{1}{(R_{n})^{k+2}}

\displaystyle<(R_{n})^{k}+(R_{n})^{k+1}+(R_{n})^{k+2}.

In the same way,

\displaystyle\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=R_{n}}\left\lvert c_{k}z^{-k}+c_{k+1}z^{-k-1}+c_{k+2}z^{-k-2}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(R_{n})^{k}}+\frac{1}{(R_{n})^{k+1}}+\frac{1}{(R_{n})^{k+2}}

\displaystyle<(R_{n})^{k}+(R_{n})^{k+1}+(R_{n})^{k+2}.

Thus,

\displaystyle(R_{n})^{k}+(R_{n})^{k+1}+(R_{n})^{k+2}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=R_{n}}\left\lvert c_{k}z^{k}+c_{k+1}z^{k+1}+c_{k+2}z^{k+2}\right\rvert,&k\in\mathbb{N}\\
\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=1/R_{n}}\left\lvert c_{k}z^{-k}+c_{k+1}z^{-k-1}+c_{k+2}z^{-k-2}\right\rvert,&k\in\mathbb{N}.\end{cases}

Up to considering
w=1/z
, we only have to consider
k
positive.
Dividing by
z^{k}
, we have

\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=R_{n}}\left\lvert c_{k}+c_{k+1}z+c_{k+2}z^{2}\right\rvert=1+R_{n}+(R_{n})^{2}.

Dividing by
c_{k}
, since
\left\lvert c_{k}\right\rvert=1
, we have

\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=R_{n}}\left\lvert 1+\frac{c_{k+1}}{c_{k}}z+\frac{c_{k+2}}{c_{k}}z^{2}\right\rvert=1+R_{n}+(R_{n})^{2}.

Using the triangle inequality, for all
z
satisfying
\left\lvert z\right\rvert=R_{n}
,

\left\lvert 1+\frac{c_{k+1}}{c_{k}}z+\frac{c_{k+2}}{c_{k}}z^{2}\right\rvert\leq 1+R_{n}+(R_{n})^{2},

with equality if and only if

0=\arg(1)\equiv\arg\left[\frac{c_{k+1}}{c_{k}}z\right]\equiv\arg\left[\frac{c_{k+2}}{c_{k}}z^{2}\right]\mod 2\pi.

Denoting
\theta=\arg[z]
, this gives

0\equiv\arg[c_{k+1}]-\arg[c_{k}]+\theta\equiv\arg[c_{k+2}]-\arg[c_{k}]+2\theta\mod 2\pi.

Finally,

\displaystyle\arg{[c_{k}]}-2\arg{[c_{k+1}]}+\arg{[c_{k+2}]}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle(\arg{[c_{k+2}]}-\arg{[c_{k}]}+2\theta)-2(\arg{[c_{k+1}]}-\arg{[c_{k}]}+\theta)\equiv 0\mod 2\pi.

We show by induction that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\arg[c_{k}]\equiv k\arg[c_{1}]\mod 2\pi
.

k=0
or
1
: It is immediate (see Step 1 for
k=0
).

Assume that the formula is valid for some nonnegative integers
k
and
k+1
. Since
\arg{[c_{k}]}-2\arg{[c_{k+1}]}+\arg{[c_{k+2}]}\equiv 0\mod 2\pi
,
using induction hypothesis, we have

k\arg[c_{1}]-2(k+1)\arg[c_{1}]+\arg[c_{k+2}]\equiv 0\mod 2\pi.

After rearranging the terms, we obtain
\arg[c_{k+2}]\equiv(k+2)\arg[c_{1}]\mod 2\pi
.

In the same way, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\arg[c_{-k}]\equiv k\arg[c_{-1}]\mod 2\pi
.

Step 5
: We have shown that under the assumption
f_{1}=c_{1}e_{1}
, for some
\left\lvert c_{1}\right\rvert=1
,

\star

For all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
f_{k}=c_{k}e_{k}
(Step 3),

\star

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
c_{k}=c_{1}^{k}
and
c_{-k}=c_{-1}^{k}
(Step 4).

The only thing left to show is that
c_{-1}=1/c_{1}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\partial K_{n}}\left\lvert(f_{-1}+f_{0}+f_{1})(z)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in\partial K_{n}}\left\lvert(c_{-1}e_{-1}+e_{0}+c_{1}e_{1})(z)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in\partial K_{n}}\left\lvert\frac{c_{-1}}{z}+1+c_{1}z\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in\partial K_{n}}\left\lvert\frac{1}{z}+1+z\right\rvert=\frac{1}{R_{n}}+1+R_{n}.

We have two choices. If the maximum is attained on
R_{n}\mathbb{T}
, then

\sup_{\left\lvert z\right\rvert=R_{n}}\left\lvert\frac{c_{-1}}{z}+1+c_{1}z\right\rvert=\frac{1}{R_{n}}+1+R_{n}.

However, we have equality if and only if
\arg(c_{-1}/z)\equiv\arg(1)\equiv\arg(c_{1}z)\mod 2\pi
, that is

0\equiv\arg(c_{-1})-\arg(z)\equiv\arg(c_{1})+\arg(z)\mod 2\pi.

Hence,
\arg(z)\equiv\arg(c_{-1})\equiv-\arg(c_{1})\mod 2\pi
, so

c_{-1}=1/c_{1}
. It is the same if the maximum is attained on
R_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{T}
.

Conclusion:
For all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
f_{k}=c_{1}^{k}e_{k}=(c_{1}e_{1})^{k}=f_{1}^{k}
. Hence,
T
is a composition operator, with symbol
\varphi=f_{1}=c_{1}e_{1}
or
c_{1}e_{-1}
, by Proposition
2.2
.
Finally, multiplying by the
\alpha
of Step 1, for all
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
,

(Tf)(z)=\alpha f(c_{1}z)\text{ or }\alpha f(c_{1}/z).\qed

Remark 4.2
.

The proof of the main theorem only needs three different seminorms:

, and two among
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,2}
,
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty,3}
and

and

,

Then there exist two constants

.

5.
Description of unimodular functions on the unit circle

There exist many approximation results for measurable unimodular functions on the unit circle such as
the Helson–Sarason Theorem
[
15
]
which says that given
\varepsilon>0
and
f
unimodular and continuous on
\mathbb{T}
, there exist finite Blaschke products
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
such that

\left\|f-\frac{B_{1}}{B_{2}}\right\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon.

In this section we investigate factorisation results instead of approximation results for unimodular function on the unit circle. The first one is due to Fatou.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
12
]
)
.

If
f
is analytic on
\mathbb{D}
and
\lim_{|z|\to 1}|f(z)|=1
,
then
f
is a finite Blaschke product.
In particular, if
f
is a function in the disc algebra
A(\mathbb{D})
, then
f
is a finite Blaschke product whenever
f
is unimodular on
\mathbb{T}
.

There is also a meromorphic version of this.

Corollary 5.2

(
[
14
]
)
.

Suppose that
f
is meromorphic on
\mathbb{D}
and extends continuously to
\mathbb{T}
, the unit circle. If
f
is unimodular on
\mathbb{T}
, then
f
is a quotient of two finite Blaschke products.

In 1986, Bourgain solved a problem of factorization posed by Douglas and Rudin.

Theorem 5.3

(
[
5
,
4
]
)
.

Let
f
be a bounded measurable function on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
.
Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

The function
\log|f|
belongs to
L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
,

(ii)

There exist
g,h\in H^{\infty}
such that
f=g\overline{h}
.

In particular, any measurable function unimodular almost everywhere on the unit circle is of the form
f=g\overline{h}
, for some functions
g,h\in H^{\infty}
.

The following theorem is a complete description of unimodular functions on the unit circle that are analytic in a neighbourhood of
\mathbb{T}
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
f
be an analytic map on
\mathbb{A}
. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

The function
f
is unimodular on
\mathbb{T}
,

(ii)

there exists
s\in(1,R]
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and an outer function
g_{0}^{s}
in
H^{\infty}
which extends analytically on
s\mathbb{D}
such that

f(z)=z^{n}\frac{g_{0}^{s}(z)}{\overline{{g_{0}^{s}(1/\overline{z})}}}\quad\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb{A}_{s}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:1/s<\left\lvert z\right\rvert<s\}.

For example, a Blaschke factor
f(z)=(z-\alpha)/(1-\overline{\alpha}z)
with
|\alpha|<1
arises on taking

g_{0}^{s}(z)=1/(1-\overline{\alpha}z)
with
n=1
and
1<s<1/|\alpha|
.

Here is an example showing that the set of unimodular functions on
\mathbb{T}
and analytic in a neighborhood of
\mathbb{T}
is much larger than the set of quotients of finite Blaschke products.

Let
f(z)=\exp(z-1/z)
. Then
f(\xi)=\exp(\xi-\overline{\xi})=\exp(2i\Im(\xi))
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
.
It follows that
f
is unimodular on
\mathbb{T}
and analytic on
\mathbb{C}^{*}
.
In this case we have
s=R
,
n=0
and
g_{0}(z)=\exp(z)
.

Our proof relies on Theorem
5.3
and the following lemma.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
s>1
. Suppose that
g
is analytic and does not vanish in the annulus
\mathbb{A}_{s}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:1/s<\left\lvert z\right\rvert<s\}
.
Then there exist
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
h
analytic in
\mathbb{A}_{s}
such that

g(z)=z^{n}\exp(h(z))\quad\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb{A}_{s}.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{A}_{s}
is not simply connected, the existence of an analytic map
h
in
\mathbb{A}_{s}
such that
g(z)=\exp(h(z))
is not necessarily true.
Now recall that the existence of
h
such that
g=\exp(h)
on a domain
\Omega
is equivalent to

\int_{C}\frac{g^{\prime}(z)}{g(z)}\mathrm{d}z=0\quad\text{ for all Jordan curves }C\text{ in }\Omega.

For
\Omega=\mathbb{A}_{s}
, this is equivalent to

\int_{C_{t}}\frac{g^{\prime}(z)}{g(z)}\mathrm{d}z=0\quad\text{ for all }t\in(1/s,s),\text{ where }C_{t}=t\mathbb{T}.

Note that
\int_{C_{t}}g^{\prime}(z)/g(z)\;\mathrm{d}z\in 2i\pi\mathbb{Z}
, and therefore there exists
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\int_{C_{t}}\frac{g^{\prime}(z)}{g(z)}\mathrm{d}z=2i\pi n.

It follows that
\tilde{g}(z):=g(z)/z^{n}
satisfies

\int_{C_{t}}\frac{\tilde{g}^{\prime}(z)}{\tilde{g}(z)}\mathrm{d}z=0\quad\text{ for all }t\in(1/s,s).

Finally,
g(z)=z^{n}\exp(h(z))
for all
z\in\mathbb{A}_{s}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.4
.

Since
f
is analytic in
\mathbb{A}
and unimodular on the unit circle, there exists
s\in(1,R]
such that
f(z)\neq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{A}_{s}
.
By Lemma
5.5
, there exist
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
h
analytic in
\mathbb{A}_{s}
such that

f(z)=z^{n}\exp(h(z))\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb{A}_{s}.

Note that
h(z)=h_{+}(z)+h_{-}(z)
, where
h_{+}
is analytic on
s\mathbb{D}
,
h_{-}
is analytic on
\mathbb{C}\backslash s^{-1}\mathbb{D}
and
h_{+}(0)=h(0)
.
It follows that
g_{0}:=\exp(h_{+})
is an outer function which extends analytically on
s\mathbb{D}
. Indeed,
\exp(h_{+})
has no zero in
\mathbb{D}
and moreover, since it extends analytically on
s\mathbb{D}
, its singular inner part is trivial. In addition,

g_{1}(z):=\begin{cases}\overline{\exp(h_{-}(1/\overline{z}))},&0<\left\lvert z\right\rvert<s\\
1,&z=0\end{cases}

is also analytic on
s\mathbb{D}
and outer. Therefore, we have

f(z)=z^{n}g_{0}(z)\overline{g_{1}(1/\overline{z})},\quad z\in s\mathbb{D}.

Since
f
is unimodular on
\mathbb{T}
, we get
\left\lvert g_{0}(\xi)\right\rvert\left\lvert g_{1}(\xi)\right\rvert=1
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
. It follows that the outer functions
g_{0}
and
g_{1}
satisfy
g_{1}(z)=1/g_{0}(z)
, so for all
z\in\mathbb{A}_{s}
,

f(z)=z^{n}\frac{g_{0}(z)}{\overline{{g_{0}(1/\overline{z})}}}.

The reverse implication is immediate, since for all
z\in\mathbb{T}
,
1/\overline{z}=z
.
∎

6.
Spectral study of linear isometries on the annulus

In this section we consider the spectrum
\sigma
and point spectrum
\sigma_{p}
of the operators

T_{\alpha,\beta}
and
S_{\alpha,\beta}
defined in (
2
).
It will be convenient to begin with the simplest of these, namely
S_{\alpha,\beta}
,
where
S_{\alpha,\beta}f(z)=\alpha f(\beta/z)
.

Theorem 6.1
.

For
|\alpha|=|\beta|=1
we have
\sigma(S_{\alpha,\beta})=\sigma_{p}(S_{\alpha,\beta})=\{\alpha,-\alpha\}
. Moreover,
\ker(S_{\alpha,\beta}-\lambda\mathrm{Id})
is infinite-dimensional
for
\lambda\in\{\alpha,-\alpha\}
.

Proof.

For
f(z)=z^{k}\pm\beta^{k}/z^{k}
with
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have

S_{\alpha,\beta}f(z)=\alpha\beta^{k}/z^{k}\pm\alpha\beta^{k}/(\beta^{k}/z^{k})=\pm\alpha f(z),

and so we see that
\{\alpha,-\alpha\}\subseteq\sigma_{p}(S_{\alpha,\beta})

and the eigenspaces are infinite-dimensional.

Moreover,
S_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}=\alpha^{2}\mathrm{Id}
, and so

(S_{\alpha,\beta}-\lambda\mathrm{Id})(S_{\alpha,\beta}+\lambda\mathrm{Id})=S_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}-\lambda^{2}\mathrm{Id}=(\alpha^{2}-\lambda^{2})\mathrm{Id},

and thus
S_{\alpha,\beta}-\lambda\mathrm{Id}
is invertible if
\lambda^{2}\neq\alpha^{2}
.
That is,

\sigma_{p}(S_{\alpha,\beta})=\sigma(S_{\alpha,\beta})=\{\alpha,-\alpha\}.\qed

We now turn our attention to
T_{\alpha,\beta}
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{T}
and
T_{\alpha,\beta}:\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})\to\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
defined by

T_{\alpha,\beta}(f)(z)=\alpha f(\beta z),\quad f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A}).

(i)

If there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\beta^{n}=1
, then

\sigma_{p}(T_{\alpha,\beta})=\sigma(T_{\alpha,\beta})=\{\alpha\beta^{k}:0\leq k\leq n-1\}.

Moreover, for all
\lambda\in\sigma_{p}(T_{\alpha,\beta})
,
\mathrm{Ker}(T_{\alpha,\beta}-\lambda\mathrm{Id})
has infinite dimension.

(ii)

If for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\beta^{n}\neq 1
, then

\sigma_{p}(T_{\alpha,\beta})=\{\alpha\beta^{k}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}\subset\sigma(T_{\alpha,\beta})\subset\mathbb{T}.

Moreover, for all
\lambda\in\sigma_{p}(T_{\alpha,\beta})
,
\mathrm{Ker}(T_{\alpha,\beta}-\lambda\mathrm{Id})
has dimension 1.

Proof.

(i)

Let
e_{k}(z)=z^{k}
,
0\leq k\leq n-1
. Then,

T_{\alpha,\beta}(e_{k})(z)=\alpha e_{k}(\beta z)=\alpha(\beta z)^{k}=\alpha\beta^{k}z^{k}=\alpha\beta^{k}e_{k}(z).

Therefore,
\alpha\beta^{k}\in\sigma_{p}(T_{\alpha,\beta})
, and
e_{k}\in\mathrm{Ker}(T_{\alpha,\beta}-\alpha\beta^{k}\mathrm{Id})
. In addition, it is easy to show that for all
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
,
e_{k+n\ell}\in\mathrm{Ker}(T_{\alpha,\beta}-\alpha\beta^{k}\mathrm{Id})
, since
\beta^{k+n\ell}=\beta^{k}
, so the eigenspaces have infinite dimension.

Moreover, note that
T_{\alpha,\beta}^{n}=\alpha^{n}\mathrm{Id}
, so

(\alpha^{n}-\lambda^{n})\mathrm{Id}=T_{\alpha,\beta}^{n}-\lambda^{n}\mathrm{Id}=(T_{\alpha,\beta}-\lambda\mathrm{Id})\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda^{k-1}T_{\alpha,\beta}^{n-k}.

Hence,
T_{\alpha,\beta}-\lambda\mathrm{Id}
is invertible if and only if
\lambda^{n}\neq\alpha^{n}
, i.e.
\lambda\not\in\{\alpha\beta^{k}:0\leq k\leq n-1\}
since
\beta^{n}=1
.

(ii)

Let
f\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
. Note that we can write

f(z)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{k}z^{k}=a_{0}+\sum_{k\geq 1}a_{k}z^{k}+\sum_{k\geq 1}a_{-k}z^{-k},

where the first series converges for
|z|<R
and the second one converges for
|z|>1/R
. Then,

(T_{\alpha,\beta}-\lambda\mathrm{Id})(f)(z)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(\alpha\beta^{k}-\lambda)a_{k}z^{k}.

Let
g=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}b_{k}z^{k}\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
. If
\lambda\not\in\mathbb{T}
, then consider, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
a_{k}=\frac{b_{k}}{\alpha\beta^{k}-\lambda}
.
Thus,

\frac{\left\lvert b_{k}\right\rvert}{1+\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert}<\left\lvert a_{k}\right\rvert<\frac{\left\lvert b_{k}\right\rvert}{\left\lvert 1-\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert\right\rvert}.

Hence, the power series determined by the sequences
(a_{k})
,
(b_{k})
,
(a_{-k})
and
(b_{-k})
have again the same radius of convergence, namely
R
. This means that
f=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{k}z^{k}\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
. Moreover, by construction,
(T_{\alpha,\beta}-\lambda\mathrm{Id})(f)=g
, so
(T_{\alpha,\beta}-\lambda\mathrm{Id})
is invertible:
\lambda\not\in\sigma(T_{\alpha,\beta})
.

Now, let
e_{k}(z)=z^{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then,
T_{\alpha,\beta}(e_{k})(z)=\alpha e_{k}(\beta z)=\alpha\beta^{k}e_{k}(z)
, so we have
\alpha\beta^{k}\in\sigma_{p}(T_{\alpha,\beta})
, and
e_{k}\in\mathrm{Ker}(T_{\alpha,\beta}-\alpha\beta^{k}\mathrm{Id})
.

Let
h\in\mathrm{Ker}(T_{\alpha,\beta}-\alpha\beta^{k}\mathrm{Id})
. Then,

\alpha h(\beta z)=\alpha\beta^{k}h(z)\iff h(\beta z)=\beta^{k}h(z).

This implies that
h(z)\neq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{A}
. Indeed, if
h(z_{0})=0
for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{A}
, then for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
h(\beta^{k}z_{0})=0
. Since
\{\beta^{k}z_{0}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a dense subset of
\left\lvert z_{0}\right\rvert\mathbb{T}
, we obtain
h\equiv 0
.

By Lemma
5.5
,
h(z)=z^{j}\exp(\theta(z))
for some
j\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\theta\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
.
Denote
\psi=\exp(\theta)
. Then,

\displaystyle h(\beta z)=\beta^{k}h(z)

\displaystyle\iff\beta^{j}z^{j}\psi(\beta z)=\beta^{k}z^{j}\psi(z)

\displaystyle\iff\psi(\beta z)=\beta^{k-j}\psi(z).

Let
\sum c_{n}z^{n}
be the Laurent series associated with
\psi
. Then,

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{n}\beta^{n}z^{n}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{n}\beta^{k-j}z^{n}.

Since the Laurent series is unique, we obtain
c_{n}=0
if
n\neq k-j
. Hence,
\psi(z)=c_{k-j}z^{k-j}
for some
c_{k-j}\in\mathbb{C}
. However, if
k-j\neq 0
, then for all
r\in(1/R,R)
,

\int_{r\mathbb{T}}\frac{\psi^{\prime}(z)}{\psi(z)}\mathrm{d}z=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{c_{k-j}(k-j)(re^{it})^{k-j-1}}{c_{k-j}(re^{it})^{k-j}}ire^{it}\mathrm{d}t=2i\pi(k-j)\neq 0.

Therefore, in this case,
\psi
cannot be written as
\exp(\theta)
. We conclude that
j=k
, so
\psi
is constant, and
h\in\mathop{\rm span}\nolimits(e_{k})
. This proves that
\mathrm{Ker}(T_{\alpha,\beta}-\alpha\beta^{k}\mathrm{Id})
has dimension 1. ∎

The spectrum of
T_{\alpha,\beta}
can be different from
\mathbb{T}
if
\beta
is aperiodic. We take our inspiration from the results on the disc
[
2
]
.

Example 6.3
.

Let
\tau>2
. The set of
Diophantine numbers of order
\tau
is defined by

\mathcal{D}(\tau)=\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:\exists\gamma>0,\forall p\in\mathbb{Z},\forall q\in\mathbb{N},\left\lvert\frac{p}{q}-\xi\right\rvert\geq\frac{\gamma}{q^{\tau}}\right\}

Let
\xi\in\mathcal{D}(\tau)
, and
\beta=e^{2i\pi\xi}
. Then, we show that for all
r\in\mathbb{Q}\backslash\mathbb{Z}
,
\lambda=e^{2i\pi r}\not\in\sigma(T_{1,\beta})
. Since multiplying by
\alpha
only rotates our problem, this is also true for
T_{\alpha,\beta}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
.

Indeed, let
\gamma>0
such that
\left\lvert\xi-p/q\right\rvert\geq\gamma q^{-\tau}
, for all
p\in\mathbb{Z}
and
q\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
r=p_{0}/q_{0}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Choose
p\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\left\lvert\xi k-r-p\right\rvert\leq 1/2
. Then,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\beta^{k}-\lambda\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert e^{2i\pi\xi k}-e^{2i\pi r}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert e^{2i\pi\xi k}-e^{2i\pi(r+p)}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert e^{i\pi(\xi k-r-p)}-e^{-i\pi(\xi k-r-p)}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=2\sin\left\lvert\pi(\xi k-r-p)\right\rvert\geq 2\cdot\frac{2}{\pi}\cdot\pi\left\lvert\xi k-r-p\right\rvert,

since the sine function is concave on
[0,\pi/2]
and
\left\lvert\pi(\xi k-r-p)\right\rvert\in[0,\pi/2]
. Consequently,

\frac{1}{\left\lvert\beta^{k}-\lambda\right\rvert}\leq\frac{1}{4\left\lvert\xi k-r-p\right\rvert}=\frac{1}{4k}\frac{1}{\left\lvert\xi-\frac{p_{0}+pq_{0}}{q_{0}k}\right\rvert}\leq\frac{1}{4k}\frac{(q_{0}k)^{\tau}}{\gamma}=ck^{\tau-1},\;\;c:=\frac{q_{0}^{\tau}}{4\gamma}.

Finally,

\displaystyle 1=\lim_{k\to+\infty}\frac{1}{2^{1/k}}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{k\to+\infty}\frac{1}{\left\lvert\beta^{k}-\lambda\right\rvert^{1/k}}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{k\to+\infty}\frac{1}{\left\lvert\beta^{k}-\lambda\right\rvert^{1/k}}\leq\limsup_{k\to+\infty}c^{1/k}k^{(\tau-1)/k}\leq 1.

Hence, if
g(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{n}z^{n}\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
, then

f(z):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{a_{n}}{\beta^{n}-\lambda}z^{n}\in\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A}),

and
f(\beta z)-\lambda f(z)=g(z)
. Finally,
T_{1,\beta}-\lambda\mathrm{Id}
is surjective, and injective by Theorem
6.2
. Thus,
\sigma(T_{1,\beta})\subset\{e^{2i\pi s}:s\not\in\mathbb{Q}\}\cup\{1\}
.

Likewise, it is possible for
\sigma_{p}(T_{\alpha,\beta})
to be strictly contained in
\sigma(T_{\alpha,\beta})
.

Example 6.4
.

For our example, we take
\alpha=1
.

Let
p_{1}=1
and for
n\geq 1
,
p_{n+1}=2^{n^{p_{n}}}
.
Then, the map
g
defined by
g(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(z/R)^{p_{n}}
is in
\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(R\mathbb{D})\subset\mathop{\rm Hol}\nolimits(\mathbb{A})
, where
\mathbb{A}=\{1/R<\left\lvert z\right\rvert<R\}
. Moreover,

\star

For all
n\geq 1
,
p_{n+1}=2^{n^{p_{n}}}\geq 2^{p_{n}}\geq p_{n}
.

\star

For all
n\geq 1
,
p_{n}
is a power of two, hence an integer.

\star

For all
n\geq 1
, denote
p_{n}=2^{q_{n}}
.
Then,
q_{n+1}=n^{p_{n}}\geq p_{n}\geq q_{n}
, so

\frac{p_{n+1}}{p_{n}}=2^{q_{n+1}-q_{n}}\in\mathbb{N}.

Moreover,
q_{n+1}-q_{n}=n^{p_{n}}-q_{n}\geq np_{n}-q_{n}\geq(n-1)p_{n}\geq n-1
, so
p_{n+1}/p_{n}\geq 2^{n-1}
.

Now we take

\beta=\exp(i\pi\theta),\quad\hbox{where}\quad\theta=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p_{n}}.

Note that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we may write

\theta=\frac{\ell_{n}}{p_{n}}+\varepsilon_{n},

where
\ell_{n}
is an integer and

0<\varepsilon_{n}=\sum_{k\geq n+1}\frac{1}{p_{k}}=\frac{1}{p_{n+1}}\sum_{k\geq n+1}\frac{p_{n+1}}{p_{k}}\leq\frac{1}{p_{n+1}}\sum_{k\geq n+1}\frac{1}{2^{k-2}}\leq\frac{2}{p_{n+1}}.

In addition,
p_{n+1}\geq p_{n}^{n+1}
(this is immediate for
n=1,2
; when
n\geq 3
, it comes from the fact that if
p_{n}=2^{q_{n}}
, then
(n+1)q_{n}\leq(n+1)p_{n}\leq n^{p_{n}}=q_{n+1}
). Therefore

0<\varepsilon_{n}\leq\frac{2}{p_{n}^{n+1}}\leq\frac{1}{p_{n}^{n}}.

This means that
\theta
is a Liouville (hence, irrational) number.
Thus, we have
-1\not\in\sigma_{p}(T_{\alpha,\beta})
.

We claim that
-1\in\sigma(T_{\alpha,\beta})
.
To solve the equation
f(\beta z)+f(z)=g(z)
, with
g(z)=\sum_{k\geq 1}(z/R)^{k}
, we require

f(z)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{1+\beta^{k}}(z/R)^{k}
. However,

\beta^{p_{n}}=\exp(i\pi p_{n}\theta)=\exp\left(i\pi\sum_{k\geq n}\frac{p_{n}}{p_{k}}\right)=-\exp\left(i\pi\sum_{k\geq n+1}\frac{p_{n}}{p_{k}}\right),

so we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lvert 1+\beta^{p_{n}}\right\rvert=2\left\lvert\sin\left(\pi\sum_{k\geq n+1}\frac{p_{n}}{p_{k}}\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}2\pi\sum_{k\geq n+1}\frac{p_{n}}{p_{k}}

\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}2\pi\frac{p_{n}}{p_{n+1}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}2\pi\frac{p_{n}}{2^{n^{p_{n}}}}.

Looking at the coefficient
c_{n}=\frac{1}{\beta^{p_{n}}+1}
of
(z/R)^{p_{n}}
in
f
, we see that

|c_{n}|^{1/p_{n}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}\frac{2^{\frac{1}{p_{n}}n^{p_{n}}}}{p_{n}^{1/p_{n}}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\infty.

The radius of convergence of the series for
f
is then
0
, which implies that
-1
is in the spectrum. Note that if we take
R=1
in the above estimations, then we obtain an example of composition operator induced by a rotation on the disc for which the point spectrum is different from the spectrum, thus providing an example that complements the results of
[
2
]
.
