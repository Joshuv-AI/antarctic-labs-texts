A qualitative study on family health needs of community-dwelling disabled older adults based on existence-relatedness-growth theory
Authors: Mengyuan Zhao, Yan Zhang, Peiyao Yuan, Xinjie Zhang, Lijun Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs the primary living environment for disabled older adults, families play a crucial role in disease prevention and maintaining their health. However, research has found that both disabled older adults and their family members experience numerous physiological, psychological, and social adaptation problems when adjusting to the changes brought by disability, severely impacting the overall health status of the family. Therefore, guided by the ERG (Existence-Relatedness-Growth) theory, this study aims to understand the family health needs of families with disabled older adults in the community, providing a basis for improving the health level of these families and developing targeted intervention programs. From December 2024 to February 2025, this study employed purposive and snowball sampling to select 12 pairs of disabled older adults and their primary caregivers from communities under the jurisdiction of Zhengzhou City, Henan Province for semi-structured interviews. Thematic analysis was applied to organize and analyze the interview data. Deductive analysis indicated that the famliy health needs of families with disabled older adults in the community can be summarized into the following three themes: existence needs (daily living needs, economic support needs, environmental modification needs), relatedness needs (family communication needs, social resource connection needs, social participation needs), and growth needs (autonomy and dignity maintenance needs, family development needs, demand for technology-enabled solutions). The results show that the family health needs of families with disabled older adults in the community are unique and diverse. Community health workers and social workers can develop and implement effective strategies based on the different levels of family needs to promote the health level of families with disabled older adults and improve the overall quality of life of these families. Keywords: Disabled older adults, Family health needs, ERG theory, Community-dwelling

Background

The global population aging has been accelerating since the 21st century. It is projected that by 2030, the population aged 60 and above will increase to 1.4 billion worldwide, reaching 2.1 billion by 2050 [1]. This trend is characterized not only by an expansion of the older population, but also by a concurrent and progressive intensification of both population aging (increasing proportion of the oldest-old) and functional disability. The World Health Organization (WHO) published the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) [2], which defines disability as an umbrella term covering impairments, activity limitations, and participation restrictions. It reflects the negative aspects of the interaction between an individual (with a health condition) and their contextual factors (environmental and personal factors).Data from the Global Burden of Disease Study reveal that functional limitations arising from chronic conditions and aging have emerged as a paramount challenge to health in the older adult population [3]. Chronic diseases, exemplified by cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), not only cause impairments in physical functioning—such as mobility restrictions and diminished respiratory capacity—but are also frequently accompanied by mental health issues, including cognitive decline, depression, and anxiety. The coexistence and interaction of physical, cognitive, and psychosocial functional limitations result in highly complex and diverse care needs among disabled older adults, placing substantial pressure on both formal care systems and informal care networks worldwide [4].

This global demographic and health challenge is particularly acute in China. China’s “14th Five-Year Plan for Healthy Aging“ [5] indicates that the health status of Chinese older adults remains suboptimal, with a gradual rise in semi-disabled and disabled older adults. According to China’s population census data, approximately 77.25% of disabled older adults currently live with family members, and 53.32% rely primarily on family members as their main income source [6]. This data underscores the central role of families as the primary living and caregiving environment for disabled older adults in China [7] — a role deeply rooted in the cultural tradition of filial piety and closely associated with the current developmental stage of China’s formal long-term care system.

The link between family and health originated in the 1930s and has gained increasing recognition within the medical and health fields [8]. Family health is defined as “a resource at the family unit level, developed through the intersection of each member’s health, capabilities, behaviors, characteristics, and internal interactions, combined with the family’s external resources in physical, social, emotional, economic, and medical domains“ [9]. Research demonstrates that improvements in family health levels can enhance health literacy, modify health behaviors among older adults [10], and elevate their quality of life [11]. To address the challenges of population aging, the Chinese government has shifted its national aging strategy from a singular focus on older adults care to a comprehensive, multi‑pronged model centered on community and family [12], advancing family health has also become an important component of the Healthy China initiative. Healthy families foster a sense of belonging and cultivate members’ capacities for personal development, mutual care, and accountability [9], thus serving as a vital foundation for addressing China’s challenges of population aging and functional disability.

Despite these efforts, families caring for disabled older adults face profound challenges. Disabled older adults and their family members face numerous physiological, psychological [13], and social adaptation challenges when adjusting to disability-related changes. These challenges not only severely compromise individual health status but also, through family interactions, profoundly undermine overall family health levels.The specific physical, cognitive, and mental health status of disabled older adults significantly shapes the caregiving burden experienced by their families. Older adults with total physical dependency exhibit 24-hour dependency, necessitating frequent assistance with basic nursing care tasks such as repositioning, hygiene, and medication administration. Caregivers often provide over 12 h of daily support, and the sustained high-intensity physical demands predispose them to musculoskeletal strain and sleep disorders [14]. In contrast, caring for older adults with cognitive impairment involves managing symptoms like wandering, day-night reversal, and aggressive behaviors. This requires constant vigilance, imposing significantly higher psychological stress on caregivers compared to those assisting individuals with solely physical limitations [15]. Furthermore, disabled older adults experiencing depression or anxiety may exhibit food refusal or social withdrawal. Caregivers in these situations must not only manage daily living tasks but also invest substantial emotional resources in psychological support, consequently exhibiting increased rates of depression compared to the general population [16].

The China Family Planning Association, in its national Family Health Promotion Initiative [17], explicitly advocates a family health needs-oriented approach to guide families in establishing health-conscious values and improving health literacy. Consequently, understanding the family health needs of older adults with functional limitations is an essential prerequisite for effectively enhancing family health levels and, ultimately, improving the well-being of these older adults. Most existing studies have focused on the individual needs of disabled older adults or their primary caregivers [18, 19], while research exploring family health needs from a holistic family perspective remains limited [20]. This research gap is significant, as family health needs are not merely a simple sum of individual needs, but a unique system of demands shaped by family interactions, role division, and shared goals. The ERG (Existence-Relatedness-Growth) theory posits three core human needs: survival (Existence), social connectedness (Relatedness), and self-development (Growth) [21]. It provides a robust theoretical framework for investigating family health needs from a holistic perspective. From a family-oriented lens, addressing the health needs of disabled older adults families requires not only addressing individual physiological and psychological demands but also prioritizing relational harmony and collective growth among family members to meet their diversified health requirements.Thus, guided by the ERG (Existence, Relatedness, Growth) needs theory, this study employs in-depth interviews with families of older adults with functional limitations. It aims to identify and elucidate their multidimensional and multilevel family health needs, thereby providing a scientific foundation for developing targeted and effective family health promotion intervention strategies.

Methods

Study design and setting

This qualitative study was conducted between December 2024 and March 2025. Qualitative data were collected from disabled older adults and family caregivers in communities under the jurisdiction of Zhengzhou, Henan Province. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Zhengzhou University (ZZUIRB2025-17).

This study employed semi-structured interviews [22]to systematically investigate the family health needs of disabled older adults. Grounded in deductive thematic analysis methodology [23], the analytical process was structured using ERG theory framework [21], which categorizes fundamental human motivations into three hierarchical dimensions: Existence needs, Relatedness needs, and Growth needs. Existence needs pertain to the most fundamental requirements for material survival, encompassing both physiological and safety needs. Relatedness needs refer to the individual’s requirement for maintaining significant interpersonal relationships. Growth needs primarily represent the intrinsic desire for personal development.The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist was used to report the study design and results [24].

Researcher characteristics

The interviews were conducted by two female nursing graduate students (MZ and LX) trained in qualitative research methods. One researcher (MZ) conducted the interviews, while the other (LX) documented real-time observations and non-verbal cues. Following data collection, both researchers collaboratively engaged in verbatim transcription and thematic coding of the interview materials. To ensure methodological rigor, both investigators possessed master’s-level expertise in qualitative data collection and analysis, with specialized training in minimizing observer bias and enhancing inter-coder reliability through consensus-building discussions.

Participants

The participants were recruited through purposive and snowball sampling. Community healthcare workers in three communities identified and contacted potential participants. Community doctors and nurses provided initial information about the study to eligible individuals during outpatient visits. Then, the research team contacted interested participants to further explain the research objectives, voluntary nature, and confidentiality measures before obtaining informed consent. In addition, registered participants were encouraged to recommend other eligible individuals. Eventually, 7 disabled older adults and their main caregivers were directly included, and another 5 disabled older adults and their main caregivers were included through snowball sampling.

Inclusion criteria for disabled older adults : (1) Aged 60 or above; (2) Having lived in the community for over a year; 3)Assessed as at least mildly disabled using the Physical Self - Maintenance Scale [25]; 4)Having basic communication skills; 5)Providing informed consent and willingly participating in the study. Exclusion criteria: Individuals with severe mental illnesses who are unable to cooperate. Inclusion criteria for family caregivers: (1) Averaging four or more hours of care daily; (2) Living with the disabled older adults individual; 3)Aged 18 or above; 4)Normal comprehension and clear verbal expression; 5)Signing an informed consent form.Exclusion Criteria: (1) Caring for other seriously ill patients and unable to commit enough time and energy to the study; (2) Paid caregivers.

Data collection

Two principal investigators conducted semi-structured face-to-face interviews with disabled older adults and family caregivers between December 2024 and March 2025. The disabled older adults and their primary caregivers were interviewed together, which allowed the researchers to observe the interactive dynamics of the family while collecting information about family health needs. The interviews took place at community health service centers and in the homes of the disabled older adults participants. The entire interview process was audio-recorded, while researchers simultaneously observed and objectively documented participants’ non-verbal communication cues, including eye contact, tone of voice, pitch, and body language. Each interview lasted between 30 and 90 min. Guided by an interview protocol (see Table 1), researchers focused on participants’ demographic characteristics and family health needs. Within 24 h post-interview, all verbal content was transcribed verbatim, incorporating annotations of non-verbal expressions such as gestures and facial expressions, as well as notes on family interaction dynamics observed during the interview. The transcribed text was returned to participants for verification. Finally, the verified Chinese transcripts were translated into English for analysis.

Data analysis

Two researchers agreed to end the participant recruitment process when no novel information seemed to emerge from participant interviews. Interview records were stored on a secured device to fully protect the privacy of participants. The audio-taped interviews were listened repeatedly and transcribed verbatim. Before the formal analysis, the interviewees were coded with letters and numbers P1-P12, C1-C12 to process personal sensitive information such as their names. Then directed content analysis method was used to extract the effective content, code, classify and simplify the relevant content, extract the theme and return to the interviewees for confirmation, and finally refine the interview theme.

An analysis team comprised two analysts (MZ and LX). Firstly, the data were open coded using a line-by-line coding process. The two analysts engaged in data immersion to be fully familiar with the data. A letter, word, sentence, or a part of the page was coded as the unit of analysis. The analysts carefully read the transcripts to identify evidence of the theoretical derived codes. Repetitive words, concepts and phrases were recognized. Then, these codes were divided into broader categories to reflect the family health needs of disabled older adults within the community. Finally, the codes and categories of each transcript was compared between the analysts, and differences were discussed and resolved to ensure consistency of the research team [26]. The analysis continued until no new information emerged from the interviews (data saturation) [27].

To ensure credibility, the two coders consulted with each other to resolve any methodological issues or ambiguities in data analysis. A third senior researcher with professional expertise in the field reviewed the study findings to confirm whether these descriptions accurately reflected the family health needs of disabled older adults.

The data were analyzed and are presented according to the three core themes of the ERG theory: existence needs, relatedness needs, and growth and development needs:

Theme 1: existence needs: core needs for survival and safety

Daily living needs

The vast majority of respondents reported significant difficulties in providing daily care, with disabled older adults being completely or partially dependent on caregivers for basic activities of daily living (such as bathing, dressing, turning over in bed, and taking medication). Older adults expressed marked frustration over their inconvenience in daily life and loss of independence. For disabled older adults who require assistance with intimate care tasks, fundamental care needs often remain unmet due to considerations of gender ethics and familial norms.

P11: “Usually, my daughter-in-law takes care of me. But since I’m not her biological father, she doesn’t feel comfortable helping me with things like bathing and dressing. I have to wait for my son to come back for those tasks. If he doesn’t come home, I just stay in my clothes…"

Caregivers, on the other hand, emphasize the sustained physical and time-related burdens of daily care. C12: “Caring for her keeps me busy all day. I also have household chores to do. I often don’t have time to eat properly; I just grab something quick to eat…"

C5: “Before he got sick, it was my husband who took care of me. But after he suddenly became paralyzed, I had to learn everything all over again. And with his large build, sometimes it takes us a long time just to get him into bed for the night…"

Economic support needs

Most respondents reported that severe disability significantly exacerbates household financial pressure. Families face not only direct costs (medication, rehabilitation, assistive devices) but also indirect costs (caregivers losing employment), factors that collectively contribute to an ever-increasing economic burden.

C4: “Since I have to care for her, I can’t go out to work, so I had to raise a few ducks to make ends meet. The wheelchair’s wheel broke, and the cushion is also torn. I can’t afford to replace them. It would be great if they could provide us with a new wheelchair…"

P6: “The monthly subsidy is less than 100 yuan. Medication alone costs hundreds of yuan. With occasional illnesses, every month is tight. We can only eat whatever vegetables are available. It would be better if the subsidy could be increased…"

Environmental modification needs

Most respondents reported that their living environments present numerous safety hazards and mobility barriers. Due to physical limitations, disabled older adults are extremely concerned about falling and urgently call for home and community environmental renovations to improve accessibility; caregivers, meanwhile, emphasize that unsuitable environments create additional caregiving burdens.

P10: “Every door has a threshold. With my walking stick, I’m always at risk of falling. Last time I visited a neighbor, I saw they had installed handrails and ramps. It would be great if I could have something like that at my home…"

C11: “Our community is an old one with uneven ground. Pushing the older adults person out for sunbathing is quite an effort. The fitness area has steps around it, and it takes a lot of effort to get the wheelchair in…"

This sense of environmental insecurity also breeds constant fear and activity restriction among disabled older adults, further diminishing their quality of life.

P5: “After my stroke and paralysis, I now sit on the ground all day. I dare not sit anywhere slightly elevated for fear of falling. I’m really scared…"

Theme 2: relatedness needs: building a support network and belonging

Family communication needs

Disabled older adults, due to partial or complete loss of physical functions, are prone to negative emotions such as anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem. Family members caring for them also face significant psychological pressure. This mutual understanding, acceptance, and emotional support within the family are crucial for alleviating and releasing these negative emotions.

Some disabled older adults described deliberately creating conflicts as a way to attract their family members’ attention and concern.

P3: “During the day, everyone goes to work, leaving me alone at home with nothing to do. I feel empty inside. I know it’s not right to deliberately create conflicts, but only through such behavior can I attract the concern of my family…"

However, caregivers often fail to recognize the underlying emotional needs behind the complaints, typically responding with withdrawal or irritability, which only serves to deepen the conflict between both parties.

C3: “My father always finds fault with everything. Today it’s the food isn’t cooked well, tomorrow it’s that I didn’t buy his medicine. Actually, there’s nothing serious, but it makes us very irritable…"

Moreover, given that caregiving labor is both continuous and confined to the domestic sphere, it is often not recognized as “work” by other family members, consequently leading to emotional exhaustion among caregivers.

C7: “I take care of her every day but never get any appreciation. I’m so tired that I can’t even lift my arms. When my family comes back and doesn’t help with any chores, and I say something to them, they accuse me of doing nothing at home except caring for the her. (Wipes tears)…"

Social resource connection needs

Almost all respondents reported encountering numerous barriers when seeking external social support. Caregivers, who bear the primary responsibility for accessing resources, highlighted issues such as cumbersome application processes and having nowhere to turn for help.

C12: “Every time I take her to the hospital, my son have to wake up early and wait for the online registration. After arriving at the hospital, we have to go through a series of complex procedures and tests. The process is so tiring that I feel exhausted even before the consultation begins…”

C2: “I heard there’s some subsidy for people with disabilities, but I have no idea what it entails or how to apply for it…"

Disabled older adults, as the primary users of these resources, mainly reported a mismatch between the available resources and their actual needs, making it difficult to meet their practical requirements.

P12: “My child heard that there are subsidies for home modifications to accommodate the older adults, but since my spouse and I are not registered in this community, we have to pay for the handrails ourselves…”

P8: “Sometimes the community offers door-to-door services for disabled older adults people like us, such as cleaning or bathing. However, my spouse can already handle these tasks. It would be better if they could provide door-to-door rehabilitation services…”

Social participation needs

Almost all respondents indicated that their level of social participation significantly decreased after becoming disabled. Disabled older adults have a strong desire to reintegrate into society and regain their sense of self-worth, but this is difficult to achieve due to physical limitations and fear of social prejudice.

P5: “I used to be a Party member and active in community service. Now, even speaking is an effort, and I feel like a burden to everyone. I sometimes think it would be better to just disappear…”

C2: “Every time I wanted to take my mother out for a walk, she was afraid that neighbors would gossip about her incontinence. In the end, we just stayed at home, looking at each other…”

Caregivers also indicated that, due to their caregiving responsibilities, their own social activities have significantly decreased.

C7: “If I finally get a chance to go out with friends, when it’s time for dinner I have to go back home to cook for my mom. I hardly ever get to eat out anymore…"

Some caregivers, resenting others’ negative evaluations of their caregiving abilities, deliberately avoid social activities.

C9: “At family gatherings, my father’s siblings often comment on how he’s lost weight and doesn’t seem as lively as before. Their words imply that I’m not taking good care of him. Eventually, I started to avoid these gatherings…”

Theme 3: growth and development needs: the pursuit of dignity and capability enhancement

Autonomy and dignity maintenance needs

Disabled older adults commonly report experiencing violations of personal privacy and a loss of dignity during daily care and medical visits, which evokes strong feelings of shame and powerlessness.

P9: “My children change my clothes and wipe my body without drawing the curtain. Although they are family members, it’s embarrassing with people coming and going all the time…"

P4: “The two doctors just lifted my clothes to look at my bedsores and shouted loudly, ‘How are your family members taking care of you?’ At that moment, I wished the ground would swallow me up…"

Caregivers also face the loss of their personal identity and encounter social prejudice due to their caregiving work, severely undermining their sense of dignity and self-worth.

C1: “I haven’t been to a mall for three years. I wear pajamas as going-out clothes and can’t wear skirts because it’s not convenient for taking care of the older adults. In community registration, I am called the ‘family member of Patient XX’. It’s as if I don’t have a name of my own…"

C6: “I take care of him every day, cleaning up his waste. When I go out and chat with others, some people say I have a strange smell. It’s really awkward…"

Family development needs

Long-term care for disabled older adults forces families to abandon or postpone their development plans. The costs of care are passed down through generations, affecting not only current caregivers but also the developmental opportunities available to younger family members.

Marriage involves considerations at the family level, and visible caregiving responsibilities can indeed impact marriage prospects. Disabled older adults often experience deep self-reproach, feeling that they hinder their family’s life planning.

P7: “I know without them saying it that because of me, my grandson has a disadvantage in finding a wife. I’m considered a burden to the family…”

In Chinese families, adult children often pool their resources together with their parents, and a parent’s disability can disrupt the children’s life plans and career advancement.

C8: “After my spouse became paraplegic, everything requires money—diapers, medicine, rehabilitation. My child had saved money to buy a new house, but most of their savings have been spent. (sigh)…”

C3: “I used to have a job, but I had to quit to take care of the older person and just do odd jobs nearby. I was almost promoted to store manager…”

Technology-enabled solutions needs

With the advancement of information technology, an increasing number of smart devices and applications are being used in health management. However, these technological products often fail to fully consider users’ actual capabilities and usage contexts during the design phase, and there is a lack of training on their use. As a result, such devices struggle to fulfill their intended functions.

P1: “The smart wristband provided by the government can monitor health data in real time—I find it really practical! There’s a manual, but my education level isn’t high. If there were a pictorial version, I could get the hang of this ‘little health assistant’ sooner…"

P10: “My child bought me a little robot that can chat, check the weather, and watch plays, but I’m still not very good at using it. Someone needs to show me a few more times…"

C11: “I found the medication record feature on this app is quite thoughtfully designed, though I’m still not entirely familiar with the page navigation logic when operating it. But I’ve specifically written down the steps in a notebook and plan to practice a few more times when I’m free. In the future, I can just show this app when taking the older person for follow-up visits.”

Discussion

Addressing the multidimensional survival needs for families with disabled older adults

In this study, families of disabled older adults not only urgently need to address difficulties in daily care but also require professional health guidance and environmental safety guarantees. Disabled older adults are highly dependent on others, and family members, as the main informal care resources, bear the vast majority of care responsibilities, which seriously affects their quality of life. Moreover, multiple survey results show that the care needs of most disabled older adults remain unmet [28]. The reason for this may be that formal care resources have not been fully utilized [29].This situation can be improved by strengthening community nursing services. Professional nursing staff can be regularly arranged to visit homes and assist with basic daily care tasks such as bathing and dressing, thereby alleviating the care burden on families. Regarding the acquisition of health information and care skills, respondents reported chaotic information sources and a lack of professional guidance. In response, community medical staff can regularly organize health lectures and practical training [30], conduct standardized and standardized skill demonstrations and guidance for common problems among disabled older adults, such as choking and turning over. At the same time, authoritative health knowledge can be pushed through online platforms to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the information.Due to the decline in physical function, disabled older adults have high requirements for environmental safety, and there are many potential safety hazards in the existing environment [31]. Community medical staff can collaborate with the community to conduct home environment assessments. Based on the assessment results, they can provide professional environmental renovation suggestions to the older adults’ families, such as installing handrails and laying non - slip mats. They can also guide family members on how to reduce the risk of falls by reasonably arranging furniture and clearing passageways. Additionally, subsidies for renovations can be applied for families in need, creating a safe living environment for disabled older adults, effectively meeting their survival needs, and improving their quality of life and safety.

Strengthening the support network for families with disabled older adults

The study reveals that families of disabled older adults urgently need to establish a family - centered supportive network to meet the family’s emotional, resource, and social participation needs [32]. In terms of emotional support, disabled older adults are prone to negative emotions due to physical disabilities, and family caregivers also bear enormous psychological pressure, making it difficult to effectively meet the emotional demands of both parties. Nursing staff can conduct home visits [33] to assess the psychological state of the older adults and caregivers, provide psychological counseling using empathetic communication skills, and organize family emotional support groups to guide family members to understand and express their emotions, thereby promoting emotional bonds within the family.When it comes to accessing social resources, respondents face problems such as information scarcity and unclear access channels. Community workers can sort out and integrate information on local caregiver agencies, day - care centers, subsidy policies for disabled older adults, and other social resources, compile them into brochures and distribute them to families. They should also build an online resource platform for real - time updates, conduct regular home assessments, and guide families to accurately connect with their needs.Disabled older adults and their families aspire to have convenient and abundant medical resources. Communities should establish and improve a hierarchical medical service system, introduce the family doctor contract service [34], promote the establishment of green channels for medical treatment for disabled older adults, provide home - based medical services, and popularize the sharing system of electronic health records to ensure the continuity of medical treatment for the older adults in different locations [35]. After becoming disabled, the older adults often experience a loss of self - worth, and family social interactions are restricted, leading to a decrease in social participation. Community workers can organize low - intensity social activities suitable for disabled older adults within the community, such as handicraft making and story - sharing sessions, to help them integrate into society. At the same time, caregiver experience - sharing meetings can be organized, enabling caregivers to gain emotional resonance and care - giving experience through communication, reducing social isolation caused by care - giving work, and facilitating the establishment of a sound social support network and a sense of belonging for disabled older adults and their families [36].

Focusing on dignity and development for families with disabled older adults

As a high - order demand in the hierarchy of human needs, the need for growth and development is not only crucial for maintaining the dignity and promoting the ability enhancement of disabled older adults but also serves as an important pillar for the sustainable development of their families [37]. In terms of maintaining autonomy and dignity, the physical disabilities of older adults often lead to the neglect of their personal privacy and loss of life autonomy [38], while family caregivers also experience a loss of self - identity in their caregiving roles. Community healthcare workers can conduct dignity care assessments [39] and formulate privacy protection plans, such as using privacy curtains during caregiving and respecting the older adults’ living habits. At the same time, caregiver training programs can be carried out to guide them in adopting a consultative care approach. For example, involving the older adults in decision - making processes by jointly formulating diet and activity plans, which helps to enhance the autonomy of the older adults [40]. Additionally, in community services, the individuality of caregivers should be respected, and they should be encouraged to participate in community activities to realize their personal values.The long - term care of disabled older adults severely restricts family development plans [41]. Nursing teams can collaborate with social workers to provide families with economic resource integration and planning services, such as assisting in applying for medical assistance and disability subsidies [42] to alleviate financial pressures. Moreover, phased family development plans can be formulated according to the actual situation of each family. For instance, for families with childcare needs, community nursery services can be arranged, enabling families to progress with important life plans while taking care of the older adults.Smart devices often fail to fulfill their intended functions due to complex operations and mismatched features [43]. Community healthcare workers can collaborate with volunteers to conduct one - on - one device usage training, create simple operation manuals, and collect feedback from the older adults and their families. In addition, they can work with technical teams to optimize device functions, develop aging - friendly designs such as dialect recognition and simplified voice commands [44], improving the practicality of the devices and effectively meeting the growth and development needs of families with disabled older adults.

Strengths and limitations

This study deeply analyzes the family health needs of disabled older adults based on ERG theory, providing clear intervention targets for healthcare professionals to assess and formulate intervention measures. Specifically, it enables the precise allocation of medical resources and optimization of care services according to survival needs; the design of family support and communication intervention programs targeting relational needs; and the implementation of capacity-building training and development plans centered around growth needs. Ultimately, this approach ensures a high degree of alignment between intervention measures and family health needs, effectively improving the health status of families with disabled older adults. A notable methodological feature of the study is the use of joint interviews with disabled older adults and their caregivers, which allowed direct observation of family interaction dynamics, facilitated real-time cross-verification of data, and better captured holistic family health needs consistent with the study’s family-centered perspective.

However, the study has several limitations. First, disabled older adults with impaired consciousness or communication difficulties were excluded due to the interview-based design. Second, the presence of community health service staff during some interviews may have led to overly positive evaluations of community services by participants. Third, joint interviews carried the risk of social desirability bias: participants may have avoided expressing negative or conflicting views in each other’s presence, and one participant’s emotional state may have influenced the other’s expression, limiting the full disclosure of individual needs. Relevant hesitant or incomplete expressions were documented in detail for in-depth analysis to mitigate such biases.

Conclusion

Based on the ERG Theory of Needs, this study conducted semi - structured interviews with families of disabled older adults within the community. It revealed that these families have special and diverse health needs, including existence, relatedness, and growth needs. Community health workers and social workers can develop tailored support strategies that address the different levels of family needs, aiming to meet the specific health requirements of disabled older adults’ families in a targeted and individualized manner. These findings can inform the further development of support to enhance the overall quality of life for these families.
