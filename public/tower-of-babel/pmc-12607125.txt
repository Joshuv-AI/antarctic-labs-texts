Current status and time trends in the burden of environmental heat and cold exposure among the population aged 60 years and older: an analysis for the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021
Authors: Jiawei Zhang, Zimeng Xu, Jinshi Zhang, Yicheng Huang, Bin Zhu, Yueming Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClimate change significantly challenges public health, particularly through extreme weather events that disproportionately affect older adults. Those aged 60 and older are especially vulnerable to environmental heat and cold exposure (EHCE) due to physiological and healthcare access challenges. This study aims to analyze EHCE-related prevalence, incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) using the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 database.MethodsWe analyzed data from the GBD 2021 to estimate global, regional, and national EHCE-related metrics for adults aged 60 and older, including age-standardized prevalence (ASPR), incidence (ASIR), mortality (ASMR), and DALY rates (ASDR). Temporal trends from 1990 to 2021 were assessed using joinpoint regression and annual average percentage changes (AAPCs). Projections for 2022–2030 were developed using a Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model.ResultsIn 2021, there were 4.4 million global EHCE cases (ASPR: 413.2 per 100,000; 95% UI: 362.4–475.6), with 301.2 thousand incident cases (ASIR: 28.2 per 100,000; 95% UI: 18.9–41), 19,078 deaths (ASMR: 1.8 per 100,000; 95% UI: 1.1–2.3), and 507.4 thousand DALYs (ASDR: 47.2 per 100,000; 95% UI: 34.4–57.5). High-SDI countries exhibited the highest prevalence (ASPR: 707.4), while low-SDI countries had the highest mortality (ASMR: 3.0). Regionally, Eastern Europe reported the highest burden, whereas Southeast Asia showed the lowest. Nationally, Greenland had the highest prevalence (ASPR: 2221.2), while Mauritius had the lowest (ASPR: 75.4).From 1990 to 2021, significant global declines were observed in ASPR (AAPC: -1.33), ASIR (AAPC: -1.47), and ASMR (AAPC: -0.83). However, reductions varied across SDI regions, with middle-SDI countries showing the largest decreases in ASPR (AAPC: -2.31) and ASIR (AAPC:-2.14). The largest regional reductions in ASPR occurred in East Asia (AAPC: -1.91) and Eastern Europe (AAPC: -1.65), while Western Europe experienced a slight increase (AAPC: 0.10). Nationally, Albania exhibited the steepest decline in ASPR (AAPC: -3.68), whereas France recorded the largest increase (AAPC: 2.55). Projections suggest continued global declines in all metrics through 2030.ConclusionsThe global burden of EHCE among the elderly remains substantial; however, it has exhibited a slight yet significant decline over the past three decades, with variations across different regions and sociodemographic profiles. These findings underscore the importance of targeted public health strategies to mitigate the impact of extreme temperatures on vulnerable older populations. By providing comprehensive data and trend analyses, this research informs climate resilience policies and adaptive interventions to address the growing challenges posed by climate change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25115-8.

Introduction

Climate change represent a significant public health issue, linked to a considerable increase in mortality and morbidity [1]. As climate change progresses, it brings about a range of challenges, including more frequent and intense extreme weather events, such as heatwaves and cold snaps, which disproportionately affect older individuals [2]. The increased vulnerability of older adults can be attributed to a variety of physiological and social determinants. Key contributors include living alone, the widespread presence of multiple comorbidities, and the extensive use of medications. In addition, there is a reduced capacity for physiological adaptation and behavioral response to extreme temperatures, which is further exacerbated by limited access to healthcare services and inadequately climate-controlled living environments [3].

Environmental heat and cold exposure (EHCE) refer to the impact that extreme temperatures—either hot or cold—have on the human body and its ability to maintain normal physiological functions. Previous study [4] utilized data from the Emergency Events Database to analyze extreme temperature-related events and mortality in Europe, revealing that while cold waves were the most frequent extreme temperature events (54.7%) from 1988 to 2019, heat waves were far deadlier, accounting for 95.5% of the 137,533 deaths recorded, with major incidents notably affecting Russia and parts of Western Europe. Another report analyzed weather-related mortality in the U.S. from 2006 to 2010, finding that [5] most deaths were due to excessive cold (63%) and heat (31%), with variations observed across demographic and area-based subgroups. The discrepant findings underscore that temperature‑related mortality is context dependent, driven by region, urbanization, and socioeconomic status. Accounting for these determinants is essential to identify vulnerable populations. In Europe, excess heat mortality is magnified by increasingly extreme heatwaves, population aging, limited cooling and heat‑resilient building adaptations, and pronounced urban heat‑island effects. In the United States, the higher share of cold‑related deaths reflects methodological choices (death‑certificate coding vs. disaster databases), a broad climatic gradient, inadequate heating in rural and low‑income areas, and the longer, diffuse lag structure of cold exposure. These factors—rather than intrinsic thermal lethality—explain the regional divergence. And a comprehensive study [6] on temperature-mortality associations in South Africa from 1997 to 2013 revealed that both extreme cold and heat significantly elevated mortality risks, especially among the elderly population. Recent research [3] has demonstrated that aging negatively affects thermoregulatory functions, including diminished vascular responses and reduced sweat production, resulting in increased heat storage and elevated core body temperatures in older adults during both heat and cold exposure. These age-related impairments, primarily attributed to reduced nitric oxide bioavailability and altered autonomic regulation, heighten the risk of heat-related illnesses, such as heat stroke, and cold-related conditions, such as hypothermia. Consequently, this decline in thermoregulatory efficiency compromises the body’s ability to maintain thermal balance, thereby increasing vulnerability to extreme temperature exposures.

Although there is currently considerable research [7, 8] on the health impacts of extreme temperatures on the elderly, gaps remain in understanding its global disease burden and long-term trends of EHCE on this population. To date, there has not been a comprehensive analysis of EHCE based on latest GBD 2021 data. In this study, we aimed to describe the EHCE-related disease burden including prevalence, incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) and to further analyze these factors’ temporal trends among adults aged 60 years and older.

Methods

Date sources

The GBD 2021 study (https://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-2021) evaluated the burden of 371 diseases, injuries and impairments and 88 risk factors from 1990 to 2021 in 204 countries and territories and 21 regions. Detailed descriptions of the study design and methods have been reported [9]. The data on prevalence, incidence, mortality, and DALYs were directly obtained from the GBD 2021 database. All metrics are presented with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) and all rates were expressed per 100,000 population. GBD 2021 defines EHCE as deaths or disability resulting from exposure to high or low temperatures in the environment, not including contact with fire, heat, or hot substances. The relevant ICD-9 codes are E900-E902.99, E926-E926.99, E929.5, and the ICD-10 codes include L55-L55.9, L58-L58.9, W88-W99.9, X30-X32.9, and X39-X39.9. According to WHO definitions [10], “elderly” were defined as aged 60 years and older, and were further categorized into 5 age subgroups: (1) 60 to 64 old, (2) 65 to 69 years old, (3) 70 to 74 years old, (4) 75 to 79 years old, (5) 80 to 84 years old, (6) 85 to 89 years old, (7) 90 to 94 years old, and older than 95 years old. Sociodemographic index (SDI) is a composite index the country’s income, education, and fertility, as a representation of social and economic development. Expressed on a scale of 0 to 1, and a higher SDI indicates a theoretical higher level of development relevant to health.

Statistical analysis

All data analysis and visualization were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2), and the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.3.0). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value of < 0.05. Initially, the EHCE disease burden of 2021 was illustrated by estimating the prevalence, incidence, mortality, and DALYs rates, along with their corresponding 95% UIs. Subsequently, a linear regression model was used to calculate the average annual percentage changes (AAPCs) and 95% confidence intervals. In this model, the rates were taken as the dependent variable on a logarithmic scale, with each year as the independent variable. Thirdly, joinpoint regression analysis was performed using the Joinpoint Regression Program developed by the National Cancer Institute (NCI). The Grid Search Method (GSM) was applied to identify all possible joinpoint locations for segmented regression models. For each candidate model, the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE) and the Mean Squared Error (MSE) were computed. The model with the minimum MSE was selected as the optimal configuration, and the corresponding joinpoints were determined. Subsequently, parameter estimation was conducted for each segment based on the selected joinpoints. Model selection and statistical inference were further refined using the Monte Carlo permutation test to ensure robustness and minimize overfitting. Global, regional and national trends in EHCE-related incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs were explored using the approach just described. Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model was used to forecast disease burden of EHCE from 2022 to 2030. To achieve smooth estimation and address model identification issues [11], the age effects, period effects, and cohort effects were all assigned the commonly used second-order random walk (RW2) priors. This prior assumes that the second differences of the effects follow a zero-mean normal distribution, which helps to extract smooth nonlinear trends from the data. For the precision parameters in the RW2 model and the intercept µ, the standard weakly informative priors recommended in the BAPC package were used. The estimation and inference of the posterior distribution of the model parameters were conducted in the R software environment by utilizing the BAPC package. The computation process typically employs the integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA) technique to enhance efficiency [12]. A sensitivity analysis was performed utilizing ARIMA models to evaluate the robustness of the predictions.

Results

Global distribution of EHCE in adults aged 60 + years in 2021

In 2021, there were 4.4 million cases of EHCE-related prevalence among the elderly reported globally (95% UI: 3.0–5.1.0.1), resulting in an age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) of 413.2 per 100,000 population (95% UI: 362.4–475.6) (Table 1). The global EHCE-related incidence involved 301.2 thousand cases (95% UI: 201.9–439.5), yielding an age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of 28.2 (95% UI: 18.9–41), (Table 1). EHCE accounted for 19.1 thousand (95% UI: 12.0–23.7) deaths in 2021, with an age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) of 1.8 (95% UI: 1.1–2.3) (Table 1). The number of DALYs attributed to EHCE globally was 507.4 thousand (95% UI: 369.1–618) in 2021, with an age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR) of 47.2 (95% UI: 34.4–57.5) per 100,000 population (Table 1).

Global trends

From 1990 to 2021, a decline was observed in ASPR, ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR associated with EHCE across global and five categories of the SDI (Fig. 4 and Table S1-S4). ASPR for EHCE (per 100 000 population) in individuals aged 60 + decreased from 622.3 in 1990 to 413.2 in 2021, with an AAPC of −1.33 (95%CI − 1.40 to − 1.25). Specifically, ASPR related to EHCE for males decreased by −1.47 (95% CI: −1.60 to −1.35), while for females, it decreased by −1.18 (95% CI: −1.23 to −1.12) (Fig S1). ASIR of EHCE per 100,000 individuals aged 60 and over showed a decline from 44.5 in 1990 to 28.2 in 2021, reflecting an AAPC of −1.47 (95% CI: −1.58 to −1.37). In detail, males experienced a reduction in ASIR of −1.61 (95% CI: −1.75 to −1.47), while females had a decrease of −1.33 (95% Cl: −1.40 to −127) (Fig S2). From 1990 to 2021, the ASMR of EHCE among those aged 60 + dropped from 2.56 to 1.82 per 100,000, with an AAPC of −0.83 (95% CI: −2.28 to −0.64). Males saw a decline of −0.70 (95% CI: −1.89 to 0.49), while females showed a decrease of −1.07 (95% CI: −2.76 to 0.65) (Fig S3). For individuals aged 60 and above, ASDR of EHCE dropped from 71.8 per 100,000 in 1990 to 47.2 in 2021, corresponding to an AAPC of −1.22 (95% CI: −2.24 to −0.19). Breaking it down by gender, males showed a decrease with an AAPC of −1.16 (95% CI: −2.16 to −0.15), while females exhibited a reduction of −1.26 (95% CI: −2.12 to −0.39) (Fig S4).Fig. 4Joinpoint regression analysis of global and five SDI categories EHCE burden among the population aged 60 years and older from 1990 to 2021 By prevalence (A), incidence (B), mortality (C), and DALYs (D). The joinpoint regression analysis of EHCE-related ASR revealed a consistent and noticeable downward trend from 1990 to 2021 (Fig. 4). Specifically, the ASPR exhibited four distinct periods of gradual decline: 1999–2002, 2002–2010, 2010–2018, and 2018–2021, while remaining stable from 1990 to 1999. Similarly, the ASIR showed four phases of slight decreases in 2001–2005, 2005–2011, 2011–2018, and 2018–2021, with no significant variation observed from 1990 to 2001. For ASMR, the trend presented three intervals of declining rates—1990–1994, 2003–2007, and 2007–2021—interspersed with stable periods from 1994 to 2000 and 2000–2003. Likewise, the ASDR demonstrated three notable periods of reduction: 1990–1994, 2003–2007, and 2007–2021, while remaining relatively unchanged during 1994–1999 and 1999–2003.

Regional trends

Upon analysis by SDI region, EHCE-related prevalence decreased most significantly in high-middle SDI countries (AAPC: −2.31; 95% CI: −2.54 to − 2.09), while high-SDI countries experienced only a slight decrease (AAPC: −0.13; 95% CI: −0.21 to − 0.06) (Table S1). Similarly, the incidence dropped most in high-middle SDI countries (AAPC: −2.14; 95% CI: −2.60 to − 1.68), compared to a smaller decline in high-SDI countries (AAPC: −0.46; 95% CI: −0.52 to − 0.40) (Table S2). In terms of mortality, the most notable reduction was in middle SDI countries (AAPC: −2.17; 95% CI: −2.60 to − 1.74), while high-SDI countries saw an increase (AAPC: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.33 to 1.50) (Table S3). EHCE-related DALYs saw the largest decrease in middle SDI countries (AAPC: −2.20; 95% CI: −2.63 to − 1.77), whereas there was an increase in high-SDI countries (AAPC: 0.45; 95% CI: 0.13 to 0.78) (Table S4). The results of the joinpoint regression analyses for the prevalence, incidence, mortality, and DALYs related to EHCE globally from 1990 to 2021, stratified by SDI, for adults aged 60 years and older, are presented in Table S5.

Regionally, the largest decreases in ASPR for EHCE among the elderly from 1990 to 2021 occurred in East Asia (AAPC: −1.91; 95% CI: −1.96 to − 1.85), Eastern Europe (AAPC: −1.65; 95% CI: −1.86 to − 1.43), and Central Latin America (AAPC: −1.42; 95% CI: −1.48 to − 1.36), while only two regions exhibited increases: High-income Asia Pacific (AAPC: 0.19; 95% CI: 0.17 to 0.21) and Western Europe (AAPC: 0.10; 95% CI: 0.05 to 0.16) (Fig. 5A and Table S1). During this period, all regions, except High-income Asia Pacific (AAPC: 0.64; 95% CI: 0.61 to 0.68), reported a decrease in ASIR due to EHCE, with the largest decreases in Andean East Asia (AAPC: −2.75; 95% CI: −2.79 to −2.70), Tropical Latin America (AAPC: −2.73; 95% CI: −2.88 to −2.58), and Central Europe (AAPC: −2.21; 95% CI: −2.29 to −2.12) (Fig. 5B and Table S2). Changes in ASMR varied across different regions during the same period, with Andean Latin America (AAPC: −3.74; 95% CI: −10.62 to −3.68), East Asia (AAPC: −3.13; 95% CI: −3.50 to −2.76), and Australasia (AAPC: −2.85; 95% CI: −5.85 to 0.24) showing the largest decreasing trends. In contrast, High-income Asia Pacific (AAPC: 2.83; 95% CI: 2.31 to 3.36), Central Asia (AAPC: 1.28; 95% CI: 0.15 to 2.42), and Western Sub-Saharan Africa (AAPC: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.80 to 1.11) exhibited the largest increasing trends (Fig. 5C and Table S3). Distinct patterns in the temporal trends of ASDR were observed among different regions. East Asia (AAPC: −3.15; 95% CI: −3.49 to −2.81), Andean Latin America (AAPC: −1.91; 95% CI: −4.56 to 0.82), and Central Latin America (AAPC: −1.74; 95% CI: −1.98 to −1.51) experienced the most significant decreases in ASDR, while High-income Asia Pacific (AAPC: 1.54; 95% CI: 0.99 to 2.10), Western Europe (AAPC: 1.05; 95% CI: −0.01 to 2.11), and Central Asia (AAPC: 0.31; 95% CI: −0.52 to 1.14) encountered the largest increases (Fig. 5D and Table S4). The joinpoint regression results for reginal EHCE are presented in Tables S10-S13.

National trends

The percentage changes in ASPR from 1990 to 2021 varied significantly across countries. Notably, France (AAPC: 2.55; 95% CI: 2.40 to 2.70), Turkmenistan (AAPC: 1.77; 95% CI: 1.73 to 1.82), and Finland (AAPC: 0.66; 95% CI: 0.60 to 0.72) experienced the greatest increases. Conversely, Albania saw a pronounced decrease (AAPC: −3.68; 95% CI: −4.99 to − 3.37), followed by Afghanistan (AAPC: −3.08; 95% CI: −3.15 to − 3.00) and Armenia (AAPC: −2.95; 95% CI: −3.12 to − 2.78) (Fig. 6A and Table S6). During the same period, Turkmenistan (AAPC: 2.79; 95% CI: 2.63 to 2.95), France (AAPC: 1.41; 95% CI: 1.20 to 1.61), and Finland (AAPC: 1.17; 95% CI: 1.12 to 1.21) recorded the largest increases in ASIR. In contrast, Afghanistan (AAPC: −5.49; 95% CI: −5.79 to − 5.20), Albania (AAPC: −5.06; 95% CI: −5.33 to − 4.78), and Mexico (AAPC: −3.33; 95% CI: −3.40 to − 3.27) observed the most significant decreases (Fig. 6B and Table S7). Belgium (AAPC: 10.03; 95% CI: 0.82 to 20.09), Georgia (AAPC: 6.93; 95% CI: 5.06 to 8.84), and Cabo Verde (AAPC: 6.84; 95% CI: 5.41 to 8.28) showed the largest increases in ASMR for EHCE from 1990 to 2021. Conversely, Bangladesh (AAPC: −7.71; 95% CI: −11.66 to − 3.59), Mauritius (AAPC: −6.48; 95% CI: −13.14 to − 0.68), and Seychelles (AAPC: −5.15; 95% CI: −5.92 to − 4.38) demonstrated the most notable decreases (Fig. 6C and Table S8). Between 1990 and 2021, Belgium (AAPC: 9.56; 95% CI: 2.11 to 17.55), the Netherlands (AAPC: 5.04; 95% CI: 0.91 to 9.35), and Turkmenistan (AAPC: 4.54; 95% CI: 2.85 to 6.25) had the most considerable increases in ASDR for EHCE. On the other hand, Afghanistan (AAPC: −4.89; 95% CI: −18.90 to 11.53), Sri Lanka (AAPC: −3.99; 95% CI: −4.45 to − 3.53), and Cyprus (AAPC: −3.92; 95% CI: −7.21 to − 0.51) witnessed the largest reductions (Fig. 6D and Table S9). The joinpoint regression results for national EHCE are detailed in Tables S10 to S13.

Discussion

In this study, it was found that EHCE accounted for 4.4 million cases prevalent cases, 301.2 thousand incident cases, 19.1 thousand deaths, and 507.4 thousand DALYs globally in 2021 among individuals aged 60 and above. Over the past three decades, ASPR, ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR due to EHCE globally showed a general downward trend, especially in high-middle SDI countries. However, the observed decline in ASMR and ASDR is minimal, and the BAPC model forecasts a potential increase beyond 2025. The projected increase may be driven by several converging factors. First, the rapid expansion of the elderly population is outpacing improvements in adaptive infrastructure and healthcare systems. Second, the escalating frequency and severity of extreme weather events—amplified by climate change—disproportionately affect older adults, who are more vulnerable to thermal stress. Third, conventional intervention measures, such as early warning systems and housing insulation improvements, may have reached their maximum efficacy, highlighting the need for more innovative, targeted approaches. This trend emphasizes the urgent need to strengthen both preventive and adaptive strategies aimed at mitigating environment-related health risks among older populations, and to advance research and policy development to reduce the long-term public health burden of exposure to extreme heat and cold. Numerous studies have demonstrated that extreme temperatures, both high and low, have detrimental effects on human health, particularly among older adults. Some showed the effect of heat and cold on emergency room admissions [13], cognitive performance [14], all-cause mortality [15], and cause-specific mortality [16]. However, there is a lack of comprehensive epidemiological research specifically examining the effects of EHCE on the elderly population. According to our understanding, this is the first study that extensively documents the current state and shifting trends of the EHCE burden among those aged 60 and above across global, regional, and national levels from 1990 to 2021.

Extreme temperatures—both high and low—pose substantial threats to older adults, as aging impairs multiple components of thermoregulatory homeostasis, including sweat gland function, skin vasodilation, thirst perception, and cardiac reserve, while concurrently increasing the prevalence of chronic diseases [17]. A large-scale U.S. study involving 23.7 million Medicare enrollees aged 65 years and older across 1,943 counties [18] found that extreme heat events significantly elevate the risk of hospitalization for fluid and electrolyte disorders, renal failure, urinary tract infections, septicemia, and heat stroke. Conversely, a nationwide analysis of over 23 million hospitalizations in Brazil [19] revealed that low ambient temperatures were associated with increased cardiovascular and respiratory admissions, disproportionately affecting older adults and men. Pharmacological factors further exacerbate temperature-related vulnerability. Heat-sensitizing medications—such as diuretics, anticholinergics, antipsychotics, beta-blockers, stimulants, and antihypertensives—have been shown to increase the risk of heat-related hospitalizations among older adults with chronic conditions, even in the absence of heatwaves [20]. Against this backdrop, future climate projections underscore growing concern: the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report [21] estimates a 2.4–4.8 °C rise in global mean temperature by 2100 under high-emission scenarios, with more frequent and intense heat events expected worldwide. These changes are likely to exacerbate existing adaptation inequalities, as high-income countries (HICs) have broader access to cooling infrastructure, healthcare, and early warning systems, whereas low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) often lack such protective resources [22]. Consequently, older adults especially in LMICs face disproportionate risks of climate-related thermal stress. These findings collectively highlight the urgent need for climate-adaptive geriatric care, including weather-responsive medication management, personalized thermal and hydration protocols, and targeted outreach to socially isolated elders. Between 1990 and 2021, a downward trend was observed in the global EHCE-related ASPR, ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR across both male and female populations .

However we found sex variations in EHCE-related disease burden. For example, in 2021, the ASMR and ASDR of EHCE in males were 2.56 and 64.42 per 100 000 populations, which was more than 2-fold that of females. This may be attributed to several factors: older women generally exhibit lower levels of physical activity but engage more in household tasks [23]; gender differences are evident in terms of thermal comfort [24]; and there are notable disparities in how elderly individuals utilize health-care services based on gender [25]. We also have observed significant variations in the burden of diseases related to EHCE across different regions and countries. The higher ASPR and ASIR reported in high-SDI countries compared to other SDI categories can be attributed to several factors. High levels of urbanization intensify urban heat island effects, and dense populations mean that even minor weather events can affect many individuals [26]. Additionally, these countries benefit from advanced data systems that ensure comprehensive and accurate record-keeping. Furthermore, high-SDI countries generally have robust healthcare and emergency response systems in place to effectively manage EHCE risks. Nevertheless, high-SDI countries typically exhibit lower ASMR and ASDR, primarily as a result of their advanced healthcare systems, effective public health policies, and elevated standards of living [27]. Nationally, Greenland, along with several other high-latitude countries in the Northern Hemisphere, exhibits notably high ASPR and ASIR. While isolated heat waves pose substantial health risks, recent studies have illuminated a more intricate and unexpected relationship between temperature and public health [2]. The disproportionately high EHCE burden observed in Greenland and other high-latitude countries is likely primarily driven by cold-related mortality under present climates. This aligns with prior studies showing that extremely low temperatures contribute substantially more to mortality in cold regions than high temperatures [28]. Nevertheless, we also note that increasing heatwaves in traditionally cold climates (attributable to climate variability and climate change) are an emerging concern. Recent record-breaking Arctic heat events and heatwave-related deaths in Northern Europe demonstrate that even in these regions, heat-related health impacts – particularly among older adults – are beginning to rise [29]. This is why countries in colder regions typically exhibit higher EHCE-related ASPR, ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR, along with more pronounced increasing trends, compared to countries in tropical regions.

As climate change advances, temperature-related health risks are influenced not only by the outdoor environment but also by factors such as building design [30], urban and neighborhood planning [31], and the implementation of targeted communication strategies to alleviate the health effects of extreme temperatures. Numerous efforts have been made to describe and review intervention strategies and adaptation measures. For instance, a New Zealand study [32] exemplifies how installing insulation in low-income homes can serve as an effective adaptation measure, as it improved indoor conditions, which in turn led to better self-rated health, and reductions in healthcare visits, energy consumption, and absenteeism. In addition to infrastructure-based interventions, measures such as establishing accessible cooling centers, enhancing risk communication tailored to older populations, and integrating climate risk awareness into primary care education represent crucial, yet underutilized, strategies to strengthen resilience and reduce heat- and cold-related morbidity among vulnerable groups. A major strength of this study lies in its provision of up-to-date, comprehensive estimates regarding the levels and trends of EHCE across global, regional, and national contexts spanning the period from 1990 to 2021. However, the study has several limitations. Firstly, the study is constrained by discrepancies in data quality and incomplete data. The lack of sufficient raw data in certain regions forces reliance on statistical models, which can lead to inaccuracies and an underestimation of the true burden of EHCE-related diseases. This is particularly problematic in regions with poor health infrastructure, where data integrity and quantity are significantly compromised. Secondly, in the trend analysis of EHCE impacts, the study primarily used the year as an independent variable, without considering other critical parameters such as socioeconomic factors, healthcare access, lifestyle choices, and environmental influences. Including these additional variables could enhance the understanding of trends and provide a more comprehensive analysis. Thirdly, while the methodologies employed are rigorous, they introduce uncertainties, especially when data for specific diseases are absent, which can complicate real-world interpretations for health policy and resource allocation. Lastly, the impact of environmental heat and cold exposure can vary significantly based on regional climate conditions, which the GBD models do not always capture accurately. Furthermore, the GBD framework does not distinguish between acute weather events and chronic temperature exposures, preventing us from differentiating the cumulative effects of prolonged temperature exposure from mortality spikes due to extreme events. This limitation constrains our ability to fully capture the nuances of temperature-related health burdens over time.

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of the global burden of extreme heat and cold events (EHCE) among adults aged 60 years and older, based on the most recent data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021. Although age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), incidence rate (ASIR), mortality rate (ASMR), and disability-adjusted life years (ASDR) have generally declined worldwide from 1990 to 2021, significant regional disparities persist. Notably, certain indicators have increased in regions such as the high-income Asia Pacific and Western Europe. These findings highlight the urgent need for context-specific public health interventions to mitigate regional inequities and strengthen protection for vulnerable older populations. To address the rising EHCE burden, healthcare systems must enhance preparedness by implementing early warning systems, developing climate-sensitive care pathways, and fostering geriatric resilience. This includes adopting tailored clinical protocols, expanding community-based healthcare services, and improving risk communication strategies targeted at the elderly. Effective cross-sector collaboration is essential to enable timely, proactive responses. Ultimately, integrating climate adaptation measures into health system planning is critical for reducing EHCE-related morbidity and mortality in aging populations.
