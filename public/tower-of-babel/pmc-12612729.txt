REBOA in shocked penetrating abdominal trauma patients: impact on outcomes
Authors: Justin S Hatchimonji, Diane N Haddad, Lydia Maurer, Phillip M Dowzicky, Andrew J Benjamin, Niels D Martin, Patrick M Reilly, Jay Yelon, Mark J Seamon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe role of resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA) in trauma is debated. We hypothesized that the use of REBOA for patients presenting in shock after penetrating abdominal trauma is associated with delay to laparotomy and increased mortality.Study designWe used 2017–2021 Trauma Quality Improvement Project data to identify adult (≥16 years) penetrating abdominal trauma patients with systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≤90 mm Hg undergoing laparotomy. REBOA was defined by International Classification of Diseases-10 code, with a procedure timestamp preceding or simultaneous to laparotomy incision. We propensity score matched REBOA to non-REBOA patients on demographics, mechanism, injury characteristics and severity, solid organ injury, abdominal vascular injury, SBP, heart rate, and Glasgow Coma Scale motor score. Outcomes were time to incision, transfusion requirements, complications, and in-hospital mortality. We additionally performed a survival analysis stratified by presenting SBP.ResultsThere were 148 REBOA patients with complete data for matching to 280 non-REBOA patients. Among patients with REBOA timestamps preceding laparotomy incision, there was a delay to laparotomy (time to incision 40 (31–50) vs 31 (24–43) min, p=0.001). Overall, REBOA was associated with increased transfusion volume (median (IQR) packed red blood cells 5,125 (2,100–9,100) vs 2,925 (1,250–5,500) ccs in the first 4 hours, p<0.001), leg amputations (3.4% vs 0.4%, p=0.010), and mortality (53.4% vs 42.5%, p=0.032). The mortality relationship persisted on 30-day survival analysis.ConclusionREBOA for patients in shock after penetrating abdominal trauma is associated with delay to operation, greater transfusion requirement, leg amputation, and mortality. Our data support the need for expeditious definitive hemorrhage control in these patients.Level of evidenceIII, retrospective cohort.

Background

Non-compressible torso hemorrhage (NCTH) can be one of the most time-sensitive conditions encountered by the trauma surgeon.1 2 NCTH is associated with a mortality rate as high as 45%,3 generating great interest in outcomes improvement. Though iterations of balloon occlusion of the aorta have existed for many years,4 5 interest in using resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA) in trauma surgery has become more widespread in the last decade.68

The use of this technique has been the subject of fierce debate in the trauma community. Much of this debate has centered on the comparison of REBOA with resuscitative thoracotomy (RT) in the patient who presents pulseless or in extremis.7 9 10 In propensity-matched studies, REBOA use has been associated with similar or improved survival when compared with RT,11 with the highest odds of survival for penetrating abdominal trauma patients when compared with RT.10 However, it may be difficult to sufficiently match two fundamentally different groups of patients, that is, those with or without a pulse. Indeed, the postmatch RT groups in the aforementioned studies are patients with a demonstrable blood pressure, which may be an indication that the wrong question is being asked.

On the other hand, less work has been undertaken to study the patient who presents in shock with NCTH, but may not necessarily require RT. It is theoretically plausible that REBOA may be helpful in the context of penetrating abdominal trauma as a means to temporize prior to laparotomy. REBOA is used by some centers in this context, but there is little data to support this. For this reason, we undertook this study with the hypothesis that REBOA use in shocked penetrating abdominal trauma patients would be associated with delay to laparotomy and increased mortality.

Methods

This was a retrospective analysis of 2017–2021 Trauma Quality Improvement Project (TQIP) data. We included adults (≥16 years) presenting to Level 1 and 2 centers in shock, with penetrating abdominal trauma, and proceeding to emergency laparotomy. The penetrating mechanism was defined as either gunshot or stab wounds. Patients were considered to have an abdominal injury if any of their listed abbreviated injury scale (AIS) codes began with 5, the abdominal or pelvic injury designation. Shock was defined by initial emergency department (ED) systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≤90 mm Hg. Finally, TQIP includes a field defining “type of surgery for hemorrhage control”; patients were considered to have a laparotomy if this field indicated so. Time to laparotomy is measured in minutes, from arrival to incision. TQIP excludes patients considered dead on arrival.

The use of REBOA was defined by the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 procedure code, with the procedure timestamp simultaneous to or preceding the timestamp for the laparotomy. Specifically, we used codes 04L03DZ (occlusion of abdominal aorta with intraluminal device, percutaneous approach), 04L03DJ (occlusion of abdominal aorta with intraluminal device, temporary, percutaneous approach), 04L04DZ (occlusion of abdominal aorta with intraluminal device, percutaneous endoscopic approach), and 02LW3DJ (occlusion of thoracic aorta, descending, with intraluminal device, temporary, percutaneous approach). These are the codes used to define REBOA use in the existing literature.9

The primary outcome of interest was in-hospital mortality. Additional outcomes measures included 30-day mortality, time to laparotomy, transfusion requirements, intensive care unit and hospital length of stay, and complications including deep venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, stroke, myocardial infarction, unplanned return to the operating room, extremity compartment syndrome, leg amputation, and acute kidney injury. All of the aforementioned complications are included as fields in the TQIP dataset with the exception of lower extremity amputations; these were defined by ICD-10 codes for hip disarticulation, above-knee amputation, knee disarticulation, and below-knee amputation.

To quantify the effect of REBOA on these outcomes, we performed 2:1 nearest neighbor propensity score matching using Mahalanobis distance. Patients were matched on sex, age, race, mechanism, injury severity score, presenting SBP, heart rate, and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) motor score, and the presence or absence of: severe head injury, severe chest injury, severe abdominal injury, solid organ injury, and abdominal vascular injury. “Severe” head, chest, and abdominal injuries were defined as AIS ≥4 in the respective regions. Solid organ injury was defined as any AIS ≥1 in the liver, kidney, or spleen. Abdominal vascular injury was defined using AIS predot codes corresponding to “minor” and “major” lacerations of the abdominal aorta, inferior vena cava, celiac and superior mesenteric arteries, hepatic, renal, and splenic arteries, portal, superior mesenteric, splenic, and renal veins, iliac arteries, and iliac veins.

Once matched, differences in outcomes were analyzed using χ² and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. The difference in time to laparotomy was analyzed on the subset of patients with REBOA timestamps preceding (not coinciding with) laparotomy incision time. A time-based survival analysis using a log-rank test was undertaken to analyze differences in 30-day mortality in the overall matched cohort and the subset of patients in profound shock with SBP ≤70 mm Hg on arrival. Finally, multiple sensitivity analyses were undertaken on the matched cohort, to analyze the impact of a variety of factors on outcomes.

A p value of 0.05 was selected a priori to be significant. We did not seek institutional review board approval for this project, as it uses only widely available, deidentified data. Analyses were performed using Stata V.15.1 (College Station, Texas, USA).

Ethics approval statement

This study does not involve human participants and therefore was not subject to institutional review board approval.

Results

After exclusions, there were 110,577 patients aged 16 years or older with a penetrating abdominal injury and presenting to a Level 1 or 2 trauma center. Of these, 6,119 presented in shock and were taken for immediate laparotomy for hemorrhage control. Of these, 51 patients had an ICD code for REBOA but either had a timestamp after laparotomy or were missing one or both timestamps, and thus were excluded. Another 228 patients were missing data for other variables of interest, leaving 5,840 complete cases for analysis.

The prematch characteristics of the REBOA (n=148) and non-REBOA (n=5,692) patients are shown in table 1. The majority of patients in both groups were young, non-white males suffering from gunshot wounds; the REBOA group was slightly younger (median (IQR) age 30 (22.5–38.5) vs 32 (24–42)), with a higher proportion of gunshot wounds (93.9% vs 81.2%, p<0.001). The REBOA group presented with lower mean SBP (56.8 vs 70.7, p<0.001) and with a higher proportion of severe (AIS ≥4) abdominal injuries (67.6% vs 50.1%, p<0.001).

In the propensity score matched cohort, the use of REBOA in penetrating abdominal trauma patients presenting in shock was associated with increased 4-hour packed red blood cell transfusion (5,125 (2,100–9,100) vs 2,925 (1,250–5,500) ccs, p<0.001), increased 4-hour plasma volume transfused (3,330 (1,000–5,700) vs 1,750 (500–3,300) ccs, p<0.001), and increased 4-hour platelet volume transfused (500 (200–900) vs 250 (0–600) ccs, p<0.001). REBOA patients also experienced higher rates of extremity compartment syndrome (4.1% vs 0.4%, p=0.004), leg amputation (3.4% vs 0.4%, p=0.010) and higher in-hospital mortality (53.4% vs 42.5%, p=0.032). Excluding those with simultaneous REBOA and laparotomy timestamps from the initial cohort and thus comparing the subset of patients who had a REBOA timestamp preceding the laparotomy timestamp (n=55) to the non-REBOA patients, REBOA was associated with a delay to laparotomy (time to incision 40 (31–50) vs 31 (24–43) minutes, p=0.001). Because the American College of Surgeons (ACS) verification or state designation of the trauma center may carry some indication of that center’s comfort or familiarity with REBOA, we stratified the mortality analysis by level. In the matched cohort, there were 262 patients treated at an ACS level 1 center, 70 at an ACS level 2 center, and 96 with no ACS status, noting that this indicates state designation without ACS verification, or verification in process at the time of data collection. Of those with no ACS verification status, 63/96 (66%) were treated at a state level 1 center. Among those managed at a hospital with either ACS or state level 1 status (n=331), there was no significant mortality difference with REBOA (50.8% vs 40.5%, p=0.067); there was also no significant difference among the remainder of the cohort (neither ACS nor state level 1 status, n=97) (68.2% vs 48.0%, p=0.096).

Recognizing that there was a significant proportion with a GCS motor score of 6, we examined this least-altered subset of the matched cohort. When the cohort was limited in this way, the groups remained reasonably well matched, with 67 patients in the REBOA group and 135 in the non-REBOA group and no statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics. The increase in red blood cell requirement with REBOA was no longer significant (3,500 (1,050–7,350) vs 2,275 (1,050–4,200) cc, p=0.094), but the increased plasma (2,250 (600–5,000) vs 1,500 (429–2,948) cc, p=0.043) and platelet transfusion requirements (225 (0–500) vs 280 (2–865) cc, p=0.031) persisted. There was no increase in mortality in this group (23.9% vs 25.2%, p=0.85).

Discussion

In this study, we sought to analyze whether the use of REBOA is associated with improved mortality and blood transfusion volumes in patients presenting in shock with penetrating abdominal trauma. REBOA use in this population was associated with increased in-hospital mortality. The REBOA group also experienced increased blood product transfusion and higher rates of complications, including extremity compartment syndrome and leg amputations. Finally, placement of REBOA prior to laparotomy was associated with a delay to the operating room.

This adds to the current literature in that it provides data on REBOA use in a specific subset of patients presenting in shock, without limiting to pulseless patients or comparing to RT. Recent work by Ordonez et al uses complex modeling to analyze an appropriate SBP threshold for REBOA placement prior to hemodynamic collapse; they analyzed that patients with presenting SBP 60–80 mm Hg may benefit.15 However, that study included a broad population of trauma patients, some of whom may have warranted immediate exploration and some of whom may not have, without a comparison group. Here, we examine a more specific subset—patients with penetrating abdominal trauma—in which it is still at least theoretically plausible that REBOA could be of some use, given the presumed site of bleeding below the diaphragm. Evidence exists for the use of REBOA in patients with gastrointestinal (GI) or peripartum hemorrhage, for example, and one may be tempted to extrapolate this evidence to other patients with intra-abdominal hemorrhage.1618 However, it should be noted that these are patients whose bleeding is either expected to resolve (peripartum) or may not be amenable to definitive surgical control (unlocalized GI bleeding). The rarity of REBOA use for this indication (fewer than 200 in the USA over 5 years in these data) suggests that, although spirited debate continues in the literature, there is less enthusiasm for real-life clinical use.

In contrast, in patients for whom a laparotomy is indicated, one might also reasonably conclude that an additional procedure may delay definitive control and lead to worse outcomes, as we have shown here. REBOA placement is a relatively high-stakes, low-frequency procedure that requires repeated practice to maintain proficiency.19 Indeed, an early description of the Aortic Occlusion for Resuscitation in Trauma and Acute Care Surgery registry reports a median time from admission to aortic occlusion (AO) of 25 min.8 Subsequent studies using the same database have not reported times to AO.10 20 In patients requiring a laparotomy, the minutes spent performing this procedure may be valuable time not spent gaining definitive control as patients continue to hemorrhage. Indeed, in the present study, the REBOA group had a higher transfusion requirement despite similar injury severity and physiology after matching, which may be attributable to this delay to control. Finally, whereas the rates of compartment syndrome and amputation remain low with REBOA use overall, the increase in these complications is notable.

As noted above, there is little data on this question in this population. However, a very recent study by Colosimo et al has examined outcomes in all TQIP patients undergoing laparotomy for hemorrhage control (not limited to penetrating mechanisms), and limited the intervention group to those definitively undergoing REBOA before surgery. It should again be noted that the present study included those patients with simultaneous REBOA and laparotomy timestamps, raising the possibility that some of these patients had a REBOA deployed in the operating room. We felt it important to include these patients. Findings from the Colosimo study were similar to those presented here, with an average delay to laparotomy of 15 min, a greater rate of complications, and adjusted odds of mortality greater than 3.21 Their findings echo those presented here, suggesting that REBOA prolongs time to definitive hemorrhage control and results in more bleeding (higher transfusion requirements in this study), ultimately resulting in higher mortality.

Our study is not without limitations. By definition, we have only included patients who made it to the operating room for a laparotomy and have not included those who underwent REBOA and died in the ED. An examination of the TQIP data prior to the aforementioned exclusion demonstrates that, among patients presenting in shock with penetrating abdominal injuries and dying in the ED, there was a 1.3% rate of REBOA use. Thus, we think this has minimal impact on our results. It would have been ideal to stratify this analysis by center volume, but this is not possible using TQIP data. However, we have some idea that mortality is similar based on the sensitivity analysis stratified by center level. The data used here lack some of the granularity provided in other REBOA studies. For example, the presence of a high number of patients with REBOA timestamps coinciding with laparotomy may represent a lack of granularity and may be representative of approximate timestamps recorded under hectic circumstances and misclassification bias; it is also unclear whether other ED procedures may have contributed to the delay to laparotomy. Additionally, we lack information on the zone of deployment, though it is likely that most of the patients with abdominal wounds underwent zone 1 deployment. Only the first vital signs measured on arrival to the ED are reported; we lack the ability to capture acute changes (either worsening or improving) in vitals. Finally, we reported an increased incidence of leg amputations in the REBOA group; however, we were unable to analyze the laterality in relation to REBOA placement due to limitations of the dataset. These limitations notwithstanding, our data does not appear to support the role of REBOA in shocked penetrating abdominal trauma patients.

Conclusion

REBOA use in shocked patients after penetrating abdominal trauma is associated with delay to operation, greater transfusion requirement, leg amputation, and increased mortality. Our results suggest that expeditious operative hemorrhage control alone in these critically injured patients may improve outcomes.
