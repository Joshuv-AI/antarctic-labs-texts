Echocardiographic findings of cardiac abnormalities in children and adolescents with anorexia nervosa: a systematic review
Authors: Marek Wiśniewski, Piotr Wieniawski, Tomasz Wolańczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnorexia nervosa (AN) is an eating disorder primarily affecting female adolescents and has the highest mortality rate among psychiatric disorders. Many patients with AN experience cardiovascular complications. These structural and functional cardiac changes can be detected through echocardiography.ObjectivesTo systematically review abnormalities in the structure and function of the heart observed in echocardiography in children and adolescents with AN.MethodsWe assessed the eligibility of publications from the MEDLINE database, which was last searched on November 30, 2025. Studies were included if AN was the primary eating disorder, the average age of the subjects was 18,0 or younger at the time of the study, and the study described cardiac abnormalities found with echocardiography. Non-English studies, studies not pertaining to AN, and studies where AN was not the primary disease were excluded. Two reviewers (M.W., P.W.) screened the references independently to reduce the risk of bias. Our systematic review follows PRISMA guidelines.ResultsWe identified 23 eligible studies involving a total of 1246 patients. Of these, 19 studies (1181 participants) provided data suitable for systematic review. The most commonly reported structural and functional heart abnormalities in AN compared with the control group were: decreased left ventricular mass and left ventricular mass indexed to body surface area (15/19 studies), left ventricular systolic and diastolic dysfunction (11/19), valvular abnormalities (4/19), and an increased incidence of pericardial effusion (9/19). Four studies compared diagnostic subgroups of AN, mainly restricting type (AN-R) and binge-eating/purging type (AN-BP). One study compared echocardiographic abnormalities between patients with hyperactive (ANH+) and non-hyperactive (ANH-) types of AN. Six studies documented the resolution of symptoms in some patients following weight restoration.DiscussionAnalyzed studies suggest that patients with AN may be at increased risk for cardiovascular disease. Many studies had small sample sizes, and some had a low to moderate risk of bias. Further research with larger sample sizes is recommended. All but one of the research papers included come from high-income countries, which limits external validity. The experience of the person performing the echocardiography, potential feedback from the supervisor, the anatomical conditions of the patient, and the patient’s willingness to cooperate may also impact the reliability of the studies included in this review.OtherThis research received no specific funding from any public, commercial, or non-profit organization. The authors declare no conflicts of interest.

Introduction

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is an eating disorder that typically begins in adolescence and mainly affects girls (female-to-male ratio 10:1). The DSM-5 criteria for AN include: (A) Restriction of energy intake compared to requirements, resulting in significantly low body weight given age, sex, development, and health (less than minimally normal or expected); (B) Intense fear of gaining weight or becoming fat, or behaviors that interfere with weight gain; (C) Preoccupation with body weight or shape, self-worth influenced by body weight or shape, or persistent failure to recognize the seriousness of low body weight [1]. AN has the highest mortality rate among psychiatric disorders [2, 3], with a standardized mortality ratio of 5.06, supported by recent meta-analytical data by Lai et al. [4]. Cardiovascular complications mainly involve sinus bradycardia, hypotension, prolonged QT interval, decreased left ventricular mass, systolic and diastolic dysfunction of the left ventricle, mitral valve prolapse, and increased incidence of pericardial effusion. Many of these can be detected through echocardiography. We have conducted a comprehensive review of the recognized cardiovascular complications linked to AN to help establish the most effective clinical assessment of the cardiac issues that may arise during treatment.

Methods

A systematic literature search was conducted independently by two authors, using a major electronic medical database (PubMed) up to November 30, 2025. The search strategy included the following terms: “anorexia” and “echocardiography, " and an age filter „child,” which narrowed the results to individuals up to 18 years old. Preliminary research using the database showed that one systematic review and meta-analysis had been previously carried out on the same subject [5].

The number of records identified in PubMed with these terms was 61. The exclusion criteria included: studies not related to AN, studies not written in English, a mean patient age over 18 years, primary disease other than AN, and studies not describing echocardiographic findings. Case reports were excluded due to concerns about quality and evidence level. While a comparison group was not strictly required, healthy controls were included where available. Studies focusing on atypical AN (where weight is within or above the normal range despite significant weight loss) were excluded to maintain a homogenous cohort of underweight patients. After applying these exclusion criteria, 23 articles were reviewed in full text, and 4 additional studies were excluded because the mean age of the patients exceeded 18,0 years. Two authors (M.W., P.W.) independently excluded papers based on titles and abstracts, then separately reviewed the full texts. Data were extracted and summarized based on standard aspects: main author, publication date, sample size of patients with AN, mean age of patients with AN, and key echocardiographic findings reported in each study.

The largest study included in this systematic review was a meta-analysis with 960 patients [5], which analyzed 23 studies—of which we additionally included 3. The rest of the studies either did not meet the criteria of this systematic review or were duplicates of reports already included.

Nineteen studies (1181 participants) reported data suitable for systematic review, with study characteristics summarized in [Table 1]. The mean age of the studied patients was 15,53 years old. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses [PRISMA] guidelines were followed while conducting this study [Figure 1].

The risk of bias of the included studies was rated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), while the quality of the meta-analysis by Smythe et al. [5] was assessed using the AMSTAR 2 tool [Table 2]. All risk of bias assessments were verified in duplicate to ensure consistency in scoring, particularly regarding the comparability of study groups.

Risk of bias of the included studies

The methodological quality of the systematic review and meta-analysis by Smythe et al. [5] is rated as very high. It was conducted correctly and meets almost all critical criteria, including the use of a pre-registered protocol (PROSPERO), a comprehensive literature search, and independent, duplicate study selection and data extraction.

Results

Of the studies included, 15 out of 19 showed decreased Left Ventricular Mass (LVM); 11 out of 19 showed reductions in both Left Ventricular End-Diastolic diameter (LVED) and End-Systolic diameter (LVES); 9 out of 19 indicated signs of Pericardial Effusion (PEf); and 4 out of 19 reported valvular abnormalities—most commonly Mitral Valve Prolapse (MVP). One report suggests that patients with AN may have an increased risk of aortic stiffness. A total of 6 out of 19 studies documented symptom resolution in some patients after weight restoration; the remaining studies did not compare symptoms across subsequent measurements. Other less common cardiac pathologies identified in the studies are detailed in the table above [Table 1].

Myocardial atrophy/decreased LVM

The assessment of left ventricular mass through echocardiography depends on geometric assumptions regarding the shape of the ventricle. It involves measurements of wall thickness and internal diameter. The most commonly used equation is the Devereux formula, which considers the thickness of the interventricular septum, the posterior wall, and the diastolic diameter of the left ventricle [27]. LVM is then indexed to the patient’s body surface area (LVMI), enabling comparisons between individuals with different body types.

A total of fifteen studies [6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 14, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 26] included in this review showed a decrease in LVM in patients with AN compared to healthy controls. One study compared LVM, LVMI, and LVMH (LVM indexed to height) in 44 patients with AN, divided into 24 hyperactive (ANH+), 20 non-hyperactive (ANH-), and 20 healthy controls (HC). It found that, despite its negative impact on disease severity and outcome, hyperactive patients had LVM values closer to healthy controls [16]. Four studies classified AN into restricting type (AN-R) and binge-eating/purging type (AN-BP). No significant difference in LVM was observed between these subgroups [10, 19, 20, 25].

Left ventricular systolic dysfunction

Measurements of LV dimensions are recorded during the end-diastolic and end-systolic phases. The end-diastolic phase is characterized as the point following the closure of the mitral valve, or the segment of the cardiac cycle where the LV volume reaches its maximum (LVED). The end-systolic phase is described as the moment preceding the opening of the mitral valve, or the segment of the cardiac cycle where the LV volume is at its lowest (LVES).

Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF) represents the volume of blood expelled from the left ventricle in every cardiac cycle and is calculated using the formula: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{LVEF} = \left(\mathrm{LVED} -\mathrm{LVES}\right)/\mathrm{LVED} \times 100\% = \mathrm{SV}/\mathrm{LVED} \times 100\%$$\end{document}

Cardiac output (CO) is the product of Heart Rate (HR), which is the number of heartbeats per minute, and Stroke Volume (SV), which is the volume of blood ejected from the LV.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{CO} = \mathrm{SV} \times \mathrm{HR}$$\end{document}

A total of eleven studies [8, 9, 10, 11, 14, 16, 17, 19, 20, 23, 25] included in this analysis indicated decreased LVES and LVED dimensions in AN patients compared with the normal range. Three reports showed reductions in CO and SV [8, 14, 20], and four studies described smaller Left Ventricular Posterior Wall Dimension in diastole (LVPWDd) and Interventricular Septal Wall Dimension in diastole (IVSDd) [7, 10, 17, 20].

Left ventricular diastolic dysfunction

Diastolic function is evaluated using pulsed wave Doppler, which measures mitral inflow velocities. The Doppler beam is aligned at the mitral valve leaflets to calculate the E/A ratio. This ratio reflects the proportion of peak blood flow velocity during early diastole (the E wave) to the peak velocity during late diastole caused by atrial contraction (the A wave). An elevated E/A ratio indicates restricted filling due to stiffening of the left ventricular wall.

A total of five studies [8, 10, 16, 20, 23] reported a significant increase in E/A ratio, which may suggest diastolic dysfunction.

Pericardial effusion

Pericardial effusion (PEf) is characterized by the abnormal buildup of fluid in the pericardium. Normally, there are about 10–50 milliliters of pericardial fluid between the layers of the pericardium [28, 29]. There are several theories explaining the mechanism of PEf in AN. One hypothesis links PEf to decreased osmotic pressure caused by albumin and total protein deficiency [13, 30]. Another potential mechanism involves low serum triiodothyronine (T3) hormone levels [13, 25, 30, 31]. Docx et al. suggest that PEf may result from reduced pericardial fat and myocardial muscle wasting, leading to an enlarged pericardial space relative to the heart, which can then be filled with fluid [18].

In severe cases, massive PEf may pose a risk of pericardial tamponade, an emergency condition where fluid pressure compresses the heart, reducing cardiac output and potentially causing hypovolemic shock [32, 33].

Few case reports describe tamponade in patients with AN, requiring urgent cardiological intervention [34, 35, 36, 37]. Depending on the size of the largest fluid pocket, pericardial effusion can be trivial (detected only in systole), mild (< 10 mm), moderate (10–20 mm), or large/severe (> 20 mm) [38, 39, 40].

A total of nine studies [11, 12, 13, 15, 17, 18, 21, 25, 26] showed signs of PEf, mostly mild to moderate in severity. None of the included articles showed signs of tamponade; four [13, 17, 18, 25] reported the disappearance of PEf in some patients after refeeding, as indicated in subsequent measurements.

Valvular aberrations

Physiologically, the Mitral Valve (MV) opens during diastole to ensure one-way blood flow from the left atrium (LA) to the left ventricle (LV), and closes during systole to prevent blood from returning to the atrium. The anterior and posterior mitral leaflets are held in place by the papillary muscles, which attach to them via tendinous cords, preventing them from opening incorrectly.

Mitral Valve Prolapse (MVP) is the most commonly described valvular abnormality in patients with AN. MVP is characterized by the movement of one or both leaflets of the mitral valve into the atrium with each contraction of the left ventricle [41]. The mechanism by which MVP occurs in AN has yet to be fully understood. One theory suggests that a decrease in left ventricular mass and reduced left ventricular cavity size may lead to a relative valvular laxity and subsequently to mitral valve prolapse [5, 42]. The cohort study by Oka et al. revealed that there is no significant correlation between left ventricular dimension and mitral valve prolapse; however, all patients exhibited low heart rates, and the severity of bradycardia was correlated with the severity of MVP [43]. It has been proposed that AN is linked to autonomic dysfunction, characterized by an elevation in vagal tone, as indicated by bradycardia [43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48].

A total of four studies [12, 14, 15, 26] included in the review described a valvar abnormality; each study reported at least one case of mitral valve prolapse. One study specifically examined different valvar anomalies—Jomah et al. assessed 170 anorexic patients, of whom 15 exhibited one or more vavulvar deformities. Among these, 10 had tricuspid regurgitation (TR), 9 had mitral regurgitation (MR), and/or other issues. Aortic regurgitation (AR), pulmonary regurgitation (PR), and mitral valve prolapse (MVP) were seen in just 1, 2, and 3 patients respectively. Of the 15 valvural abnormalities observed, 14 were reported as mild or trivial, with one case of MR assessed as moderate [12].

Aortic stiffness

Arterial stiffness is a pathophysiological condition of blood vessels characterized by reduced elasticity and increased rigidity of the arterial walls. This process is linked to natural aging but can be hastened by various disease factors. The gold standard method for noninvasively assessing arterial stiffness is the carotid-femoral pulse-wave velocity (cfPWV) measurement, which is defined as the speed at which a pulsatile blood wave travels along the length of an artery [49, 50]. This velocity is closely related to the flexibility or rigidity of the arterial walls—stiffer arteries cause the pulse wave to move faster, resulting in a higher PWV reading. Elevated arterial stiffness is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction, hypertension, heart failure, and stroke [51, 52].

The processes leading to increased aortic stiffness in AN are likely complex and multifactorial. Few reports have linked AN with aortic stiffness [9, 53, 54]. One hypothesis suggests that a low body mass index (BMI) may contribute to increased arterial stiffness due to heightened oxidative stress, reduced nitric oxide availability, and endothelial dysfunction, which can accelerate vascular aging and arteriosclerosis [53, 54, 55]. Another hypothesis proposes that structural changes in the vascular system, autonomic nervous system dysfunction, and hormonal factors—such as estrogen deficiency—may also affect arterial compliance [53, 56, 57, 58, 59].

One study [9] demonstrated increased arterial stiffness in patients with AN. Escudero et al. found that PWV was significantly higher in adolescents with AN compared to controls, with a mean increase of 15%. Aortic stiffness in AN patients was not predicted by BMI. According to Tee et al.‘s pilot study, arterial stiffness may improve with weight restoration; however, the small sample size warrants cautious interpretation of these findings [53]. The underlying mechanisms behind PWV changes associated with AN are complex and require further investigation exploration.

Follow-up after weight restoration

A total of six studies [8, 13, 14, 17, 18, 25] documented the resolution of symptoms in some patients after weight restoration: two studies [8, 14] described significant increases in left ventricular mass, left ventricular systolic and diastolic dimensions, as well as cardiac output. Four studies [13, 17, 18, 25] showed the disappearance of pericardial effusion in some patients after refeeding, with resolution of pericardial fluid ranging from 62% to 100%. One study [14] noted that mitral valve prolapse found in eight patients with AN persisted in three of them after weight recovery.

Correlation with body mass index

A total of nine studies [10, 13, 15, 16, 18, 19, 20, 21, 25] demonstrated the existence of direct associations between BMI and the following echocardiological findings: two studies [18, 25] report a strong correlation between BMI and the incidence of pericardial effusions (although only one [18] correlates the size of PEf with BMI). Two studies [13, 21] noted a correlation between BMI and hypotension, while four studies [10, 15, 16, 18, 20] outlined a correlation between BMI and a reduction in left ventricular mass. One study [20] identified a correlation between BMI and decreased cardiac output, as well as reduced left ventricular end-systolic and end-diastolic diameters.

Exercise duration was independently predicted by BMI percentile in one study [19]. Aortic stiffness in patients with AN, as described in the study by Escudero et al., was not predicted by BMI [9]. One study [16], although highlighting the correlation between BMI and LVM, states that “even subjects with a BMI of 12 kg/m2 did not show any sign of impairment of cardiac performance”.

Discussion

The analyzed studies were published between 1983 and 2024, including 11 case-control studies, 4 retrospective cohort studies, 3 case series, 2 longitudinal observational studies, 1 cross-sectional study, and 1 systematic review and meta-analysis. There were no prospective studies, and none of them analyzed cardiological risk factors or predictors specific to AN.

The broad timeframe of the included studies (1983–2024) implies that patients were diagnosed using different versions of the DSM. Earlier criteria, such as in DSM-III and DSM-IV, used more rigid weight thresholds for diagnosis. The transition to DSM-5 in 2013 introduced more flexible weight criteria, which may have led to the inclusion of patients with less extreme physiological depletion in recent research. This shift could explain the differences in pericardial effusion rates or left ventricular mass reduction between older and newer studies. Patients diagnosed under older, stricter criteria might have shown more pronounced cardiac remodeling due to the higher degree of starvation required for a diagnosis at that time.

There is a lack of sufficient established data to suggest predictive markers for the onset of AN that could inform preventive measures in pre-adolescent or adolescent populations. Future research should continue to assess potential risk factors that have been previously identified in case-control and retrospective studies, within larger prospective studies focusing on preadolescent populations. At the time of writing this study, there is one systematic review of prospective studies evaluating risk factors for predicting the onset of AN [60], which included 3 research papers [61, 62, 63]. These studies suggest many potential factors that may predict future occurrence of AN, none of them explicitly addressed the cardiovascular system.

The results of this review demonstrate that AN is linked to a decrease in left ventricular mass, impaired systolic and diastolic functions, reduced cardiac output, the presence of pericardial effusions, and valvular abnormalities, especially mitral valve prolapse. This study also notes a significant reduction in symptoms in some patients after weight restoration.

Routine echocardiography for every asymptomatic patient with AN remains a subject of clinical debate regarding resource allocation, our findings provide essential baseline data for this population. Rather than universal screening, echocardiography should be prioritized for patients with clinical symptoms, severe malnutrition, or abnormal physical findings. In such cases, our review helps clinicians distinguish expected adaptations to starvation—such as small pericardial effusions or reduced LV mass—from more concerning pathologies, preventing unnecessary investigations. Repeating echocardiography after weight restoration allows for assessing the effectiveness of treatment on cardiovascular function.

This is the first systematic review to catalog echocardiographically observed structural and functional cardiac abnormalities in patients with AN, focusing solely on those under 18 years of age. AN is a psychiatric disorder primarily managed by child and adolescent psychiatrists, with their care usually concluding when the patient reaches adulthood.

Our findings align with the meta-analysis by Smythe et al. [5], which also identified decreased myocardial mass and impaired function as hallmarks of AN. While some primary studies overlap, our review focuses exclusively on children and adolescents. The fact that findings in youth match those in older patients suggests that cardiac changes are not just a result of long-term malnutrition. Instead, they occur rapidly during development. This confirms that pediatric patients show similar cardiac remodeling to adults, despite a much shorter duration of illness.

In children and adolescents, the cardiovascular system undergoes a period of active development: the heart continues to grow in size and mass, while the ejection fraction and chamber wall dimensions change. During this growth phase, a decrease in muscle mass and cardiac output often indicates a failure to grow properly rather than simple atrophy. Cardiovascular symptoms related to AN in teenagers are likely to improve after weight is regained, as demonstrated by the studies in this systematic review. As people age, more factors can influence how the cardiovascular system functions. Factors such as multimorbidity, reduced blood vessel elasticity, or weakened contractile ability can limit the heart’s adaptability, possibly leading to different compensatory responses. Additional research is needed to understand the mechanisms behind cardiovascular changes in adolescent patients with AN.

The present review focuses specifically on echocardiographic alterations as primary indicators of structural and functional cardiac changes in adolescent patients with AN, it is essential to consider these findings within the broader context of cardiovascular risk markers. In line with the consensus statement of the World Federation of Societies of Biological Psychiatry (WFSBP) [64], it is well-established that AN is frequently associated with significant ECG abnormalities. These include sinus bradycardia, T-wave flattening or preterminal T-wave negativization, the presence of U-waves, and QT interval changes. Disturbances such as supraventricular replacement rhythms, as well as sinoatrial and atrioventricular block patterns, are often documented in this population. Although our research is delimited to echocardiographic parameters, the coexistence of these electrophysiological disturbances highlights the multifaceted nature of cardiac involvement in AN and underscores the need for a comprehensive cardiological assessment in clinical practice.

Limitations

A significant proportion of studies (7/19) had small sample sizes (fewer than 30 patients). Further research with larger sample sizes is recommended. Most reviewed literature focused on female patients, which limits our understanding of cardiac remodeling in males with AN. In the large cohort study by Jomah et al. [12], a higher proportion of male participants (18.6%) was included; the authors however did not provide specific echocardiographic data broken down by sex for the analyzed subgroup. This leaves a significant gap in our understanding of how AN affects the male heart specifically. Inconsistent reporting of illness duration and weight suppression also makes it difficult to determine how these factors independently influence the severity of heart abnormalities.

In children and adolescents, raw BMI values do not account for expected developmental growth. Most reviewed studies used BMI, age- and sex-adjusted metrics—such as BMI z-scores or percentiles—are the gold standard for this age group. This limitation is important when comparing different pediatric cohorts, as a specific BMI value can represent different degrees of malnutrition depending on a patient’s age and sex.

According to the World Bank’s classifications of countries by income level for 2024–2025, including all literature except for one study from Turkey (classified as Upper-middle Income), most studies originate from high-income countries: the USA with 6 studies, Italy with 5, Canada with 3, Spain with 2 studies plus 1 systematic review, Germany with 2, Belgium with 1, and Israel with 1 [65]. This concentration limits the external validity. Every research paper followed a specific echocardiographic protocol, but not all adhered to the same one, which may lead to discrepancies in measurements. Other factors that could affect the reliability of the results include the experience and practical training of the person performing the echocardiography, possible feedback from the supervisor, the anatomical conditions of the patient, and the patient’s willingness to cooperate.

A potential limitation of this systematic review is the exclusive inclusion of studies published in English, which may introduce a geographic bias and lead to the omission of relevant data from lower-income countries. The findings may not fully reflect the clinical reality in resource-limited settings, where differences in medical guidelines, healthcare access, and diagnostic technology could influence patient outcomes. The search was restricted to a single database (PubMed), which creates the possibility that some pertinent studies were not identified. However, the primary objective of this review was to delineate the general structural and functional cardiac alterations typical of children and adolescents, which may not be directly comparable to those observed in adult populations, where a longer duration of illness and age-related comorbidities could influence the cardiovascular response to chronic malnutrition.
