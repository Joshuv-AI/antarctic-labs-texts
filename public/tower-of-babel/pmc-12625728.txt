Epidemiological and clinical characteristics of West Nile virus infections in Istanbul, Türkiye: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Abdullah Emre Güner, Mehmet Burhan Küçükoğlu, Bekir Aktura, Pınar Kıran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWest Nile virus (WNV) infection is a mosquito-borne infection that is usually asymptomatic but can also cause serious neurological diseases. This study aimed to determine the incidence and seasonal distribution of WNV infections in Istanbul, Turkiye, as well as risk factors for neuroinvasive disease and mortality.MethodsA population-based cohort study was conducted using surveillance data collected by the Health Directorate of Istanbul between 2010 and 2024. Cases meeting the national and EU case definitions for confirmed or probable WNV infection were included. Clinical, demographic, laboratory data, and outcomes were extracted from medical records. Relative risks (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using univariate analyses and log binomial regression to identify risk factors for neuroinvasive disease and mortality.ResultsA total of 159 reported WNV cases were included in the study, with incidence rates of 0.53 and 0.48 per 100,000 person-years calculated for the 2019 and 2024 outbreaks, respectively. All infections were reported between June and October. The mean patient age was 52.9 years; 65.4% were male. West Nile neuroinvasive disease (WNND) was diagnosed in 50.9% of cases, with encephalitis as the most common manifestation. The overall case fatality rate was 5.7% in all case, and 9.8% among WNND cases. Age was identified as an independent risk factor for WNND (RRadj=1.016, p < 0.001) according to the log binominal regression model. In univariate analyses, non-survivors were older (RR = 1.08; 95% CI: 1.02–1.14) and more frequently had diabetes mellitus (RR = 3.98; 95% CI: 1.13–14.08) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (RR = 7.29; 95% CI: 1.90–27.91) compared with survivors.ConclusionsThis study highlights the epidemiological and clinical characteristics of WNV infections over a 15-year period in Istanbul, Turkiye, identifying two major outbreaks in 2019 and 2024, with seasonal peaks in summer and early fall. The findings highlight the importance of increased clinical awareness and the inclusion of WNV in the differential diagnosis of encephalitic presentations, particularly in elderly patients during periods of high transmission.Keywords: West Nile virus, West Nile fever, Neuroinvasive disease, Mosquito-borne diseases, Outcomes

Background

West Nile Virus (WNV) is an arthropod-borne VİRUS primarily transmitted to humans through the bite of infected mosquitoes, following a mosquito–bird–mosquito transmission cycle [1]. Transmission through blood transfusion and organ/tissue transplantation has also been documented [2]. WNV is a neurotropic virus that is most often asymptomatic or presents with mild symptoms such as fever, fatigue, and myalgia; however, it may rarely progress to WNND [1]. Approximately 80% of human WNV infections are asymptomatic; less than 1% of infected individuals develop neuroinvasive disease [3].

Although WNV has been isolated or detected by molecular and serological methods in more than forty mosquito species, mosquitoes of the Culex genus are recognized as the primary vectors [4]. The transmission cycle of the virus predominantly occurs between birds and mosquitoes, with wild birds serving as the main reservoir, and during periods of high viremia, playing a critical role in infecting vector [5]. Humans, by contrast, are incidental hosts and do not contribute to sustaining the transmission cycle [6].

WNV was first isolated in 1937 from a human in the West Nile region of Uganda and was later identified in birds in Egypt’s Nile Delta in 1953 [7]. In the 1990s, WNV outbreaks were observed in Romania, Russia, Italy, Spain, and Morocco [8]. A lineage 2 WNV strain, which emerged in Central Europe in 2004 and spread to Central and Southern Europe, has caused outbreaks of neuroinvasive diseases in humans and animals [9, 10].

Currently, there is no specific antiviral agent for the treatment of WNV infection, nor is there a licensed vaccine for its prevention in humans. Therefore, avoiding exposure to mosquito bites remains the primary strategy for preventing WNV infection [11]. Of the WNV cases reported in non-human mammals, 96.9% have occurred in horses, for which vaccination is recommended as a preventive measure [12]. Additionally, the use of genetically modified mosquitoes has emerged as a novel preventive strategy for disease control [13].

WNV seropositivity has been detected in humans and animals in Türkiye since 1971; however, the first case of WNND was reported in 2009 [14, 15]. During the first nationwide outbreak in 2010, a total of 47 West Nile fever cases were identified, with a case fatality rate of 21%. Most of these cases were reported from the western regions of the country [16]. The aim of this study was to determine the epidemiological and clinical features of WNV infections reported in Istanbul province, where more than one-fifth of the Turkish population lives, using surveillance data collected over a 15-year period. We sought to ascertain the incidence and seasonal distribution of WNV infections in this area, as well as risk factors of neuroinvasive illness and mortality, by evaluating WNV cases reported in two outbreaks. The study offers valuable information to improve clinical recognition, assist public health planning, and guide prevention and control tactics in endemic areas.

Method

WNV infection is a notifiable disease in Türkiye, and human surveillance is conducted passively, with clinicians required to report suspected or confirmed cases to local health authorities. This population-based cohort study included all confirmed and probable WNV infection cases reported by healthcare facilities in Istanbul to the Health Directorate of Istanbul as part of the national surveillance system between January 2010 and December 2024. The study excluded cases with inadequate clinical or laboratory data, as well as those that did not fit the criteria for probable or confirmed WNV infection. The case definitions were based on the West Nile Virus Infection Case Management Guideline issued by the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Türkiye and the Commission Implementing Decision (EU). A confirmed case is a patient who satisfies the clinical case definition and is supported by at least one confirmatory laboratory finding. These laboratory data were as follows: (1) isolation of WNV from blood or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF); (2) detection of WNV nucleic acid in blood or CSF; (3) identification of WNV-specific IgM in CSF; (4) demonstration of WNV- specific IgM high titre and IgG in serum confirmed by neutralization testing. Individuals meeting the clinical case definition who had at least one epidemiological criterion (e.g., person-to-person transmission through blood transfusion or organ transplantation, travel to an endemic area, or vector-borne transmission via mosquito bites) or a supporting laboratory finding (e.g., detection of WNV-specific IgM antibodies in serum or WNV nucleic acid in urine) were classified as probable cases [17, 18].

Data for this study were obtained from medical records and included age, gender, comorbidities, and clinical symptoms such as fever, neurological involvement, maculopapular rash, and headache. Additional variables included the clinical form of the disease (West Nile fever or WNND), date of diagnosis, presence and type of neurological involvement, history of mosquito bites, hospitalization status, and hospital mortality. Laboratory data were also collected and comprised results of WNV RT-PCR testing on serum, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and urine samples; immunofluorescence assay findings for WNV-specific IgM and IgG antibodies; and confirmation of seropositivity using plaque reduction neutralization testing (PRNT). All laboratory analyses were performed at the Republic of Turkiye Ministry of Health, Department of Microbiology Reference Laboratories and The Biological Product Unit.

Continuous variables were reported as either mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), based on the data distribution, and categorical variables were displayed as numbers and percentages (n [%]). Annual incidence rates were calculated and expressed per 100,000 person-years, using the mid-year population of Istanbul for each calendar year obtained from Turkish Statistical Institute data as the denominator.

Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data and the Student’s t-test for normally distributed data were used to evaluate continuous variables. To identify independent risk factors associated with WNND, variables that were statistically significant in univariate analyses were included in a log binominal regression model. Adjusted relative risks (RRadj) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. IBM SPSS version 26.0 was used for the statistical analysis.

Result

A total of 159 confirmed and probable WNV cases were identified in Istanbul—1 in 2017, 74 in 2019, and 84 in 2024—all of which were indigenous. WNV-specific IgM antibodies were identified in 98.8% (n = 84) of the 85 probable cases tested serologically, while WNV nucleic acid was detected by PCR in urine samples from 12 of these cases. Among the 74 confirmed cases, 68.9% tested positive for WNV-specific IgM antibodies and 72.9% for WNV-specific IgG antibodies. Seropositivity was further confirmed by PRNT in 45 sera. WNV nucleic acid was also detected by PCR in cerebrospinal fluid samples from 14 and in serum/plasma samples from 19 confirmed cases. According to the surveillance data, two outbreaks of WNV infection have been identified in Istanbul since 2010. The incidence rate was calculated as 0.48 per 100,000 person-year in 2019 and 0.53 per 100,000 in 2024. All reported cases were detected between June and October. In 2019, cases of WNV infection were determined between June and September; the highest incidence rate was detected in July with 0.28 per 100,000 person-year. In 2024, cases were identified between July and October with the highest incidence rate of 0.20 per 100,000 person-year in August (Fig. 1).

The mean age of the reported patients was 52.9 ± 21.7 [Range: 2–90] years, with 65.4% (n = 104) being male. Regarding comorbidities, 6.9% of the patients were immunosuppressed, 23.9% had diabetes mellitus, 28.3% had hypertension, 3.8% had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), 2.6% had chronic kidney disease, and 12.6% had chronic heart disease. Fever was the most common symptom, present in 138 (86.8%) cases, followed by headache in 102 (64.2%) and maculopapular rash in 53 (33.3%). 66 patients (41.5%) had a history of mosquito bites.

WNND was present in 50.9% of cases (n = 81). Neurological involvement included encephalitis (26.4%), meningitis (11.3%), meningoencephalitis (11.3%), and acute flaccid paralysis (6.9%). A total of 123 cases (77.4%) required hospitalization. The case fatality rate was 5.7% (n = 9) in all cases and 9.8% in WNND cases.

Patients with WNND were significantly older and had a higher prevalence of hypertension compared with those with West Nile fever (p < 0.001 and p = 0.004, respectively). The characteristics of the cases are summarized in Table 2. Variables found to be significant in the univariate analysis were further evaluated using binary logistic regression to identify independent risk factors for WNND. Age was found to be an independent predictor (RRadj = 1.016; 95% CI:1.007–1.024; p < 0.001), while hypertension was not statistically significant in the multivariate model (RRadj = 0.942; 95% CI: 0.697–1.274; p = 0.698).

A comparison of survivors and non-survivors with WNV infection revealed several significant differences. A one-year increase in age was associated with an 8% higher risk of mortality (RR = 1.08; 95% CI: 1.02–1.14; p = 0.003). Among comorbidities, diabetes mellitus was significantly more common in non-survivors (55.6%) compared with survivors (22.0%), conferring nearly a four-fold increase in mortality risk (RR = 3.98; 95% CI: 1.13–14.08; p = 0.036). Similarly, COPD was observed in 22.2% of non-survivors versus 2.7% of survivors, also representing a statistically significant difference (RR = 7.29; 95%:1.90-27.91; p = 0.038) (Table 3).

Discussion

This population-based cohort study presents a comprehensive overview of WNV cases in Istanbul over a 15-year period, identifying two significant outbreaks in 2019 and 2024. This study, which examines the epidemiological and clinical features of WNV infection in a densely populated urban setting, includes the largest sample size published to date from Turkiye.

The first case of WNV infection in Türkiye was reported in 2009 [15]. Seroprevalence studies conducted across various regions have shown WNV seroprevalence in humans ranging from 2.4% to 17% in Türkiye [19–21]. According to the findings of our study, two outbreaks of WNV infection were subsequently identified in the province, occurring in 2019 and 2024 in Istanbul, Turkiye. Additionally, WNV outbreaks have been reported from many countries in the Mediterranean basic and European region [22]. During the 2024 outbreak in Israel, the median age of patients was 77 years—higher than in our cohort—and the case fatality rate reached 12%. The epidemic in Israel also peaked earlier, in June and July, compared with Istanbul where peaks occurred later in the summer [23]. Similarly, in a WNV outbreak in southern Greece, the median patient age was 64.5 years, the incidence of WNND was 2.5 times higher in men than in women, and outbreaks often began as early as May and extended until August, demonstrating variability in seasonal onset [24]. In contrast, the Istanbul cohort was characterized by a younger median age, a smaller male predominance, and a consistent seasonal peak between July and October. These distinctions indicate that the unique features of the outbreaks in Istanbul are likely influenced by local demographic factors and climatic conditions.

WNV has been a notifiable disease in Türkiye since 2010, and vector control efforts have been carried out effectively. Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry monitors bird popuiations for WNV and has been vaccinating horses since 2019 in Turkiye. WNV seroprevalence was determined as 13.5% in horses and 2.5% in sentinel neurological surveillance of backyard hens [25, 26]. Municipalities, on the other hand, monitor and control mosquito populations and reduce breeding sites. These activities, together with climate variability, increasing clinician awareness, and evolving vector control strategies, may have influenced the number of detected cases and the dynamics of outbreaks over the 15 years of surveillance.

Rising global temperatures caused by climate change lead to droughts, which facilitate the spread of vector-borne diseases [27]. The epidemiology of WNV is shaped by bird hosts, mosquito vectors, and human activities, all of which are impacted by climatic factors [28]. Positive correlations were found between the average temperature of the previous 1 week to 1 month and WNV incidence in human [27]. The incidence of WNV cases in humans was positively associated with summer temperatures between 15 °C and 28 °C [29]. There is also data that high spring temperatures extend the epidemic season [30]. The average temperatures in Istanbul during the months of July and August, when epidemic peaks were recorded in 2019 and 2024, were 25.8 °C and 26.5 °C, respectively. The optimal range for WNV transmission is encompassed by these values. In addition, the average temperatures in all months in which cases were reported ranged between 17.5 °C and 27.6 °C, supporting the association between WNV and temperature in previous publications [31].

Male gender, age, immunocompromised, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus have been previously reported as independent risk factors for WNND, with age increasing the risk by approximately 2% per year [32, 33]. In our study, both hypertension and age were significantly associated with the development of neuroinvasive disease in univariate analyses; however, only age identifed as an independent risk factor. Furthermore, we found that each additional year of age was associated with a 1.6% increase in the risk of WNND.

Previous studies have identified advanced age, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, secondary bacterial infections, and immunosuppression as factors associated with an increased risk of mortality in WNV infections [34–36]. In our findings, advanced age and comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus and COPD were significantly associated with mortality among WNV infection cases. However, due to the limited number of fatal cases (n = 9), we were unable to perform multivariable modeling to estimate adjusted risks. Therefore, these variables could not be identified as independent risk factors. According to the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, the case fatality rate for individuals with WNND may reach up to 17% [37]. We found the case fatality rate to be 9.8% in WNDD cases.

While less than 1% of WNV infections progress to WNND, approximately 20% result in West Nile fever [38]. In our study, 81 cases of WNND were identified in Istanbul, Turkiye. Since WNND cases accounts for a small proportion of total WNV infections, this number probably represents a considerably higher, unrecognized infection burden in the community.

WNND primarily affects the central nervous system, with encephalitis being the most common clinical manifestation [39, 40]. It can also result in neuromuscular impairments [41]. West Nile virus has been reported as the leading cause of epidemic meningoencephalitis in many regions [42]. In our study, encephalitis was the most frequently observed form of WNND. Healthcare professionals should consider WNV infection in the differential diagnosis, particularly in elderly patients presenting with an encephalitic clinical picture between June and October in İstanbul.

This study has some limitations. First, as it relies on surveillance data, unreported cases could not be included. Moreover, because WNV surveillance in Türkiye is passive, sporadic or subclinical infections during the inter-epidemic period may have been underreported. Second, because a large proportion of WNV infections are asymptomatic or present with mild symptoms, many individuals may not have been tested, potentially leading to underreporting and misclassification. Third, since mortality was observed in only 9 cases, it was not possible to perform multivariable modeling; risk ratios (RR) were calculated but could not be adjusted for confounding factors. The small number of fatal cases precludes robust statistical analyses to identify independent risk factors for mortality. Although age, diabetes, and COPD were associated with mortality in univariate analyses, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as potential confounders could not be controlled.

Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths. It includes 15 years of WNV surveillance data from Istanbul, a province that is home to approximately one-fifth of Türkiye’s population. As such, it represents the most comprehensive study to date on WNV infection in humans in Türkiye. The detailed assessment of patient demographics, comorbidities, and clinical outcomes provides critical insights into disease severity and associated risk factors, offering valuable evidence to inform public health planning and clinical management strategies.

Conclusion

This study highlights the epidemiological and clinical characteristics of WNV infections over a 15-year period in Istanbul, Türkiye, with most cases occurring during two major outbreaks in 2019 and 2024. According to our findings, both hypertension and age were significantly associated with the development of neuroinvasive disease in univariate analyses; age was an independent risk factor, with each additional year increasing the risk of severe disease by approximately 1.6%. Comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus and COPD, and advanced age were found to be significantly associated with mortality in WNV infection cases in univariate analyses. The outbreaks have shown a seasonal pattern with peak incidence in summer and early fall. This seasonality, together with the demographic characteristics of those affected, highlights the need for increased clinical awareness and a focus on vulnerable groups during periods of high transmission. Additionally, our results suggest that clinician education on WNV is essential to ensure early diagnosis, targeted prevention strategies should be directed toward priority groups such as the elderly and individuals with comorbidities, and vector surveillance and control should be intensified during high-risk months to reduce the impact of outbreaks.
