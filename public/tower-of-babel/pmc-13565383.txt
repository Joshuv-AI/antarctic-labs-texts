Forearm Nonunion and Segmental Bone Defects: A Focused Review of Masquelet Reconstruction and Free Vascularized Bone Grafting
Authors: Rita Saad, Johnny Saadeh, Fouad Assaf, Antoine Saber, Saadeddine Abouzahr, Elyssa Kiwan, Wendy Ghanem, Mohamad Badra, Ramzi Moucharafieh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSegmental bone loss and chronic nonunion of the forearm present major reconstructive challenges due to the forearm’s unique functional anatomy and the frequent coexistence of biological compromise, infection, and soft-tissue deficiency. The purpose of this review is to analyze the pathophysiology of forearm nonunion and to compare the Masquelet induced membrane technique (IMT) and free vascularized bone grafting (VBG) as contemporary reconstructive strategies, focusing on indications, biological rationale, outcomes, and complications. A narrative review of the English-language literature was conducted using PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library, with searches performed through December to February 2026. Search terms included combinations of: forearm nonunion, segmental bone defect, Masquelet technique, induced membrane, vascularized bone graft, free fibular graft, upper extremity reconstruction, and forearm fracture. Clinical and experimental studies addressing forearm nonunion or segmental defects treated with IMT or VBG were included; non-English publications and studies not reporting union or functional outcomes were excluded. Emphasis was placed on biological principles, surgical techniques, union rates, functional outcomes, complication profiles, and factors influencing technique selection. Based on predominantly retrospective, single-center series with heterogeneous defect characteristics, both IMT and VBG appear to demonstrate favorable union rates when applied in appropriately selected patients; however, direct forearm-specific comparative evidence remains limited and largely low-level. IMT provides a staged, biologically favorable environment for graft incorporation and is particularly effective in infected or moderate-sized defects, while avoiding microsurgical complexity. VBGs, especially free vascularized fibular grafts, offer intrinsic vascularity and structural stability, making them advantageous for large defects, compromised local biology, or salvage after failed reconstruction, though with increased technical demands and donor-site morbidity. IMT and VBG are complementary rather than competing strategies in forearm reconstruction. Given the scarcity of high-quality comparative data, outcome optimization relies on individualized decision-making informed by defect size, biological environment, soft-tissue status, patient factors, and surgical expertise, pending more robust prospective evidence.

Introduction

Segmental bone loss and chronic nonunion of the forearm present one of the more formidable reconstructive challenges in orthopaedic and hand surgery. The unique anatomy and function of the forearm, two parallel long bones acting as a unit to provide precise length, alignment, and rotational motion, mean that even small residual deformities may result in substantial loss of pronation-supination and hand function.1 Traumatic bone loss, infected or atrophic nonunions, and sequelae of failed prior reconstructions, therefore, carry the biological burden of impaired osteogenesis and a high functional cost that influences reconstructive strategy. The surgical planning for these problems ranges from traditional non-vascularized cancellous grafting to biologically sophisticated techniques such as the induced membrane (Masquelet) technique (IMT) and microsurgical free vascularized bone grafts (FVBGs), each with distinct biological rationales, technical demands, and risk–benefit profiles.2

Standard management of forearm fractures with compression plating and conventional cancellous bone grafting remains effective for many nonunions and small defects. However, this approach reaches its biological and mechanical limits in several well-defined clinical scenarios: segmental defects exceeding 4–6 cm, where graft volume and revascularization capacity are insufficient for reliable incorporation; infected nonunions, where residual biofilm and impaired local biology undermine graft survival; atrophic nonunions with depleted osteoprogenitor populations and poor vascularity; cases with compromised soft-tissue envelopes that cannot support graft revascularization; and following repeated failed reconstructions, where progressive scarring and devascularization exist. In these circumstances, escalation to advanced reconstructive strategies, specifically the IMT or FVBG becomes necessary to restore a biologically competent environment and achieve durable union.

Alain Masquelet’s induced membrane technique (IMT) has risen to prominence since its formal description because it provides a pragmatic, two-stage approach to reconstructing segmental bone defects without necessarily requiring microsurgical transfer.3 The first stage combines radical debridement, skeletal stabilization, and an antibiotic-loaded polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) spacer eliciting a vascularized pseudosynovial membrane. In the second stage the spacer is removed, and the membrane-enclosed defect is filled with autologous cancellous graft, leveraging the membrane’s biologic milieu to protect and vascularize the graft.4 IMT is appealing for many forearm reconstructions because it can manage infection and create a locally favorable environment for graft incorporation while limiting donor-site morbidity relative to structural vascularized graft harvest.3,4

FVBGs, particularly fibular grafts, provide living bone with blood supply, osteogenic cells, and preserved structure, ideal for long-segment defects, compromised biology, or failed reconstructions. They offer reliable mechanical outcomes and hypertrophy but demand microsurgical expertise, longer surgery, and careful donor-site management.5

Both IMT and FVBG achieve high union rates in upper extremity reconstruction but differ in complexity and morbidity. IMT is staged, avoids microsurgery, and suits moderate defects or infected beds, while FVBG offers single-stage structural restoration for large or biologically compromised defects at the cost of microsurgical risk and donor-site morbidity. Technique selection should consider defect size, infection, soft tissue, patient factors, and surgical expertise.6 While prior reviews have addressed segmental bone defects broadly across long bones, this review addresses a gap in the literature by focusing specifically on the forearm, a biomechanically unique segment where restoration of length, rotation, and interosseous anatomy is critical to hand function. By synthesizing recent clinical and biological evidence and proposing a practical decision-making framework, this review aims to guide individualized reconstruction and optimize functional results in forearm nonunion and segmental bone defects. This review is intended to serve as a practical reference for orthopedic trauma surgeons, hand surgeons, and reconstructive microsurgeons who encounter complex forearm bone defects and nonunions, providing a framework to guide technique selection and perioperative decision-making in these challenging cases.

Methods

A narrative review of the English-language literature was conducted using PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. The databases were searched from inception through February 28, 2026. Search terms were used individually and in combination and included forearm nonunion, segmental bone defect, Masquelet technique, induced membrane technique, vascularized bone graft, free vascularized fibular graft, upper extremity reconstruction, forearm fracture nonunion, and bone defect reconstruction.

The database search identified 82 records. After removal of 7 duplicate records, 75 titles and abstracts were screened for relevance. Articles considered potentially eligible underwent full-text review, with 62 full-text articles assessed against the predefined eligibility criteria. Following full-text assessment, 50 studies were retained for inclusion in the narrative synthesis. Relevant references from the retrieved articles were also reviewed to identify additional studies not captured by the primary database search.

Inclusion criteria were peer-reviewed clinical or experimental studies published in English that addressed forearm nonunion or segmental bone defects treated with the induced membrane technique (IMT), free vascularized bone grafting (VBG), or relevant comparative reconstructive techniques, and that reported union rates, functional outcomes, complications, or other clinically relevant outcomes.

Exclusion criteria were non-English-language publications; studies focused exclusively on lower-extremity reconstruction without data or principles applicable to forearm reconstruction; abstracts, conference proceedings, and non-peer-reviewed literature; and studies lacking reportable union, functional, or clinically relevant outcome data.

Given the clinical and methodological heterogeneity of the included literature and the absence of a formal pooled analysis, the findings were synthesized narratively.

Pathophysiology of Forearm Nonunion

Forearm nonunion represents the endpoint of a complex interplay between mechanical instability, biological failure, vascular compromise, infection, and soft-tissue problems. Because the forearm functions as a unit, nonunion of one bone can lead to progressive deformity, altered load-sharing, stiffness, and functional impairment. The key pathophysiological drivers can be organized into mechanical versus biological categories.

Mechanical Factors and Stability

Stable fixation and restoration of length and rotation are fundamental to fracture healing. In the forearm, rotational alignment is particularly critical, as small angular or rotational malreductions amplify distal radioulnar joint incongruity and impair pronation–supination.1,7 Mechanical failure occurs when fixation is inadequate (insufficient rigidity, poor plate/bone contact, screw loosening), when large segmental defects prevent bony contact, or when cyclic loading in the context of comminution promotes micromotion that exceeds the biology’s capacity for callus formation.1 Chronic mechanical instability causes fibrous nonunion (interposed soft tissue and sclerotic bone ends), which is characterized radiographically by rounded, sclerotic bone ends and histologically by a paucity of active osteoblasts. The clinical implication is clear: without restoration of stability, biological measures alone are unlikely to succeed.8,9

Biological Failure: Cellular and Molecular Deficits

Successful bone healing proceeds through a coordinated cascade of inflammation, recruitment of mesenchymal progenitors, chondrogenesis/osteogenesis, neovascularization, and remodeling.8 Failure at any stage produces a biological nonunion phenotype. In the setting of atrophic nonunion, the local cellular milieu is often depleted: reduced numbers of osteoprogenitor cells, diminished osteoblastic activity, and poor angiogenesis. Histologically, atrophic nonunion shows scant calluses and little bone formation; conversely, hypertrophic nonunion displays abundant callus with instability.10 Cellular senescence, prior surgical trauma, radiation, or repeated infection can all blunt the regenerative response. Strategies that bring viable cells or restore a pro-osteogenic microenvironment directly address these deficits.5

Vascularity and Microcirculation

Bone healing is an energy-dependent process that relies on robust perfusion. The periosteal and endosteal microcirculation supply oxygen, nutrients, and cellular trafficking essential for osteogenesis and remodeling. Traumatic injury, open fractures, multiple debridements, or local scarring can disrupt these vascular networks. In chronic osteomyelitis, devascularized necrotic bone (sequestra) further worsens local biology.11 Vascular compromise reduces delivery of osteoprogenitors and impairs immune cell access and antibiotic penetration, complicating infection control. In this context, FVBGs offer an attractive physiological advantage: they provide immediate intrinsic blood flow, living osteocytes, and a periosteal sleeve capable of producing new bone and facilitating revascularization.5

Infection and Chronic Osteomyelitis

Infected nonunions are particularly recalcitrant because infection both perpetuates local tissue damage and antagonizes regenerative processes. Bacterial biofilms on necrotic bones or implants protect organisms from host defenses and antibiotics, necessitating aggressive debridement. Infection drives continued inflammation, proteolytic tissue destruction, and impaired osteogenesis.8,12 The staged Masquelet approach, which first aims to control contamination with debridement, spacer placement, and targeted antibiotics, also directly targets this pathophysiology by creating an environment where the membrane both isolates and biologically primes the defect for subsequent grafting. For persistent or extensive infection, where radical resection leaves large defects, transfer of vascularized viable bone can improve local immune access and antibiotic delivery, used as a single definitive reconstruction.5

Soft-Tissue Envelope and Composite Defects

The soft-tissue envelope is not merely a passive covering. It contributes to vascular inflow, provides a bed for revascularization, and maintains the mechanical milieu.13 Severe soft-tissue loss, scarring, or requirement for flap coverage complicates timing and type of reconstruction. Composite defects that include skin, tendon, nerve, or joint surface loss demand integrated reconstructive strategies, sometimes combining an osteocutaneous vascularized flap that addresses bone, soft tissue, and monitoring requirements simultaneously. The presence of inadequate soft tissue favors vascularized transfers or staged reconstructions incorporating flap coverage.14

Systemic and Patient Factors

Systemic host factors modulate local healing potential. Diabetes, smoking, malnutrition, chronic corticosteroid use, and certain medications are associated with impaired fracture healing and higher nonunion rates. Nicotine and poor glycemic control reduce microvascular perfusion and cellular function.8 Optimizing these systemic conditions is therefore an essential adjunct to any surgical plan and can tip the balance in favor of union once mechanical and local biological issues are addressed.15

Integration: How Pathophysiology Informs Technique Selection

Understanding these pathophysiological categories clarifies why no single reconstructive method is universally optimal. IMT is biologically rational where infection control and a pro-osteogenic membrane can convert a hostile bed into one receptive to cancellous graft, particularly when stability can be secured and defects are moderate. FVBGs are favored when host vascularity is poor, defects are large or structurally demanding, or when repeated prior failures suggest the need to supply living bone and immediate blood flow. In practice, reconstructive choice is individualized: surgeons weigh defect length, infection history, soft-tissue status, prior procedures, patient comorbidities, and local surgical expertise to select a strategy that best addresses the dominant pathophysiological impediments to union.

The Masquelet Induced Membrane Technique

History and Development

The IMT, commonly called the Masquelet technique, was first described by Alain C. Masquelet in the 1980s and has become well-known as a two-stage approach for reconstruction of segmental bone defects and chronic infected nonunions. Initially presented as a relatively simple alternative to free vascularized bone transfer, IMT has been extensively utilized for large bone defects and is increasingly applied to nonunions in the upper limb, including the forearm.2,16

Biological Principles

IMT relies on a membranous structure induced by a foreign body that develops around a PMMA spacer, which is often loaded with antibiotics. This induced membrane is highly vascularized and secretes angiogenic and osteoinductive factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) −2, fibroblast growth factor (FGF), establishing a biologically conducive environment for the incorporation of bone grafts.3,17 In experimental and translational models, many of these growth factors have been shown to reach their highest levels reach their highest levels in the initial weeks following spacer placement, usually noted around 4 to 6 weeks, which is the rationale for performing the second stage within that timeframe. Nonetheless, the actual duration between stages can vary significantly based on infection control and soft-tissue status.17

The membrane also contains osteoprogenitor cells and pericytes that directly contribute to osteogenesis within the graft space, supplementing the osteoinductive signals provided by the growth factor milieu. Angiogenesis within the membrane is driven primarily by VEGF and FGF signaling, establishing a vascular network that supports graft revascularization and creeping substitution in the second stage. Bone remodeling thereafter follows the standard coupled resorption-formation cycle, though this process may be delayed in previously infected or irradiated beds due to impaired osteoclast-osteoblast coupling and residual growth factor depletion.3,17

In contrast, FVBGs bypass these membrane-dependent biological steps by delivering living osteocytes, an intact periosteum, and a functioning microvasculature directly to the defect. The periosteum contributes osteoprogenitor cells capable of direct intramembranous ossification at the graft-host interface, while the preserved endosteal circulation supports osteocyte survival and early load-bearing capacity. This intrinsic biological sufficiency is the principal advantage of FVBG in environments where induced membrane formation would be unreliable, such as severely scarred, irradiated, or repeatedly operated beds.3,17

Surgical Technique

The first phase involves careful debridement, where all necrotic bone, fibrous tissue, and affected soft tissue are excised and several deep tissue cultures are obtained to guide antimicrobial therapy.15 After eliminating the infected or non-viable tissue, it’s crucial to restore skeletal stability. For the forearm, stable internal fixation using compression plating is usually preferred to reestablish anatomical length, alignment, and rotation, while external fixation might be an option if the soft-tissue conditions are unfavorable.9,18

Following fixation, the bone defect is filled with a PMMA cement spacer, which is frequently loaded with antibiotics.2,4 The spacer is carefully designed to maintain the length and rotational characteristics of the forearm while creating a suitable environment for membrane formation. Soft tissues are then closed directly whenever possible, or else, local or regional flaps are required.19 Systemic antibiotic treatment is initiated and adjusted according to culture results to ensure adequate infection control.15

The second phase is generally performed after a period of approximately 4 to 8 weeks, though the exact timing may be individualized based on the infection status, maturation of the membrane, and recovery of the soft tissues.4,20 At this stage, the induced membrane is meticulously incised, and further culture samples are collected to confirm eradication of infection.21 Care is taken to remove the PMMA spacer, while preserving the membrane, as it acts as a biological chamber essential for the incorporation of the graft.2

Following spacer removal, the edges of the bone are refreshed, and the site for the graft is prepared. The defect is filled with autologous cancellous bone graft, which is usually harvested from the iliac crest or obtained using the Reamer–Irrigator–Aspirator (RIA) technique.9,18 In cases with larger defects, the autograft may be supplemented with morselized allograft or bone substitutes to ensure adequate volume.15,16 Once the graft has been placed, the induced membrane is closed over it to contain the graft material and sustain a conducive biological environment. Subsequently, the soft tissues are repaired, and stable fixation is maintained until healing is complete.9,15

Advantages and Limitations

The Masquelet technique offers several notable advantages making it attractive for forearm nonunion. One of its main benefits is that it does not require microsurgical skills, resulting in shorter operative and ischemia times compared with free vascularized fibular grafting (FVFG).22 Additionally, the technique is highly flexible: the PMMA spacer can be loaded with antibiotics, which is particularly valuable in the management of infected defects.6

The induced membrane itself serves as a biologically active chamber that protects the graft while promoting revascularization and incorporation, enhancing the likelihood of successful bone healing.6 Moreover, the procedure generally results in lower donor-site morbidity compared with harvesting large structural vascularized grafts, reducing patient morbidity and postoperative complications.23

Despite these advantages, the technique has important limitations and potential complications. A fundamental consideration is that it requires two separate operations, which can prolong the overall treatment course and delay functional recovery.24 Graft-related complications, such as resorption or failure of incorporation, may occur, particularly if the graft volume is inadequate, fixation is suboptimal, or residual infection persists.25 Infection recurrence or persistent osteomyelitis may also necessitate additional debridement or delay the second stage.24

In the forearm, achieving and maintaining precise length, alignment, and rotational orientation is technically demanding; even minor malalignment can lead to functional deficits and poor outcomes.26 Finally, for very large segmental defects typically exceeding 6–8 cm in certain series or in situations where immediate structural support is required, vascularized structural grafts, such as a free fibula, may offer advantages over the Masquelet technique.27,28

Free Vascularized Bone Grafting

FVBGs are an important tool for nonunion of forearm fractures and segmental bone loss. FVBGs allow transfer of living bone with their intrinsic blood supply and often a periosteal sleeve. This delivers viable osteogenic cells, preserved microcirculation, and immediate capacity for remodeling and hypertrophy at the recipient site.5 In the forearm, where restoration of length, alignment, and rotation is critical, FVBGs become valuable when bone defects are large (approximately 4–6cm), when the biological environment is poor or when previous grafting failed.28,29 It should be noted that defect-size thresholds reported in the literature vary across series, ranging from approximately 4 cm to 8 cm, and should be interpreted as approximate clinical guides rather than absolute cutoffs, as technique selection ultimately depends on the combination of defect size, biological environment, and surgical expertise.

Free Vascularized Fibular Grafts in Forearm Reconstruction

FVFGs are the most used FVBGs for long defects of the radius and ulna. This is mainly due to its length, straight tubular geometry, reliable peroneal pedicle, and predictable hypertrophy potential.30 FVFGs also play a major role when skin coverage or monitoring is required, attributed to their high osteocutaneous healing capability with promising results. FVFGs have become a highly reliable choice for segmental forearm defects and one-bone forearm reconstructions.14 A retrospective study by Battiston et al looked at 25 patients who had bone loss at the forearm with a mean bone defect of 8cm and found bone healing was achieved at a mean of 13.6 months for all patients except one.31

Alternative Vascularized Bone Grafts: Medial Femoral and Other Options

Medial femoral trochlea or condyle grafts (MFTCGs) are another option for smaller corticocancellous or periosteal flaps and are based on the descending genicular vessels.32 MFTCGs are useful for short defects and small diameter bones, such as the proximal pole of the scaphoid, in addition to augmenting small diameter bones where the fibula would be oversized.33 Petrella et al showed that MFTCGs effectively promote early union where conventional bone grafting has failed in the upper extremity.29 Vascularized iliac crest, scapular, or rib grafts are less commonly used, although they may serve their purpose when specific shapes or osteochondral segments are required.34

Comparison Between Vascularized and Non-Vascularized Bone Grafts

FVBGs offer superior biology and immediate viability as opposed to non-vascularized bone grafts (NVBGs). They bring living osteocytes, periosteum, and an intact microvasculature to the recipient bed, bypassing creeping substitution. This improves osteoinduction and osteogenesis, which is essential in sclerotic or previously infected or irradiated beds, and has shown superior perfusion and maintained bone quality relative to NVBGs.5 FVBGs have additionally shown higher success for nonunions that have failed previous grafting or in the setting of chronic osteomyelitis.31 Due to the already perfused nature of FVBGs, revascularization of the construct and incorporation in the host may be faster than NVBGs. In a small mixed upper-extremity series, Liu et al looked at 10 patients that received either NVBGs or FVBGs and found time to union to be 30.5 weeks and 21.3 weeks respectively. Although this finding was statistically insignificant, the population size was small, and perhaps larger trials may offer more data on comparative union time in the future.35 FVBGs may permit earlier structural recovery and earlier use of the upper limb, particularly in hostile biological environments.

Limitations and Complications of Free Vascularized Bone Grafting

Limitations and downsides exist in the application of FVBGs, most prominent of which are technical demand, need for microsurgical expertise, and long operative times. Free transfers require microsurgical vascular anastomosis, careful flap harvest, precise skeletal fixation, and sometimes two teams.36 Donor site morbidity and recipient site complications present another major drawback for the use of VBGs. Recipient site complications mainly revolve around uncontrolled bleeding due to poor anastomosis, thrombosis due to poor surgical technique or choice of recipient vessel, infection, and poor soft tissue coverage.30 Donor site morbidity is an important consideration, especially in FVFGs with a total reported complication rate of 11% including ankle pain, sensory changes, motor dysfunction, and valgus ankle deformity.30 For MFTCGs, the donor site morbidity is relatively low with the majority of complications revolving around chronic knee pain and sensory changes seen more in medial femoral trochlea harvests rather than condyle harvests.37

Overall, FVBGs play a central role when local biology and defect size exceed the scope of conventional grafting methods. When performed in appropriately selected patients and by experienced teams, FVBGs, especially FVFGs and MFTCGs, seem to offer biological superiority and reliable outcomes for challenging forearm nonunion.

Comparative Outcomes

Direct comparison of outcomes between IMT and FVBG is substantially complicated by baseline differences between treatment groups across published series. Patients selected for IMT tend to present with smaller or moderate defects, active or recently eradicated infection, and intact or recoverable soft-tissue envelopes, whereas those selected for FVBG more commonly have larger defects, severely compromised local vascularity, failed prior reconstruction, or soft-tissue deficiency requiring composite coverage. These systematic differences in case complexity introduce significant selection bias, making it difficult to attribute outcome differences to the technique itself rather than to the underlying patient and defect characteristics. Union Rates and Time to Union

In comparative studies centered on upper extremity reconstruction, Singh et al reported a union rate of 94.1% for forearm nonunions treated with the Masquelet technique, with an average healing time of 5.8 months, compared to a 94.9% union rate and an average of 4.4 months for cases managed with VBG, indicating nearly identical success rates, but slightly faster union with the vascularized graft.41 Similarly, Zhou et al, in a direct comparison between FVFG and the Masquelet technique, reported favorable clinical outcomes with both approaches, noting, however, that the Masquelet technique was linked to a significantly shorter operative time.38 Richard and Vu explained that both techniques play well-defined roles in reconstruction based on defect size and biological environment, with neither method demonstrating clear superiority across all scenarios.42

In studies involving large bone defects, VBGs, particularly FVFGs, have been shown to promote faster union. In a forearm-specific series, Konstantinou et al reported that in reconstructions using FVFG for defects averaging 9.8 cm, all patients achieved graft union within about four months, accompanied by favorable functional outcomes (mean DASH score ≈ 17.7).14 These findings indicate that in extensive defects, VBGs may provide quicker graft incorporation. Nevertheless, the Masquelet technique also demonstrates strong union outcomes. In a forearm-specific cohort, Ma et al reported successful management of 32 infected forearm nonunions using the Masquelet method, achieving consistent bone integration with minimal reinfection.6 Across broader long-bone series, the IMT has shown union rates exceeding 90% in upper-extremity series, highlighting its reliability for upper-limb reconstruction.24

Therefore, although union rates for the Masquelet technique and VBG are generally comparable in many forearm studies, VBGs may achieve slightly faster union, particularly in larger defects or biologically compromised sites, while the Masquelet method remains highly effective in moderate defects and infection-prone environments.

Functional Outcomes

Functional recovery, particularly regarding forearm pronation and supination, grip strength, and patient-reported outcomes, serves as a critical measure of success. In the FVFG series reported by Konstantinou et al, patients achieved an average pronation of 56.9°, supination of 52.5°, and favorable DASH scores.14 In studies of the Masquelet technique for upper-limb defects, El Farhaoui et al reported complete union in all seven patients, with pronation–supination reaching up to 125° and favorable wrist and elbow mobility at follow-up.40

A narrative review by Richard and Vu highlights that functional outcomes are generally similar, though the benefits of VBG may be more evident in large defects or cases with severely compromised soft tissue, where anatomical reconstruction is more difficult.42 Overall, when both techniques result in healing, functional differences are usually minimal, but VBGs may offer a slight advantage in mechanical restoration for complex cases.

Complication Profile and Morbidity

The two techniques vary in their associated complications and morbidity. The primary risks of the Masquelet method include membrane failure, graft resorption, recurrent infection, and nonunion if the induced membrane is insufficient or the graft fails to revascularize. In upper-limb series, Masquelet demonstrated low donor-site morbidity.24,40 The absence of microsurgical work generally reduces surgical complexity and risks.

In contrast, VBG involves inherent microsurgical risks, including flap failure, thrombosis, longer operative times, and donor-site complications such as ankle instability, peroneal nerve injury, or fibular fracture. Higher technical complexity can lead to increased perioperative risk. Konstantinou et al reported that graft fracture was a relatively common complication in forearm FVFG reconstructions, although overall outcomes remained favorable.14 Battiston et al also highlighted the critical role of microsurgical safety and surgical expertise in VBG procedures.31 Furthermore, Zhou et al concluded that hospital stay, and intraoperative blood loss were greater in the FVFG group than in the MT group for bone defect reconstruction.38

Suitability and Defect-Size Considerations

A key practical distinction lies in defect size and the biological environment. Several authors suggest using a threshold of roughly 5–6 cm (and up to 8 cm in certain series), when considering VBG for forearm reconstruction.42 Nonvascularized grafts, as employed in the Masquelet technique, may be less reliable for defects exceeding this size due to limitations in vascularization. Richard and Vu note that Masquelet is particularly suitable for moderate defects (generally <6 cm) in well-vascularized environments, whereas VBG is preferable for larger defects or biologically compromised settings.42 In a direct comparison, Zhou et al found that Masquelet provided shorter operative times and reduced complexity for small to moderate defects, but they cautioned against extending its use beyond its biological limits.38

Selection among techniques is influenced by defect size and vascularity, infection status, soft-tissue quality, surgical complexity, and donor-site morbidity.38,39,42–45 It is worth emphasizing that because IMT and FVBG tend to be applied to fundamentally different patient profiles in the literature, reported union rates and complication rates are not directly comparable between techniques. Current Knowledge

In a comparative series of 43 patients with forearm defects, Zhou et al found that IMT was associated with shorter mean operative time (≈123 vs 284 minutes), lower blood loss (≈108 vs 219 mL), and shorter hospital stay (≈9.3 vs 12.7 days) compared with FVFG, while bone-healing times were comparable between groups.38 Extrapolating from tibial fracture data, Lan et al reported that VBG was preferentially used for larger defects and showed longer union times, whereas IMT was more commonly selected for smaller or contaminated wounds.43 For infected forearm nonunions, Dhar et al achieved union in all 12 reconstructed bones using staged IMT after radical debridement.39 In experimental and narrative review literature, scaffold-guided bone regeneration (SGBR) and IMT biology emphasize the membrane’s supportive environment and the feasibility of incorporating patient-specific scaffolds into staged reconstruction.44,45

Key Themes and Findings

Evidence suggests that for smaller defects with adequate biology and stable soft-tissue conditions, IMT can achieve results comparable to FVFG while imposing less perioperative burden, as shown in forearm comparative data.38 Extrapolating from tibial trauma studies, IMT appears a reasonable option in smaller or contaminated defects, though direct application to the forearm requires caution.43 Its two-stage approach, involving radical debridement with spacer placement followed later by grafting into the induced membrane, makes IMT particularly well-suited to infected or contaminated environments. In Dhar’s series of 12 infected forearm nonunions, all cases united, underscoring IMT’s effectiveness.39 Mechanistic work further highlights angiogenic and osteoinductive signaling within the induced membrane, consistent with these clinical results.45

For larger segmental defects or compromised vascularity, VBG supplies living cortical bone with intrinsic circulation, offering mechanical and biological advantages. Based on narrative reviews across long bones, FVFG is supported as a reliable solution for substantial bone loss.43 In upper-extremity series, Konstantinou et al reported union in all eight patients undergoing FVFG for massive forearm skeletal defects, with a mean time to union of approximately four months, further validating its role in complex reconstructions.14 Beyond initial reconstruction, VBG is also supported as a salvage option following failed prior grafting procedures, particularly in compromised biological environments.46

Future Directions

Currently Available and Clinically Emerging Options

Emerging strategies are being explored to augment or refine current reconstructive approaches. Human induced membranes have been shown to express BMP-4, −6, −7, and −9, highlighting their endogenous osteoinductive capacity.47 Reviews of IMT biology describe angiogenic and osteogenic pathways and propose the addition of exogenous growth factors or stem cells, although standardized clinical augmentation protocols remain limited.3,5,17 In translational and technical reports, advances in microsurgical technique, including perforator-based harvest and anastomoses to sub-millimeter vessels, seek to preserve the biologic advantages of VBG while reducing donor-site morbidity.47 In a prospective cohort of 157 patients, fibula harvest was associated with acceptable long-term donor-site outcomes, while medial femoral condyle flaps have been validated as a low-morbidity alternative in systematic review.48

Translational and Preclinical Concepts

Biomaterial-based strategies also hold promises. Early integration of 3D-printed titanium cages within IMT demonstrated feasibility in segmental femoral reconstruction and enabled characterization of induced membrane biology. Broader scaffold-guided bone regeneration frameworks emphasize the potential of patient-specific scaffolds for managing large defects, with retrospective and case-based reports documenting positive results using porous titanium prostheses or custom SGBR constructs in combination with IMT.49 Meanwhile, bioprinting research has outlined experimental strategies for fabricating pre-vascularized bone constructs and underscored persistent challenges including bioink development, mechanical stability, and host integration.50 Together, these advances represent promising but still largely translational frontiers.

Clinical Synthesis and Practical Considerations

Infected Nonunion Work-Up and Culture Strategy

Pre-operative work-up for suspected infected nonunion should include clinical assessment and laboratory evaluation, including inflammatory markers such as CRP, ESR, and white blood cell count, supplemented by appropriate imaging when infection is suspected. Definitive microbiological assessment relies on intraoperative deep-tissue sampling obtained from representative sites during debridement. Multiple deep-tissue specimens should be obtained using separate sterile instruments, while superficial swabs should be avoided because of their limited diagnostic reliability. Where available, sonication of removed implants may provide additional microbiological information, particularly in biofilm-associated infection. These principles are consistent with contemporary approaches to the diagnosis and management of infected nonunion and fracture-related infection.8,25,39

Timing of Second-Stage IMT

The second stage of IMT should not be performed on a fixed schedule but rather after adequate debridement, control of infection, and recovery of the soft-tissue envelope have been achieved. Although the second stage is commonly undertaken after a period of membrane formation, often approximately 4–8 weeks after spacer placement, timing should be individualized according to the clinical condition of the patient, the status of the soft tissues, and evidence of infection control.2,4,15,16,25,39 Persistent clinical or microbiological evidence of infection should prompt further evaluation and, when necessary, repeat debridement rather than automatic progression to bone grafting.2,15,25,39

Fixation Strategy: Radius versus Ulna

Fixation principles differ between the two forearm bones. The radius requires strict restoration of its length, alignment, and physiologic radial bow because these anatomical relationships contribute substantially to pronation and supination.1,42 Stable fixation is essential in both bones and should be selected according to defect size, bone quality, reconstructive technique, and the mechanical demands of the reconstruction.22,23,26,38,42 The ulna, being straighter, also requires restoration of length and alignment to preserve overall forearm mechanics and distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ) congruity.1,42 In both bones, plate positioning and screw fixation should provide sufficient stability throughout the prolonged healing period that may accompany segmental reconstruction.22,23,26,42

Prevention of Radioulnar Synostosis

Radioulnar synostosis is a serious complication of forearm reconstruction, particularly following extensive soft-tissue injury or reconstruction involving the interosseous space. Preventive measures include minimizing unnecessary disruption of the interosseous membrane and avoiding inadvertent placement of graft material across the interosseous space. Preservation of the anatomical separation between the radius and ulna should therefore be considered throughout reconstruction, particularly in complex defects involving both bones.1,42 Postoperative rehabilitation should subsequently be individualized according to fixation stability, soft-tissue healing, and the reconstructive procedure performed.22,23,26,42

DRUJ and PRUJ Alignment and Forearm Rotation

Restoration of distal and proximal radioulnar joint alignment is essential for forearm rotation and grip function. Restoration of radial length, radial bow, and rotational alignment should be assessed carefully because malalignment may compromise DRUJ congruity and pronation-supination. Reconstruction should aim for the closest feasible restoration of native forearm length and anatomy.1,42 PRUJ stability depends on maintenance of the radiocapitellar relationship and annular ligament integrity. Intraoperative fluoroscopic assessment may be used to confirm restoration of forearm length, alignment, and joint congruity following reconstruction. Where DRUJ instability persists after reconstruction, additional stabilization should be considered according to the underlying pathology and intraoperative assessment.42

Rehabilitation Considerations

Rehabilitation after forearm reconstruction must balance protection of the healing construct with prevention of stiffness. Postoperative rehabilitation should be individualized according to fixation stability, soft-tissue condition, graft incorporation, and the reconstructive technique used.22,23,26,28,42 Early digital motion and appropriate wrist and forearm mobilization may be initiated when permitted by wound healing and construct stability; however, the timing of forearm rotation and progressive loading should not be defined by a universal postoperative interval.22,23,26,42 Serial clinical and radiographic assessment should guide progression of motion and loading, particularly after segmental reconstruction or vascularized bone grafting.28–31,42 Occupational and physical therapy may assist with restoration of motion, prevention of stiffness, strengthening, and functional retraining during recovery.28–31,42 Patients should be counseled that recovery after complex forearm reconstruction may be prolonged and depends on defect characteristics, biological healing, complications, and the reconstructive strategy employed.28–31,42

Donor-Site Morbidity and Patient Selection: Fibula versus Medial Femoral Condyle/Trochlea

Selection between the FVFG and MFC vascularized graft should account not only for defect size and geometry but also for the biological requirements of reconstruction and potential donor-site morbidity.5,29,30,32,33,37,46 The fibula provides a long structural vascularized graft suitable for reconstruction of large segmental defects, including extensive forearm defects.14,28–31 Nevertheless, donor-site complications must be considered when selecting this option.30,32,46,48 Medial femoral condyle–based vascularized grafts provide corticocancellous or periosteal tissue and may be advantageous when a smaller, biologically active graft is required rather than a long structural segment.33,37,46 Accordingly, graft selection should be individualized according to defect length, structural requirements, recipient-site biology, vascular anatomy, donor-site considerations, and patient-specific functional demands.5,29,30,32,33,37,46

Limitations of Literature

Direct comparative evidence for forearm segmental defects remains limited and mostly Level IV, restricting definitive claims of superiority beyond context-specific advantages.38 Furthermore, extrapolation from tibial to forearm reconstruction requires caution despite thematic similarities regarding defect size and contamination.43 In addition, much of the literature consists of single-center series and narrative reviews with heterogeneity in defect size, contamination status, fixation method, and use of adjuvants.38,39,43–45 Evidence for emerging strategies remains largely preclinical or limited to small case series, with little standardization and sparse long-term follow-up.45,47

Conclusion

Segmental bone loss and chronic nonunion of the forearm are demanding reconstructive problems due to the need for precise restoration of length, alignment, and rotation to preserve forearm function. Successful treatment requires addressing both mechanical stability and the underlying biological environment to achieve durable union and satisfactory functional outcomes.

The Masquelet induced membrane technique and free vascularized bone grafting are both effective options for these complex cases. The Masquelet technique is particularly suited for infected or moderate-sized defects and offers a staged, biologically favorable approach with lower technical complexity. In contrast, free vascularized bone grafts provide living, structurally robust bone and are advantageous for large defects, compromised vascularity, or salvage situations after failed reconstruction.

Overall, union rates and functional outcomes are comparable when each technique is applied to appropriate indications. Therefore, reconstructive strategy should be individualized based on defect size, local biology, soft-tissue conditions, patient factors, and surgical expertise. Continued advances in biologic augmentation and reconstructive techniques may further refine treatment algorithms for forearm nonunion. It must be acknowledged, however, that the available evidence remains largely retrospective and heterogeneous, and the treatment algorithms proposed in this review should be interpreted as clinical guidance informed by the best current literature rather than definitive comparative evidence.

A clinically oriented decision framework may be summarized as follows: IMT is favored for infected or moderate-sized defects where stable fixation and a biologically favorable environment for graft incorporation can be achieved; FVBG is preferred for large segmental defects, compromised local vascularity, severe composite injuries, or salvage cases following failed reconstruction; and combined or staged approaches should be considered in selected complex cases where infection control, soft-tissue reconstruction, and definitive structural restoration must be sequenced.
