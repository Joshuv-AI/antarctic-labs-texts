Combined effects of sound and temperature on the composition and function of bacterial and fungal communities in loess
Authors: Liping Zhao, Mingkai Li, YaLing Wang, Li Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In Northwest China, the dominant soil type is loess, which is highly susceptible to various environmental factors. Of these, limited research has focused on the impacts of sound disturbance and temperature fluctuations on the microbial communities in loess. An orthogonal experiment was conducted by varying sound intensity (70 dB, 90 dB, 110 dB), sound duration (2 h, 4 h, 6 h), and temperature (− 5 °C, 15 °C, 35 °C). Metagenomic sequencing was then applied to investigate the effects of sound and temperature on the composition and function of bacterial and fungal communities in loess. Our results show that under the combined effects of sound and temperature, the dominant phyla and genera of bacteria and fungi have different responses and preferences to temperature and sound decibels. Alpha diversity analysis revealed that the Shannon index of the bacterial community differed significantly under the 90 dB treatment at − 5 °C and under the 110 dB treatment at 15 °C (P < 0.05). For the fungal community, both the Simpson and Shannon indices showed significant differences under the 70 dB treatment at − 5 °C and under the 110 dB treatment at 15 °C (P < 0.05). Notably, the richness of rare fungal taxa and overall species richness in the loess fungal community were significantly enhanced at 90 dB compared with the control and other treatment groups, while these indices were significantly reduced at 110 dB. In the loess microbial treatment groups subjected to the combined effects of sound and temperature, the gene abundance of CAZy family genes was lowest under high decibel (110 dB) sound stimulation. Among the six enzyme-encoding gene categories within the CAZy family, the highest number of annotated species was observed in Group A (2 h, 70 dB, − 5 °C), whereas the lowest was recorded in Group C (6 h, 110 dB, − 5 °C). Among the metabolic pathway functional genes annotated in the KEGG database, the abundance of metabolic genes in Group C (6 h, 110 dB, − 5 °C) was significantly lower than that in other treatment groups.

Introduction

Loess, a porous aeolian sediment characterized by its friable texture, is highly susceptible to erosion and weathering. It is extensively distributed across northwest China [1, 2]. However, the combined effects of multiple factors—including earthquakes, rainfall, and anthropogenic activities—have severely degraded the soil structure in loess regions. This degradation has led to progressively worsening soil erosion and contributed to an increasingly fragile ecological environment [3]. Soil microorganisms are integral components of the loess region ecosystem, playing crucial roles in nutrient cycling and energy flow [4, 5]. Bacteria and fungi are the two most abundant and critical microbial groups within the soil microbial community, participating in soil organic matter decomposition and humus formation, driving nutrient cycling, and playing essential roles in maintaining soil health and ecological balance [6, 7]. The study on the composition structure and functional characteristics of soil microbial community in the loess region is helpful to further elucidate the ecological function of soil, and provide the basis for selecting suitable soil microorganisms for soil improvement and restoration. Previous studies on soil microbial structure and function have primarily focused on factors such as climate [8–10], soil properties, water availability [11–13] and pollution [14–16] with limited research addressing the impacts of sound and temperature.

Sound is omnipresent in nature and interacts with nearly all forms of life. It influences the growth, metabolism, and gene expression of microorganisms. Previous studies have demonstrated that music within the frequency range of 38–689 Hz can influence the growth, metabolism, and antibiotic sensitivity of both prokaryotic and eukaryotic microorganisms [17]. Jake M. Robinson et al. [18] found that acoustic stimulation at 80 dB increased fungal biomass and enhanced the activity of Trichoderma hartsi conidia. Harris et al. [19] demonstrated that 90 dB sound stimulation increased fungal growth in liquid culture, specifically enhancing growth rate and biomass yield. Kothari V et al. [20] demonstrated that sound at 86 dB can regulate the synthesis of secondary metabolites in bacteria. Gu et al. [21] investigated the differential gene expression of E. coli K12 exposed to 80 dB sound and identified significant alterations in energy metabolism, nucleotide synthesis, and 49 transcriptional and translational pathways. Temperature is one of the main abiotic factors affecting soil microbial communities. Variations in temperature can lead to alterations in the structure and ecological functions of these communities, thereby influencing species diversity, community composition, functionality, microbial activity, and the biomass of soil bacteria and fungi [22–24]. However, different microorganisms exhibit varying temperature sensitivities, and under natural selection pressures, they evolve adaptive mechanisms to cope with environmental temperatures. For example, thermophilic bacteria modify their cell membranes by increasing the proportion of saturated to unsaturated fatty acids, which enhances the production of heat shock proteins and thermostable enzymes. Conversely, psychrophilic bacteria adjust their membrane lipid composition to maintain fluidity, synthesize cold-adapted proteins or cryoprotective molecules to ensure survival at low temperatures, and may enter a dormant state to reduce metabolic activity [25].

Sound and temperature, as abiotic environmental factors, influence organisms and ecosystems. However, the combined effects of these factors on soil microorganisms remain insufficiently elucidated. We propose the following hypotheses: (1) Sound and temperature interact significantly to alter the relative abundance of specific microbial groups. (2) High-intensity sound exposure acts as a physical stressor that reduces bacterial and fungal diversity, with this negative effect being more pronounced under extreme temperature conditions. (3) The interaction between sound and temperature influences microbial cell membrane receptors, thereby triggering changes in gene expression, modifying the abundance of functional genes, and enhancing metabolic activity. Therefore, this study employed metagenomics to investigate the combined effects of sound and temperature on the composition and functions of soil bacterial and fungal communities in the loess region. This approach revealed the unique characteristics of soil microbial communities under such conditions and provided novel insights and strategies for ecological protection and restoration in the loess region.

Materials and methods

Study site description

The study site is located on a loess slope along Provincial Highway 101 near Northwest Minzu University in Xiaguanying Town, Yuzhong County, Lanzhou City, Gansu Province. The location of the study area is shown in Fig. 1 The area remains undisturbed by significant human activities. The soil is classified as loess, the ambient noise level ranges from 25 to 35 dB, the altitude is 1689 m, the average annual temperature is 7.5 °C, the average annual rainfall is 428 mm, and the frost-free period is 153 days. The region exhibits a temperate semi-arid climate with four pronounced seasons, characterized by a synchronous distribution of water and heat during the growing season, accompanied by dry springs, hot summers, rainy and cool autumns, and cold, dry winters.Fig. 1Location of study area. a Map of China; b Map of Gansu province; c Sampling sites

Study materials

Soil samples were collected in April 2023 using a five-point sampling method. The soils were collected at a depth of 10–20 cm at each sampling site, thoroughly mixed to form a composite sample, and then sieved through a 20-mesh sieve to remove residual plant debris. The processed soil samples were subsequently transported to the laboratory for further analysis. Building upon our research group's prior findings [26], we conducted an orthogonal experimental design to investigate the combined effects of three factors: sound pressure levels (70 dB, 90 dB, 110 dB), exposure durations (2 h, 4 h, 6 h), and temperatures (− 5 °C, 15 °C, 35 °C) (Table 1). Soil samples were subjected to combined sound and temperature treatments based on an orthogonal experimental design, with three biological replicates per treatment. Sound treatment apparatus and parameter settings

Sound treatments were conducted in indoor soundproof, constant-temperature incubators (Model ZHP-250E, Shanghai Sanfa Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd.). The inner walls of each incubator were lined with 5-cm-thick polyurethane foam to minimize sound wave reflection and prevent crosstalk between chambers. Soil samples from different treatment groups were placed in separate incubators. Sound and temperature treatments were applied simultaneously, with temperature precisely regulated by the incubators’ automatic control system. The sound source consisted of white noise audio (8 h, looped) obtained from the QQ Music platform. Playback was controlled by a Huawei laptop (MateBook 13, Huawei Terminal Co., Ltd.) and transmitted via Bluetooth to a speaker (Model C2, Shenzhen Yinqiao Trading Co., Ltd.). The sound frequency was verified using the Decibel X iOS application in A-weighting mode (dB(A)). The frequency range corresponding to the 70 dB, 90 dB, and 110 dB treatments was determined to be stable between 2060–2090 Hz. Sound pressure levels were calibrated using a digital sound level meter (Model DL333211, Ningbo Deli Tools Co., Ltd.). The probe was positioned centrally above the soil sample in the Petri dish. Playback volume was adjusted to ensure that the measured sound pressure matched the target levels (70, 90, or 110 dB). These values represented the actual sound pressure received by the samples. For each treatment, 10 g of soil was weighed and placed in a sterile Petri dish, which was set on a dedicated stand at an optimized height determined in preliminary tests to minimize interference from incubator walls. All constant-temperature incubators were arranged independently, with at least 3 m of spacing between units. To verify the effectiveness of sound isolation, an interference test was conducted prior to the formal experiment: a 110 dB sound was played in one incubator while background noise was measured in an adjacent unit. The results confirmed the absence of sound interference, demonstrating the acoustic independence of each incubator.

Metagenomic sequencing of loess samples following combined sound and temperature treatments

DNA was extracted from soil samples using CTAB method. DNA degradation degree, potential contamination and DNA concentration was measured using Agilent 5400. Sequencing library was generated using NEBNext® UltraTM DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina(NEB,USA,Catalog#:E7370L) following manufacturer's recommendations and index codes were added to each sample. Briefly, genomic DNA sample was fragmented by sonication to a size of 350 bp. Then DNA fragments were end repaired, A-tailed, and ligated with the full-length adapter for Illumina sequencing, followed by further PCR amplification. PCR products were then purified by AMPure XP system (Beverly, USA). Subsequently, library quality was assessed on the Agilent 5400 system (Agilent, USA) and quantified by QPCR (1.5 nM). The qualified libraries were pooled and sequenced on Illumina platforms with PE150 strategy, according to effective library concentration and data amount required. DIAMONDsoftware was used to blast non-redundant proteins to the NCBI NR protein database(parameters:–evalue 1e-5–max-target-seqs 50–id 70). BASTA software was used to analyze the result and obtain non-redundant protein species annotation information. Kraken2 and the self-build microbial database were used to identify the species contained in the samples, and then Bracken was used to predict the actual relative abundance of species in the samples. The de-redundant protein sequence (corresponding to the de-redundant gene nucleic acid sequence) was blast to the EggNOG database to obtain the KEGG annotation information of the protein by Eggnog-mapper software (based on DIAMOND). The de-redundant protein sequences are blast to the CAZy database to obtain the annotation information of CAZy by DIAMOND software.

Data processing and analysis

Data were organized using Microsoft Excel 2019, and graphs were prepared using Origin 2024. Soil microbial alpha diversity indices were completed using the Wekemo Bioincloud (https://www.bioincloud.tech). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 20.0: one-way ANOVA was applied to assess significant differences among treatments, and post hoc multiple comparisons were performed using Duncan's test at a significance level of P < 0.05. Raw sequencing reads were deposited into the National Center for Biotechnology Information Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database (accession number: PRJNA1248215).

Effects of sound and temperature on the fungi community structure in loess soils

Metagenomic sequencing of loess samples exposed to sound and temperature treatments revealed 7 phyla, 27 classes, 64 orders, 141 families, 237 genera, and 440 fungal species across all samples. At the phylum level, three fungal phyla with a relative abundance ≥ 1% were identified (Fig. 5). These were, in descending order of abundance, Mucoromycota (58.30–75.57%), Ascomycota (13.36–24.96%), and Basidiomycota (0.35–1.67%). Figure 5 illustrates variations in the relative abundance of fungal phyla across experimental groups. Specifically, Mucoromycota exhibited the highest relative abundance in Group G (2 h, 110 dB, 35 °C) and the lowest in Group C (6 h, 110 dB, 35 °C). Ascomycota showed the highest relative abundance in Group H (4 h, 70 dB, 35 °C) and the lowest in Group CK (0 h, 0 dB, 23 °C). Basidiomycota displayed the highest relative abundance in Group A (2 h, 70 dB, − 5 °C) and the lowest in Group I (6 h, 90 dB, 35 °C). These findings suggest that Mucoromycota is highly sensitive to the duration of sound exposure. At consistent sound intensity and temperature (110 dB, 35 °C), prolonged sound exposure (6 h) exerts a pronounced negative impact on Mucoromycota. Ascomycota appears to thrive in high-temperature environments, with moderate sound exposure (4 h, 70 dB) promoting its growth, whereas milder conditions (25 °C, no sound exposure) are less favorable. Basidiomycota favors low-temperature environments (− 5 °C), with short-term, low-intensity sound exposure (2 h, 70 dB) enhancing its growth and reproduction, whereas prolonged sound exposure at high temperatures (35 °C) inhibits these processes.Fig. 5Relative abundance of soil fungi phyla level under combined sound and temperature treatments

Effects of sound and temperature on the diversity of loess bacterial communities

This study employed the Chao 1 and ACE indices to assess the richness of the bacterial community, and the Shannon and Simpson indices to evaluate the diversity and evenness of the community, thereby characterizing alpha diversity. The alpha diversity analysis of soil bacterial communities in the Loess region (Fig. 7) revealed significant differences in the Shannon index among treatment groups subjected to various sound and temperature treatments (P < 0.05). At 15 °C, sound disturbance had a negative impact on the growth of loess bacteria. Specifically, in Groups D (2 h, 9 dB, 15 °C), E (4 h, 110 dB, 15 °C), and F (6 h, 70 dB, 15 °C), increasing sound intensity and duration led to a decline in the Simpson, Shannon, Chao1, and ACE indices. Disscussion

This study used metagenomic sequencing technology to explore the combined effects of sound and temperature on loess microorganisms. It was found that the dominant phyla and dominant genera of bacteria and fungi had different sensitivities to sound decibels and temperature. Although the core bacterial community in loess remained stable under the combined influence of sound and temperature, its relative abundance displayed distinct patterns in response to these factors. We speculate that under suitable temperature conditions, specific sound stimuli may promote the growth and reproduction of certain microorganisms. However, at excessively high or low temperatures, this stimulatory effect is likely to be diminished or even eliminated. Thus, sound and temperature interact to jointly regulate microbial abundance. When the environmental temperature changes, microorganisms can adapt to the environment by adjusting the fluidity of cell membranes, changing the fatty acid composition type, and up-regulating or down-regulating the expression of some genes [27–29]. In addition to temperature, microorganisms also respond to sound stimuli. Sound stimulation has been shown to alter the morphology of Escherichia coli K-12 cells and to promote their growth, metabolism, and cell division [30]. Sound is essentially a mechanical vibration that can influence soil structure through physical processes, such as altering soil porosity and modifying the distribution of water and gases, thereby indirectly affecting microorganisms. On the one hand, sound stimulation can directly affect microbial cells by altering membrane permeability or interfering with metabolic processes. Certain sound frequencies may change membrane ion permeability and influence free radical formation [19]. On the other hand, sound can create alternating regions of high and low pressure, which may physically stress cell membranes, leading to bending and increased membrane tension. Such physiological stress on the membrane may, in turn, modify the production of secondary metabolites, including volatile compounds. Sound may also influence ATP synthase activity or membrane proton gradients, as well as other transmembrane enzymes and transporters that are susceptible to vibrational effects [31]. This study investigated the combined effects of sound and temperature on loess microorganisms by simulating natural physical conditions through their interaction. However, the precise mechanisms by which sound, as an independent physical factor, influences specific microorganisms remain unresolved. Future research should employ more simplified experimental systems, such as pure culture approaches of dominant strains identified in this study, to directly examine the effects of sound on microbial growth dynamics under strictly controlled acoustic conditions.

The diversity and stability of soil microorganisms is one of the important indicators of soil ecosystem health [32]. The effects of temperature on soil microbial diversity have been extensively studied. Warming treatment can significantly reduce soil bacterial alpha diversity, but has no significant effect on fungal diversity [33]. There are no relevant research reports on the impact of sound on microbial diversity, but studies have shown that sound has an impact on the diversity of plants and animals. Noise has long-term negative effects on tree seedling recruitment, woody plant uniformity, and vegetation communities, and seedling recruitment and plant community composition do not recover after noise removal [34]. Research [35] found that oyster larvae showed a clear preference for the sounds of snapping shrimp. By playing the sounds of snapping shrimp on speakers, they successfully attracted large groups of oyster larvae to settle there, thus helping the endangered oyster reefs to revive. In this study, bacterial and fungal alpha diversity responded differently to the trend of sound decibel changes. This is because bacteria and fungi play different roles in soil ecosystems, and their ecological characteristics and life strategies in the ecosystem are also different [36, 37]. Fungi are mainly responsible for decomposing complex organic matter in the soil. They are less susceptible to short-term environmental changes and thus show stronger resistance. Bacteria, on the other hand, are more inclined to decompose and utilize soluble organic matter [38]. One of the most striking findings of our study was that fungal richness and the abundance of rare taxa increased significantly under 90 dB sound but decreased significantly under 110 dB sound. This result is consistent with the findings of Harris et al., who reported that 90 dB sound stimulation promoted fungal growth in liquid culture. We speculate that a 90 dB sound pressure may serve as a mild physical stimulus, promoting mycelial extension, spore germination, and exoenzyme secretion through micromechanical vibrations, thereby enhancing fungal resource utilization and niche competitiveness. In contrast, a high-intensity sound pressure of 110 dB likely exceeds the physiological tolerance of many fungal groups, acting as a stressor. Prolonged exposure to such levels may cause fatigue or damage to cell membranes, altering permeability and ion homeostasis and ultimately disrupting energy metabolism.

Metagenomic sequencing can be employed to investigate the combined effects of sound and temperature on the functional genes of soil microorganisms in loess. Carbohydrate active enzymes (CAZy) are involved in the degradation and synthesis of carbohydrates, have the ecological function of maintaining the material cycle in nature, and play an important role in the life activities of organisms such as bacteria and fungi [39]. A high content of carbohydrate active enzymes means that the soil can provide more energy for microbial activities to participate in metabolic activities in the soil. In this study, the relative abundance of the CAZy family as a whole was highest in glycoside hydrolases (GH), carbohydrate binding domains (CBM), and glycosyltransferases (GT). The six genes of the CAZy family were all in the highest number under low-decibel sound stimulation, indicating that high-decibel sound can inhibit the activity of carbohydrate enzymes in loess microorganisms. The abundance of different types of microorganisms determines the function of the microbial community, while the abundance of functional genes can reflect the response of the microbial community function to environmental changes [40]. The migration of carbon and nitrogen by microorganisms is mainly achieved by controlling key enzymes through functional genes to regulate the element cycle process [41]. Therefore, the abundance of relevant functional genes becomes a key factor in predicting the potential and metabolic characteristics of microorganisms participating in carbon and nitrogen cycles. In this study, the functional genes annotated in the KEGG database exhibited reduced abundance under the combined conditions of low temperature and high-intensity sound. These genes encode proteins such as ABC-2 transport system ATP-binding proteins, ABC-2 transport system permease proteins, and peptide/nickel transport system ATP-binding proteins.At present, the soil element cycle process involved in these genes is still unclear. It is speculated that sound and temperature jointly increase the abundance of these functional genes, thereby absorbing more nucleotides, amino acids, and carbohydrates from the soil, increasing the diversity of microorganisms, and thus affecting the microbial process through the abundance of functional genes and then affecting the transformation of ecological processes.

Conclusion

Under both control and treatment conditions, the bacterial community in loess was consistently dominated by the phyla Actinomycetota, Pseudomonadota, and Bacillota, and by the genera Jiangella, Streptomyces, and Micromonospora. Similarly, the fungal community was predominantly composed of the phyla Mucoromycota, Ascomycota, and Basidiomycota, and the genera Rhizophagus, Pseudogymnoascus, and Fusarium.Variations in their relative abundances were observed in response to the combined effects of sound and temperature.The dominant phyla and genera of bacteria and fungi have different responses and preferences to temperature and sound decibels.

Both temperature and sound intensity significantly influenced the alpha diversity of bacterial and fungal communities. The Shannon index of the bacterial community differed significantly under the 90-dB treatment at − 5 °C and under the 110-dB treatment at 15 °C (P < 0.05). For the fungal community, both the Simpson and Shannon indices showed significant differences under the 70-dB treatment at − 5 °C and under the 110-dB treatment at 15 °C (P < 0.05). This suggests that sound interference exerts a stronger influence on the distribution uniformity of bacterial and fungal communities under medium- and low-temperature conditions.Specifically, at a sound intensity of 90 dB, fungal richness and the abundance of rare taxa significantly increased, whereas at 110 dB, both parameters decreased markedly.

Under the combined effects of sound and temperature, the gene abundance of carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZy) families in loess microbial communities was lowest at the high-intensity sound treatment of 110 dB, suggesting that high-intensity sound disturbance may inhibit carbohydrate enzyme activity. Among the metabolic pathway functional genes annotated in the KEGG database, the metabolic genes in group C (6 h, 110 dB, − 5 °C) were significantly less than those in other treatment groups, indicating that the metabolic genes of loess microorganisms would be inhibited under the combined effects of low temperature and high-decibel sound. This study provides preliminary insights into the combined effects of sound and temperature on the structure and function of microbial communities in loess. Future research should focus on isolating and culturing the dominant microbial taxa to further elucidate these combined environmental effects.
