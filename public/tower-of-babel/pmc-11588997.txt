M̓ṇúxvʔit model for centering Indigenous knowledge and governance
Authors: Elroy White (Q̓íx̌itasu), Kyle A Artelle, Ed Brown (H̓úṃpas ƛ̓úx̌v), Kelly Brown (ƛ̓áqvamut), Diana E Chan, William Housty (Dúqva̓ísḷa)
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe importance of Indigenous (and local) knowledge and governance systems for addressing social and ecological crises is increasingly recognized. Unfortunately, attempts to incorporate Indigenous knowledge into Western approaches, often without the full leadership, consent, and participation of the peoples holding those knowledges, can cause harm and can constitute extractive activities. However, there remains considerable potential in collaborations bringing together multiple perspectives and knowledges. We introduce the M̓ṇúxvʔit model, which centers Indigenous governance systems as the natural starting point for respectful, cross‐knowledge system collaborations. M̓ṇúxvʔit means “to become one” in Haíɫzaqvḷa, the language of the Haíɫzaqv Nation from which this model originates, in this case referring to outside knowledges being incorporated into Indigenous systems (not vice versa). In collaborations following this model, Indigenous communities and governments lead the overall direction, Indigenous knowledge systems are foundational, local protocols are followed, benefits flow at least as much to communities as to collaborators, and collaborations are authentic and transparent. M̓ṇúxvʔit can occur at scales including a single person, such as Q̓íx̌itasu (Elroy White) complementing his Haíɫzaqv knowledge with Western archeology; a project, such as the Xvíɫm̓ístaƛ Hákq̓áṃ Qṇtxv Bákvḷásu (our foods will return) multispecies restoration program led by the Haíɫzaqv Nation and supported by invited collaborators; and a community, exemplified by the Heiltsuk (Haíɫzaqv) Integrated Resource Management Department leading resource stewardship collaborations across Haíɫzaqv Territory. Collaborations following the model uphold Indigenous and local sovereignty while avoiding superficial or tokenistic approaches. We share this model as a successful, locally born approach that we hope provides inspiration elsewhere and as a contribution to the conversation about how Western actors can work with local and Indigenous systems such that their collaborations constructively add to, not harmfully extract from, those systems.Keywords: cross‐cultural collaboration, Indigenous and local knowledge, Indigenous‐led governance, Indigenous‐led stewardship, M̓ṇúxvʔit model, administración indígena, colaboración intercultural, conocimiento local e indígena, gestión indígena, Modelo M̓ṇúxvʔit, 土著人民主导的治理, 土著人民主导的管理, 土著人民和当地知识, 跨文化合作, M̓ṇúxvʔit模式

INTRODUCTION

The importance of Indigenous and local knowledge and governance systems for addressing ongoing social and ecological crises worldwide is increasingly well recognized (Jessen et al., 2022; Littlechild et al., 2021; Ogar et al., 2020; Reid et al., 2021). Indigenous knowledge is often more complete than Western equivalents, and local peoples often better understand local dynamics and have better informed governance systems compared with newly imposed outside systems (Atleo, 2011; Brown & Brown, 2009; Gadgil et al., 1993; Kimmerer, 2002; Turner & Spalding, 2013). An egregious example of this is when Western scientists are credited with discovering environmental phenomena long known to Indigenous communities (Nicholas, 2018). Assuming superiority of outside knowledge or governance systems have led to outside agencies imposing new governance systems that rapidly cause ecological or other damages (e.g., millennia‐old Indigenous approaches to salmon fishing being interrupted by colonial agencies based on apparent sustainability concerns and replaced with mixed‐stock fisheries that deplete stocks rapidly) (Atlas et al., 2017; Brown & Brown, 2009). Although the importance of upholding local and Indigenous knowledge and governance systems was likely never in doubt for Indigenous and local communities, considerable work has been done recently to educate outside groups on this, including as a step in addressing ongoing social and ecological harms of settler colonialism (e.g., Bartlett et al., 2012; Battiste, 2005; Borrows, 2018; Kimmerer, 2013; Whyte et al., 2016; Wildcat et al., 2014).

Perhaps in recognition of the importance of Indigenous knowledge, non‐Indigenous collaborators now often seek to work with Indigenous communities and to incorporate Indigenous knowledge in their work. Unfortunately, this can lead to approaches that, instead of redressing harmful patterns, further settler colonialism (Latulippe & Klenk, 2020; McGregor, 2004; Nadasdy, 1999; Simpson, 2001). Outside actors might extract Indigenous and local knowledge out of context and use it without involvement of those from whom the knowledge was taken (Klenk et al., 2017), including toward ends that run counter to their interests (Adams et al., 2014). Harm can be caused even when Indigenous knowledge is not explicitly used against the interests of a community. Outside actors seeking to incorporate small amounts of Indigenous knowledge and a few collaborators to otherwise Western research or governance (e.g., “add Indigenous and stir” [Littlechild et al., 2021]) can ultimately center Western approaches (Simpson, 2001), furthering the assumption that Western science and institutions are the natural foundation for inquiry and governance (Nadasdy, 1999). Extracting Indigenous and local knowledge to incorporate in outside research or governance systems often does not uplift or provide a voice to often‐marginalized communities; rather, it supplants the governance systems of those who are the rightful decision makers (and rights holders) and often better positioned than outsiders to make locally relevant decisions. Extracting Indigenous knowledge by outside actors often occurs concurrently with or following the removal of local people, seizing of land, and extraction of natural resources, characteristic actions in settler colonialism. As quoted in McGregor (2004): “You have taken our lands; now you are after our minds.” When outside actors seek to collaborate with Indigenous communities under the assumption that outside governance systems (laws, policies, institutions) are foundational, ultimate authorities and that Indigenous groups merely participate or are consulted, this further solidifies settler colonial power relationships.

The decades of cautions (e.g., McGregor, 2004; Nadasdy, 1999; Simpson, 2001) about the risks of outside actors extracting Indigenous knowledge to incorporate in otherwise Western approaches and institutions remain as pertinent now as ever, especially because interest in outside groups working with Indigenous communities (and interest in the relevant incentives, e.g., grants supporting collaboration with Indigenous communities) has increased recently.

Despite the potential pitfalls of extractive approaches to Indigenous knowledge, considerable value remains for diverse knowledge sources and peoples to come together to tackle problems from multiple angles, perspectives, and areas of expertise, in a mutually respectful way that deliberately avoids harm, grounded in accountability for research processes, relations, places, and subjects (Kimmerer & Artelle, 2024; Kovach, 2010; Smith, 2013; Wilson, 2008). In considering how diverse knowledges might come together, the question should not be how Indigenous knowledge can contribute to Western science, but how Western science can contribute to Indigenous knowledge and place‐based governance (Moko‐Painting et al., 2023).

We describe the M̓ṇúxvʔit (becoming one) (Anglicized pronunciation: muh‐noo‐hweet) model for collaboration. This model was developed in the territory of the Haíɫzaqv (Heiltsuk) First Nation in the area now commonly referred to as the Central Coast of British Columbia, Canada. The model centers Indigenous knowledge and governance systems in collaborations and uses Western knowledge, people, and technologies to complement local and Indigenous knowledge in ways that support, uplift, and contribute to, not marginalize and extract from, local and Indigenous systems. We examined general attributes of the model and how they apply at multiple scales and provide examples from within Haíɫzaqv territory. We considered how this model shares key characteristics with other models for collaboration and knowledge integration and its general benefits. We do not see this model as a panacea but believe it can provide insights into how outside actors can collaborate with local and Indigenous communities in ways that bolster those communities’ local knowledge and capacity while strengthening and uplifting their sovereignty and agency.

M̓ṆÚXVʔIT MODEL

M̓ṇúxvʔit roughly translates to becoming one or being one. However, translation between English and Haíɫzaqvḷa (the Haíɫzaqv language) is necessarily incomplete and does not convey the nuance and context of the term, a problem described as the “tyranny of translation” (Dáduqvḷá Committee, 2018). Primary applications of the term, as amassed by E.W. (i.e., Q̓íx̌itasu) in discussion with language speakers, include reference to the unity of the Haíɫzaqv Nation and territory, including its membership and those who work closely with the nation; the unifying role of language in tying together all that is central to the Haíɫzaqv Nation (E.W. notes, “Without language, we are not Haíɫzaqv.”); unifying efforts that benefit and represent the Haíɫzaqv Nation; cultural and potlatch (governance system of Haíɫzaqv and other Coastal Nations) ways unifying and unified by Ǧvíḷás (Haíɫzaqv law) and Ɫaxvai (Haíɫzaqv authority); strength of the territory and governance passed down through generations; unity across generations (intergenerational representation); uniting or collaborating with outsiders who respect Haíɫzaqv laws, strengths, and cultural values; unity of Haíɫzaqv knowledge across people and generations that grows through space and time; and the unifying power of working together professionally and respectfully.

The M̓ṇúxvʔit model (Figure 1) describes an approach to guide cross‐cultural collaborations that ideally benefit all those involved, but that explicitly treat Indigenous and local knowledge and governance systems as foundational.

Visual representation of M̓ṇúxvʔit. The basket represents knowledge held by an Indigenous Nation or local community (in this case, the Haíɫzaqv Nation). In the basket, the eagle, bear, and salmon represent knowledge of the sky, land, and sea that has been held since time immemorial. The test tube, drone, DNA, and microscope going into the basket represent other newer knowledge sources and approaches being added to and further increasing the knowledge already held by the Haíɫzaqv Nation. The circle represents the cyclical nature of everything and the celestial that encapsulates the world. Image created by Haíɫzaqv artist Ben Humchitt with input from the authors.

Projects following the M̓ṇúxvʔit model (Figure 2), although situation‐specific, have the following elements. First, Indigenous and local communities and governments lead the overall direction of the collaborations: identifying the research questions or focus, deciding which research avenues are priorities, and determining with whom collaborations should occur. Coproduction models might be based on the assumption that outside researchers should be involved in all steps of collaborations. However, given the complexities and nuances of many collaborations, it might often be most appropriate for communities to first determine high‐level direction (subject, research questions, which collaborators are involved and in which capacities) themselves before outside collaborators join.

The M̓ṇúxvʔit model. Graphic created by Fuse Consulting.

Second, Indigenous and local knowledge systems are foundational and treated as the starting point for research or knowledge advancement. When outside partnerships, expertise, or approaches are incorporated into Indigenous‐led projects, they are done so strategically to complement, support, and add to local knowledge systems in ways that support their continued growth and evolution. Outside knowledge can also grow as a result of collaborations, but the centering and benefit to local knowledge and governance is the primary consideration. Designing collaborations to contribute to the local knowledge base contrasts with the more common practice of Indigenous knowledge being incorporated into Western science and approaches, which, whether intentional or not, can be highly extractive and compromise sovereignty of Indigenous and local peoples’ knowledges.

Third, collaborative work follows local governance systems and protocols, recognizing the rights of local communities. This might include following local requirements for how research is conducted, including who is involved; local protocols to be followed; how the environment, species, and samples collected should be treated; how research and results are communicated; and who owns data and how they are stewarded consistent with expectations of the First Nations principles of OCAP (ownership, control, access, and possession) for data governance (First Nations Information Governance Centre, 2023). At a broader scale, recognizing and respecting the inherent rights and authority of Indigenous and local governance systems is a key element for addressing and disrupting colonial power relations that assume the primacy of Western institutions and agencies. This is consistent with the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (e.g., Article 31: “Indigenous peoples have the right to maintain, control, protect and develop their cultural heritage, traditional knowledge and traditional cultural expressions” [Ignace et al., 2023; UN General Assembly, 2007]) and is a step toward avoiding academic institutions and colonial governments treating decolonization as an aspirational sentiment instead of a fundamental shift in power relationships (Tuck & Yang, 2012).

Fourth, benefits flow at least as much to communities as to those collaborating with them. This does not mean that knowledge flows only in one direction or that outside collaborators cannot benefit and learn from collaborative work (in our experience they do); rather, it means benefits for communities must be a central, not secondary, goal for work being undertaken.

Fifth, collaborations are authentic and meaningful, and the nature of collaboration is transparent. Tokenistic approaches that misconstrue the true nature of collaborations are not M̓ṇúxvʔit.

M̓ṆÚXVʔIT AT THE SCALE OF THE INDIVIDUAL

The M̓ṇúxvʔit model was first applied in Haíɫzaqv territory at the individual scale. E.W. is a Western‐trained archeologist and a Haíɫzaqv Yím̓ás (hereditary chief) and knowledge holder, with extensive knowledge of Haíɫzaqv history, núyṃ́ (stories), laws, and territorial history. At university, while pursuing his master's degree in archeology, he was told that, in the interest of objectivity, he ought to disregard his knowledge of Haíɫzaqv history to focus instead on material objects (e.g., cultural artifacts, archeological sites) that could be examined “objectively.” E.W. rejected this approach, recognizing that the existing body of Haíɫzaqv knowledge was complementary to the knowledge obtainable from material objects. Working with Piikani archeologist Eldon Yellowhorn, E.W. used an “internalist approach” (Yellowhorn, 2002), which combines tools of archeology with oral histories. In his master's thesis, Heiltsuk Stone Fish Traps: Products of My Ancestors' Labour (White, 2006), E.W. combined Haíɫzaqv knowledge and núyṃ́ (as shared with him via conversations with local knowledge holders) with in situ examination of stone fish traps, a millennia‐old technology used to catch salmon in Haíɫzaqv territory and beyond.

In subsequent work, E.W. increasingly saw his approach as an example of broader knowledge integration that centered Haíɫzaqv knowledge, governance, language, worldview, and territory that extended beyond archeological inquiry. On E.W.’s behalf, Ǧvu̓í (Rory Housty) consulted with Elder and fluent Haíɫzaqvḷa speaker Káníɫmi (Liz Brown) to find a Haíɫzaqvḷa word for reconciliation (commonly applied in Canada to the process of addressing past and present harms caused by settler colonialism). She suggested the word M̓ṇúxvʔit. E.W. was inspired by how this word described his approach in a way that English words could not. E.W.’s university training added to his skillset and knowledge base. Western knowledge and archeological tools are no longer external to E.W. (or the Haíɫzaqv Nation); they have become one with, have added to, and have expanded his (and his nation's) existing tool set.

M̓ṇúxvʔit extends to other members of E.W.’s team and to the broader Haíɫzaqv Nation. Drone technology has recently become increasingly valuable in many fields, and E.W. and his team, including his grandson Gíƛa (Keegan Nappie‐Edgar), have incorporated it in their workflow. K.A. employs drones for ecological research and trains others in their use. He initially partnered with E.W. to conduct drone work on some archeological applications. Over time, E.W.’s team, especially Gíƛa, increasingly took the lead on drone flying and analysis. Knowledge of how to use drones, and the insights they provide, has added to E.W. and Gíƛa's existing knowledge and to Haíɫzaqv knowledge in general through a M̓ṇúxvʔit process.

W.H. (i.e., Dúqva̓ísḷa), K.B. (i.e., ƛ̓áqvamut), and E.B. (i.e., H̓úṃpas ƛ̓úx̌v) have similarly combined outside knowledge with their existing Haíɫzaqv knowledge in their work within the Haíɫzaqv Nation. W.H. combines his knowledge of local ecology passed down from his mentors and from his direct experience on the land with the knowledge he obtained through a Vancouver Island University degree in Natural Resource Management. He has combined these knowledges in his own research projects (e.g., Housty et al., 2014), in collaborations with outside partners, and in his work with the Heiltsuk Integrated Resource Management Department (HIRMD) to guide stewardship activities across the territory. K.B. similarly combines his core foundation of lived and inherited Haíɫzaqv knowledge and experience with his University of British Columbia Bachelor of Education and Future Generations University Master of Arts in Applied Community Change and Conservation, as well as knowledge from his past and ongoing partnerships with outside academics, other nations, governments, and other entities. He combined these knowledges in his role as director of HIRMD (held between 2012‐2024) and in other roles with and for the Haíɫzaqv Nation, including being lead negotiator with the haíɫcístut (meaning to turn things around and make them right again) process of reconciliation with the governments of British Columbia and Canada. E.B. advises and partners with outside scientists, gaining proficiency in and adding Western science approaches to his extensive knowledge of the territory and stewardship approaches, and drawing on all of these in his work throughout the territory.

In each of these cases, the authors added new skills, technologies, and knowledge to an already strong foundation of Indigenous knowledge and have used this expanding knowledge base and skillset to benefit the stewardship of the Haíɫzaqv Nation.

M̓ṆÚXVʔIT AT THE SCALE OF AN INDIVIDUAL RESEARCH PROJECT OR PRODUCT

Following the M̓ṇúxvʔit model, knowledges can also be brought together at the scale of a collaboration, not necessarily within individual people (though it can apply at these scales concurrently). The Xvíɫm̓ístaƛ h̓ákq̓áṃ qṇtxv bákvḷásu (our food will return): Multi‐Species Food Systems Restoration Program is an example of M̓ṇúxvʔit occurring at the scale of a broad collaboration. The program was created to restore marine food resources in the Gale Creek area of Haíɫzaqv territory, where the Nathan E. Stewart tug and barge grounded in 2016 and spilled over 100,000 L of marine diesel oil and other petroleum products. Before this, Gale Creek supported the territory's most productive commercial clam beds and dozens of additional food species. The commercial harvest from there, which supported dozens of jobs in Bella Bella, a village of 1200 people, has yet to resume.

The Haíɫzaqv Nation conducted an investigation into how different actors (e.g., the tug and barge operator, the Province of British Columbia, the federal government of Canada) contributed to the spill and response to it. The Haíɫzaqv Nation then convened a committee to assess this investigation in the context of ǧvíḷás, Haíɫzaqv Law. They reported (Dáduqvḷá Committee, 2018) that many aspects of ǧvíḷás were broken. For example, respect (xáɫa) was not shown to Haíɫzaqv knowledge, governance, and sovereignty, which led to chaos on the water and impacts on Haíɫzaqv people and sacred places. Inadequate and ineffective response by outside agencies broke the principle of h̓íkila (to take good care of something), as did their lack of care for Haíɫzaqv members. The Dáduqvḷá Committee provided multiple recommendations, including characterizing the state of the ecosystem following the spill through monitoring and research to inform restoration activities. They also recommended additional mitigation throughout the territory.

HIRMD initiated the Xvíɫm̓ístaƛ h̓ákq̓áṃ qṇtxv bákvḷásu program as part of honoring the Dáduqvḷá Committee's recommendations. It initially focused on clam populations directly affected by the spill. A Haíɫzaqv knowledge team was formed that consisted of harvesters, leaders, and knowledge holders charged with assessing the condition of these areas based on intergenerational and lived knowledge and experiences. The team visited clam beaches regularly to assess beaches and surrounding areas and to compare them with prespill conditions. This work identified key changes, including reduced juvenile clam abundance, differences in the smell, appearance, and texture of beaches, and fewer visible insects and other wildlife. The team found that although conditions were improving, they had not yet returned to their prespill state. These residual impacts on the environment were only identified through the Haíɫzaqv knowledge team; outside agencies deemed the spill resolved once diesel fuel was no longer detectable. With a marine diesel spill being a new threat here, however, there was no Haíɫzaqv knowledge yet on how to assess the mechanisms by which this fuel was having an impact. To gain further insights, HIRMD invited outside ecotoxicologists to collaborate, for example, by testing the condition of clams and the sediments in which they live. Lab‐based experiments are helping to improve understanding of the immediate and long‐term effects of marine diesel oil on resident clams and other marine organisms and to inform actions to protect clams in the event of future spills. Throughout this work, there has been a reciprocal flow of information, with local experts helping to shape research questions and methodologies through their knowledge of the local ecology and ecotoxicologists contributing insights and approaches more specific to their field.

Concurrently, there is an effort to assess the impacts of and to inform the response to an expanding invasion of European green crabs (Carcinus maenas) (EGCs) in the area. This highly invasive species voraciously consumes native species and can destroy habitats, including eelgrass meadows that provide fish nursery habitats among other roles (Howard et al., 2019; Jamieson et al., 1998). People first noticed EGCs in the Haíɫzaqv Nation about a decade ago (Gillespie et al., 2015), including in the area affected by the oil spill, and have observed increases in individual sizes and overall abundance through time. In Gale Creek, this recent expansion occurred after commercial clam harvest activities were halted due to the spill, meaning that harvesters were no longer spending weeks at a time there each year. Harvester absence might have delayed detection of the EGC expansion and hence the response to it. The Haíɫzaqv Nation sought insight into the extent to which EGCs might be further hindering recovery of clam populations. HIRMD recruited academic collaborators to support research on this invasion, including assessing effectiveness of various trapping methods, the degree to which green crabs might currently be limiting clam populations and how trapping might be optimized to address that, and development of monitoring approaches (e.g., eDNA paired with trapping) for early detection of future invasions to facilitate early response. Similar to the spill research, this project evolved through a combination of knowledges. One emergent research project (exclosures to test predation effects of crabs) arose from a full‐day meeting of local and outside collaborators. This project's driving question and methodology were a direct result of everyone being present in the room and contributing at that time.

The Xvíɫm̓ístaƛ h̓ákq̓áṃ qṇtxv bákvḷásu program has expanded beyond Gale Creek. As part of a Haíɫzaqv Nation‐led effort to recover local Pacific herring (Clupea pallasii) populations, collaborations with academics and consultants are resuming recently interrupted, millennia‐old herring transplantation approaches used across the North Pacific (Thornton, 2015; Thornton & Moss, 2021). An additional subproject is assessment of population dynamics and individual health (e.g., contaminant load) of local Steller sea lion (Eumetopias jubatus) populations as part of an integrated stewardship approach to restoring harvest of sea lions for human consumption while limiting sea lion use of key habitats for other important local species, such as herring. Future focal research avenues include restoring salmon populations and advancing understanding of the contribution of other species in the resilience of local ecosystems to invasions or other perturbations.

The project as a whole and each of its subcomponents center Haíɫzaqv governance and knowledge. The objectives and underlying knowledge guiding this work come from those who know these species and places best. Overall direction is guided by HIRMD and an advisory committee that includes harvesters, knowledge holders, Elders, and other local experts. The Haíɫzaqv Nation's strong knowledge of the territory and its ecology and governance approaches are foundational; outside knowledge sources and collaborations are treated as complementary. This arrangement is facilitated by outside collaborators being brought in by invitation of the Haíɫzaqv Nation to support work in ways that the nation has identified. Through this work, HIRMD and the Haíɫzaqv Nation as a whole gain not only the knowledge produced by these collaborations but also insights into how to use additional research tools. For example, the Haíɫzaqv Nation now increasingly has ecotoxicology insights and comfort with its approaches. All parties learn and benefit from the work, which has been conducted in a reciprocal and respectful way, while intentionally centering the questions and governance needs of the Haíɫzaqv Nation.

M̓ṇúxvʔit occurs at the project level in additional collaborations in Haíɫzaqv Territory. One high‐profile example includes research dating habitation of N̓úlaw̓ítx̌v (Triquet Island) to at least 14,000 years before present (e.g., Dyck et al., 2020; Gauvreau et al., 2023). This work combined Haíɫzaqv knowledge and archeology. HIRMD used ancestral knowledge of the area as a starting point that then guided further archeological inquiry. The combined knowledge sources and methodologies provided deep insights into this specific island and the prehistory of the general region. Other examples of Haíɫzaqv Nation‐organized M̓ṇúxvʔit collaborations include inquiry into the ecology and conservation of grizzly bears (Ursus arctos horribilis) (e.g., Artelle et al., 2021; Housty et al., 2014; Service et al., 2014) and salmon (Atlas et al., 2017).

Finally, the process of writing this paper provides a further example of many (though perhaps not all) aspects of the M̓ṇúxvʔit model. Although this paper might not have explicitly added to Haíɫzaqv knowledge, its intent is to encourage work that does. It arose from collaboration among Haíɫzaqv (E.W., W.H., K.B., E.B.) and non‐Haíɫzaqv (K.A., D.C.) collaborators. In 2021, K.A. and E.W. conducted surveys of Haíɫzaqv λápac̓i (clam gardens). On the boat, E.W. explained how he was using the term M̓ṇúxvʔit to describe his approach to archeology and how the research they were doing that day, using drones to add to the already rich place‐based knowledge of λápac̓i, was itself a form of M̓ṇúxvʔit. K.A. found the framing and definition to be very powerful and thought that they fit the Haíɫzaqv Nation‐led, partner‐supported Xvíɫm̓ístaƛ h̓ákq̓áṃ qṇtxv bákvḷásu program perfectly. In the authorship team's conversations, excitement for the term and its broader applicability grew. Through subsequent discussions, the model was more crisply articulated and the paper's shape was iteratively formed. Although E.W. and K.A. led the writing, we all contributed to its development throughout. Central to the M̓ṇúxvʔit approach, this paper's foundation and concept are grounded in Haíɫzaqv territory; we developed it physically within the territory; and its creation was characterized by working together and iteratively among ourselves and across our diverse knowledges. The artwork (Figure 1) depicting M̓ṇúxvʔit also involved a M̓ṇúxvʔit process. Haíɫzaqv artist Ben Humchitt created a draft based on our description and refined it over multiple iterations with input from all of us until the concept was fully encapsulated. This process advanced further in the development of Figure 2, building off Ben Humchitt's artwork, in further iterative collaboration with us and digital artists from Fuse Consulting.

M̓ṆÚXVʔIT AT THE SCALE OF THE COMMUNITY

M̓ṇúxvʔit can occur at even broader scales, for example, across community‐level collaborations. HIRMD is mandated with stewardship of lands, waters, air, and cultural resources across Haíɫzaqv Territory (similar stewardship departments exist in neighboring coastal nations). HIRMD administers activities, including territory‐wide ecological monitoring; wildlife, forestry, and fisheries stewardship, conservation, and harvest management; protection of cultural and heritage features; response to emergencies (e.g., oil spills); and leadership and coordination of research collaborations. HIRMD works under the mandate of the Heiltsuk Tribal Council (an elected body), the Yím̓ás Council (Haíɫzaqv Hereditary Chiefs), and Ḿṇúyaqs Council (Women's Council) and on behalf of the Haíɫzaqv people. In addition to being an arm of and operating within Haíɫzaqv governance structures, HIRMD operates under the imperative to káxḷáya ǧvi̓ḷás (uphold the laws of our ancestors), under the guidance of Haíɫzaqv ǧvi̓ḷás and ɫáxvái, a system of laws, rights, and responsibilities. In collaborations, HIRMD ensures that at the very least cultural and environmental resources are protected, but most partnerships go well beyond that.

HIRMD works to ensure direct (e.g., stewardship activities) and secondary benefits (e.g., opportunities for youth to be out on the territory and gain job shadow experiences, skill development and training for employees, community school and other Haíɫzaqv Nation entity partnerships, and employment opportunities for community members) to the Haíɫzaqv Nation from collaborations. In the many collaborations HIRMD orchestrates with academics, organizations, consultants, and governments, it works to ensure that Haíɫzaqv knowledge and governance is treated as foundational—proposed work that might duplicate, ignore, extract from, or seek to validate Haíɫzaqv knowledge is either not permitted or steered toward useful endeavors that complement existing knowledge sources. Through research and data‐sharing agreements, HIRMD articulates the acceptable uses of Haíɫzaqv knowledge that may be shared in the course of a project, including ownership and intellectual property rights (which remain with the nation or nation member) and the right to review and correct any cultural descriptions or depictions before publication.

HIRMD also leads or partners in many collaborative projects, such as the Gale Creek project, that fully encapsulate M̓ṇúxvʔit. These research initiatives provide ample opportunities for Haíɫzaqv knowledge holders, including HIRMD staff, to participate in and engage with academic research, allowing the sharing of insights based on Haíɫzaqv knowledge that can lead to a deeper mutual understanding of a particular subject and, reciprocally, the internalization and incorporation of new information into the ever‐growing Haíɫzaqv knowledge base. By articulating the Haíɫzaqv Nation's research priorities and establishing a M̓ṇúxvʔit‐based approach, HIRMD reverses a history of extractive research and empowers the nation to lead or collaborate in research that contributes to its stewardship goals.

PRIMACY OF INDIGENOUS KNOWLEDGE AND GOVERNANCE SYSTEMS

Central to the M̓ṇúxvʔit model is recognizing the primacy of Indigenous knowledge and governance systems. Although in many cross‐knowledge system collaborations the primacy of Western approaches is often assumed, with the M̓ṇúxvʔit model, it is local, Indigenous, community knowledge and governance that are primary; Western knowledge is complementary and supportive. This follows from the assertion of Will Ngakuru (quoted in Moko‐Painting et al. [2023]) that “science needs to learn to be on tap, not on top.” Accordingly, with the M̓ṇúxvʔit model, outside science is seen as a set of tools ready to be used if and when needed. Outside collaborators need not lead that work when they are not asked to or when the Haíɫzaqv Nation has interest and capacity in doing so themselves. Science being on tap does not necessarily mean outside actors must do the science. An outcome of M̓ṇúxvʔit collaborations is that the science taps can be moved in‐house because not only outside knowledge but also tools and approaches from outside become one (M̓ṇúxvʔit) with Haíɫzaqv knowledge.

The need for science to be on tap, not on top, might confuse outside collaborators. In some instances, outside collaborators, often with the best intentions, are keen to collect, use, or incorporate Indigenous knowledge in their work. However, although this is situation specific, a community might not want outsiders to have access to or use their knowledge, which might be sensitive and not for outside consideration. In other instances, a community might have a strong handle on their own knowledge and not require outside support with it specifically but might instead seek support with other specialized approaches. In the Gale Creek example, some student research did not directly incorporate Indigenous knowledge and instead used primarily Western approaches, which is what the Haíɫzaqv Nation requested. It was not that Indigenous knowledge was overlooked. Haíɫzaqv knowledge and Haíɫzaqv governance are foundational to the Gale Creek work. Instead, it is the Haíɫzaqv Nation that brings together different knowledge sources and orchestrates the M̓ṇúxvʔit. The students are still very much a part of M̓ṇúxvʔit. Their knowledge was added to Haíɫzaqv knowledge, supported Haíɫzaqv governance, and benefited the Haíɫzaqv Nation, but the nation led the knowledge integration. By contrast, had these students insisted on incorporating Indigenous knowledge in their work, for example, by expecting to interview local knowledge holders despite such steps not being deemed necessary by the Haíɫzaqv Nation, this would have been less in line with a M̓ṇúxvʔit approach because it would not have respected Indigenous governance and the valuable time of governing bodies and individuals (Kitasoo Xai'xais Stewardship Authority, 2021). However, this does not mean students did not still learn immensely from working with Haíɫzaqv members and in the territory or that Haíɫzaqv knowledge was not shared in other ways with the students through the course of their work. Moreover, students still pursued their interests, devising creative ways to answer the questions being asked and finding additional secondary research questions that could also be of interest to the Haíɫzaqv Nation. However, a key insight from this project is that coproduction does not need to involve outside actors from the very outset or in determining questions of interest and research focus. Nations and communities likely know their specific needs best and outside actors willing to respect that and tackle identified priorities provide opportunities for scholarship of outsized value to all involved.

RECOGNIZING INDIGENOUS KNOWLEDGE AS LIVING, BREATHING, AND EVOLVING

The centrality of Indigenous knowledge in M̓ṇúxvʔit emphasizes how Indigenous knowledges (as all knowledges) are continually evolving and growing through time. Some might assume that Indigenous knowledge pertains only to ancestral knowledge that stopped evolving at an arbitrary point (e.g., onset of settler colonialism), whereas Western knowledge is assumed to continually grow and adapt. We have yet to come across a logical explanation for this false dichotomy. By contrast, Haíɫzaqv (and other Indigenous) knowledge is living, breathing, and continually growing and could be defined simply as the sum of the knowledge held by the Haíɫzaqv Nation in the past, present, and future, ranging from ancestral knowledge passed down for millennia to boundary‐pushing knowledge acquired in real time at micro‐ to macroscales, qualitative to quantitative, physical to spiritual, and concrete to abstract. With M̓ṇúxvʔit, knowledge gained through collaborations becomes part of that growing Indigenous knowledge base. When E.W. employs ancient núyṃ́ to inform his archeology, he is using Haíɫzaqv knowledge. When he uses data his grandson collects with drones, he is also using Haíɫzaqv knowledge. That knowledge is not frozen in time is consistent with the acknowledgment that Indigenous rights are also not frozen in time. In Canada, this was affirmed in Canada's Supreme Court R. v. Sparrow 1990 decision regarding Indigenous fishing rights. Sparrow noted that Indigenous peoples’ inherent rights to resources allow for the evolution of fishing practices and technologies: rights are not tied to technologies used at a specific historical period. Knowledge, technologies, and practices have evolved through time and continue to do so, a fact central to M̓ṇúxvʔit.

NOT A SIMPLE REVERSAL OF EXTRACTIVE APPROACH

Although the M̓ṇúxvʔit model emphasizes knowledge flowing from outside sources into Indigenous and local communities’ knowledge and governance, it is not simply a reversal of settler colonial extraction of Indigenous knowledge. When Indigenous knowledge is extracted, it occurs within a broader settler colonial context, in lockstep with direct subjugation, extraction, and other harms. With M̓ṇúxvʔit, knowledge flows from outside into the Haíɫzaqv Nation, but this does not involve concurrent harms to outside communities. An Indigenous community using technology developed from other cultures does not occur in tandem with that Indigenous community taking the land, people, or resources of the societies making that technology. Moreover, partnerships built on reciprocity support benefits flowing in both directions: outside collaborators involved in M̓ṇúxvʔit learn from and benefit directly from these collaborations, for example, through learning, publications, and postsecondary degrees earned. Moreover, the M̓ṇúxvʔit model can support advancement of knowledge outside the community through research published broadly or approaches developed in the community and shared elsewhere.

TOKENISM IS NOT M̓ṆÚXVʔIT

As interest in (and incentive for) academics and others to partner with Indigenous nations grows, the risk of tokenism (and its relative performativity) might also increase. We have all experienced or witnessed an Indigenous person being asked to be listed as an author on an academic publication in which the person had little or no involvement, or at a late stage in the process, precluding the opportunity to meaningfully engage. This might happen with good intentions in an attempt to correct a history of Indigenous knowledge extraction without acknowledgment. However, such tokenism provides few of the benefits of a truly collaborative, M̓ṇúxvʔit‐type approach. It typically benefits the outside authors (signals collaboration with an Indigenous community), with little benefit to the tokenized author (e.g., no tangible benefits if that author is not an academic). Tokenistic inclusion could cause direct harm by misrepresenting a community's perspective on a topic. We have witnessed Indigenous people being listed as authors on papers without having read them or knowing their contents, effectively putting words into those authors’ (and their communities’) mouths. Tokenistic outputs might also crowd out truly collaborative work. Work with only a veneer of collaboration might take less time to conduct and publish, creating a situation where researchers falsely signal a collaborative approach might be more rewarded by the academic system (e.g., more publications, grants, citations) than those taking the time to engage in authentic collaborations.

Tokenistic inclusion is further contrary to expectations of M̓ṇúxvʔit (or good research or governance more broadly) because it does not transparently and authentically represent the true nature of collaboration. Contrary to M̓ṇúxvʔit, by not meaningfully engaging with Indigenous systems or people, tokenistic approaches further center outside knowledge or governance systems.

It would be difficult to judge whether a purported collaboration is authentic, and we do not propose policing other people's collaborations. What constitutes meaningful collaboration is likely best defined by communities themselves. For example, contributions might not always appear via tracked changes in a manuscript but instead appear through iterative discussions in person. Diverse forms of engagement and contributions are likely more the norm than the exception. Instead, it might be most useful for practitioners and outside authors to reflect on their own approaches and honestly assess whether they are engaging tokenistically or authentically. Many characteristics of the M̓ṇúxvʔit model (e.g., work being guided or led by communities, following local governance systems and protocols, centering Indigenous governance or knowledge systems, research questions set by communities, collaborators brought in by invitation) might help preclude tokenistic approaches. The tokenism we have witnessed lacked most of the M̓ṇúxvʔit model's characteristics.

COMMONALITIES AMONG M̓ṆÚXVʔIT AND OTHER MODELS

The M̓ṇúxvʔit model was created within the Haíɫzaqv Nation and was shaped by the unique circumstances of the governance and collaborations there, but it shares characteristics with other models for bringing knowledges together respectfully. For example, the two‐eyed seeing approach (Marshall et al., 2015; Reid et al., 2021) similarly describes ways in which “learning to see from one eye with the strengths of Indigenous knowledges and ways of knowing, and from the other eye with the strengths of mainstream knowledges and ways of knowing, and to use both these eyes together, for the benefit of all” (Bartlett et al., 2012) and has a similar focus on mutual respect among knowledge systems. Two‐eyed seeing encapsulates how some productive collaborations within Haíɫzaqv territory operate, where knowledge sources work respectfully side by side. In some cases, early collaborations in the territory begin with a two‐eyed seeing approach, but as collaborators work closely through time the approach might shift more toward M̓ṇúxvʔit; outside knowledge and approaches increasingly are incorporated into Haíɫzaqv knowledge. Even in broad Haíɫzaqv Nation‐led M̓ṇúxvʔit collaborations, the relationship between individual practitioners might be described as two‐eyed seeing, with the overarching M̓ṇúxvʔit occurring concurrently at a higher level, with the Haíɫzaqv Nation incorporating these outside knowledge sources into Haíɫzaqv knowledge. Similar to M̓ṇúxvʔit, the Pūtaiao model in Aotearoa New Zealand (Moko‐Painting et al., 2023) explicitly centers Indigenous (in this case Māori) knowledge, governance, and sovereignty in research. In Nunavut, Inuit youth have developed ScIQ (combining “Science” and “Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit”) to help educate outside researchers about what communities need and expect in collaborations and to guide them in bringing their knowledge and research to communities in a meaningful, respectful, safe, and productive way (Pedersen et al., 2020). We suspect that countless models of respectfully centering local and Indigenous authority and knowledge have been developed or are being developed worldwide that are not yet broadly known but that could similarly help inform and inspire appropriate collaborations elsewhere. It is important to uphold these models as key components of respecting the sovereignty and knowledge of the peoples and territories from which they evolved.

CONCLUSION

The M̓ṇúxvʔit model is not a one‐size‐fits‐all proclamation of how knowledges ought to intersect. Rather, M̓ṇúxvʔit is a particular successful approach that we believe can provide inspiration elsewhere and can advance broader thinking about how science and other Western aspects of thought and governance can intersect with local and Indigenous systems in ways that explicitly add to, not extract from, those systems. We suspect that in many cases, by centering Indigenous and local knowledge and treating it as the foundation, collaborations might begin on more solid footing, given local knowledge often far exceeds outside knowledge; support better informed decisions pertaining to a given geography based on better knowledge of local ecology and culture and hence knowledge of which solutions might work; support more respectful methods overall by acknowledging sovereignty of those on whose lands collaborations are occurring; and be more appropriate by centering the knowledge and governance of those most affected by the research, conservation, or other decisions flowing from such collaborations (i.e., those who live in or are otherwise connected to the areas of interest).

In 1913, Haíɫzaqv Bob Anderson famously told the McKenna–McBride Commission, a colonial commission that aimed to determine the adequacy of reserve sizes in Canada, that “We feel that we own the whole of this Country, every bit of it, and ought to have something to say about it.” This assertion was in reference to sovereignty over Haíɫzaqv lands and waters, but the pressing relevance of this statement to Indigenous communities’ sovereignty over all aspects of their lifeways, including their knowledge, is becoming increasingly clear. M̓ṇúxvʔit provides a model for communities to have something to say about how, when, and with whom their own knowledge is used: on their own terms.
