Effect of Activator Type on the High-Temperature Properties of Alkali-Activated Slag Cementing Fluid
Authors: Yang Li, Shenglai Guo, Ye Zhang, Danzhu Zheng, Rong Wang, Yuanhai Zhang, Jiajun Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The type of activator has a significant impact on the performance of alkali-activated slag, and there is little research on the effect of activator type on the high-temperature performance of alkali-activated slag. The effects of activator type on the thickening time, compressive strength, and rheological properties of alkali-activated slag under a high-temperature condition were studied in this article. Six activators were designed using Ca(OH)2, Na2CO3, and Na2SO4 and their combination. The results show that there are obvious differences for the thickening time, compressive strength, and rheological properties of alkali-activated slag with different activators. In addition, the effect of activator type on AAM performance at elevated temperatures is distinct from that observed at room temperature. Among alkali-activated slag with six different activators, Ca(OH)2–Na2SO4-activated slag has the best performance by considering the properties of thickening time, strength, and rheology under high temperatures. The low alkalinity of Na2SO4, producing NaOH by the reaction of Ca(OH)2 and Na2SO4, and the reaction rate of Ca(OH)2 and Na2SO4 can easily be controlled by the retarder, which may be responsible for the excellent performance of Ca(OH)2–Na2SO4-activated slag.

Introduction

Cementing quality is the key to zonal isolation and wellbore integrity. During the cementing operation, a large amount of oil well cement slurry will be used to seal the casing and the annulus of the well wall. Usually, hundreds or even thousands of tonnes of cement are used for each well. Cementing slurries are usually made from oil-well-grade G or H cement (both silicate cements). The production of OPC consumes a lot of energy and produces a lot of pollutants (dust, SO2, etc.) as well as the greenhouse gas CO2.1,2 In China, for example, carbon emissions from the cement industry alone will be 1.23 billion tonnes in 2020, accounting for about 12.4% of the country’s total carbon emissions.3 Silicate cement, as a “big carbon emitter”, has become an important breakthrough point in the national carbon emission reduction strategy.

Alkali-activated materials (AAM) made from industrial solid waste (blast furnace slag, fly ash, etc.) as raw materials have the advantages of high strength, low carbon emission (about 1/6 of the carbon emission of cement), green and environmental protection, etc. AAM are considered to be the most promising “OPC substitutes”.1,4−6 The application in the field of cementing globally shows that the AAM have excellent resistance to carbon dioxide and salt corrosion, low permeability, resistance to contamination of drilling fluids, and self-healing ability and other outstanding characteristics.1,7−12

Although AAM have many excellent properties, under the activation of different activators, AAM still have many problems that need to be solved. For example, although strong bases such as NaOH and Na2SiO3 as activators can provide good strength, there are problems such as the thickening time not being easy to regulate or the poor “right-angle-set” performance.8,13 Ca(OH)2 as an activator has low early strength, while Na2CO3 and Na2SO4 as activators suffer from long setting time and low strength.14−16 The effect of the activator type on the macroscopic properties of AAM is very significant. Moreover, no single activator can fully meet the macroscopic performance requirements of AAM. Therefore, using different activators has become a common way to improve the comprehensive performance of AAM.6,17,18 At present, a large number of studies have shown that the performance of alkali-excited materials can be significantly improved through the activator adjustment, but the relationship between the activator and the AAM property has not been clarified.6,19 It is necessary to study the effect of the activator type on each property of AAM. A substantial body of research has been conducted into the properties of AAM in response to the influence of various activators. However, two significant issues still need to be addressed.20,21 First, the activating temperature of the studies was mainly room temperature, with a paucity of data at elevated temperatures. Second, the activator concentration and source of raw materials in previous studies varied greatly. There was a lack of comparison under the same conditions (e.g., the same experimental temperature, the same activator dosage, and the same source of raw materials). Therefore, it is important to clarify the effect of activator type on AAM high-temperature performance while ensuring the same experimental conditions.

In terms of raw materials, slag has wide sources, high activity, and high amorphous content and the choice of slag as raw material is representative.6 In terms of activators, the production process of NaOH and Na2SiO3 is energy intensive, and there are problems with fast setting time or poor “right-angle-set” performance.4,22−24 The use of Ca(OH)2 with Na2CO3 (Na2SO4) instead of NaOH has now become a potential way.25−27 In our previous study, we discovered that Ca(OH)2 undergoes a chemical reaction with Na2CO3 and Na2SO4. The addition of a retarder enables the reduction of the reaction rate, thereby extending the thickening time of the AAM. This undoubtedly offers new insights into the potential for high-temperature performance modulation in AAM. Therefore, in this work, Ca(OH)2, Na2CO3, Na2SO4, and mutual compounding were used as activators.28−30 The rheology, thickening time, compressive strength, and other properties of all systems were systematically tested by fixing the total mass of activator as 10% of slag. Then, XRD and hydration exothermic rate were combined to reveal the mechanism of the influence of each activator on the performance of AAM. This paper clarifies the connection between activators and the macroscopic properties of AAM by studying the influence of activator types on the comprehensive performance of slag cementing fluids and lays a theoretical foundation for the application of AAM in the field of oil and gas cementing.

Sample Preparation and Formulation Design

Six systems of Ca(OH)2 (CH), Na2CO3 (NC), Na2SO4 (NS), Ca(OH)2–Na2SO4 (CHNS), Ca(OH)2–Na2CO3 (CHNS), and Ca(OH)2–Na2SO4–Na2CO3 (CHNSNC) were designed by fixing the total equivalence of the activator as 10% of slag. The specific formulations are listed in Table 2. Due to the difference in solubility of Ca(OH)2, Na2CO3, and Na2SO4 and the mutual reaction in solution, all the formulations in this paper were made by dry mixing slag with the activator presently for 5 min, homogenizing and then mixing the dry mixture with slurry mixing water. The slurry was first stirred in a mixer at low speed (4000 rpm) for 15 s and then at high speed (12,000 rpm) for 35 s (Figure 2).

Rheology Test

The rheological properties of the systems were tested at room temperature using a six-speed rotational viscometer according to API RP 10B-2 (2010). The slurries were sheared at different speeds first in ascending order and then in descending order. The rheological measurements reported in this paper are based on the average of ascending and descending order readings. In testing the rheological properties of each system at 90 °C, the slurry was first preheated in the thickener for 30 min at 90 °C, and a 1% retarder was added to prevent the slurry from solidifying during the thickening preheating process. At the end of the preheating process, the slurry was removed from the thickener and poured into a viscometer cup to measure the rheological properties.

Thickening Time Test

The thickening time of a cement slurry measures how long it remains pumpable downhole. The consistency value required to ensure pumpability is usually less than 30 Bc. According to the API RP 10B-2 standard (2010), the slurry’s thickening time is measured by rotating the slurry cup continuously in the thickening apparatus at 125 rpm.

Hydration exothermic data can accurately describe the activator type on the slag solid solution hydration reaction rate; this section describes the experimental selection of instruments for isothermal calorimetry (Calmetrix I-Cal Flex), the experimental test conditions for the atmospheric pressure of 90 °C, and the specific operating procedures with the author’s previous research consistent28 (Figure 3).

Compressive Strength Test

The compressive strength was obtained by crushing the cured cement samples in a universal testing machine. The prepared slurry samples were first poured into 50 mm × 50 mm × 50 mm molds and placed at different temperatures for water bath conditioning. To ensure the accuracy of the compressive strength of the samples, the samples were tested three times for the same formulation, and the results were averaged.

X-ray Diffraction Test

The crushed dry samples were finely ground in an agate mortar, and then XRD samples were produced by the press method. The samples were analyzed for XRD physical composition using a Bruker D2 phaser X-ray diffractometer with a scanning angle of 5–70°, a rate of 2°/min, and a step size of 0.02° per step.

Results and Discussion

Rheology

Figure 4 demonstrates the effect of activator type on the rheological properties of the alkali-activated slag slurry. Due to the differences in activator types, there are large differences in the rheological properties of the different systems. Due to the viscosity of alkali-activated slag under the high shear rate being more important for the cementing slurry to ensure the safety of cementing construction, the viscosity of alkali-activated slag under the shear rate of more than 90 s–1 is discussed in this section. At 25 °C, the CH exhibits the highest viscosity at all shear rates and displays poor rheological property. The solubility of Ca(OH)2 in water is minimal among the selected activators in this paper, and its small particle size and large surface area result in a significant requirement for water. The rheological properties of NC and NS are markedly superior to those of CH. This phenomenon can be attributed to the high solubility of Na2CO3 and Na2SO4. Na2CO3 has higher solubility and relatively strong alkalinity. The reaction rate of Na2CO3 and slag is relatively fast. Accordingly, the rheological property of the NC exhibits certain differences compared to those of the NS. CHNS exhibits favorable rheological characteristics, whereas CHNC displays a markedly inferior rheological property. Although the solubility of Na2CO3 and Na2SO4 is large, Na2CO3 has much stronger alkalinity than Na2SO4. The Ca(OH)2 in the compound system will react with Na2CO3 to generate NaOH and nanosized CaCO3. With a small particle size and a large surface area, the generated CaCO3 will seriously damage the rheological properties of the slurry, so the rheological property of the CHNC system is poor.22,29 It can be concluded that the rheological property of CHNSNC exhibits an intermediate position between those of CHNS and CHNC. This finding is corroborated by the results of the actual test.

Rheology at different temperatures: (a):25 °C and (b) 90 °C.

Figure 4b demonstrates the effect of activator type on the rheological property of slag cementitious fluids at 90 °C. The rheological property of all systems improved significantly with increasing temperature, which is consistent with the rheological property of the OPC. It is worth noting that with the increase in temperature, the rheological property of the CH system became the worst in comparison, while the rheological property of CHNC, which was poor at 25 °C, was better than that of the CH system with the increase of temperature; the rheological properties of CHNS, NC, and CHNSNC were relatively close to each other, and the rheological properties of the NS system were still the best. This is due to the fact that the solubility of calcium hydroxide decreases significantly with the increase of temperature, which directly leads to the smaller specific surface area of calcium hydroxide in the slurry. The smaller specific area will lead to more water adsorption, so that the slurry rheological property deteriorates. This is also the reason that the original rheology of the CHNS system is better in the environment at 90 °C, and CHNS, NC, and other systems are closer. In summary, activator type (the solubility, alkalinity, and particle size) will affect the rheological performance of alkali-activated slag slurry to different degrees.

Thickening Time

Thickening Time at 90 °C

The quality of the cementing slurry’s thickening property is a determining factor in whether the cement can safely reach the desired depth at the bottom of the well. In the elevated temperature conditions at the bottom of the well, the hydration rate of the cementing material is much faster and the adjustment difficulty of the thickening time is significantly greater than that at room temperature. The thickening property at high temperature is the most important property of cementing cement. It is important to study the effect of activator type on the thickening performance of the slag cementing fluid. Therefore, the effect of activator type on the thickening performance of the slag cementing fluid was tested. Figure 5 shows the consistency change curves of different activated-slag systems at 90 °C. The transition time with the consistency increasing from 30 to 70 Bc for CH was approximately 90 min, and the consistency grew slowly during the experiment. The consistency change curve of CH is a typical “non-right-angle-set”, which is highly detrimental to the safety of cementing construction. This is due to the slow dissolution of Ca(OH)2 and the low reaction rate between Ca(OH)2 and slag, which is extremely unfavorable to the safety of cementing construction.23 The consistency of NC and NS without retarder at 300 min was still less than 20 Bc, which may be related to the weak alkalinity of the activator. Moreover, the long thickening time usually brings some safety hazards to the cementing progress. Therefore, NC and NS are also not very suitable as the cementing slurry. The thickening curve of CHNS is deemed to be satisfactory for the purposes of cementing construction. However, the thickening times for CHNC and CHNSNC were too short, which did not facilitate the deep well cementing construction. The NS and NC systems with very long thickening time (>300 min) changed to less than 25 min of thickening time after adding Ca(OH)2. The results suggested that some reactions in the system resulted in a severe reduction in the thickening time of the system, which may be due to the reaction of Ca(OH)2 with Na2CO3 or Na2SO4 to produce NaOH with stronger alkalinity.

Thickening time measured under 90 °C (without retarder).

Since the thickening time of CHNC, CHNS, and CHNSNC was very short, the consistency change curves after adding 0.5% retarder were tested. It can be seen from Figure 6 that 0.5% retarder can prolong the thickening time of CHNS to more than 250 min, while the thickening time of CHNC is only 25 min, and the thickening time of CHNSNC is about 120 min. It can be seen that there are large differences in the retarding effect of retarders on the thickening time of different activated-slag systems. It is noteworthy that both the CHNC and CHNSNC systems exhibit excellent “right-angle-set”.

Thickening time measured under 90 °C (with retarder).

In order to further clarify the effect mechanism of the retarder on the different alkali-activated slags, XRD analysis was conducted. The activator solutions containing different activators and retarders with the same concentration with the different alkali-activated slag systems were maintained at 90 °C for 24 h in a water bath and then filtered to obtain a mixture containing the reacted solid activator and the reaction product. The filtered mixture was vacuum dried for 24 h at 40 °C. The samples were milled into powder and then subjected to XRD testing and analysis, as shown in Figure 7. The results of XRD analysis show that under the same conditions, the diffraction peak of Ca(OH)2 in CHNC is the lowest, which indicates that the reaction between Ca(OH)2 and Na2CO3 in CHNC is the most violent, and Ca(OH)2 is consumed the most. The diffraction peak of Ca(OH)2 in CHNS is the highest, which indicates that the remaining Ca(OH)2 in CHNS is the most and the reaction is the slowest. The reaction of CHNSNC is in between. It can be seen that the worst effect of the retarder on the thickening time of CHNC is caused by the most violent reaction between Ca(OH)2 and Na2CO3 in this system. The mutual reaction between activators is an important factor affecting the effect of thickening time regulation in compound–activator systems.

Thickening Time at 120 °C

The number of wells with bottom-hole temperatures exceeding 100 °C is rapidly increasing. Figures 8–13 show the effect of activator type on the slag cementing fluid consistency change curve at 120 °C and 30 MPa. The retarder has a good effect in regulating the thickening time of CH, but the “right-angle-set” property of this system is still poor, which is not conducive to the safety of cementing construction. The longer the time with the consistency increased from 30 to 100 Bc, the worse the system’s “right-angle-set” thickening property. NC, which has a thickening time of more than 300 min at 90 °C, has a thickening time of less than 100 min at 120 °C. It is noteworthy that the addition of 12% retarder cannot still extend the thickening time of NC. The thickening time of NC cannot be regulated by the retarder, which is obviously not able to satisfy the needs of cementing construction. These findings are wholly distinct from those of the preceding experiments conducted in a low-temperature environment.18,22,30 NS and CHNS show superior thickening characteristics at 120 °C, and the thickening time can be extended to more than 500 min with 0.5% of retarder. Although the thickening time of CHNC and CHNSNC systems is controllable, the amount of retarder required is too large (about 10 times that of CHNS). The excessive amount of retarder will greatly contribute to the cost of cementing.

For CH, Ca(OH)2 is insoluble in water, so the OH– concentration in the slurry was kept at a low level. The insoluble Ca(OH)2 adsorbs a large number of water molecules, which will lead to the poor rheological property and “nonright-angle-set” of CH. Despite the high solubility of Na2SO4, its alkalinity is relatively weak, and its destructive effect on the slag vitreous structure is also weak. The hydration rate of NS is slower than those of other activators, and the thickening time can be readily controlled. Na2CO3 has the characteristics of high solubility and high alkalinity, although the hydration process of Na2CO3-activated slag at room temperature has a long induction period.14,30 However, the elevated temperature of 120 °C markedly augmented the potential activity of the slag and accelerated the hydration rate of the NC. Moreover, the hydration rate of the NC is hard to be adjusted by the retarder due to the relatively high alkalinity and rapid dissolution rate of Na2CO3. The high-temperature thickening times of CHNS, CHNC, and CHNSNC can all be regulated by the retarder, but the required retarder dosage varies considerably depending on the activator type.

Thickening Time at 150 °C

In order to further study the high-temperature resistance of alkali-activated slag, the thickening property of alkali-activated slag with different activators and 5% retarder was tested under 150 °C and 50 MPa. The thickening curve is shown in Figure 14. The 5% retarder can extend the thickening time of CH, NS to more than 500 min. The high-temperature thickening time of the NC could not be tested due to the very fast reaction rate under 150 °C. The thickening time of CHNC and CHNSNC is less than 200 min, and these two systems are obviously insufficient to withstand the high temperature of 150 °C. The thickening time of CHNS can reach more than 500 min. This may be because the alkali of Na2SO4 is much lower than that of Na2CO3, and the reaction rate between Ca(OH)2 and Na2SO4 can be retarded by the retarder more easily.

Hydration Exotherm

In order to clarify the influence of activator type on the thickening performance of alkali-activated slag, the effect of activator type on the exothermic rate of alkali-activated slag at 90 °C was tested in this section, as shown in Figure 15. The hydration exothermic peaks of CH, NC, and NS appeared around 3, 20, and 58 h, respectively, which indicated that the reaction rate of the alkali-activated slag with a single activator at 90 °C was relatively slow. The hydration exothermic peaks of CHNC, CHNS, and CHNSNC with thickening time less than 25 min at 90 °C all appeared within 1 h, which coincided with the thickening curve of each system at 90 °C. For the single activator system of CH, NC, and NS, the hydration exothermic peak appeared later, and the hydration reaction rate of the system was slower. The hydration exothermic peaks of CHNC, CHNS, and CHNSNC appeared within 1 h after the activator was combined, which indicated that the hydration rate of the system was extremely fast at this time. Combined with the XRD test results in Section 3.2.1, it can be seen that this is due to the reaction of Ca(OH)2 with Na2CO3 (Na2SO4) to produce NaOH with stronger alkalinity. The violent reaction between slag and NaOH causes the system to hydrate too quickly and thus leads to the system thickening time being very short.

Compressive Strength

The compressive strength of the cementing materials is closely related to the quality of cementing31. Therefore, the effect of activator type on the strength of alkali-activated slag was tested in this section, as shown in Figure 16. The compressive strength of NC develops most rapidly at 90 °C, with a 1-day strength of more than 19 MPa and a 3-day strength of about 20 MPa. In contrast, some studies have reported that Na2CO3-activated slag not only has low strength but also has a long setting time at room temperature, which is obviously different from the research results presented in this study.14 This proves that the temperature has a significant impact on the development of strength for Na2CO3-activated slag. NS did not solidify for 24 h, but its 3d strength was only 7 MPa, which may be related to the weak alkalinity of Na2SO4. Although Na2SO4-activated slag has a good high-temperature thickening property, the poor strength makes it difficult to meet the need for cementing construction. The strengths of CHNS and CHNC are similar, and both are higher than those of the CH system, which is in accordance with the findings of refs (19) and (32). The 3d strength of CHNSNC was about 8 MPa, which illustrates the adverse effect on strength caused by the simultaneous use of Na2CO3 and Na2SO4.

Compressive strength cured at 90 °C.

The type of activator has a large impact on the strength of the alkali-activated slag. XRD analysis was carried out on the samples of 90 °C for 3 days. As can be seen from Figure 17, the hydration products of each system are mainly dominated by C–S–H, calcite, and hydrotalcite. In addition to this, the presence of an ettringite phase is observed in NS and CHNS, which may be related to the Na2SO4 in the activator. The content of calcite and C–S–H in the NC is much higher than that in the other systems. Moreover, the calcite and C–S–H diffraction peaks in the NS and CHNSNC are significantly lower than those in the other systems. The content of calcite and C–S–H in the hydration products are the main reasons for the differences of the compressive strength of the different systems, which are closely related to the type of activator used.

Conclusions

In this paper, the effect of activator type on the comprehensive performance of alkali-activated slag under a high temperature was systematically investigated. Three single activators Ca(OH)2, Na2SO4, and Na2CO3 and three compounded activator systems Ca(OH)2–Na2SO4, Ca(OH)2–Na2CO3, and Ca(OH)2–Na2SO4–Na2CO3 were selected as the activators, and the dosage of the activators were all 10% by weight of slag. The thickening property, rheology, compressive strength, and heat of hydration were tested and XRD analysis was performed. The detailed conclusions are as follows:(1)There is some effect of the activator type on the rheological performance of the alkali-activated slag slurry. In general, CH and CHNC exhibited the poorest rheological performance, whereas NS and CHNS demonstrated the most favorable rheological performance. The disparate rheological performances may be primarily ascribed to the heightened concentration of Ca(OH)2 or CaCO3 in the slurry.(2)The thickening time of the alkali-activated slag differs significantly when using different activators and in the absence of a retarder at 90 °C. The thickening time of CHNC and CHNS is less than 10 min, while that of CHNSNC is 25 min. The thickening time of CH is 100 min, and that of NC and NS is more than 300 min. The much shorter thickening time of CHNC, CHNS, and CHNSNC could be attributed to the production of NaOH by the reaction of Ca(OH)2 and Na2CO3 or Ca(OH)2 and Na2SO4. The higher alkalinity of Ca(OH)2 relative to those of Na2CO3 and Na2SO4 results in a shorter thickening time for CH compared to NC and NS.(3)The effect of the activator type on the adjustable ability of the thickening time of alkali-activated slag is very obvious. It is exceedingly challenging to modify the thickening times of NC, CHNC, and CHNSNC when the temperature exceeds 120 °C. The thickening times of NS, CHNS, and CH are very easy to adjust above 120 °C. This is mainly due to the much stronger alkalinity of Na2CO3 compared to Na2SO4, and the dissolution process of Na2CO3 and Na2SO4 is very hard by the retarder. Otherwise, the “right-angle-set” performance of CHNS is far superior to that of CH.(4)The compressive strength of the alkali-activated slag also shows much difference. In general, the NC, CHNC, and CHNS variants demonstrate superior compressive strength, whereas the NS, CH, and CHNSNC variants exhibit comparatively weaker compressive strength. The higher compressive strength is mainly due to the more C–S–H and CaCO3 produced.(5)In light of the aforementioned characteristics, CHNS is deemed a more suitable cementing slurry, particularly in consideration of its adjustable thickening time and compressive strength.
