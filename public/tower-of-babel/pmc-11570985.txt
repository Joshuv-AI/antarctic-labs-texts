Transcriptomic and bioinformatic analysis of Histone H2A-genes in Lates calcarifer and Oreochromis niloticus upon Vibrio vulnificus challenge
Authors: K.L. Dhanya Lenin, Theivanayagam Maharajan, S. Antony Ceasar, Swapna P. Antony
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Expression profiling of histone H2A-derived AMP genes, GIFT-Histone, and Lc-Histone upon in vivo bacterial challenge has been investigated.•Immune responses against Gram-negative bacteria in Oreochromis niloticus and Lates calcarifer has been explored.•Species-specific variations in expression patterns were noted in the study.

Introduction

Fish, the aquatic vertebrate poikilotherms, thrive in nutrient-rich environment teeming with microbes. Their innate immune system acts as a rapid antimicrobial defense barrier and sets the stage for a precise acquired immune response against antigens identified by the innate system [1]. Among the fish species, Asian seabass (Lates calcarifer) stands out as a highly euryhaline catadromous teleost with high growth and fecundity rates and tolerance to high stocking density. These are widely distributed and are an economically profitable culture species in SouthEast Asian countries [2]. Another significant fish species involved in aquaculture is the Genetically Improved Farmed Tilapia (GIFT), a highly productive strain of Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus), valued globally as an economically important food fish and an ideal teleost model for immune system research [2]. Tilapia has been bestowed the capacity of becoming one of the most important food fish [3]. To improve the growth performance and resilience to variable environments a project for genetic improvement of the farmed tilapia was implemented by ICLARM [4]. Selective breeding was adopted and the present GIFT strain has a much faster growth rate and is a favored strain globally [5]

However, a major concern for the aquaculture industry is the prevalence of vibriosis, a bacterial infection affecting both finfish and shellfish, with mortality rates reaching up to 50 % in cultured species [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Vibrio vulnificus is a Gram-negative species of the Vibrio genus, which is motile with a curved rod shape, pathogenic, and found in marine, brackish, and coastal waters. They are found to impact cultured fish particularly and have been reported as a zoonotic pathogen causing serious illnesses in humans, including gastroenteritis, wound infections, and septicemia, with antimicrobial resistance adding to the challenge [[12], [13], [14]]. With the rise of antibiotic resistance in Vibrio strains, there is a critical need for natural alternatives, and this is where antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are deemed an inevitable component of research.

AMPs, as natural antibiotics, exhibit multifunctional therapeutic potential with a broad spectrum of activity against various microorganisms, including the very prevalent Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, fungi, parasites, viruses, and tumor cells under normal physiological conditions [15]. Due to their unique mode of action and pharmacodynamic properties, AMPs are considered promising antimicrobials with limited potential for bacterial resistance development [16]. These peptides can be constitutively expressed or induced by microbial presence in tissues such as the skin, respiratory, and gastrointestinal tracts, and their broad-spectrum activity is determined by their mode of action and physicochemical properties [17]. Histones, discovered by Albrecht Kossel in 1884, are basic proteins abundant in lysine and arginine [18], associated with negatively charged DNA in the nucleus and falling into linker (H1/H5) and core (H2A, H2B, H3, and H4) family. The N-terminus of histone H2A serves as a precursor for AMPs found in fish, including the 51-mer Hipposin [19]. However, only a limited number of histone H2A-derived AMPs have been functionally characterized to date.

This study serves as a preliminary investigation into the transcriptomic variations of the histone H2A-derived AMP gene upon V. vulnificus challenge, facilitating a comparative analysis between the immune responses in two aquaculture species, Asian sea bass (L. calcarifer), predominantly cultured in marine or brackish water environments, and Genetically Improved Farmed Tilapia (GIFT – O. niloticus), commonly reared in freshwater. The functional insights derived from this analysis will significantly contribute to understanding the role and functionality of the peptide, providing a platform for further research on its potential therapeutic applications in aquaculture. Furthermore, the contrasting culture environments of these two species highlight the significance of their adaptability and resilience, thereby underscoring the practical implications for enhancing aquaculture practices, particularly in addressing challenges related to antibiotic resistance and biosecurity.

Materials and methods

Fish acquisition, experimental setup, and ethical considerations

Fingerling stage fishes of Lates calcarifer (Asian seabass) and Oreochromis niloticus (GIFT), measuring 6.42 ± 0.75 cm in length and weighing approximately 4.58 ± 1.53 g, were obtained from the Rajiv Gandhi Centre for Aquaculture (RGCA), Kochi, India. Upon arrival, the fish were transferred to the aquarium facility of the department and acclimatized for 14 days. Ten fish each were housed in six fiber tanks with a water capacity of 40 liters. Throughout the acclimatization period, continuous aeration was provided to maintain optimal oxygen levels. Also, regular waste removal and 50 % water exchange were done daily to maintain optimal conditions such as water temperature (24 °C), pH (7), and salinity. The GIFT fingerlings were maintained at 0 ppt salinity whereas the sea bass fingerlings were reared at 15 ppt salinity. The fishes were fed a standard formulated floating pellet feed ad libitum. Towards the end of the acclimatization period, active feeding behavior was observed, indicating the successful adaptation of the fish to the new environment. All experimental procedures involving the handling of the fish and the subsequent experiments were done following the guidelines and regulations set forth by the Institutional Animal Ethical Committee of Cochin University of Science and Technology (Ref: 363/Go/Re/S/01/CPCSEA/27).

Sample extraction, identification, and in silico characterization of the peptide

Total RNA isolation of the samples

The surgical instruments used for RNA isolation were treated with DEPC (Di Ethyl Pyro Carbonate) and made RNase-free. The fish tissues like skin, muscle, gills, fore, and mid gut were extracted and handled under sterile, RNase-free conditions to facilitate the isolation of intact total RNA. TRI® (Total RNA Isolation) reagent (Sigma-Aldrich), which is a mixture of guanidine thiocyanate and phenol in a monophase solution was used to preserve the samples. 1 mL TRI® reagent was used to preserve the extracted tissues which amounted to approx. 10 % of the volume of reagent used. The preserved samples were stored at −80 °C and subjected to total RNA isolation following the manufacturer's (Sigma-Aldrich) protocol. The precipitated RNA was dissolved in 25 µL of RNase-free water, and its quantity and quality were assessed using spectrophotometry and 0.8 % agarose gel electrophoresis. Samples with an absorbance ratio (A260/A280) in the range of 1.8–1.9 and showing a distinct electrophoretogram were selected for cDNA synthesis and subsequent downstream analysis.

Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, and PCR amplification

cDNA was synthesized as a 20 µL reaction mixture containing M-MLV reverse transcriptase, RT buffer, dNTPs, oligo d(T20), RNase inhibitor, and 5 µg total RNA. The synthesized cDNA with a concentration of 5 µg/µL was further diluted to a concentration of 1 µg/µL for subsequent screening studies. The efficiency of cDNA synthesis was confirmed by screening for the presence of a reference gene. Three reference genes, EL-1α, β-actin, and 18S RNA were screened for, and the one with a consistent expression profile was marked for further analysis. For the present study, the targeted gene was Histone H2A-derived AMP, and screening was performed using gene-specific forward and reverse primers (F: 5′-atgtccggrmgmggsaarac-3′ and R: 5′-gggatgatgcgmgtcttcttgtt-3′) [16]. PCR reactions were conducted in a 10 µL volume using EmeraldAmp® GT PCR Master Mix (Takara Bio Inc., Japan). The amplification progressed through denaturation, annealing (at 60 °C), and elongation steps. The amplified product was resolved on a 1.5 % agarose gel aligning with a 100 bp marker and visualized using the Gel Doc XR system (Syngene, Australia).

Sequencing

The amplified product after PCR was treated with the Exo-CIP Rapid PCR clean-up kit (NEB) to remove the residual PCR primers and to dephosphorylate excess dNTPs present after the amplification. The purified product was sequenced using the gene-specific primers on an ABI Prism 377 DNA sequencer by Sanger dideoxy sequencing at AgriGenome Sequencing Facility, India.

In silico physicochemical characterization of the histone H2A-derived peptide

The in silico physicochemical characterization of histone H2A-derived peptide from L. calcarifer used for the present gene expression study has been done earlier and reported [20]. To build upon this research, the present study aimed to characterize the GIFT histone H2A-derived AMP and investigate its functionality along with the sea bass histone H2A-derived AMP. The nucleotide sequence obtained from sequencing was translated using the ExPASy tool to obtain the corresponding amino acid sequence. The BLAST algorithm was employed to search for sequence homology in the NCBI database and to determine its statistical significance [21]. The ProtParam tool was used to compute the physical and chemical parameters of the peptide. ProP 1.0 and SignalP5.0 were utilized to predict propeptide cleavage sites and the presence of signal peptide if any, respectively. The Antimicrobial Peptide Database (APD3) provided insights into the GRAVY score, Wimley-White whole-residue hydrophobicity, molecular weight, and protein-binding potential (Boman index) of the peptide. The SMART tool was employed to identify genetically mobile domains and peptide architecture. The SWISS-MODEL server [22] predicted the three-dimensional structure of the peptide, and the generated PDB file was visualized using the PYMOL software. The Ramachandran plot and PROCHECK software analyzed the geometric properties of the homology model [23,24]. The HeliQuest online tool predicted the helical wheel structure of the peptide, while the Kyte & Doolittle plot of Protscale depicted its hydropathicity.

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis of the histone H2A-derived peptide from GIFT

To explore the evolutionary relationships and conservation of histone H2A-derived peptide, the sequence obtained from the present study, and those identified from various fish species, vertebrates (e.g., guinea pig, birds, cow), invertebrates (e.g., molluscs, crustaceans), and plants were retrieved from GenBank. Multiple sequence alignment was conducted using the ClustalW algorithm with the MEGA X software to identify conserved regions and determine the level of sequence conservation. The phylogenetic analysis of the aligned sequences was carried out by the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) statistical method with 1000 bootstrap replications. This analysis allowed for the construction of a phylogenetic tree, providing insights into the evolutionary relationships among the histone H2A-derived peptides across different organisms.

Computational insights into the functional attributes of the histone H2A-derived peptide

The in silico functional characterization of the histone H2A-derived peptide was done involving various online tools. Properties of the peptide were explored through screening platforms dedicated to antiviral, antibacterial, antifungal, and antiangiogenic activities, like AVPpred, AntiBP2, AntiFP, and AntiAngioPred. Hemolytic activity prediction was conducted using the HAPPENN tool [25], while the dPABBs web server [26] assessed its anti-biofilm activity. The Codon Adaptation Index (CAI) calculator [27] evaluated codon usage bias and PSIPRED 4.0 [28] predicted the secondary structure and its transmembrane topology of the peptide. The FFPred software [29] enabled the prediction of gene ontology domains associated with the peptide. These computational analyses provide insights into potential functions like activity against pathogens, hemolytic properties, biofilm disruption capability, codon usage bias, secondary structure, transmembrane topology, and putative functional domains according to gene ontology.

In vivo bacterial challenge

Experimental design and pathogenicity in vivo assay

The experimental design incorporated rigorous measures to accurately assess the immunological role of the peptide in case of bacterial infections in the selected host fish species (O. niloticus and L. calcarifer). Throughout the experiment, utmost care was taken to maintain optimal water quality parameters, such as temperature, pH, and salinity, creating a conducive environment for the fish. For the study, the bacterial pathogen V. vulnificus was cultured until the logarithmic phase, and its density was determined using spectrophotometry. To establish a reliable model for infection, a 15 % saline bacterial cell suspension of V. vulnificus was prepared following the Miles & Misra method [30] to achieve the desired number of colony-forming units (CFU/ml). The intraperitoneal injection (i.p.) technique was employed to ensure the precise delivery of a controlled volume of the V. vulnificus suspension. Triplicate sets of experimental and control tanks, containing ten fish each, were set up to ensure statistical rigor and accuracy for O. niloticus and L. calcarifer. After the acclimatization period, each fish was i.p. injected with 125 µl of the saline cell suspension of V. vulnificus, containing 1.99×102 bacterial cells. The control groups were injected with sterile saline. Throughout the experiment, mortality, abnormal behavior, and other clinical symptoms were monitored and recorded. To minimize stress and discomfort to the fish, anesthesia was administered during experimental procedures. Sampling was carried out with utmost precision, collecting triplicate samples from both the experimental and control groups at specific time intervals post-infection (0, 3, 6, 9, 24, and 48 h) for both L. calcarifer and O. niloticus. The sampled fish were transferred to an anesthetic bath containing 0.2 ml clove oil per 500 ml of water, ensuring a highly controlled and precise concentration. Once the fish exhibited a gradual slowing down of movements and sank to the bottom of the bath, they were carefully transferred to the laboratory for tissue extraction. This approach to handling the samples ensured the preservation of sample integrity and the reliability of further downstream analyses.

Quantitative gene expression analysis using real-time PCR

After extracting total RNA from the samples, the RNA was quantified and quality estimated. The triplicate samples were then pooled and cDNA synthesized. qPCR was conducted using specific real-time PCR primers designed for histone H2A-derived AMP (F: ctcgtgccgggcttcagttc; R: gttgccagccagctccagga). The quantitative time course analysis encompassed all experimental samples collected at various time points following the challenge, including the unchallenged control samples from both L. calcarifer and O. niloticus. Alongside the gene of interest, the reference genes β-actin, 18S rRNA, and EL-1α were also quantified and the one with consistent gene expression was selected for this analysis. Technical triplicates were quantified and the relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. A graph on temporal variation of relative expression of histone H2A-derived peptide upon V. vulnificus challenge with the calculated standard error bars was plotted.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 22 software was utilized to perform one-way ANOVA and Tukey post-hoc tests to compare the gene expression patterns of interest in L. calcarifer and O. niloticus, post-challenge V. vulnificus. The statistical significance of the expression pattern of the three replicates analysed was determined at p < 0.05.

Results

BLAST analysis of both the nucleotide and amino acid sequence demonstrated significant alignment with the histone H2A mRNA of O. niloticus. Consequently, the sequence was submitted to GenBank, under the designation O. niloticus histone H2A-derived peptide with the accession number MZ422436, for convenience, the peptide is referred to as GIFT-Histone in this study (Fig. 1). The AMP database APD3 provided several predictions and characteristics of the GIFT-Histone peptide. According to APD3 prediction, the peptide has a hydrophobic ratio of 42 % and a net charge of +6.25. The Grand Average hydropathy value (GRAVY) was calculated to be −0.16875, and the Wimley-White whole residue hydrophobicity of the peptide was determined to be 15.41. The amino acid composition analysis revealed that Ala is the most abundant amino acid, accounting for 16 % of the peptide, followed by Leu and Arg at 13 % each. The computed molecular weight of GIFT-Histone was 6.99 kDa, and its protein-binding potential, as measured by the Boman index, was 1.77 kcal/mol. APD3 predicted that the peptide had a chance to interact with membranes and hence an antimicrobial agent, with a possibility of forming alpha helices where 16 of its residues are located on the same hydrophobic surface. When aligned in APD3, GIFT-Histone exhibited 48.78 % similarity to histone H2A from the hemocytes of the Pacific white shrimp Litopenaeus vannamei (AP02804), which is known to have activity against Gram-positive bacteria. The ProtParam tool of ExPASy predicted the theoretical isoelectric point (pI) of GIFT-Histone as 0.57. The estimated half-life of the peptide was 1 hr in mammalian reticulocytes (in vitro) and 2 min in yeast and E. coli (in vivo). The aliphatic index provides information about protein thermostability and was computed to be 105.31, which indicates relatively high stability. However, the instability index of 44.20 suggests that the peptide may be prone to degradation. The Codon Adaptation Index (CAI) values of GIFT-Histone were calculated for selected expression systems, including Drosophila melanogaster (0.73), E. coli (0.65), and Pichia pastoris (yeast) (0.54). Among these systems, Drosophila and E. coli showed above-average values for synonymous codon usage in heterologous expression systems, indicating their potential for efficient expression of the peptide. Additionally, the dPABBs webserver predicted that the GIFT-Histone peptide exhibits biofilm activity, with an SVM score of 0.86.

The N-terminus of GIFT-Histone has the histone H2A signature motif A5GLQFPV11, and the PSIPRED tool predicted a transmembrane region (V33YLAAVLEYLTAEILE48) within the helical C-terminal end of the peptide which corresponded to the AGGRESCAN-predicted hot spot at V33-L49. To generate a three-dimensional protein structure, the SWISS-MODEL web server performed automated homology modeling based on the histone H2A type 1 5mlu.1.C template, which exhibited 100 % sequence identity. The predicted structure revealed two helical regions, a smaller one near the N-terminus and a longer helix near the C-terminus, connected by coil regions. The generated structure was visualized using the PYMOL software (Fig. 3a). The tertiary structure was assessed using the PROCHECK program, which generated the Ramachandran plot indicating that 96.3 % of the residues were in the favored region, with the remaining 3.7 % in the additional allowed regions. Since the percentage of residues in the most favored region exceeded 90 %, the model was considered to have good quality (Fig. 3b). The helical wheel structure of GIFT-Histone revealed a hydrophobic face consisting of a 10-residue sequence (ALLLGIGLVP) (Fig. 4a). The Kyte & Doolittle plot predicted a hydropathic region toward the C-terminus (Fig. 4b). Transcriptional response of histone H2A-derived antimicrobial peptide in L. calcarifer and O. niloticus during V. vulnificus infection

The transcriptional response of Histone H2A-derived AMPs, namely Lc-Histone and GIFT-Histone, to V. vulnificus infection, was investigated by quantitative real-time PCR. No significant mortality events were observed throughout the study, allowing for a focused analysis of the intricate expression patterns exhibited by the histone H2A-derived AMP.

GIFT-Histone exhibited distinct temporal dynamics in its expression profile following infection (Fig. 5a). A gradual rise in the expression profile of the peptide was noted throughout the sampling hours with a peak expression manifesting at the 9th hr with a 7.69-fold increase. After which again a decreasing trend was noted till the 48th hr of sampling (Fig. 5a). A dynamic mode of expression was pointed out throughout the post-challenge hours with a definite pattern, but an unexpected slight dip was noted during the 6th hr post-challenge. Rigorous statistical analysis employing one-way ANOVA revealed significant differences between different time durations of the expression patterns (p ≤ 0.05). Discussion

Histones, highly conserved proteins involved in DNA transcription and host defense mechanisms, have been found to produce histone H2A-derived antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) with potent antimicrobial activity across different species. Previous studies have emphasized the therapeutic potential of AMPs, including histone-derived peptides, like Buforin I [31,32], hipposin [19], and piscidins [33] due to their remarkable antimicrobial properties [34]. Histone-derived peptides from various fish species, such as Atlantic salmon, catfish, and olive flounder, have demonstrated effective antibacterial activity even at low concentrations [[35], [36], [37], [38], [39]]. Katzenback, 2015 has noted the wide anti-bacterial activity spectrum of histone-derived peptides from teleost fish [40]. Additionally, histone-derived fragments in frogs have been shown to have antimicrobial functions [41]. Histones also play a crucial role in neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) - mediated immunity, an innate immune response against pathogens, where histones, DNA, and antimicrobial proteins form NETs [42,43]. Similar structures resembling extracellular traps (ETs) containing DNA, histones, and antimicrobial proteins have been observed in fish species such as Pimephales promelas, carp, rainbow trout, and Oreochromis niloticus [[44], [45], [46], [47], [48]]. These findings highlight the significance of histones and their derived AMPs in host defense mechanisms. This study aimed to characterize the Histone H2A-derived AMPs and conduct bioinformatic analyses to gain insights into their structural and functional properties. Furthermore, the transcriptional response of these AMPs in two aquaculturally significant teleosts Lates calcarifer, and O. niloticus during Vibrio vulnificus infection was also investigated. The bioinformatics data, combined with in vivo studies, support further application-oriented research.

Bioinformatic analysis revealed conserved histone sequence motifs in histone H2A-derived AMPs across various species, including a highly conserved motif (AGLQFPV) and a conserved region (RLLR) towards the N-terminus. Phylogenetic analysis showed the evolutionary conservation of GIFT-Histone with the vertebrates (Fig. 2b). The hydrophilic nature and 42 % hydrophobic ratio of GIFT-Histone, as indicated by its negative GRAVY value, contribute to its potent antibacterial activity, even against multidrug-resistant pathogens [49]. Moreover, the SMART prediction revealed a conserved H2A domain throughout GIFT-Histone, highlighting its functional and lineage significance. The dPABBS webserver predicted GIFT-Histone to be biofilm-active. Most AMPs exhibited preventive or inhibitory effects on biofilm synthesis [50], and considering the importance of biofilms as causative agents of infections, the biofilm-active properties could be manipulated. Additionally, the above-average Codon Adaptation Indexing (CAI) values for GIFT-Histone in E. coli and Drosophila suggest its suitability for efficient protein expression in heterologous systems, opening up possibilities for downstream applications.

The in vivo study further explored the potential of histone H2A-derived AMP in combating V. vulnificus infection. The results provided valuable insights into the dynamic interplay between these AMPs and the bacterial challenge, shedding light on the host defense mechanisms against V. vulnificus. In both L. calcarifer and O. niloticus, the expression profiles of the AMP exhibited notable temporal dynamics of gene expression highlighting the intricate regulation of these AMPs in response to the pathogen following infection. The fluctuations in histone H2A-derived peptide expression suggest a complex regulatory network involved in the immune response system of both fishes and the transcriptomic profile indicated a peak upregulation at the 6th & 9th hr, after infection. The expression exhibited a generally upregulated and further declining pattern, peaking at the 6th and 9th hr. The observed downregulation at intermittent 6th hour in the case of O. niloticus suggests a potential modulation of the immune response as the infection progresses and a possible biological delay in the recruitment of immune molecules. The immunological property of histone H2A-derived AMPs, manifested in gene expression and its regulation in response to V. vulnificus infection, has not been reported previously. Additionally, this study on the transcriptomic expression pattern of histone H2A-derived AMPs in O. niloticus and L. calcarifer is groundbreaking. The upregulation of AMP expression post-infection can be attributed to the immunological property of histone H2A-derived AMPs, where the innate immune mechanism is triggered upon infection. The slight dip in expression at a specific hour post-infection may be due to disparities in the recruitment of cells producing peptides, differences in the transcription level of the gene, or apoptosis of the concerned cells [51]. The epigenetic process can also be considered a factor that modulates gene expression at the transcriptional level during immune responses [52]. The significant variation observed between the means of Lc-Histone and GIFT-Histone, as well as the expressions at different time intervals in the same fish, confirms the species-specific and unpredictable variation in the immunological response. However, the fact that histone H2A-derived AMP expression is triggered post-infection confirms its immunological role in fish health.

These findings provide valuable insights into the immunological role of histone H2A-derived AMPs in fish health. The upregulation of AMP expression post-infection indicates their involvement in the innate immune response against pathogens. Given the challenges posed by diseases and mortality in aquaculture, understanding the immunological potential of histone-derived AMPs is crucial. The observed temporal dynamics in the transcriptional response of Histone H2A-derived AMPs in both L. calcarifer and O. niloticus highlight the dynamic nature of the host immune response to bacterial infection. The significant upregulation of AMP expression indicates their essential role in combating V. vulnificus and underscores their potential as antimicrobial defense molecules.

V. vulnificus is a significant pathogen in aquaculture and poses a threat to human health. The antimicrobial activity of histone H2A-derived AMPs, including their effectiveness against multidrug-resistant pathogens, suggests their potential as therapeutic agents. Further studies are required to elucidate the modes of action of these AMPs and determine the optimal concentrations for antimicrobial activity. Thus, this study provides novel insights into the transcriptional response of histone H2A-derived AMPs in L. calcarifer and O. niloticus during V. vulnificus infection. The findings contribute to our understanding of the immunological mechanisms in fish and highlight the potential of histone H2A-derived AMPs as candidates for developing antimicrobial therapeutics in aquaculture and beyond.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides comprehensive insights into the immunological roles and antimicrobial potential of histone H2A-derived antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) within the context of Vibrio vulnificus infection in Lates calcarifer and Oreochromis niloticus, highlighting their adaptability in diverse culture systems. The comprehensive analysis of gene expression dynamics has revealed distinct temporal patterns, underscoring the active involvement of these AMPs in the innate immune response against the pathogen. Moreover, the observed evolutionary conservation of histone H2A-derived AMPs emphasizes their promising potential as effective therapeutic agents in aquaculture, offering a solution to challenges posed by antibiotic resistance and biosecurity concerns in both freshwater and marine culture systems. Further in-depth investigations are warranted to unravel the precise mechanisms of action underlying the antimicrobial efficacy of these peptides. Additionally, determining the optimal dosage and application methods for maximizing their antimicrobial effectiveness in different culture systems will be crucial in harnessing their full therapeutic potential. This study significantly contributes to our understanding of the intricate interplay between AMPs and bacterial challenges, offering valuable insights into the intricate host defense mechanisms prevalent in aquaculture settings. The findings hold promise for the development of innovative strategies aimed at enhancing the overall health and sustainability of diverse aquaculture practices.
