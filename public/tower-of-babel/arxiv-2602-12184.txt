One-, two-, and three-dimensional photon femtoscopy
D. Miśkowiec, K. Reygers

Abstract
Femtoscopy with photon pairs is a particularly attractive tool for studying high-energy nuclear collisions. Proposed and extensively discussed in several influential theory articles, it has seen only few applications in experiment because of statistics limitations. With the progress of detectors and electronics, only now it is coming within reach. In this paper we discuss the choice of kinematic variables for two-photon correlation functions. In particular, we argue against C ⁡ ( Q inv ) C(Q_{\rm inv}) and in favor of C ⁡ ( Δ ​ E , Q inv ) C(\Delta E,Q_{\rm inv}) .



I
Introduction

Femtoscopy, the analysis of momentum correlations between pairs of particles,
is a powerful and well established technique for assessing the geometry of
particle-emitting systems created in energetic hadronic and nuclear
collisions
Lisa:2005dd
.
It is most commonly applied to pions, for which the
correlations are dominated by the Bose-Einstein (BE) enhancement of pairs
of identical bosons with small relative momenta.
The width of the BE peak located at zero relative momentum is inversely
proportional to the width of the distribution of pions of a given momentum
at the point of their last scattering (kinetic freeze-out).
This, along with momentum spectra, gives access to the final stage of
the collision.

Even more valuable information can be obtained from the analysis of direct photons,
which are produced mostly at the beginning of the collision and do not scatter
subsequently.
Unfortunately, these photons are buried under a background of
photons coming from the decays of hadrons, mostly
\pi^{0}
. The decay photons
dilute the correlation function, reducing the amplitude of the BE peak by
a factor of
f^{2}
, where
f
is the fraction of direct photons.
One could argue that this does not spoil the measurement of the geometry
(width of the peak), and the reduction of the peak height can even be used
to measure the direct-photon yield, complementing the standard method
based on the cocktail subtraction
WA98:2000vxl
. The price for this, however,
is the necessity to deal with large statistical uncertainties coming from the
background photons.

In this situation, the choice of the data representation is particularly important.
In the experimental photon femtoscopy studies published so far the invariant momentum
difference
Q_{\rm inv}
(see below for definition), well established in identical-hadron
femtoscopy, has been adopted. Our central claim is that the one-dimensional correlation
function C(
Q_{\rm inv}
) is not optimal for photon femtoscopy and should be replaced by a
two-dimensional analysis.

II
Photon femtoscopy in theory

The Bose-Einstein correlations between photons have been the subject of several
seminal theoretical articles.
In Ref.
Neuhauser:1986ija
by Neuhauser, the two-photon correlation functions
are considered in two dimensions, as a function of their opening angle and the
energy difference.
Sinyukov
Sinyukov:1989xz
discussed a longitudinally expanding source
of photons and analyzed the correlation function in transverse (to the beam
axis) and longitudinal momentum difference.
Srivastava and Kapusta
Srivastava:1993js
studied the shape of the two-photon
correlation functions vs the rapidity and transverse momentum differences as
well as the azimuthal opening angle.
In the subsequent papers
Srivastava:1993pt
;
Srivastava:1994ab

they switched to the Bertsch-Pratt
Bertsch:1989vn
;
Pratt:1986cc

coordinates, which around that time entered the scene and became the de facto
standard axes in pion femtoscopy.
The relative momentum here is decomposed into

{\bf q}={\bf p}_{2}-{\bf p}_{1}=(q_{\rm out},q_{\rm side},q_{\rm long})\ ,

(1)

with the “out” axis pointing along the pair
transverse momentum, the “side” axis perpendicular to it in the transverse
plane, and the “long” axis along the beam.
This three-dimensional coordinate system was also used by Bass, Muller, and
Srivastava
Bass:2004de
, Frodermann and Heinz
Frodermann:2009nx
,
and Alam et al.
Alam:2003gx
. The latter article also shows
one-dimensional correlation functions
C(Q_{\rm inv})
,

Q_{\rm inv}=\sqrt{-(p_{2}^{\mu}-p_{1}^{\mu})^{2}}\,,

(2)

albeit under the condition that
q_{\rm out}=q_{\rm long}=0
.

While the two-particle correlation functions of identical and nonidentical
hadrons exhibit structures coming from quantum statistics and Coulomb and
strong interactions at small relative velocities (two particles
“flying together” in the laboratory),
for photon pairs the situation is different. The photon velocity in vacuum
does not depend on the photon energy and thus any two photons emitted close
to each other in space-time and in the same direction “fly together” even
if they differ in energy.
In this situation one might wonder if the BE enhancement occurs also for
such pairs.
Intuition suggests that this should not be the case, and indeed in all the
above theory papers the BE peak is restricted to small
q_{\rm out}
.

III
Photon femtoscopy in experiment

The experimental data on two-photon Bose-Einstein correlations are scarce.
The first analysis was performed on data collected in heavy-ion collisions
at 40–60 MeV per nucleon (i.e. well below the
\pi^{0}
production threshold)
by TAPS at GANIL
Ostendorf:1992ah
;
Marques:1997sw
.
The correlation function was represented as
C(q,q_{0})
, with
q_{0}

defined as the energy difference
q_{0}=\Delta E=E_{2}-E_{1}
. In order to reduce
the statistical uncertainties, the
q_{0}
dependence was dropped,
arguing that the energy spectrum of direct photons is exponential,
hence
q_{0}
small, and thus the averaging over
q_{0}
does not destroy the
signal. A significant fraction of photons originated from decays of
subthreshold
\pi^{0}
, as clearly seen in the invariant mass spectrum.
Since for photons
Q_{\rm inv}=M_{\rm inv}
, for the sake of keeping the
\pi^{0}

peak narrow the authors went one step further and switched from
C(q)

to
C(Q_{\rm inv})
.
The same representation was used in a subsequent centrality
dependent analysis of the same collision system with increased
statistics
Piasecki:2006yx
.

At the SPS, the WA98 collaboration published a two-photon
C(Q_{\rm inv})

measured in central Pb–Pb collisions
WA98:2003ukc
;
Peresunko:2004jt
;
Peressounko:2004ha
.
The fitted correlation strength (Bose-Einstein peak height)
\lambda

was used to calculate the direct-photon fraction.

The STAR collaboration at RHIC presented a preliminary
C(Q_{\rm inv})
from Au-Au
collisions in the QM2005 conference
Das:2005qz
.
In QM2006, the PHENIX collaboration showed their preliminary results
Peressounko:2007mp
.
Also in this case
C(Q_{\rm inv})
has been used.

IV
Abscissa: choice of the kinematic variables

As shown above, the theory predictions and the experimental measurements
of two-photon correlation functions so far were performed in terms of

C(q_{\rm out},q_{\rm side},q_{\rm long})
and
C(Q_{\rm inv})
, respectively.
The measurements were plagued by statistical uncertainties resulting from
small number of events at GANIL and from
\pi^{0}
decay background at the
SPS and above.
The representation chosen by experimentalists,
C(Q_{\rm inv})
, keeps the

\pi^{0}
peak narrow and exposes the flat part of the correlation function
between the Bose-Einstein peak and
\pi^{0}
peak, facilitating the
normalization of the correlation function
Marques:1997sw
.
Let us look more closely at the meaning of
Q_{\rm inv}
.
We start from the quantities measured in the experiment, the photon momenta
(i.e. energies)
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
and the pair opening angle
\alpha
.
The invariant momentum difference
Q_{\rm inv}
coincides with the invariant mass
and is equal to

\displaystyle Q_{\rm inv}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\,p_{1}\,p_{2}\,(1-\cos\alpha)}

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\sqrt{p_{1}\,p_{2}}\,\sin\frac{\alpha}{2}\ .

(4)

In the limits of collinear and back-to-back pairs it becomes

Q_{\rm inv}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{p_{1}p_{2}}\,\alpha\,,&for $\alpha\rightarrow 0$ ,\\
2\,\sqrt{p_{1}p_{2}}\,=2p\,,&for $\alpha\rightarrow\pi$ .\end{cases}

(5)

As one can see,
Q_{\rm inv}
is not directly sensitive to the energy
difference between the two photons.
For pairs of massive identical particles a vanishing
Q_{\rm inv}
implies
{\bf p}_{1}={\bf p}_{2}
.
For photons it does not.

Q_{\rm inv}
mostly reflects
q_{\rm side}
and
q_{\rm long}
and is only weakly related to
q_{\rm out}
.
This means that the Bose-Einstein peak, which sits at
q_{\rm out}=q_{\rm side}=q_{\rm long}=0
,
in
C(Q_{\rm inv})
is diluted by uncorrelated pairs with large
q_{\rm out}
.
This deficiency of
C(Q_{\rm inv})
has been noticed
(Refs.
WA98:2003ukc
;
Peresunko:2004jt
;
Peressounko:2004ha
;
Peressounko:2007mp
;
Peressounko:2005ce
;
see also Eq. (38) in Ref.
Lisa:2005dd
) and
the resulting reduction of the peak amplitude
\lambda
was estimated
and corrected for by performing a simulation accounting for the detector
acceptance, the shape of the photon spectrum, and the assumed

R_{\rm out}

Peressounko:2005ce
.
The direct photon yield published by WA98
WA98:2003ukc
was
presented for two cases,
R_{\rm out}=0
and
R_{\rm out}=6
fm.
The magnitude of this effect increases with the collision energy (see Appendix).

Preserving the full height of the BE peak and at the same time keeping
the
\pi^{0}
peak narrow is possible in a two-dimensional analysis

C(\Delta E,Q_{\rm inv})\ .

(6)

The additional axis represents the energy difference between the two
photons
\Delta E=p_{2}-p_{1}
.
It is easy to show that the length of the vector
(\Delta E,Q_{\rm inv})
is equal to
q

\Delta E^{2}+Q_{\rm inv}^{2}=q_{\rm out}^{2}+q_{\rm side}^{2}+q_{\rm long}^{2}\ .

(7)

The representation (
6
) is similar to Ref.
Neuhauser:1986ija

except the opening angle is replaced by
Q_{\rm inv}
, and similar to
Ref.
Marques:1997sw
but with
q
replaced by
Q_{\rm inv}
.
The suppression of the BE peak and the systematic uncertainty related to its
correction are absent in this case.

Our representation (
6
) is closely related to

C(q_{\rm out},\sqrt{q_{\rm side}^{2}+q_{\rm long}^{2}})\ .

(8)

In order to demonstrate this, we first note that the components of
the momentum difference
\mathbf{q}
parallel and perpendicular to the pair
momentum
\mathbf{K}:=(\mathbf{p}_{1}+\mathbf{p}_{2})/2
are

q_{||}=\frac{(\mathbf{p}_{2}-\mathbf{p}_{1})\cdot(\mathbf{p}_{1}+\mathbf{p}_{2})}{2\,K}=\frac{p_{2}^{2}-p_{1}^{2}}{2\,K}

(9)

and

q_{\perp}=\frac{(\mathbf{p}_{2}-\mathbf{p}_{1})\times(\mathbf{p}_{1}+\mathbf{p}_{2})}{2\,K}=\frac{p_{1}\,p_{2}\,\sin\alpha}{K}\ .

(10)

Secondly, we eliminate
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
,
\alpha
and get the direct relation
between
(\Delta E,Q_{\rm inv})
and
(q_{||},q_{\perp})
:

\displaystyle Q_{\rm inv}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[q^{2}-4K^{2}+\sqrt{(q^{2}+4K^{2})^{2}-(4Kq_{||})^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\Delta E

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2Kq_{||}}{\sqrt{Q_{\rm inv}^{2}+4K^{2}}}

(11)

with
q^{2}=q_{||}^{2}+q_{\perp}^{2}=q_{\rm out}^{2}+q_{\rm side}^{2}+q_{\rm long}^{2}
.
The inverse transformation reads

\displaystyle q_{||}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta E\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{Q_{\rm inv}}{2K}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle q_{\perp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{\rm inv}\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{\Delta E}{2K}\right)^{2}}\ .

(12)

Third, in the longitudinally comoving coordinate system (LCMS), commonly used in
femtoscopy
Lisa:2005dd
, the pair momentum
{\bf K}
is perpendicular
to the beam direction,
K=K_{\rm T}
, and

\displaystyle q_{||}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q_{\rm out}\ ,

\displaystyle q_{\perp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{q_{\rm side}^{2}+q_{\rm long}^{2}}\ .

(13)

The resulting relation between the
(\Delta E,Q_{\rm inv})
coordinates and
(q_{\rm out},\sqrt{q_{\rm side}^{2}+q_{\rm long}^{2}})

in LCMS is shown in Fig.
1
.
In the region below 0.1 GeV/
c
, where the Bose-Einstein peak is expected to
be located for direct photons from heavy-ion collisions, the two sets of
variables are very close to each other.

In order to make a more quantitative statement, let us look at the shape of
the BE peak in both representations.
We start from a Gaussian in
C(q_{\rm out},q_{\rm side},q_{\rm long})

Frodermann:2009nx
:

C=1+\lambda\exp\left(-R_{\rm out}^{2}q_{\rm out}^{2}-R_{\rm side}^{2}q_{\rm side}^{2}-R_{\rm long}^{2}q_{\rm long}^{2}\right)\,.

(14)

Its parameters can be directly extracted from a fit to
C(\Delta E,Q_{\rm inv})
:

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\lambda\exp\left(-R_{\rm out}^{2}\Delta E^{2}-R_{\perp}^{2}Q_{\rm inv}^{2}\right)\times

(15)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[\left(R_{\perp}^{2}-R_{\rm out}^{2}\right)\left(\frac{\Delta E\ Q_{\rm inv}}{2K_{\rm T}}\right)^{2}\right]\ .

Here,
R_{\perp}
is a weighted mean of
R_{\rm side}
and
R_{\rm long}
, with the weight
factors depending on the experiment’s acceptance and two-photon resolution.
The exponent in the second line is close to unity for
q\ll K_{\rm T}
and thus fitting
the BE peak in
C(\Delta E,Q_{\rm inv})
is practically equivalent to fitting it in

C(q_{\rm out},\sqrt{q_{\rm side}^{2}+q_{\rm long}^{2}})
, as already suggested by Fig.
1
.

Around midrapidity,
an additional practical advantage of the representation (
6
) is that
the experimental effects related to the determination of the photon momentum
and the opening angle act nearly separately on the two dimensions of the correlation
function. The finite opening-angle resolution affects only
Q_{\rm inv}
, and
\Delta E
is
mostly affected by the energy resolution.

Similarly, correlated background may manifest mostly in one of the two dimensions.
Not only the
\pi^{0}
decay but also anisotropic flow and, for calorimeters,
photon conversion and cluster splitting will result in background pairs with small
opening angles but not necessarily close in energy.

At this point we would like to mention an intermediate stage between

C(\Delta E,Q_{\rm inv})
and
C(q_{\rm out},\sqrt{q_{\rm side}^{2}+q_{\rm long}^{2}})
, namely

C(q_{\rm out},Q_{\rm inv})\ .

(16)

This representation spares the transformation between
\Delta E
and
q_{\rm out}
.
It is, however, less elegant in that only at low
\Delta E
and
Q_{\rm inv}
the vector
(\Delta E,0)
is orthogonal to
(0,Q_{\rm inv})
and the length
is preserved as in Eq. (
7
).

V
Ordinate: statistical uncertainties in various representations

The tendency to reduce the number of dimensions of the correlation function
is common in experiments encountering large statistical uncertainties.
Reducing the point-to-point scatter of the correlation function, be it by
reducing the number of dimensions or by increasing the bin width, has indeed
a great positive impact on the visual appearance of the correlation.
However, it does not lead to a more precise fit.
(If anything, an overly coarse binning can distort the fit result.)
A properly performed fit – one based on the maximum likelihood method, assuming
the Poissonian distribution of the number of counts, and taking its standard
deviation not from the numerator but from the denominator multiplied by the
fit function – can perfectly well deal with low numbers of counts per bin,
including empty bins
E877:1996zlh
;
E802:2002fwe
.
One-dimensional plots of the correlation function appropriate for visual
inspection can be constructed as usual by building a ratio between the
projections of the numerator and denominator.
The fit function will be visualized by plotting the projection of the denominator
multiplied by the fit, divided by the projection of the denominator.
Whenever such projections are made, cuts on the other dimensions will be applied
such as to maximize the BE signal.
Following these standard procedures, the additional dimension in
C(\Delta E,Q_{\rm inv})
,
compared to
C(Q_{\rm inv})
, will come at no cost.

VI
Conclusion

We advocate using the two-dimensional correlation function
C(\Delta E,Q_{\rm inv})
in
LCMS for analysis of Bose-Einstein correlations of photons in heavy-ion collisions.
This representation avoids the dilution of the BE peak happening when
the one-dimensional
C(Q_{\rm inv})
is used. At the same time it preserves
the advantage of the latter, namely it keeps the
\pi^{0}
peak narrow.
The fit precision will not be affected by the additional dimension.
