Integral Representations of Sobolev Spaces via ReLU k Activation Function and Optimal Error Estimates for Linearized Networks
Xinliang Liu, Tong Mao, Jinchao Xu

Abstract
This paper presents two main theoretical results concerning shallow neural networks with ReLU k activation functions. We establish a novel integral representation for Sobolev spaces, showing that every function in ℋ d + 2 ​ k + 1 2 ​ ( Ω ) \mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega) can be expressed as an ℒ 2 \mathcal{L}^{2} -weighted integral of ReLU k ridge functions over the unit sphere. This result mirrors the known representation of Barron spaces and highlights a fundamental connection between Sobolev regularity and neural network representations. Moreover, we prove that linearized shallow networks—constructed by fixed inner parameters and optimizing only the linear coefficients—achieve optimal approximation rates 𝒪 ⁡ ( n − 1 2 − 2 ​ k + 1 2 ​ d ) \mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2k+1}{2d}}) in Sobolev spaces.

Introduction

Deep neural networks (DNNs) are at the core of numerous breakthroughs in artificial intelligence
[
46
]
, providing a powerful framework for solving complex problems. The efficiency of deep learning depends on several critical factors, including expressive power, generalization ability, training efficiency, and robustness.

Mathematically, a deep neural network is a specialized class of functions constructed through an iterative composition of shallow neural networks. Thus, the following shallow neural networks function class serves as a fundamental building block of artificial intelligence technology:

\Sigma_{n}^{\sigma}=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\sigma\big(w_{j}\cdot x+b_{j}\big):~w_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~a_{j},b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

(1.1)

Among various activation functions, the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) has emerged as the dominant choice in modern deep learning. In this paper, we focus on ReLU activation function,
\mbox{ReLU}(x)=\max(0,x)
, and its variants,
\sigma_{k}(x)=\mbox{ReLU}^{k}(x)
(
k
is a nonnegative integer). Due to the homogeneity of
\sigma_{k}
, we assume the parameters lie on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. The corresponding function class of shallow ReLU
k
neural networks, denoted by
\Sigma_{n}^{k}=\Sigma_{n}^{\sigma_{k}}
, is then

\Sigma_{n}^{k}=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\sigma_{k}\big(\theta_{j}\cdot\tilde{x}\big):~\theta_{j}\in\mathbb{S}^{d},~a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

(1.2)

where
\displaystyle\tilde{x}=\binom{x}{1}
and
\displaystyle\theta_{j}=\binom{w_{j}}{b_{j}}
. To
ensure encodability and numerical stability, it is essential to impose restrictions on the size
of the parameters in a neural network. Consequently, given a parameter
M
, which essentially
determines the complexity of the function class, the stable shallow neural network function class is defined as

\Sigma_{n,M}^{k}=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\sigma_{k}\big(\theta_{j}\cdot\tilde{x}\big):~\theta_{j}\in\mathbb{S}^{d},~\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}|a_{j}|\leq M\right\}.

(1.3)

In the early works, qualitative convergence has been extensively investigated since the 1990s (see, e.g.,
[
19
,
34
]
). A fundamental result states that the universal approximation property holds, namely the functions in
\Sigma_{n}^{k}
can approximate any continuous function, as long as the activation function
\sigma
is not a polynomial; see, e.g.,
[
47
]
.

The approximation rate of the shallow neural networks has also been widely studied in the literature. Roughly speaking, these results pertain to three types of function spaces: Barron spaces
\mathcal{B}^{\sigma}(\Omega)
, also known as variation spaces
\mathcal{K}_{1}(\mathbb{D}_{\sigma})
(see (
2.2
) below for definition); spectral Barron space
[
41
,
85
]
; and Sobolev space (see (
2.9
) below for definition).
In most existing works, the following error estimate has been established for the aforementioned function spaces:

\inf_{f_{n}\in\Sigma_{n}^{\sigma}}\|f-f_{n}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}).

(1.4)

A key technique used to establish (
1.4
) can be traced back to
[
75
]
, which applied Maurey’s sampling method from
[
61
]
; see also
[

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

]
. An overview of aforementioned and other related results can be found in
[

,

,

]
.

Several studies have also explored improved approximation rates compared to (
1.4
), namely:

\inf_{f_{n}\in\Sigma_{n}^{\sigma}}\|f-f_{n}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{d}})

(1.5)

for some
\alpha>0
. For Barron spaces, we refer to
[

,

,

,

. For Sobolev spaces, we refer to

.

Notably,

for spectral Barron spaces
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}^{k}(\Omega)
, with further generalizations in
[

. For closer study of
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}^{k}(\Omega)

In the context of shallow ReLU
k
networks, a strong connection exists between Barron spaces
\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)=\mathcal{B}^{\sigma_{k}}(\Omega)
(see (
2.4
)) and the class of shallow networks (
1.3
) (for further details, see Theorem
2.1
):

1.

A function
f
can be approximated by neural network classes
\{\Sigma_{n,M}^{k}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
if and only if
f\in\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)

[
88
]
.

2.

The Barron space
\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)
has an integration form
[
88
]

\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)=\left\{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\tilde{x})d\mu(\theta):~\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{S}^{d})\right\}.

(1.6)

Moreover,

\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)}\simeq\inf\limits_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\left\{|\mu|(\mathbb{S}^{d}):~f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\tilde{x})d\mu(\theta)\right\}.

(1.7)

3.

Shallow ReLU
k
networks achieve the optimal approximation rate in
\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)

[
87
]
:

\inf_{f_{n}\in\Sigma_{n,M}^{k}}\|f-f_{n}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2k+1}{2d}}),

(1.8)

where
M\simeq\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)}
.

The constraint on the parameter bound
M
ensures both encodability and numerical stability, which is crucial in the generalization analysis of machine learning models.

In this paper, we further investigate the approximation properties of shallow ReLU
k
networks. Specifically, we focus on a linear subset of
\Sigma_{n}^{k}
, which we refer to as the finite neuron space (FNS). Given a predetermined set of parameters
\displaystyle\left\{\theta_{j}^{*}\right\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{S}^{d}
, we define the corresponding basis functions

\phi_{j}(x)=\sigma_{k}(\theta_{j}^{*}\cdot\tilde{x})=\sigma_{k}(w_{j}^{*}\cdot x+b_{j}^{*}),\quad j=1,\dots,n,

and the finite neuron space as

L_{n}^{k}=L_{n}^{k}(\left\{\theta_{j}^{*}\right\}_{j=1}^{n})=\mathrm{span}\left\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}\right\}.

(1.9)

Analogous to
\Sigma_{n,M}^{k}
in (
1.3
), we define the constrained version of
L_{n}^{k}
as

L_{n,M}^{k}=\biggl\{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\phi_{j}:~\Bigl(n\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}^{2}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq M\biggr\}.

(1.10)

By applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, it follows that

L_{n,M}^{k}\subset\Sigma_{n,M}^{k}.

(1.11)

A key advantage of the linear spaces
L_{n}^{k}
and
L_{n,M}^{k}
lies in their structural simplicity, which enables more efficient analysis and computation. Notably, optimization over
L_{n,M}^{k}
reduces to a convex least squares problem, whereas training in
\Sigma_{n,M}^{k}
generally involves solving a highly nonconvex optimization problem.

Similar to the results on ReLU
k
neural networks
\Sigma_{n,M}^{k}
, this paper presents a novel perspective on characterizing the finite neuron space
L_{n}^{k}
and its constrained counterpart
L_{n,M}^{k}
. Under the assumption that the parameters
\{\theta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{n}
form a well-distributed mesh over
\mathbb{S}^{d}
(see the precise definition in (
2.10
)), we establish the following results:

1.

A function
f
can be approximated by FNS
\{L_{n,M}^{k}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
if and only if
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)
;

2.

The Sobolev space
\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)
has an integration form

\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)=\left\{\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\tilde{x})\psi(\theta)d\theta:~\psi\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})\right\}.

(1.12)

Moreover,

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)}\simeq\inf\limits_{\psi\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\Bigl\{\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}:f(x)=\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\tilde{x})\psi(\theta)d\theta\Bigr\},

(1.13)

3.

The FNS achieves the optimal approximation rate in
\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)
:

\inf_{f_{n}\in L_{n,M}^{k}}\|f-f_{n}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2k+1}{2d}}),

(1.14)

where
M\simeq\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)}
.

We would like to note that the approximation estimate in (
1.14
) is related to an earlier result by Petrushev
[
73
]
. For the ReLU
k
activation function, the result in
[
73
]
yields to an approximation rate estimate similar to (
1.14
) by using a different construction of the weights. Specifically, it uses the tensor product points
\displaystyle\left\{\binom{w_{j}}{b_{i}}\right\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n_{1}\\
1\leq i\leq n_{2}\end{subarray}}
, where
\{w_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{1}}

are suitable quadrature points on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{2}}
are well-distributed points on
[-1,1]
. As demonstrated in Corollary
2.1
below, our result can be used to directly derive the result in
[
73
]
for the ReLU
k
case. However, it is important to note that our result cannot be derived from those in
[
73
]
, nor can they be obtained using the proof techniques in
[
73
]
. In fact, the proof in our paper differs significantly from that used in
[
73
]
.

Most importantly, our analysis yields the following key coefficient estimate

\sqrt{n}\|a\|_{2}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)}.

(1.15)

As studied in
[
59
]
, the bound on the coefficients such as (
1.15
) is essential in approximation theory in general. For example, it plays a central role in the generalization analysis (see Section
7
). More importantly, without a bound like (
1.15
), approximation error estimates may lose their practical relevance
[
59
]
. In fact,
[
53
]
(see also
[
59
]
) showed that deep neural networks with a fixed number of parameters can achieve universal approximation when the weights are unbounded. Therefore, a bound estimate for coefficients is indispensable for both theoretical and practical applications.

Given the continuous embedding
\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)

[
58
]
, the metric entropy of
\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)
is asymptotically same as that of
\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)
(see (
2.18
)). Thus, linearized shallow networks achieve approximation rates comparable to the nonlinear class
\Sigma_{n,M}^{k}
. Comparing this characterization with
\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)
clarifies which functions are approximable by
\Sigma_{n,M}^{k}
but not by
L_{n,M}^{k}
.

A consequence of (
1.13
) is that for
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)
, there exists
\psi\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
(not necessarily unique) such that

f(x)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(\theta)\sigma_{k}\bigl(\theta\cdot\tilde{x}\bigr)\bigr],

(1.16)

where the expectation is over the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. This identity provides a theoretical foundation for estimating approximation error in neural networks with random parameters—also known as random feature methods (see Section
6
). In particular, if
\{\theta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. uniform samples on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, then

\mathbb{E}_{n}\Bigl[\inf\limits_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigl\|f(x)-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\sigma_{k}(\theta_{j}\cdot\tilde{x})\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\Bigr]\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)}.

(1.17)

Our refined rate (
1.14

\mathcal{O}\Big(\big(\frac{n}{\log n}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2k+1}{2d}}\Big)
via intricate analysis.

independently of the data. This framework includes stochastic basis selection

\mathbb{E}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}}\Big[\|f_{n,m}-f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big]\lesssim(\|h\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}+M)Mm^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(1.18)

Here

f_{n,m}:=\arg\min\limits_{f_{n}\in L_{n,M}^{k}}\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}(f(x_{i})-f_{n}(x_{i}))^{2}.

(1.19)

Comparing with classical finite element space

(1.20)

This highlights that for smooth functions,

2
Background and main results

Before presenting our main results, we introduce the notation used in this paper and provide brief discussions of main results in this paper. Some of these notations were previously mentioned in Section
1
, where they were assumed to be familiar to the reader. Here, we provide explicit definitions for clarity.

First of all, following
[
95
]
, we adapt the notation
\gtrsim
,
\lesssim
, and
\simeq
, which express upper and lower bounds up to constant factors. When we write

f(x)\gtrsim g(x),\quad g(x)\lesssim h(x),\quad h(x)\simeq k(x)

it means that there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}
independent of
x
such that

f(x)\geq c_{1}g(x),\quad g(x)\leq c_{2}h(x),\quad c_{3}h(x)\leq k(x)\leq c_{4}h(x).

2.1
Shallow neural networks, Barron spaces and Sobolev spaces

Given an activation function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, we denote the dictionary

\mathbb{D}_{\sigma}:=\left\{\pm\sigma(\theta\cdot\tilde{x}):~\theta\in G\right\},

and the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-closure of its convex hull
B_{1}:=\overline{\mathrm{conv}(\mathbb{D}_{\sigma})}
. Following
[
87
]
(see also
[
40
,
25
,
44
,
45
,
48
,
92
,
6
]
), we denote the Barron space
\mathcal{B}^{\sigma}(\Omega)
as follows:

\mathcal{B}^{\sigma}(\Omega):=\{f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega):~\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\sigma}(\Omega)}<\infty\},

(2.1)

where

\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\sigma}(\Omega)}=\inf\left\{t>0:~f\in tB_{1}\right\}.

In particular, when
\sigma=\sigma_{k}
, we take
G=\mathbb{S}^{d}
as
\sigma_{k}
is homogeneous. In this case, we denote

\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)=\mathcal{B}^{\sigma_{k}}(\Omega).

(2.2)

The Barron spaces can be characterized by some qualitative approximation property of the following compact subset of the
\mbox{ReLU}^{k}
neural networks (
1.3
). We state the relation between the
\mbox{ReLU}^{k}
neural networks and Barron spaces as the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
87
,
88
]
)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain.

1.

f\in\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)
if and only if, with some
M\simeq\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)}
,

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\inf\limits_{f_{n}\in\Sigma_{n,M}^{k}}\|f-f_{n}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=0.

(2.3)

2.

The Barron space
\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)
can be alternatively written as

\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)=\left\{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\tilde{x})d\mu(\theta):~\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{S}^{d})\right\}.

(2.4)

Furthermore,

\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)}\simeq\inf\limits_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\left\{|\mu|(\mathbb{S}^{d}):~f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\tilde{x})d\mu(\theta)\right\}.

(2.5)

3.

Let
f\in\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)
, with some
M\simeq\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)}

\inf\limits_{f_{n}\in\Sigma_{n,M}^{k}}\left\|f-f_{n}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2k+1}{2d}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)},

(2.6)

All the corresponding constants are independent of
n
,
\{\theta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{n}
, and
f
.

Even if we remove the restriction on the coefficients, the rate (
2.6
) is optimal up to logarithmic factors
[
87
]
:

\sup\limits_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{k}(\Omega)}\leq 1}\inf_{f_{n}\in\Sigma_{n}^{k}}\|f-f_{n}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\geq c\left(n\log n\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2k+1}{2d}}.

(2.7)

We also refer to
[
55
,
43
,
7
,
82
]
for relevant results.

We now introduce the Sobolev spaces
\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)
. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded, for
r\in\mathbb{N}
, the Sobolev spaces are defined as

\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)=\left\{f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega):~\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)}<\infty\right\}.

(2.8)

where norm is given by

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)}=\Bigl(\|f\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\sum\limits_{{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d},\|{\alpha}\|_{1}=r}\|D^{\alpha}f\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(2.9)

For
r\notin\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)
is defined through a standard interpolation (see, e.g.,
[
10
]
) of
\mathcal{H}^{\lfloor r\rfloor}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{H}^{\lceil r\rceil}(\Omega)
.

In this paper, we establish an approximation rate of
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{r}{d}})
for functions in the Sobolev space
\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)
under the condition that these parameters are well-distributed on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.

Definition 2.1

(Well-distributed and quasi-uniform)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
, a set of points
\{\theta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{S}^{d}
is said to be well-distributed if

\max\limits_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d}}\min\limits_{1\leq j\leq n}\rho(\theta,\theta_{j}^{*})\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{d}},

(2.10)

Moreover, a well-distributed collection
\{\theta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{n}
is said to be quasi-uniform if

\max\limits_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d}}\min\limits_{1\leq j\leq n}\rho(\theta,\theta_{j}^{*})\lesssim\min\limits_{i\neq j}\rho(\theta_{i}^{*},\theta_{j}^{*}).

(2.11)

The corresponding constants are independent of
n
.

2.2
Main results

We now present the main results of this paper. The first theorem focuses on analyzing the approximation properties of the ReLU
k
activation function with predetermined parameters
\{\theta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{n}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary, and
\{\theta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{S}^{d}
. Then for any
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)
, with some

M\simeq\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)},

\inf\limits_{f_{n}\in L_{n,M}^{k}}\bigl\|f-f_{n}\bigr\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim h^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)}.

(2.12)

where

h=\max\limits_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d}}\min\limits_{1\leq j\leq n}\rho(\theta,\theta_{j}^{*}).

More generally, let
r\leq\frac{d+2k+1}{2}
and
s\leq\min\{k,r\}
. Then for any
f\in\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)
, with some

We now show that Sobolev Spaces can be characterized as reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS) associated with the ReLU
k
activation function. A common way to define a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) is through a feature map. Let
\Omega
be a set,
\mathcal{H}_{0}
be a Hilbert space, and
\Phi_{0}:\Omega\to\mathcal{H}_{0}
be a feature map. The space of functions

\mathcal{H}:=\left\{f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\mid\exists w\in\mathcal{H}_{0}\text{ such that }f(x)=\langle w,\Phi_{0}(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}\text{ for all }x\in\Omega\right\}

(2.29)

equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}:=\inf\left\{\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}\mid w\in\mathcal{H}_{0}\text{ and }f(\cdot)=\langle w,\Phi_{0}(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}\right\}

(2.30)

is an RKHS. The reproducing kernel for this space is given by
K(x,y)=\langle\Phi_{0}(x),\Phi_{0}(y)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}
(see, e.g.,
[
1
]
). We can apply this construction to the ReLU
k
activation function. This establishes a direct connection between the Sobolev space and the RKHS associated with the ReLU
k
activation function.

3
Spherical harmonics and Legendre polynomials

This subsection briefly reviews harmonic analysis on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}:=\{\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}:~|\eta|=1\}
, following
[
20
,
90
]
. The average integral uses the normalized surface measure
d\eta
,

\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}f(\eta)\,d\eta=\frac{1}{\omega_{d}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}f(\eta)d\eta,\qquad f\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d}),

where
\omega_{d}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}1d\eta
. The geodesic distance is
\rho(\eta,\theta)=\arccos(\eta\cdot\theta)
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
be the space of polynomials of degree at most
m
restricted to
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, with inner product

\langle p,q\rangle_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}:=\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p(\eta)q(\eta)d\eta.

The dimension of
\mathbb{P}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
is
\displaystyle\binom{d+1+m}{m}
for
m=0,1
and
\displaystyle\binom{d+1+m}{m}-\binom{d-1+m}{m-2}
for
m\geq 2
.

The space of spherical harmonics
\mathbb{Y}_{m}
is the orthogonal complement of
\mathbb{P}_{m-1}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
in
\mathbb{P}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
, which is known as the space of spherical harmonics of degree
m
. Let
\{Y_{m,\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}
be an orthonormal basis for
\mathbb{Y}_{m}
, then its dimension is
N(0)=\text{dim}(\mathbb{Y}_{0})=1
and

N(m)=\text{dim}(\mathbb{Y}_{m})=\frac{2m+d-1}{m}\binom{m+d-2}{d-1},\qquad m\geq 0.

By Weierstrass’ theorem, any
f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
has the harmonic expansion

f(\eta)=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{f}(m,\ell)Y_{m,\ell}(\eta),\qquad a.e.~\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d},

where
\widehat{f}(m,\ell)=\left<f,Y_{m,\ell}\right>_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}
. The
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-projection onto
\mathbb{Y}_{m}
is
\Pi_{m}f=\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{f}(m,\ell)Y_{m,\ell}
.

Given the definition of projections, we are ready to define the Sobolev spaces.

Definition 3.1

(Sobolev spaces on the sphere)
.

For
r>0
, the Sobolev space
\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
is defined as
\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})=\{f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d}):~\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}<\infty\}
, with norm squared

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2}=\|f\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2}+\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{2r}\|\Pi_{m}f\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2}=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}(m^{2r}+1)|\widehat{f}(m,\ell)|^{2}.

(3.1)

3.1
Legendre polynomial and Legendre expansion of
\sigma_{k}

Define the space
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{w_{d}}([-1,1])
by

\left<f,g\right>_{w_{d}}=\int_{-1}^{1}f(t)g(t)(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}dt,\qquad\|f\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}_{w_{d}}([-1,1])}=\left<f,f\right>_{w_{d}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(3.2)

The space
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{w_{d}}([-1,1])
has an orthogonal polynomial basis
\{p_{m}\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
with
\mathrm{deg}(p_{m})=m
satisfying

\left<p_{m},p_{l}\right>_{w_{d}}=0,\quad l\neq m,\quad m\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.3)

Such polynomials are called Legendre polynomials, which are known to necessarily have the form (see, e.g.,
[
91
]
)

p_{m}(t)=\lambda_{m}(1-t^{2})^{-\frac{d-2}{2}}\left({\frac{d}{dt}}\right)^{m}\left[(1-t^{2})^{m+\frac{d-2}{2}}\right],\qquad t\in[-1,1].

(3.4)

In this paper, we choose the normalization factors
\{\lambda_{m}\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
properly such that (
3.6
) below holds.

The integration formula (see, e.g.,
[
20
, Lemma A.5.2.]
) shows a univariate function
f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}_{w_{d}}([-1,1])
has the property

\omega_{d-1}\int_{-1}^{1}f(t)(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}dt=\omega_{d}\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}f(\theta\cdot\eta)d\eta,\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d}.

(3.5)

By
[
20
, Theorem 1.2.6]
, there exist Legendre polynomials
\{p_{m}\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
with
\mathrm{deg}(p_{m})=m
such that

p_{m}(\eta\cdot\theta)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}Y_{m,\ell}(\eta)Y_{m,\ell}(\theta).

(3.6)

With (
3.5
) and (
3.6
), the polynomials
\{p_{m}\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
form an orthogonal basis with respect to the weights
(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}
. Together with (
3.3
), we call
\{p_{m}\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
in (
3.6
) to be the Legendre polynomials throughout this paper.

We remark that (
3.6
) are not standard Legendre polynomials as they are not normalized to have norm equal to
1
. The norms of
\{p_{m}\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
can be determined by using the integration formula (
3.5
):

\begin{split}\left\|p_{m}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}_{w_{d}}([-1,1])}=&\int_{-1}^{1}p_{m}(t)^{2}(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}dt=\frac{\omega_{d}}{\omega_{d-1}}\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p_{m}(e_{1}\cdot\eta)^{2}d\eta\\
=&\frac{\omega_{d}}{\omega_{d-1}}\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\left({\sum_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}Y_{m,\ell}(e_{1})Y_{m,\ell}(\eta)}\right)^{2}d\eta=\frac{\omega_{d}}{\omega_{d-1}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}Y_{m,\ell}(e_{1})^{2}=\frac{\omega_{d}}{\omega_{d-1}}p_{m}(1)\\
=&\frac{\omega_{d}}{\omega_{d-1}}\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p_{m}(1)d\eta=\frac{\omega_{d}}{\omega_{d-1}}\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}Y_{m,\ell}(\eta)^{2}d\eta=\frac{\omega_{d}}{\omega_{d-1}}N(m).\end{split}

(3.7)

Although we will not need them, the normalization factors
\{\lambda_{m}\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
in (
3.4
) can be computed by comparing (
3.7
) with the norm of the standard Legendre polynomials (see, e.g.,
[
91
, Chapter 4.3]
),

\lambda_{m}=\frac{\omega_{d}}{\omega_{d-1}}\frac{N(m)}{\Gamma(m+d/2)}\sqrt{\frac{(2m+d-1)\Gamma(m+d-1)}{2^{2m+d-1}\Gamma(m+1)}}.

The function
\sigma_{k}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}_{w_{d}}([-1,1])
has the Legendre expansion in terms of the orthogonal basis
\{p_{m}\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
as

\sigma_{k}=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)p_{m},

(3.8)

where the Legendre coefficients are given as

\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)=\frac{\left<p_{m},\sigma_{k}\right>_{w_{d}}}{\|p_{m}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}_{w_{d}}([-1,1])}^{2}}.

The coefficients
\{\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(n)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
are studied in
[
80
,
13
,
69
,
2
]
.
Denote the set

E_{\sigma_{k}}:=\left\{m\in\mathbb{N}:~\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)\neq 0\right\},

(3.9)

then by
[
2
, Appendix D.2]
,

\begin{split}&E_{\sigma_{k}}=\left\{m\geq k+1:~m-k\hbox{ is odd}\right\}\cup\{0,\dots,k\},\\
&\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)=\frac{\omega_{d-1}k!\Gamma(d/2)}{\omega_{d}}\frac{(-1)^{(m-k-1)/2}\Gamma(m-k)}{2^{m}\Gamma\left({\frac{m-k+1}{2}}\right)\Gamma\left({\frac{m+d+k+1}{2}}\right)},\qquad m\in E_{\sigma_{k}}.\end{split}

(3.10)

To proceed, we introduce the standard notation of forward and backward difference. Given any
K\in\mathbb{N}
and a sequence
\{\mathfrak{a}(m)\}_{m=K}^{\infty}
, we denote the forward difference
\{(\Delta\mathfrak{a})(m)\}_{m=K}^{\infty}
by

(\Delta\mathfrak{a})(m)=\mathfrak{a}(m+1)-\mathfrak{a}(m),\qquad m\geq K

(3.11)

and the
\beta
-th forward difference
\{(\Delta^{\beta}\mathfrak{a})(m)\}_{m=K}^{\infty}
by

(\Delta^{\beta}\mathfrak{a})(m)=\underbrace{\Delta\circ\dots\circ\Delta}_{\beta}\circ\mathfrak{a}(m)=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\beta}\binom{\beta}{j}(-1)^{\beta-j}\mathfrak{a}(m+j),\qquad m\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.12)

Similarly, writing
\mathfrak{a}(K-1):=0
, we denote the backward difference
\{(\nabla\mathfrak{a})(m)\}_{m=K}^{\infty}
by

(\nabla\mathfrak{a})(m)=\mathfrak{a}(m)-\mathfrak{a}(m-1),\qquad m\geq K

(3.13)

and
\{(\nabla^{\beta}\mathfrak{a})(m)\}_{m=K}^{\infty}
by

(\nabla^{\beta}\mathfrak{a})(m)=\underbrace{\nabla\circ\dots\circ\nabla}_{\beta}\circ\mathfrak{a}(m),\qquad m\geq K.

(3.14)

It is easy to see the inverses of
\nabla
and
\nabla^{\beta}
are

\begin{split}&\left({\nabla^{-1}\mathfrak{a}}\right)(m)=\sum\limits_{\nu=0}^{m}\mathfrak{a}(\nu),\\
&\left({\nabla^{-\beta}\mathfrak{a}}\right)(m)=\underbrace{\nabla^{-1}\circ\dots\circ\nabla^{-1}}_{\beta}\circ\mathfrak{a}(m)=\sum\limits_{\nu=0}^{m}\binom{m+\beta-\nu}{\beta}\mathfrak{a}(\nu)\end{split}

(3.15)

We have the following lemma for the coefficients of
\sigma_{k}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
r\leq\frac{d+2k+1}{2}
, there exists a function
\xi:[k+1,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that

\begin{split}&\xi(m)=\widehat{\sigma}_{k}(m)^{2}m^{2r},\qquad m\geq k+1,~m\in E_{\sigma_{k}},\\
&0\leq(-1)^{\beta}(\Delta^{\beta}\xi)(m)\lesssim m^{2r-(d+2k+1)-\beta},\qquad\beta=0,1,\dots\end{split}

(3.16)

In particular, if
r<\frac{d+2k+1}{2}
,

(-1)^{\beta}(\Delta^{\beta}\xi)(m)\simeq m^{2r-(d+2k+1)-\beta},\qquad\beta=0,1,\dots.

(3.17)

Proof.

Applying the Legendre duplication formula,

\frac{\Gamma(m-k)}{2^{m}\Gamma\left({\frac{m-k}{2}}\right)\Gamma\left({\frac{m-k+1}{2}}\right)}\\
=\frac{1}{2^{k+1}\sqrt{\pi}},\qquad m-k\notin-\frac{\mathbb{N}}{2},

we have

\begin{split}\frac{\Gamma(m-k)}{2^{m}\Gamma\left({\frac{m-k+1}{2}}\right)\Gamma\left({\frac{m+d+k+1}{2}}\right)}=\frac{1}{2^{k+1}\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma\left({\frac{m-k}{2}}\right)}{\Gamma\left({\frac{m+d+k+1}{2}}\right)},\qquad m\geq k+1.\end{split}

Denote the function

\xi(t)=\left({\frac{\omega_{d-1}}{\omega_{d}}\frac{k!\Gamma(d/2)}{2^{k+1}\sqrt{\pi}}}\right)^{2}t^{2r}\left({\frac{\Gamma\left({\frac{t-k}{2}}\right)}{\Gamma\left({\frac{t+d+k+1}{2}}\right)}}\right)^{2},

(3.18)

then with (
3.10
), we have

\xi(m)=\widehat{\sigma}_{k}(m)^{2}m^{2r},\qquad m\geq k+1,~m\in E_{\sigma_{k}}.

Now for
t>k
,

\begin{split}\frac{\xi^{\prime}(t)}{\xi(t)}=&\frac{d}{dt}\log\xi(t)=\frac{2r}{t}+\psi\left({\frac{t-k}{2}}\right)-\psi\left({\frac{t+d+k+1}{2}}\right)\end{split}

(3.19)

where
\psi
is the digamma function
\displaystyle\psi(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\left({\log\Gamma(t)}\right)
. The derivatives of
\psi
are called polygamma functions and have series representations

\psi^{(j)}(t)=(-1)^{j+1}j!\sum\limits_{\nu=0}^{\infty}(t+\nu)^{-(j+1)}\approx(-1)^{j+1}(j-1)!t^{-j},

where the notation
\approx
here signifies
\displaystyle\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{\psi^{(j)}(t)}{(-1)^{j+1}(j-1)!t^{-j}}=1
.

We prove (
3.16
) by induction. Suppose for each
j=0,\dots,r
we have

\begin{split}(d+2k+1-2r)^{j}\leq&\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j}\xi^{(j)}(t)}{t^{2r-(d+2k+1)-j}}\leq(d+2k+j+1-2r)^{j},\\
&(-1)^{j}\xi^{(j)}(t)\geq 0,\qquad t\geq k+1,\end{split}

(3.20)

then

\begin{split}&\xi^{(\beta+1)}(t)=\left({\frac{d}{dt}}\right)^{\beta}\left[\xi(t)\frac{\xi^{\prime}(t)}{\xi(t)}\right]=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\beta}\binom{\beta}{j}\xi^{(\beta-j)}(t)\left({\frac{d}{dt}}\right)^{j}\left({\frac{\xi^{\prime}(t)}{\xi(t)}}\right)\\
=&\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\beta}\binom{\beta}{j}\xi^{(\beta-j)}(t)\left({2r(-1)^{j}j!t^{-(j+1)}+2^{-j}\psi^{(j)}\left({\frac{t-k}{2}}\right)-2^{-j}\psi^{(j)}\left({\frac{t+d+k+1}{2}}\right)}\right)\\
\approx&\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\beta}\binom{\beta}{j}\xi^{(\beta-j)}(t)\left[2r(-1)^{j}j!t^{-(j+1)}+2^{-j}(-1)^{j+1}(j-1)!\left({\left({\frac{t-k}{2}}\right)^{-j}-\left({\frac{t+d+k+1}{2}}\right)^{-j}}\right)\right]\\
\approx&\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\beta}\binom{\beta}{j}\xi^{(\beta-j)}(t)\left[2r(-1)^{j}j!t^{-(j+1)}+(-1)^{j+1}j!(d+2k+1)t^{-(j+1)}\right]\\
=&\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\beta}\binom{\beta}{j}(d+2k+1-2r)\frac{(-1)^{\beta-j}\xi^{(\beta-j)}(t)}{t^{2r-(d+2k+1)-\beta+j}}(-1)^{\beta+1}t^{2r-(d+2k+1)-\beta-1}.\end{split}

Then (
3.20
) yields

\begin{split}&(d+2k+1-2r)\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\beta}\binom{\beta}{j}(d+2k+1-2r)^{\beta-j}\leq\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\beta+1}\xi^{(\beta+1)}(t)}{t^{2r-(d+2k+1)-\beta-1}}\\
\leq&(d+2k+1-2r)\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\beta}\binom{\beta}{j}(d+2k+\beta-j+1-2r)^{\beta-j},\end{split}

which proves

(d+2k+1-2r)^{\beta+1}\leq\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\beta+1}\xi^{(\beta+1)}(t)}{t^{2r-(d+2k+1)-\beta-1}}\leq(d+2k+\beta+2-2r)^{\beta+1}.

(3.21)

On the other hand,

\begin{split}&\left({\frac{d}{dt}}\right)^{j}\left({\frac{\xi^{\prime}(t)}{\xi(t)}}\right)=2r(-1)^{j}j!t^{-(j+1)}+2^{-j}\psi^{(j)}\left({\frac{t-k}{2}}\right)-2^{-j}\psi^{(j)}\left({\frac{t+d+k+1}{2}}\right)\\
=&2r(-1)^{j}j!t^{-(j+1)}+\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{2^{j}}j!\sum\limits_{\nu=0}^{\infty}\left({\left({\frac{t-k}{2}+\nu}\right)^{-(j+1)}-\left({\frac{t+d+k+1}{2}+\nu}\right)^{-(j+1)}}\right)\end{split}

Some calculus estimation yields

(-1)^{j}\left({\frac{d}{dt}}\right)^{j}\left({\frac{\xi^{\prime}(t)}{\xi(t)}}\right)>0,

together with the induction hypothesis (
3.20
), it implies

(-1)^{\beta+1}\xi^{(\beta+1)}(t)=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\beta}\binom{\beta}{j}(-1)^{\beta-j}\xi^{(\beta-j)}(t)(-1)^{j+1}\left({\frac{d}{dt}}\right)^{j}\left({\frac{\xi^{\prime}(t)}{\xi(t)}}\right)\geq 0.

This completes the induction and gives

0\leq(-1)^{\beta}\xi^{(\beta)}(t)\lesssim t^{2r-(d+2k+1)-\beta},\qquad\beta=0,1,\dots

and for
r<\frac{d+2k+1}{2}
,

(-1)^{\beta}\xi^{(\beta)}(t)\simeq t^{2r-(d+2k+1)-\beta},\qquad\beta=0,1,\dots.

Consequently the formula

(-1)^{\beta}(\Delta^{\beta}\xi)(m)=\int_{m}^{m+1}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{1}\dots\int_{0}^{1}}_{\beta-1}(-1)^{\beta}\xi^{(\beta)}(t_{1}+\dots+t_{\beta})dt_{\beta}\dots dt_{1}.

proves (
3.16
) and (
3.17
).

∎

3.2
Properties of the polynomials with scattered points

In this subsection, we consider a finite subset
\{\theta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{S}^{d}
comprising distinct, scattered points. The mesh size for
\{\theta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{S}^{d}
is defined by

h=\max\limits_{\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d}}\min\limits_{1\leq j\leq n}\rho(\eta,\theta_{j}^{*}).

(3.22)

The existence of positive quadrature rules on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
based on scattered points is a known result in approximation theory (see, e.g.,
[
64
,
11
,
39
]
). For our case, one could construct a
\{\theta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{n}
-compatible decomposition as described in
[
64
]
based on the spherical shells
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}(\theta_{j}^{*},h)\setminus\mathbb{B}_{\rho}(\theta_{j}^{*},\underline{h}/2)
. Then the following lemma follows straightforwardly by
[
64
, Theorem 4.1]
.

Lemma 3.2

(Theorem 4.1.
[
64
]
)
.

Given scattered points
\{\theta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{S}^{d}
with mesh norm
h
, there exist nonnegative weights
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n}
with
\tau_{j}\lesssim h^{d}
and a constant
C_{1}
(independent of
n,h
) such that

\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}p(\eta)q(\eta)d\eta=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\tau_{j}p(\theta_{j}^{*})q(\theta_{j}^{*}),\quad\forall p,q\in\mathbb{P}_{J}(\mathbb{S}^{d}),

(3.23)

where
J=\lfloor C_{1}h^{-1}\rfloor
.

In this paper, the matrices induced by Legendre polynomials, defined as

P(m)=\left({p_{m}(\theta_{i}^{*}\cdot\theta_{j}^{*})}\right)_{i,j=1}^{n},

(3.24)

plays a crucial role in our analysis. In this subsection, we summarize the key properties of these matrices, which are essential for the proof of our main result and will be utilized in the next section.

For
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote sequences of matrices
\left\{P_{\beta}(m)\right\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
inductively by

P_{\beta+1}(m)=\sum\limits_{\nu=0}^{m}P_{\beta}(\nu)=\left({\nabla^{-\beta}P}\right)(m)=\sum\limits_{\nu=0}^{m}\binom{m+\beta-\nu}{\beta}P(\nu),\qquad m\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.25)

We also allow the summation to begin at indices other than
0
. Given the sequence
\{P_{\beta}(m)\}_{m=K}^{\infty}
, we denote the sequences
\{P_{K,\beta+1}(m)\}_{m=K}^{\infty}
by

P_{K,\beta+1}(m)=(\nabla^{-1}P_{\beta})(m)=\sum\limits_{\nu=K}^{m}P_{\beta}(\nu),\qquad m\geq K.

(3.26)

An important property of the Legendre polynomials is that the Cesàro summations,
\{P_{\beta}(m)\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
, become increasingly “diagonally dominated” as
\beta
grows (see, e.g.,
[
14
]
). Specifically, the ratio

\frac{\left|\left({P_{\beta}(m)}\right)_{i,i}\right|}{\sum_{i\neq j}\left|\left({P_{\beta}(m)}\right)_{i,j}\right|}

increases with
\beta
. This behavior indicates that the Cesàro summations of
\left\{P_{\beta}(m)\right\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
exhibit a highly localized property, which plays a crucial role in studying approximation properties (see, e.g.,
[
20
, Chapter 2.6, Chapter 11.4]
). A similar phenomenon arises in Fourier analysis, where the Cesàro summations of the Dirichlet kernels, known as Fejér kernels, exhibit localization properties (see, e.g.,
[
24
, Chapter 7]
). This highly-localized property is also significant in analyzing kernel approximation properties (see
[
66
,
67
]
).

Lemma
3.4
establishes several key properties of
P_{\beta}(m)
, enabling sharp estimations in the proof presented in Section
4
. Before proving Lemma
3.4
, we restate a related result from
[
14
, Corollary 14.7]
using our notation.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
\beta\geq 2
and all
i\neq j
,

\left|(P_{\beta}(m))_{i,j}\right|\lesssim\frac{m^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{\rho(\theta_{i}^{*},\theta_{j}^{*})^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\beta}\left({\max\left\{\rho(\theta_{i}^{*},-\theta_{j}^{*}),\frac{1}{m}\right\}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}.

(3.27)

where the corresponding constant is only dependent of
d
.

Proof.

Let
\Bigl\{p_{n}^{(\frac{d-2}{2},\frac{d-2}{2})}\Bigr\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be the Legendre polynomials defined in
[
14
]
, then each
p_{n}^{(\frac{d-2}{2},\frac{d-2}{2})}
must equal to
p_{n}
multiplied by a real number. The function
L_{n}^{(\frac{d-2}{2},\frac{d-2}{2}),\beta}
is denoted in
[
14
, (2.27)]
by, for
t\in[-1,1]
,

\begin{split}\binom{n+\beta-1}{\beta-1}L_{n}^{(\frac{d-2}{2},\frac{d-2}{2}),\beta}(t,1)=&\sum\limits_{\nu=0}^{n}\binom{n+\beta-1-\nu}{\beta-1}\frac{p_{\nu}^{(\frac{d-2}{2},\frac{d-2}{2})}(t)p_{\nu}^{(\frac{d-2}{2},\frac{d-2}{2})}(1)}{\|p_{\nu}^{(\frac{d-2}{2},\frac{d-2}{2})}\|_{L_{w_{d}}^{2}([-1,1])}^{2}}\\
=&\sum\limits_{\nu=0}^{n}\binom{n+\beta-1-\nu}{\beta-1}\frac{p_{\nu}(t)p_{\nu}(1)}{\|p_{\nu}\|_{L_{w_{d}}^{2}([-1,1])}^{2}}\\
=&\frac{\omega_{d-1}}{\omega_{d}}\sum\limits_{\nu=0}^{n}\binom{n+\beta-1-\nu}{\beta-1}p_{\nu}(t)=\frac{\omega_{d-1}}{\omega_{d}}p_{n,\beta}(t).\end{split}

(3.28)

where the last equality follows by (
3.7
).
By
[
14
, Corollary 14.7]
,

\begin{split}&\left|L_{n}^{(\frac{d-2}{2},\frac{d-2}{2})}(t,1)\right|\\
\lesssim&\left({n\left({\sqrt{\frac{1-t}{2}}+\frac{1}{n}}\right)^{d-1}\left(\sqrt{1-t}+\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}}\right)^{-1}\\
&+\left({n^{\beta-1}\left({\sqrt{1-t}+\frac{1}{n}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\left({\sqrt{1+t}+\frac{1}{n}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\left({\frac{1}{n}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\left(\sqrt{1-t}+\frac{1}{n}\right)^{\beta}}\right)^{-1}\end{split}

(3.29)

By noticing the formula

\sqrt{1-\theta_{i}\cdot\theta_{j}}=\sqrt{1-\cos(\rho(\theta_{i},\theta_{j}))}=\sqrt{2}\sin\left({\frac{\rho(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})}{2}}\right),\qquad\theta_{i}\cdot\theta_{j}\geq 0,

we have

\sqrt{1-\theta_{i}\cdot\theta_{j}}\simeq\rho(\theta_{i},\theta_{j}).

As
n\geq M\gtrsim\underline{h}
, we have

\rho(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})\geq\underline{h}\gtrsim\frac{1}{n},

then

\begin{split}\left|p_{n,\beta}(\theta_{i}\cdot\theta_{j})\right|\lesssim&n^{\beta-1}\left({\left({n\rho(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})^{d+1}}\right)^{-1}+\left({n^{\beta-1}\rho(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\rho(\theta_{i},-\theta_{j})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\left({\frac{1}{n}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\rho(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})^{\beta}}\right)^{-1}}\right)\\
\lesssim&\frac{n^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{\rho(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\beta}\left({\max\left\{\rho(\theta_{i},-\theta_{j}),\frac{1}{n}\right\}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}.\end{split}

∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
, the matrices
\left\{P_{\beta}(m)\right\}
have the following properties:

(a)

Each
P_{\beta}(m)
is semi-positive definite.

(b)

The diagonal elements of
P_{\beta}(m)
are equal and satisfy

\left({P_{\beta}(m)}\right)_{i,i}=\max\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\left|\left({P_{\beta}(m)}\right)_{i,j}\right|\simeq m^{\beta+d-1},

(3.30)

(c)

Let
\alpha=\lceil\frac{d+2}{2}\rceil
and
J
be the integer in Lemma
3.2
,

\left\|P_{\alpha}(m)\right\|_{2}\lesssim m^{\alpha+d-1}\left({\frac{h}{\underline{h}}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\alpha},\quad m\geq J+1,

(3.31)

where
\underline{h}:=\min\limits_{i\neq j}\rho(\theta_{i}^{*},\theta_{j}^{*})
.

Proof.

To prove (a), it suffices to show each
P(m)
is semi-positive definite. For any vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by (
3.6
),

\begin{split}a^{\top}P(m)a=&\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq n}a_{i}a_{j}p(\theta_{i}^{*}\cdot\theta_{j}^{*})=\sum\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}a_{i}a_{j}Y_{m,\ell}(\theta_{i}^{*})Y_{m,\ell}(\theta_{j}^{*})\\
=&\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\left({\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}Y_{m,\ell}(\theta_{j}^{*})}\right)^{2}\geq 0.\end{split}

(3.32)

This proves
P(m)
is semi-positive definite and consequently (a).

To prove (b), we apply (
3.6
), (
3.7
) and write

\begin{split}|p_{m}(\eta\cdot\theta)|&=\left|\sum_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}Y_{m,\ell}(\eta)Y_{m,\ell}(\theta)\right|\\
&\leq\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}Y_{m,\ell}(\eta)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}Y_{m,\ell}(\theta)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\\
&=p_{m}(1)=N(m).\end{split}

(3.33)

Thus
\left({P(m)}\right)_{i,j}=p_{m}(\theta_{i}^{*}\cdot\theta_{j}^{*})
attains its maximum value
p_{m}(1)=N(m)
when
i=j
. Therefore,

\begin{split}\max\limits_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\left|\left({P_{\beta}(m)}\right)_{i,j}\right|=&\left({P_{\beta}(m)}\right)_{i,i}=\left|\sum\limits_{\nu=0}^{m}\binom{m+\beta-1-\nu}{\beta-1}\left({P_{\nu,0}}\right)_{i,i}\right|\\
=&\sum\limits_{\nu=0}^{m}\binom{m+\beta-1-\nu}{\beta-1}N(\nu)\simeq m^{\beta+d-1},\end{split}

(3.34)

To prove (c), we apply Lemma
3.3
. In our case,
\alpha=\lceil\frac{d+2}{2}\rceil\geq 2
, so Lemma
3.3
holds true. Given this lemma, we divide the set
\{\theta_{i}^{*}:~1\leq i\leq n,~i\neq j\}
in terms of the distance to
\theta_{j}^{*}
and
-\theta_{j}^{*}
as

\{\theta_{i}:~1\leq i\leq n,~i\neq j\}=\mathcal{I}_{-1,j}\cup\bigcup\limits_{p=0}^{\lceil\log_{2}\left({\frac{\pi}{2\underline{h}}}\right)\rceil}\left({\mathcal{I}_{p,j,+}\cup\mathcal{I}_{p,j,-}}\right),

where
\mathcal{I}_{-1,j}:=\left\{i:\rho(\theta_{i}^{*},-\theta_{j}^{*})<\underline{h}\right\}
and for
p=0,1,\dots
,

\mathcal{I}_{p,j,+}:=\{i:2^{p}\underline{h}\leq\rho(\theta_{i}^{*},\theta_{j}^{*})<2^{p+1}\underline{h}\},\quad\mathcal{I}_{p,j,-}:=\{i:2^{p}\underline{h}\leq\rho(\theta_{i}^{*},-\theta_{j}^{*})<2^{p+1}\underline{h}\}.

By a measure argument, it is easy to verify

\#\mathcal{I}_{-1,j}\lesssim 1,\quad\#\mathcal{I}_{p,j,+}\lesssim 2^{pd},\quad\#\mathcal{I}_{p,j,-}\lesssim 2^{pd}

where the corresponding constants are only dependent of
d
.

By Lemma
3.3
,

\left|\left({P_{\alpha}(m)}\right)_{i,j}\right|\lesssim\frac{m^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{\rho(\theta_{i}^{*},\theta_{j}^{*})^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\alpha}\left({\max\left\{\rho(\theta_{i}^{*},-\theta_{j}^{*}),\frac{1}{m}\right\}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}},

then we can write

\begin{split}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\left|\left({P_{\alpha}(m)}\right)_{i,j}\right|\lesssim&\sum\limits_{p=0}^{\lceil\log_{2}\left({\frac{\pi}{2\underline{h}}}\right)\rceil}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{p,j,+}\cup\mathcal{I}_{p,j,-}}\frac{m^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{\rho(\theta_{i}^{*},\theta_{j}^{*})^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\alpha}\rho(\theta_{i}^{*},-\theta_{j}^{*})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}+\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{-1,j,-}}\frac{m^{d-1}}{\rho(\theta_{i}^{*},\theta_{j}^{*})^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\alpha}}\\
\lesssim&\sum\limits_{p=0}^{\lceil\log_{2}\left({\frac{\pi}{2\underline{h}}}\right)\rceil}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{p,j,+}\cup\mathcal{I}_{p,j,-}}(2^{p}\underline{h})^{-\left({\frac{d-1}{2}+\alpha}\right)}m^{\frac{d-1}{2}}+\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{-1,j}}m^{d-1}\\
\lesssim&\sum\limits_{p=0}^{\lceil\log_{2}\left({\frac{\pi}{2\underline{h}}}\right)\rceil}2^{pd}(2^{p}\underline{h})^{-\left({\frac{d-1}{2}+\alpha}\right)}m^{\frac{d-1}{2}}+m^{d-1}\\
\lesssim&m^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\underline{h}^{-(\frac{d-1}{2}+\alpha)}+m^{d-1},\end{split}

(3.35)

where the last inequality follows by recalling
\alpha=\lceil\frac{d+2}{2}\rceil
.

Together with (
3.30
), we get

\begin{split}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\left|\left({P_{\alpha}(m)}\right)_{i,j}\right|\lesssim m^{d-1}+m^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\underline{h}^{-(\frac{d-1}{2}+\alpha)}+m^{\alpha+d-1}\lesssim m^{\alpha+d-1}\left({\frac{h}{\underline{h}}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\alpha},\quad m\geq J+1.\end{split}

(3.36)

Thus we can estimate the matrix norm of
P_{\alpha}(m)
as

\left\|P_{\alpha}(m)\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\left\|P_{\alpha}(m)\right\|_{1}\left\|P_{\alpha}(m)\right\|_{\infty}}\lesssim m^{\alpha+d-1}\left({\frac{h}{\underline{h}}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\alpha},\quad m\geq J+1.

(3.37)

∎

4
Approximation and characterization theorems for Sobolev spaces on spheres

In this section, we establish a spherical counterpart of Theorem
2.2
and
2.3
. We begin by addressing the spherical case because the function
\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\eta)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
admits a spherical harmonic expansion, whereas obtaining such an expansion for functions of the form
\sigma_{k}(w\cdot x+b)
is challenging (as they are not periodic and prevents an application of Fourier series). This approach is partially inspired by
[
2
]
and
[
98
]
.

4.1
Approximating functions on spheres by ReLU
k
linear vector spaces

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
r\in(0,\frac{d+2k+1}{2}]
,
s\leq\min\{k,r\}
, and
\{\theta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{S}^{d}
. Then for any
f\in\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
satisfying

f(\eta)=(-1)^{k+1}f(-\eta),\qquad\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d},

(4.1)

there exists
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|a\|_{2}\lesssim h^{-\frac{2k+1-2r}{2}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}
such that

\Bigl\|f-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\sigma_{k}(\theta_{j}^{*}\cdot\circ)\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\lesssim h^{r-s}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}.

(4.2)

where

h=\max\limits_{\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d}}\min\limits_{1\leq j\leq n}\rho(\eta,\theta_{j}^{*}).

If the collection
\{\theta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{S}^{d}
is well-distributed, for any
f\in\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
, there exists
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|a\|_{2}\lesssim n^{\frac{2k+1-2r}{2d}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}
such that

\Bigl\|f-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\sigma_{k}(\theta_{j}^{*}\cdot\circ)\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\lesssim h^{r-s}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\simeq n^{-\frac{r-s}{d}}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}.

(4.3)

With
r=\frac{d+2k+1}{2}
and
s=0
, there exists
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|a\|_{2}\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}
such that

\Bigl\|f-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\sigma_{k}(\theta_{j}^{*}\cdot\circ)\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2k+1}{2d}}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}.

(4.4)

All the corresponding constants are independent of
n,\{\theta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{n}
, and
f
.

Proof.

To start up, there exists a subset of
\{\vartheta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{\tilde{n}}\subset\{\theta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{n}
with
\tilde{n}\simeq n
such that

\max\limits_{\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d}}\min\limits_{1\leq j\leq\tilde{n}}\rho(\eta,\vartheta_{j}^{*})\lesssim\min\limits_{i\neq j}\rho(\vartheta_{i}^{*},\vartheta_{j}^{*}).

(4.5)

Thus it suffices to prove the theorem for
\{\theta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{n}
satisfying
h\lesssim\underline{h}
.

For each
j=1,\dots,n
, we can write the expansion (
3.8
) as

\sigma_{k}(\theta_{j}^{*}\cdot\eta)=\sum\limits_{m\in E_{\sigma_{k}}}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)p_{m}(\theta_{j}^{*}\cdot\eta)=\sum\limits_{m\in E_{\sigma_{k}}}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}Y_{m,\ell}(\theta_{j}^{*})Y_{m,\ell}(\eta)\qquad a.e.\quad\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d}.

(4.6)

Then with
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})^{\top}
which is to be determined later,

f_{n}(\eta)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\sigma_{k}(\theta_{j}^{*}\cdot\eta)=\sum\limits_{m\in E_{\sigma_{k}}}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{f_{n}}(m,\ell)Y_{m,\ell}(\eta),\qquad a.e.\quad\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d}.

(4.7)

where

\widehat{f_{n}}(m,\ell)=\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}Y_{m,\ell}(\theta_{j}^{*}).

(4.8)

On the other hand,
since the Legendre polynomials satisfy

p_{m}(-t)=(-1)^{m}p_{m}(t),\qquad t\in[-1,1],~m\in\mathbb{N},

(4.9)

if
m-k
is even,

\begin{split}(\Pi_{m}f)(\theta)=&\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}f(-\eta)p_{m}(-\eta\cdot\theta)d\eta=\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}(-1)^{k+1}f(\eta)(-1)^{m}p_{m}(\eta\cdot\theta)d\eta\\
=&-\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}f(\eta)p_{m}(\eta\cdot\theta)d\eta=-(\Pi_{m}f)(\theta),\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d},\end{split}

which implies
\Pi_{m}f\equiv 0
and
f\in\bigoplus_{m\in E_{\sigma_{k}}}\mathbb{Y}_{m}
.

So we can write the expansion

f(\eta)=\sum\limits_{m\in E_{\sigma_{k}}}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{f}(m,\ell)Y_{m,\ell}(\eta),\qquad a.e.\quad\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d}.

By Lemma
3.2
,
there exists nonnegative numbers

\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n}\lesssim h^{d}

(4.10)

such that

\begin{split}\widehat{f}(m,\ell)=&\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}f(\eta)Y_{m,\ell}(\eta)d\eta=\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}(\Pi_{m}f)(\eta)Y_{m,\ell}(\eta)d\eta\\
=&\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\left({\sum\limits_{m^{\prime}\in E_{\sigma_{k},J}}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m^{\prime})^{-1}(\Pi_{m^{\prime}}f)(\eta)}\right)\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)Y_{m,\ell}(\eta)d\eta\\
=&\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\tau_{j}\left({\sum\limits_{m^{\prime}\in E_{\sigma_{k},J}}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m^{\prime})^{-1}(\Pi_{m^{\prime}}f)(\theta_{j}^{*})}\right)\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)Y_{m,\ell}(\theta_{j}^{*}),\qquad m\leq J.\end{split}

(4.11)

where
J
is the integer in Lemma
3.2
, and

E_{\sigma_{k},J}:=\{m\in E_{\sigma_{k}}:~m\leq J\}.

Comparing (
4.8
) and (
4.11
), by taking

a_{j}=\tau_{j}\left({\sum\limits_{m\in E_{\sigma_{k},J}}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)^{-1}(\Pi_{m}f)(\theta_{j}^{*})}\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,n,

(4.12)

we have

\widehat{f_{n}}(m,\ell)=\widehat{f}(m,\ell),\qquad m\leq J.

(4.13)

Given Lemma (
4.13
), in terms of (
3.1
) we can write

\begin{split}\left\|f-f_{n}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2}=&\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\left(\widehat{f}(m,\ell)-\widehat{f_{n}}(m,\ell)\right)^{2}(m^{2s}+1)\\
=&\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\left(\widehat{f}(m,\ell)-\widehat{f_{n}}(m,\ell)\right)^{2}(m^{2s}+1)\\
\leq&4\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{f}(m,\ell)^{2}m^{2s}+4\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{f_{n}}(m,\ell)^{2}m^{2s}.\end{split}

(4.14)

Write

\begin{split}I_{1}=\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{f}(m,\ell)^{2}m^{2s},\qquad I_{2}=\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{f_{n}}(m,\ell)^{2}m^{2s},\end{split}

then

I_{1}\leq\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{f}(m,\ell)^{2}m^{2r}J^{2s-2r}\leq J^{2s-2r}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2}\simeq h^{2r-2s}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2}.

(4.15)

Let
P(m)
be as in (
3.32
),

\begin{split}I_{2}=&\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)^{2}\left({\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}Y_{m,\ell}(\theta_{j}^{*})}\right)^{2}m^{2s}=\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)^{2}m^{2s}a^{\top}P(m)a\\
\leq&\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}\xi(m)a^{\top}P(m)a,\end{split}

(4.16)

where
\xi
is the nonnegative function determined in Lemma
3.1
, which satisfies

\xi(m)=\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)^{2}m^{2s},\qquad m\in E_{\sigma_{k}},~m\geq J+1.

Summation by parts, we have

\begin{split}&\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}\xi(m)P(m)=\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}\xi(m)\left({\nabla P_{J+1,1}}\right)(m)\\
=&\lim\limits_{m\to\infty}\xi(m+1)P_{J+1,1}(m)-\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}\left({\nabla\xi}\right)(m+1)P_{J+1,1}(m)\\
=&\lim\limits_{m\to\infty}\xi(m+1)P_{J+1,1}(m)-\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}(\Delta\xi)(m)P_{J+1,1}(m).\end{split}

where we abused the notation that
P_{J+1,1}(J)=\sum\limits_{\nu=J+1}^{J}P_{\nu,0}=0
. Similarly, for
\beta=1,\dots,\alpha-1
,

\begin{split}\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\beta}(\Delta^{\beta}\xi)(m)P_{\beta}(m)=&\lim\limits_{m\to\infty}(-1)^{\beta}(\Delta^{\beta}\xi)(m+1)P_{J+1,\beta+1}(m)\\
&+\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\beta+1}(\Delta^{\beta+1}\xi)(m)P_{J+1,\beta+1}(m).\end{split}

By Lemma
3.1
,

\begin{split}\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\beta}(\Delta^{\beta}\xi)(m)P_{\beta}(m)=&\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\beta+1}(\Delta^{\beta+1}\xi)(m)P_{J+1,\beta+1}(m)\\
\leq&\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\beta+1}(\Delta^{\beta+1}\xi)(m)P_{\beta+1}(m).\end{split}

Therefore,

I_{2}\leq\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}\xi(m)a^{\top}P(m)a\leq\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\alpha}(\Delta^{\alpha}\xi)(m)a^{\top}P_{\alpha}(m)a.

(4.17)

Together with (
3.16
) and (
3.31
),

\begin{split}I_{2}\lesssim\left({\frac{h}{\underline{h}}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\alpha}\|a\|_{2}^{2}\sum\limits_{m=J+1}^{\infty}m^{2s-2k-2}\simeq\left({\frac{h}{\underline{h}}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\alpha}\|a\|_{2}^{2}h^{2k+1-2s},\end{split}

(4.18)

Thus

\begin{split}\left\|f-f_{n}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim h^{2r-2s}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2}+\left({\frac{h}{\underline{h}}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\alpha}\|a\|_{2}^{2}h^{2k+1-2s}.\end{split}

(4.19)

For the norm
\|a\|_{2}
, we apply (
4.10
) and the quadrature formula (
3.23
) to conclude

\begin{split}\|a\|_{2}^{2}=&\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\tau_{j}^{2}\left({\sum\limits_{m\in E_{\sigma_{k},J}}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)^{-1}(\Pi_{m}f)(\theta_{j}^{*})}\right)^{2}\lesssim h^{d}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\tau_{j}\left({\sum\limits_{m\in E_{\sigma_{k},J}}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)^{-1}(\Pi_{m}f)(\theta_{j}^{*})}\right)^{2}\\
=&h^{d}\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\left({\sum\limits_{m\in E_{\sigma_{k},J}}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)^{-1}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{f}(m,\ell)Y_{m,\ell}(\eta)}\right)^{2}d\eta=h^{d}\sum\limits_{m\in E_{\sigma_{k},J}}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)^{-2}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{f}(m,\ell)^{2}\\
\leq&h^{d}\max\limits_{m\in E_{\sigma_{k},J}}\left({\frac{m^{d+2k+1}}{m^{2r}+1}}\right)\sum\limits_{m\in E_{\sigma_{k},J}}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}(m^{2r}+1)\widehat{f}(m,\ell)^{2}\\
\lesssim&h^{2r-2k-1}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2}.\end{split}

(4.20)

Substituting this into (
4.18
),

\left({\frac{h}{\underline{h}}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\alpha}\|a\|_{2}^{2}h^{2k+1-2s}\lesssim\left({\frac{h}{\underline{h}}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}+\alpha}h^{2(r-s)}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim h^{2(r-s)}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2},

where the second inequality follows by (
4.5
). Together with (
4.19
), we get (
4.2
) and complete the proof.

∎

Remark 4.1
.

We remark here that the condition (
4.1
) is necessary. This is because any
\sigma_{k}(\theta^{*}\cdot\eta)
is essentially an even (odd) function when
k
is odd (even) up to a polynomial. It suffices to notice

\sigma_{k}(\theta^{*}\cdot\eta)=(-1)^{k+1}\sigma_{k}(-\theta^{*}\cdot\eta)+(\theta^{*}\cdot\eta)^{k}.

4.2
Integral representation for Sobolev and Barron functions on spheres

In this subsection, we provide a new characterization of
\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
. While the operator

\mathcal{G}:~\mu\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\eta)d\mu(\theta),\qquad\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{S}^{d})

(4.21)

characterize the Barron space
\mathcal{B}^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
given by the norm (in comparison with (
2.5
))

\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}=\inf\limits_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\Bigl\{|\mu|(\mathbb{S}^{d}):~f=\mathcal{G}(\mu)\Bigr\}

(4.22)

From Remark
4.1
, up to a polynomial, a neuron
\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\eta)
is essentially an even/odd function on the sphere if
k
is odd/even. So for all the spaces on the sphere, it suffices to consider their even/odd subspace.

For notation simplicity, in this section, we consider
k
to be fixed. We write

\mathcal{M}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d}):=\left\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{S}^{d}):~\mu(A)=(-1)^{k+1}\mu(-A),\quad A\subset\mathbb{S}^{d}\right\},

and for any function space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
,

\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d}):=\left\{f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{S}^{d}):~f(\eta)=(-1)^{k+1}f(-\eta),\quad\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d}\right\}.

Namely,
\mathcal{M}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
(or
\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
) consists of even measures (or functions) if
k
is odd and odd (even) measures (or functions) if
k
is even.

Theorem 4.2
.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the map
\mathcal{G}:~\mathcal{M}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})\to\mathcal{B}^{k}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
is an isomorphism, and satisfies

|\mu|(\mathbb{S}^{d})=\|\mathcal{G}(\mu)\|_{\mathcal{B}^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}.

(4.23)

Furthermore,
\mathcal{G}:~\mathcal{L}_{*}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})\to\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
is an isomorphism on subspaces with

\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\simeq\|\mathcal{G}(\psi)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}.

(4.24)

Proof.

Consider the space
\mathcal{M}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
. By the earlier work (see, e.g.,
[
88
, Lemma 3]
),

\mathcal{G}:~\mathcal{M}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})\to\mathcal{B}_{*}^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d})

is surjective. To see it is an injection, let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
be different. For the harmonic basis, we denote

\widehat{\mu_{i}}(m,\ell)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}Y_{m,\ell}(\theta)d\mu_{i}(\theta),\qquad i=1,2,

then there exists some
(m^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})
such that

\widehat{\mu_{1}}(m^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})\neq\widehat{\mu_{2}}(m^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}),

(4.25)

On the other hand, by Fubini’s theorem, the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-projections satisfy

\Pi_{n}(\mathcal{G}(\mu_{i}))(\eta)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\Pi_{n}(\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\eta))d\mu_{i}(\theta)=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{n}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{\mu_{i}}(m,\ell)Y_{m,\ell}(\eta),\qquad i=1,2.

Therefore,

\begin{split}\|\mathcal{G}(\mu_{1})-\mathcal{G}(\mu_{2})\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2}=&\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left\|\Pi_{n}(\mathcal{G}(\mu_{1}))-\Pi_{n}(\mathcal{G}(\mu_{2}))\right\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2}\\
\geq&\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m^{\prime})^{2}\left(\widehat{\mu_{1}}(m^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})-\widehat{\mu_{2}}(m^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})\right)^{2}>0.\end{split}

As
\mathcal{B}^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
is a subspace of
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
,

\|\mathcal{G}(\mu_{1})-\mathcal{G}(\mu_{2})\|_{\mathcal{B}^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\gtrsim\|\mathcal{G}(\mu_{1})-\mathcal{G}(\mu_{2})\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}>0.

(4.26)

Hence
\mathcal{G}:~\mathcal{M}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})\to\mathcal{B}^{k}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
is an isomorphism. Then the definition of
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{B}^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}
gives

\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}=\inf\limits_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\left\{|\mu|(\mathbb{S}^{d}):~f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\eta)d\mu(\theta)\right\}=|\mathcal{G}^{-1}(\mu)|(\mathbb{S}^{d}),\quad f\in\mathcal{B}^{k}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d}),

(4.27)

which is exactly (
4.23
).

Now we prove the result on
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
. It suffices to apply the orthogonality and write

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}(\psi)(\eta)=&\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\left({\sum\limits_{m\equiv k+1~\mathrm{mod}~2}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{\psi}(m,\ell)Y_{m,\ell}(\theta)}\right)\left({\sum\limits_{m^{\prime}\equiv k+1~\mathrm{mod}~2}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m^{\prime})\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m^{\prime})}Y_{m^{\prime},\ell}(\theta)Y_{m^{\prime},\ell}(\eta)}\right)d\theta\\
=&\sum\limits_{m\equiv k+1~\mathrm{mod}~2}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{\psi}(m,\ell)Y_{m,\ell}(\eta).\end{split}

By Lemma
3.1
,

(m^{d+2k+1}+1)\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)^{2}\simeq 1,\qquad m\equiv k+1~\mathrm{mod}~2

then the norm
\|\mathcal{G}(\psi)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}
can be estimated by (
3.1
) as

\begin{split}\|\mathcal{G}(\psi)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2}=&\sum\limits_{m\equiv k+1~\mathrm{mod}~2}(m^{d+2k+1}+1)\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)^{2}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{\psi}(m,\ell)^{2}\\
\simeq&\sum\limits_{m\equiv k+1~\mathrm{mod}~2}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{\psi}(m,\ell)^{2}=\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2}.\end{split}

Conversely, denote
\mathcal{G}^{-1}
by

\mathcal{G}^{-1}(\psi)=\sum\limits_{m\equiv k+1~\mathrm{mod}~2}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)^{-1}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{\psi}(m,\ell)Y_{m,\ell}(\eta),\qquad\psi\in\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d}),

then

\begin{split}\|\mathcal{G}^{-1}(\psi)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2}=&\sum\limits_{m\equiv k+1~\mathrm{mod}~2}\widehat{\sigma_{k}}(m)^{-2}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{\psi}(m,\ell)^{2}\\
\simeq&\sum\limits_{m\equiv k+1~\mathrm{mod}~2}(m^{d+2k+1}+1)\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N(m)}\widehat{\psi}(m,\ell)^{2}=\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}^{2}.\end{split}

Finally, it is straightforward to check
\mathcal{G}^{-1}
is the inverse of
\mathcal{G}
.

∎

With this theorem, the difference of the Barron spaces and Sobolev spaces can be further characterized as

\begin{split}&\mathcal{B}_{*}^{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d})/\ \mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})\cong\mathcal{M}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})/\ \mathcal{L}_{*}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})\\
\cong&\mathcal{M}^{\perp}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})\oplus\left({\mathcal{L}^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})/\ \mathcal{L}^{2}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\right),\end{split}

(4.28)

where
\mathcal{M}^{\perp}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
is the space of discrete measures, and
\left({\mathcal{L}^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})/\ \mathcal{L}^{2}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\right)
can be characterized by the norm

\|[f]\|_{\left({\mathcal{L}^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})/\ \mathcal{L}^{2}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\right)}=\inf\limits_{g\in\mathcal{L}^{2}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\|f-g\|_{\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}

for any coset
[f]
represented by some
f\in\mathcal{L}^{1}_{*}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
.

5
Proof of main results on general domains

Building on Theorem
4.1
-
4.2
, we now establish their analogues on the ball. The proof relies on an operator, originally introduced in
[
2
]
, that maps ReLU
k
functions defined on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
to ReLU
k
functions on
\mathbb{B}^{d}
. We note that the existence of such an operator relies on the homogeneity of the activation function.

While there exists a more natural homeomorphism from a cap of
\mathbb{S}^{d}
to the ball
\mathbb{B}^{d}
, the operator defined below has the distinct advantage of preserving the structure of ReLU
k
functions. This property is crucial for extending the spherical result to the ball, enabling us to rigorously prove Theorem
2.2
-
2.3
as a direct consequence of its spherical counterpart.

In the remainder of this section, we define this operator, explore its key properties, and demonstrate how it facilitates the desired approximation result on the ball.

Definition 5.1
.

Given
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, let

G:=\left\{\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d}:~\eta_{d+1}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right\}.

Define operator
S_{k}:\ \mathcal{L}^{2}(G)\to\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{B}^{d})
by

(S_{k}g)(x):=|\tilde{x}|^{k}g\left(\frac{\tilde{x}}{|\tilde{x}|}\right),\quad x\in\mathbb{B}^{d}

and operator
T_{k}:\ \mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{B}^{d})\to\mathcal{L}^{2}(G)
by

T_{k}f(\eta)=\eta_{d+1}^{k}f\left({\frac{\bar{\eta}}{\eta_{d+1}}}\right),\qquad\eta\in G.

where
\tilde{x}
has been defined earlier as
\displaystyle\tilde{x}=\binom{x}{1}
, and
\bar{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{d})^{\top}
.

Following the idea in
[
2
]
and
[
98
]
, it is straightforward to verify that

S_{k}T_{k}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{B}^{d})},\qquad T_{k}S_{k}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(G)}

(5.1)

and

\begin{split}&(S_{k}\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\circ))(x)=\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\tilde{x}),\qquad x\in\mathbb{B}^{d},\\
&(T_{k}\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\tilde{\circ}))(\eta)=\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\eta),\qquad\eta\in G.\end{split}

(5.2)

Lemma 5.1
.

For any
r\geq 0
,

\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(G)}\simeq\|S_{k}g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}

(5.3)

Proof.

To start up, we assume
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Write
S_{k}g
as

S_{k}g(x)=\zeta_{1}(x)g(\lambda_{1}(x)),\qquad x\in\mathbb{B}^{d},

(5.4)

where
\zeta_{1}:\mathbb{B}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda_{1}:\mathbb{B}^{d}\to G
are given as

\lambda_{1}(x)=\frac{\tilde{x}}{|\tilde{x}|}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|x|^{2}+1}}\binom{x}{1},\quad\zeta_{1}(x)=|\tilde{x}|^{k}=\left({|x|^{2}+1}\right)^{k/2},\qquad x\in\mathbb{B}^{d}.

with
\lambda_{1}
has its inverse

\lambda_{2}(\eta)=\frac{\bar{\eta}}{\eta_{d+1}}=\left({\frac{\eta_{1}}{\eta_{d+1}},\dots,\frac{\eta_{d}}{\eta_{d+1}}}\right)^{\top},\qquad\eta\in G.

(5.5)

Suppose
g
be smooth enough, then the multivariate chain rule gives

\begin{split}\left|\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}(S_{k}g)}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots\partial x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}}(x)\right|\lesssim&\max\limits_{|\gamma|\leq|\alpha|}\left|\frac{\partial^{|\gamma|}g}{\partial x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\dots\partial x_{d+1}^{\gamma_{d+1}}}(\lambda_{1}(x))\right|\|\lambda_{1}\|_{\mathcal{W}^{|\alpha|,\infty}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}\|\zeta_{1}\|_{\mathcal{W}^{|\alpha|,\infty}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}\\
\lesssim&\max\limits_{|\gamma|\leq|\alpha|}\left|\frac{\partial^{|\gamma|}g}{\partial x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\dots\partial x_{d+1}^{\gamma_{d+1}}}(\lambda_{1}(x))\right|.\end{split}

As the trivial projection

\eta\mapsto\bar{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{d})^{\top}

is a homeomorphism from
G\to\{x\in\mathbb{B}^{d}:~|x|\leq 1/\sqrt{2}\}
,
\lambda_{2}
is then also a homeomorphism from
G
to its image
\mathbb{B}^{d}
. Consequently,

\|S_{k}g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}\lesssim\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(G)}.

(5.6)

As (
5.6
) holds for all sufficiently smooth functions
g
, it holds for all
g\in\mathcal{H}^{r}(G)
.

To prove
\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|S_{k}g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}
, it suffices to take
f=S_{k}g
and prove

\|T_{k}f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}.

(5.7)

We write

T_{k}f(\eta)=\zeta_{2}(\eta)f(\lambda_{2}(\eta)),\qquad\eta\in G,

(5.8)

where
\lambda_{2}
as in (
5.5
) and
\zeta_{2}(\eta)=\eta_{d+1}^{k}
. Then (
5.7
) can be proved using the same argument as above. Thus for
r\in\mathbb{N}
,

\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(G)}\simeq\|S_{k}g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}

For
r\notin\mathbb{N}
, it suffices to apply

\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\lfloor r\rfloor}(G)}\simeq\|S_{k}g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\lfloor r\rfloor}(\mathbb{B}^{d})},\qquad\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\lceil r\rceil}(G)}\simeq\|S_{k}g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\lceil r\rceil}(\mathbb{B}^{d})},

then the interpolation theory of linear operators yields

\|S_{k}g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}\lesssim\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(G)},\quad\|T_{k}f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(G)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}

and completes the proof of this lemma.
∎

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume

\Omega\subset\mathbb{B}^{d}

[

, there exists an extension
f_{E}

\|f_{E}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}\leq\|f_{E}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)}.

By Lemma
5.1
, we have

\|T_{k}f_{E}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(G)}\lesssim\|f_{E}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)}.

By the extension theorems again, there exists a function
g
on
\mathbb{S}^{d}

T_{k}f_{E}

\begin{split}&\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\lesssim\|T_{k}f_{E}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(G)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)},\\
&g(\eta)=(-1)^{k+1}g(-\eta),\qquad\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d}.\end{split}

Now by Theorem
4.1
, there exists a vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|a\|_{2}\lesssim h^{-\frac{2k+1-2r}{2}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}
such that

\begin{split}&\biggl\|T_{k}f_{E}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\sigma_{k}(\theta_{j}^{*}\cdot\circ)\biggr\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(G)}\leq\Bigl\|g-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\sigma_{k}(\theta_{j}^{*}\cdot\circ)\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\\
\lesssim&\left({\frac{h}{\underline{h}}}\right)^{d+1}h^{r-s}\left\|g\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\lesssim\left({\frac{h}{\underline{h}}}\right)^{d+1}h^{r-s}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

(5.9)

Applying Lemma
5.1
again, we obtain

\begin{split}&\Bigl\|f_{E}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\sigma_{k}(\circ\cdot w_{j}^{*}+b_{j}^{*})\biggr\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}=\Bigl\|S_{k}\bigl(T_{k}f_{E}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\sigma_{k}(\theta_{j}^{*}\cdot\circ)\bigr)\biggr\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}\\
\lesssim&\Bigl\|T_{k}f_{E}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\sigma_{k}(\theta_{j}^{*}\cdot\circ)\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(G)}\lesssim\left({\frac{h}{\underline{h}}}\right)^{d+1}h^{r-s}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

(5.10)

This proves

\Bigl\|f-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\phi_{j}\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\Omega)}\lesssim h^{r-s}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)}.

(5.11)

∎

5.2
Proof of Theorem
2.3

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume
\Omega\subset\mathbb{B}^{d}
. By the extension theorem again, for
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)
, there exists

g
on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, which is an extension of
T_{k}f
, such that

\begin{split}&\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\lesssim\|T_{k}f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(G)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)},\\
&g(\eta)=(-1)^{k+1}g(-\eta),\qquad\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{d}.\end{split}

By Theorem
4.2
, there exists
\psi_{0}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
such that

g(\eta)=\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\psi_{0}(\theta)\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\eta)d\theta.

Then

\begin{split}&\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\psi_{0}(\theta)\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\tilde{x})d\theta=\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\psi_{0}(\theta)|\tilde{x}|^{k}\sigma_{k}\left({\theta\cdot\frac{\tilde{x}}{|\tilde{x}|}}\right)d\theta\\
=&|\tilde{x}|^{k}g\left({\frac{\tilde{x}}{|\tilde{x}|}}\right)=S_{k}g(x)=f(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{B}^{d},\end{split}

hence Theorem
4.2
implies

\begin{split}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)}\gtrsim&\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\simeq\|\psi_{0}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\\
\geq&\inf\limits_{\psi\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\left\{\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}:~\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\tilde{x})\psi(\theta)d\theta=f(x)\right\}.\end{split}

Now suppose there is some
\psi_{1}
such that

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\tilde{x})\psi_{1}(\theta)d\theta=f(x),

then

\begin{split}\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\psi_{1}(\theta)\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\eta)d\theta=&\fint_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\psi_{1}(\theta)\eta_{d+1}^{k}\sigma_{k}\left({\theta\cdot\frac{\eta}{\eta_{d+1}}}\right)d\theta\\
=&\eta_{d+1}^{k}f\left(\frac{\bar{\eta}}{\eta_{d+1}}\right)=T_{k}f(\eta).\end{split}

Again, Theorem
4.2
implies

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\mathbb{B}^{d})}\simeq\|T_{k}f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\lesssim\|\psi_{1}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})},

which yields

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)}\lesssim\inf\limits_{\psi\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}\left\{\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})}:~\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}\sigma_{k}(\theta\cdot\tilde{x})\psi(\theta)d\theta=f(x)\right\}.

∎

6
Deterministic analysis of randomized neural networks:

questioning the necessity of randomization

Neural network training involves non-convex optimization, which is computationally challenging. While greedy methods
[
83
,
97
]
offer convergence guarantees, they can be costly. Randomized approaches, such as stochastic basis selection
[
38
]
, Extreme Learning Machines (ELM)
[
37
,
36
,
51
]
, and random features
[
76
,
78
,
49
,
3
,
71
,
62
]
, provide efficient alternatives by fixing hidden layer parameters randomly (often uniformly or Gaussian
[
33
]
) and only optimizing linear weights. These methods have shown empirical success in various tasks, including function approximation, classification, and solving PDEs
[
52
,
9
,
31
,
35
,
27
,
26
,
15
,
100
,
21
,
16
]
. However, the theoretical justification for specific sampling strategies often lags behind empirical performance.

Let
\mathbb{E}_{n,\mu}
denote expectation over
n
i.i.d. samples
\{\theta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
from a probability distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. If
\mu
is uniform, we write
\mathbb{E}_{n}
. Standard concentration inequalities applied to the integral representation (
1.16
) yield the follwoing theorem.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
d,k,n,\Omega
be as in Theorem
2.2
, then for
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)
,

\mathbb{E}_{n}\Bigl[\inf\limits_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigl\|f-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\phi_{j}\Bigr\|^{2}_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\Bigr]\lesssim n^{-1}\|f\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)}.

(6.1)

This
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})

\mathcal{L}^{2}
-rate provides theoretical backing for random feature methods. (Note: Theorem
2.3
allows deterministic points + uniform sampling, improving on
[
87
]
where the measure was unknown. We focus here on the stronger results derived from Theorem
2.2
.)

Leveraging our deterministic approximation result (Theorem
2.2
), we can refine the analysis of random sampling:

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\{\theta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. uniform samples from
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. With probability at least
1-\delta
, there exists
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|a\|_{2}\lesssim\left({\frac{n}{\log(n/\delta)}}\right)^{\frac{2k+1-2r}{2d}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)}
such that

\Bigl\|f-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\phi_{j}\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\Omega)}\lesssim\left({\frac{n}{\log(n/\delta)}}\right)^{-\frac{r-s}{d}}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)}.

(6.2)

Consequently, the expected error satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{n}\Bigl[\inf\limits_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigl\|f-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\phi_{j}\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\Omega)}\Bigr]\lesssim\left({\frac{n}{\log n}}\right)^{-\frac{r-s}{d}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}(\Omega)}.

(6.3)

In particular, for
\mathcal{L}^{2}
approximation (
s=0
,
r=\frac{d+2k+1}{2}
),

\mathbb{E}_{n}\Bigl[\inf\limits_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigl\|f-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\phi_{j}\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\Bigr]\lesssim\left({\frac{n}{\log n}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2k+1}{2d}}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)}.

(6.4)

Proof.

The proof relies on Theorem
2.2
, which depends on
h=\max\limits_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d}}\min\limits_{1\leq j\leq n}\rho(\theta,\theta_{j})
. Standard covering arguments and concentration inequalities show that for i.i.d. uniform points
\{\theta_{j}\}
,
h\lesssim(n/\log(n/\delta))^{-1/d}
with probability at least
1-\delta
. Substituting this into (
2.12
) yields the result.
∎

This analysis clarifies why randomization works: it efficiently generates parameter distributions
\{\theta_{j}\}
that are nearly well-distributed (i.e.,
h
is small) with high probability. However, comparing Theorem
6.2
with Theorem
2.2
reveals that deterministically chosen well-distributed points achieve a *better* approximation rate (lacking the
\log n
factor) and eliminate the small probability
\delta
of failure associated with random sampling. Since well-distributed point sets on spheres can be constructed deterministically, we conclude that they are sufficient and theoretically preferable to random sampling for achieving optimal approximation rates in this context. Randomization is thus a practical means to approximate a desirable deterministic configuration.

7
Generalization Analysis for FNS using Rademacher Complexity

We analyze the generalization error when approximating a target function
f
using a dataset
D_{m}
. Given the approximation rate (
2.13
) and coefficient bound
M
from Theorem
2.2
, we can estimate the expected error between
f
and an approximant constructed from
m
i.i.d. samples.

Standard generalization analysis uses concentration inequalities
[
12
]
and complexity measures like Rademacher complexity, covering numbers, or spectral complexity
[
18
,
81
]
. These bounds depend critically on the coefficient norms of the approximants.

We illustrate using a second-order linear elliptic PDE with Neumann boundary conditions on a bounded
C^{\infty}
domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|\Omega|=1
:

\begin{cases}-\Delta g(x)+g(x)=h(x),&x\in\Omega\\
\frac{\partial g}{\partial n}(x)=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(7.1)

where
h\in\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
is given. The variational formulation seeks
g\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Omega)
minimizing the energy functional:

\mathcal{E}(g):=\int_{\Omega}\Psi(g)(x)\,dx,\quad\text{where }\Psi(g)(x)=\frac{1}{2}|\nabla g(x)|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}g(x)^{2}-h(x)g(x).

(7.2)

The target function
f
is the unique minimizer of
\mathcal{E}(g)
due to strict convexity:

f:=\arg\min_{g\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Omega)}\mathcal{E}(g).

(7.3)

Using Monte Carlo, we draw
m
i.i.d. uniform samples
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
from
\Omega
and approximate
\mathcal{E}(g)
with the empirical risk:

\mathcal{E}_{m}(g):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Psi(g)(x_{i}).

(7.4)

Assuming
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)
, we use the hypothesis space
L_{n,M}^{k}
(from Theorem
2.2
). The empirical risk minimizer (ERM) is:

f_{n,m}:=\arg\min\limits_{g\in L_{n,M}^{k}}\mathcal{E}_{m}(g).

(7.5)

Since
L_{n,M}^{k}
is linear and
\mathcal{E}_{m}(g)
is quadratic in coefficients,
f_{n,m}
is unique and efficiently computable via convex optimization.

The generalization error is the expected excess risk:

\mathbb{E}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}}\Big[\mathcal{E}(f_{n,m})-\mathcal{E}(f)\Big].

(7.6)

Due to strong convexity of
\mathcal{E}(g)
, bounding this also bounds the expected squared
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Omega)
error
\mathbb{E}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}}\Big[\|f_{n,m}-f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big]
.

7.1
Rademacher Complexity

We use Rademacher complexity
[
8
]
to bound (
7.6
). For a function class
\mathcal{F}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, its Rademacher complexity is:

R_{m}(\mathcal{F})=\mathbb{E}_{x_{i},\xi_{i}}\Big[\sup\limits_{g\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}g(x_{i})\Big],

(7.7)

where
x_{i}
are i.i.d. uniform samples and
\xi_{i}
are i.i.d. Rademacher variables (
\pm 1
).

We bound
R_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{n,M})
for
\mathcal{F}_{n,M}:=\{\Psi(g):g\in L_{n,M}^{k}\}
. Since
L_{n,M}^{k}\subset\Sigma_{n,M}^{k}
, monotonicity gives
R_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{n,M})\leq R_{m}(\{\Psi(g):g\in\Sigma_{n,M}^{k}\})
.
Using standard Rademacher complexity properties (sum rule, Lipschitz composition, product rule
[
84
,
8
,
70
,
93
]
) and known bounds
R_{m}(\Sigma_{n,M}^{k})\lesssim Mm^{-1/2}
and
R_{m}(\{\partial g/\partial x_{j}:g\in\Sigma_{n,M}^{k}\})\lesssim Mm^{-1/2}

[
84
, Theorem 6]
, we get:

R_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{n,M})\leq R_{m}\Big(\Big\{\Psi(g):~g\in\Sigma_{n,M}^{k}\Big\}\Big)\lesssim(M+\|h\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)})Mm^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(7.8)

7.2
Generalization analysis

Combining Theorem
2.2
and the complexity bound (
7.8
) yields the generalization error estimate.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded
C^{\infty}
domain. Let
f
,
f_{n,m}
be defined by (
7.3
) and (
7.5
). If
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\frac{d+2k+1}{2}}(\Omega)
, the expected excess risk satisfies:

\mathbb{E}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}}\Big[\mathcal{E}(f_{n,m})-\mathcal{E}(f)\Big]\lesssim(M+\|h\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)})Mm^{-\frac{1}{2}}+M^{2}n^{-1-\frac{2k-1}{d}}.

(7.9)

Choosing
n=\lceil m^{\frac{d}{2(d+2k-1)}}\rceil
balances the terms, giving:

\mathbb{E}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}}\Big[\mathcal{E}(f_{n,m})-\mathcal{E}(f)\Big]\lesssim(M+\|h\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)})Mm^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(7.10)

By strong convexity of
\mathcal{E}(g)
, the expected squared
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Omega)
error is bounded:

\mathbb{E}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}}\Big[\|f_{n,m}-f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big]\lesssim(M+\|h\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)})Mm^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(7.11)

The proof follows standard arguments
[
8
,
70
]
. The first term in (
7.9
) arises from the Rademacher complexity bound (
7.8
) via generalization bounds. The second term is the approximation error from Theorem
2.2
. Strong convexity yields (
7.11
). See
[
84
]
for similar detailed proofs for PDE approximation.

8
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we developed a new integral representation of Sobolev space using ReLU
k
function, and compared it with the corresponding result of Barron spaces. We also showed in Sobolev spaces, the approximation properties of nonlinear shallow neural networks can be fully realized through simple linearization, and provided an upper bound of the corresponding coefficients. Such an upper bound is essential in approximation theory, and allows the corresponding generalization analysis. It is also worth noting that the techniques in this paper also inspires a Bernstein inequality for ReLU
k
neural networks, for which we are completing a new paper entitled “Bernstein Inequalities for Linearized ReLU
k
Neural Networks and Applications”.

Since a DNN is essentially a composition of shallow neural networks, we hope that the findings in this paper provide valuable insights into the role of nonlinearity within deep neural networks. Furthermore, we aim for this work to inspire further mathematical research into the interplay between nonlinearity and expressive power (approximation properties) in deep learning.
