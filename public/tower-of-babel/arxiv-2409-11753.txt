METEOR: Melody-aware Texture-controllable Symbolic Orchestral Music Generation
Dinh-Viet-Toan Le, Yi-Hsuan Yang

Abstract
Western music is often characterized by a homophonic texture, in which the musical content can be organized into a melody and an accompaniment. In orchestral music, in particular, the composer can select specific characteristics for each instrument’s part within the accompaniment, while also needing to adapt the melody to suit the capabilities of the instruments performing it. In this work, we propose METEOR, a model for Melody-aware Texture-controllable Orchestral music generation. This model performs symbolic multi-track music style transfer with a focus on melodic fidelity. We allow bar- and track-level controllability of the accompaniment with various textural attributes while keeping a homophonic texture. We show that the model can achieve controllability performances similar to strong baselines while greatly improve melodic fidelity.



I
Introduction

Style transfer systems for symbolic music
are models designed to replicate a reference piece while altering some attributes.
This control is often performed at a piece-level
[
1
]
or bar-level
[
2
]
.
For orchestral music, or more generally, multi-track music, such control can also occur at the instrument or track level.
The orchestral score can indeed be described by global characteristics such as instrument groupings or part diversity, as well as part-specific attributes such as rhythmicity or repetitiveness
[
3
]
, which create the overall
musical texture
.
More globally, musical texture refers to how different musical streams are written, organized, and combined
[
4
]
.

Homophony, defined as a musical texture composed of a main melody supported by a background accompaniment, is the most common texture in Western music
[
5
]
.
However, style transfer systems
often focus on the accompaniment complexity, leading to unintended alterations of the melody when adjusting control signals.
In other words, these systems overlook or even disregard the melodic fidelity of the generated content. For example, according to FIGARO
[
6
]
: “some salient features such as melodies are often not preserved”.
This tendency highlights a gap in the current approaches to style transfer, where the complexity of the accompaniment and the preservation of melodic integrity are not adequately addressed.

In this study, we present METEOR, a model for

Te

Or

The main approach relies on bar-level, track-level and beat-level token constraints.
The model is able to perform melodic- texture-based style transfer at a bar-level and track-level, with the option for automatic or user-specified selection of the instrumentation and the melodic instrument (Figure

Going further, it can also perform lead sheet orchestration.

We evaluate this model in Section

II
Related works

Our work centers on
style transfer
, aiming to generate a piece based on a reference while adjusting high-level musical attributes.
We specifically explore it within
homophonic
music, which consists of a melody supported by an accompaniment.

II-A

Texture-based style transfer

Musical texture style transfer systems often offer a control of a reference piece over attributes such as polyphonicity
[
2
]
, pitch or duration distributions
[
6
]
.
Multiple levels of controllability can be defined, i.e., global controllability, time-varying (often bar-level) controllability and track-level controllability in the case of multi-track music. Global features characterize the whole generated sequence; time-varying attributes only impact a single bar; and track-level controllability affects a single track either globally or locally at the bar level.

MuseMorphose
[
2
]
and MuseBarControl
[
7
]
implement bar-level controls for single-track piano music generation.
In the larger scope of multi-track music,
such style transfer systems include MuseCoco for global controllability
[
1
]
or FIGARO for bar-level controllability
[
6
]
.
With both bar- and track-level attributes, BandControlNet
[
8
]
generates pop music according to seven musical control attributes.
However, the model can only handle a fixed six-instrument ensemble composed of generic instruments, such as drums, piano or strings.

II-B

Homophonic music generation

Multiple systems based on the assumption of a homophonic texture
have been developed for harmonization or accompaniment generation.
In particular, lead sheet arrangement is tackled as an end-to-end task by Compose & Embellish
[
9
]
in the case of piano music and Accomontage-3
[
10
]
for multi-track music.
Instead, combinatorial music generation
[
11
]
is based on combining a melodic track with other accompaniment tracks (bass, riff,…) that have been generated independently.

Style transfer systems often overlook melodic content when applied to homophonic reference pieces. Models like FIGARO
[
6
]
and MuseMorphose
[
2
]
focus primarily on controllable features and harmony,
resulting in a potential loss of the melodic content in the generated tune.
This oversight can be due to the added complexity of managing both bar-wise attributes and melody.
In contrast, multi-track systems that do keep the melodic content in the generated content often attribute this part to a
fixed
instrument such as synthesizers
[
8
]
or “lead” tracks
[
10
]
, without strict physical restrictions like its ambitus or register.
However, in genres such as classical orchestral music, the melody is assigned to a specific instrument or a group of instruments which can change throughout the piece.
In such cases, the melody must respect the physical limitations of the instruments, including their range.

Thus, the main challenges in combining textural style transfer and melodic fidelity in multi-track music are twofold: maintaining both overall and melodic fidelity to the reference piece, and ensuring the choice of instruments aligns with the textural conditions while respecting the physical limitations of each instrument, such as register and range.

In this section, we present
METEOR,
a Transformer-based VAE for multi-track style transfer
with
bar-wise and track-wise texture controllability while maintaining melodic fidelity.

III-A

Textural attributes

We consider two levels of controllability: “bar-wise” (
i.e.
all tracks may be influenced by the control attribute) and “bar- and track-wise” (
i.e.
each track can be individually controlled at a bar level).
We select two control attributes for each level.

First, the user can select the playing instruments from a subset of 64 instruments among the 127 MIDI instruments defined in
[
12
]
.
We then consider “bar-wise” control attributes which globally affect all the tracks.

•

Rhythmicity
: number of sub-beat having at least one note played within a bar, regardless of the track.

•

Polyphonicity
: average number of notes played (hit or held) during a sub-beat in a bar, including all tracks.

Each bar is first characterized by a raw value of polyphonicity and rhythmicity.
Following MuseMorphose
[
2
]
, these raw values are split into 8 bins with a similar number of bars in each bins. These bins are set according to the dataset distribution of polyphonicity and rhythmicity values.

For finer-grained control, we include “bar-wise and track-wise” control attributes aiming at controlling each instrument individually among those initially selected at a bar level.

•

Average pitch
: average pitch of a track in a bar, expressed in MIDI value and rounded to the nearest ten. Levels of average pitches are thus divided into 13 classes, spanning from 10 to 130.
For instance, this attribute can be used to assign high register to melodic instruments, and low register for bass parts.

•

Pitch diversity
: number of different pitch classes played in a track in a bar. Levels of pitch diversity are divided into 13 classes, spanning from 0 to 12.
Low pitch diversity can relate to bass parts, repeated notes or arpeggios, while high pitch diversity can encourage passing notes, embellishments or extended chords.

III-B

Tokenization, model & control strategies

The METEOR model is based on MuseMorphose
[
2
]
, originally developed for piano style transfer.
The model implements a variational auto-encoder based on Transformers encoder (resp. decoder) in the encoder (resp. decoder) part.
We first extend its initial tokenization based on REMI
[
13
]
using the REMI+ tokenization
[
6
]
which handles multi-track music.
We also rely on a “pitch class+octave encoding” of the pitches
[
14
]
instead of absolute MIDI values, in particuler, to handle melodies independently of the original octave register.

For textural controls, we leverage both control embeddings
in the latent space
and control tokens in the input token sequence.
Following MuseMorphose
[
2
]
, the bar-wise polyphonicity and rhythmicity classes are encoded in a separate sequence of bar-level conditions, which is then embedded and concatenated with the latent vector and used as condition in the encoder through an “in-attention” mechanism.

Track-related control signals are handled via control tokens added to the vocabulary.
These control tokens are inserted after each
Bar
token as a condition for the generated musical content as represented in Figure
2
.
Instrument selection is handled through
DescriptionTrack-[track]
tokens that indicate instruments playing in a bar.
For bar- and track-wise controllability,
PitchAvg-[track]-[level]
tokens and
PitchDiversity-[track]-[level]
tokens control the average pitch and pitch diversity level of each track.

Regarding the overall training process, the model is trained as an end-to-end model on the SymphonyNet dataset, initially compiled for training a free generation model
[
15
]
.
Similar to MuseMorphose, the loss function used is a
\beta
-VAE objective with free bits.
The resulting model is 67M parameter-large and is trained on a single 24GB GPU.

III-C

Melodic fidelity

The melody is one of the most crucial elements in music, as it often makes a piece easily recognizable
[
16
]
.
Therefore, a main focus of our model is melodic fidelity. In other words, the model aims to keep the melody between the original piece and the generated piece, with possibly different textures in the accompaniment parts.

First, the melody is identified in the original piece during a pre-processing step using a bar-wise and track-wise skyline algorithm. The melody in each bar corresponds to the track with the highest average pitch within that bar.
The instrument playing the melody is then chosen by the model or can be specified by the user. In particular, the model or the user may choose to use different instruments to play the melody in different bars of the generated piece.

Then, the tokens identified as melody in the original piece are used as beat-level conditions at inference time.
For each
Sub-beat
token generated, a
Track
token corresponding to the chosen melodic instrument, followed by tokens corresponding to the melody (
i.e.
pitch class, octave, duration, velocity) are appended to the generated content.
In further experiments, we also allow the model to generate the
Octave
token to evaluate its relation with the instrument’s register.
The next tokens (
i.e.
all the tokens from the accompaniment until the next melodic tokens) are then generated auto-regressively.

In this section, we present an objective evaluation of the model in terms of fidelity and controllability. We extend this quantitative evaluation with qualitative analyses of the instrumentation impact and the instrumental playability of the melody.
We only focus on evaluating the controllable style transfer task, omitting the lead sheet orchestration task
(
i.e.
generating an orchestral arrangement of a lead sheet).
In particular, the model is not explicitly trained on this task but manages to perform it in a zero-shot learning setting.
For this task, audio extracts are available on the demo page
1
.

IV-A

Metrics

We first consider metrics to evaluate the full piece fidelity with respect to the reference piece, both overall and specifically for the melody.
We then consider bar-level metrics for polyphonicity and rhythmicity and bar-level and track-level metrics for average pitch and pitch diversity.

Overall fidelity –
Following
[
6
]
, we consider the overall fidelity as the chroma similarity between the original piece and the generation.
Let
\mathbf{v}_{b}^{\text{init}}
(resp.
\mathbf{v}_{b}^{\text{gen}}
) the chroma vectors of bar
b
of the initial (resp. generated) piece, including all the tracks, and
N
the number of bars. The chroma similarity is defined as the average of the bar-wise cosine similarities
S_{C}(\cdot,\cdot)
between these chroma vectors:

\text{sim}_{\text{chr}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{b=1}^{N}S_{C}(\mathbf{v}_{b}^{\text{init}},\mathbf{v}_{b}^{\text{gen}})

Melodic fidelity –

For a piece, let
X_{b,\text{mel}}
the token sequence representing the melody in bar
b
. For a track
t
in the generation, let
X_{b,t}
the token sequence of one track
t
at this bar
b
. We consider the Levenstein edit distance between two sequences
d(\cdot,\cdot)
and normalize it so that
\lvert d(X_{1},X_{2})\rvert\leq 1
for
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
two sequences.
We define the melodic fidelity of a track
t
at a bar
b
as
d(X_{b,\text{mel}},X_{b,t})
.
By taking the minimum of these distances among the tracks, we aim at selecting the track which is playing the melody within a bar.
Therefore, the smaller the distance, the greater the melodic fidelity.
Namely, we define the melodic fidelity
\varphi_{{}_{b}}

at a bar
b
as:

\varphi_{{}_{b}}=1-\min_{t\in\text{tracks}}d(X_{b,\text{mel}},X_{b,t})

Finally, we define the melodic fidelity
\varphi
of a full multi-track generation of
N
bars as the average of these bar-wise fidelities:

\varphi=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{b=1}^{N}\varphi_{{}_{b}}

Bar-controllability –

Polyphonicity and rhythmicity are evaluated at the bar level by including all tracks.
Following
[
2
]
, we consider the Spearman correlation between the user-specified polyphonicity or rhythmicity class and the class computed from the model generations given the user inputs.

Track-controllability –

Similar to the bar-level control attributes, average pitch and pitch diversity are evaluated with a Spearman correlation between the user input and the class computed from the generation, for each track and each bar.

IV-B

Results

We evaluate the generations through these objective metrics, by comparing METEOR with open-source and state-of-the-art style transfer models, FIGARO
[
6
]
and MuseMorphose
[
2
]
, both of which allow only bar-level controls.
We focus only on instrument-agnostic models (
i.e.
which can handle any type of musical ensemble, unlike
[
8
,
10
]
) for a fair comparison with our model.
For FIGARO, no controls over polyphonicity are provided in the original work.
As for MuseMorphose which was originally designed for piano music, we implement a multi-track version that adopts a REMI+ tokenization.
We compare these models with two versions of METEOR: one with bar-level and track-level controllability, and another that adds melody constraints.
We evaluate each model on 20 generated tracks, each 8 bars long, with the reference pieces and the control signals being randomly selected.

The comparison metrics are shown in Table
I
.
MuseMorphose-based models manage to outperform FIGARO in all metrics.
In contrast, multi-track MuseMorphose performs similarly to METEOR on shared metrics.
This may result from using independent control methods, either latent or token-based, for bar- and track-level attributes. The compatibility of these control levels within a latent space or token-based system remains unexplored and could be investigated in future research to improve the understanding of controllable models.
Finally, adding melodic constraints to METEOR naturally leads to great improvement in melodic fidelity.
In particular, while all models can perform good overall fidelity, ours greatly improves the melodic fidelity.
However, this results in a minor drop in controllability metrics compared to a controllable-only model.

Instrumentation impact –

Beyond this quantitative evaluation, we first conduct a qualitative study on the impact of the chosen instrumentation on the models’ performances (Table
I
).
We select three musical ensembles: woodwind duet, quintet,
and a classical orchestra, assigning the melody to the flute in each case.
For melodic METEOR, beyond a slight drop in performance between the quintet and the orchestra in terms of bar-wise rhythmicity and track-wise average pitch, increasing the number of instruments helps the model maintaining better fidelity to the reference piece and improves track-wise pitch diversity and bar-wise polyphonicity.
With more instruments, the model has a larger instrumental flexibility and a broader range of options to assign each track a part that aligns with the control signals.
Moreover, all the models demonstrate better polyphonic controllability when the instrumentation is chosen automatically compared to each user-defined ensembles. In other words, they manage to effectively select the most suitable ensemble to match the requested polyphonicity.

Melodic instrument playability –

We then study the playability of generations in terms of physical constraints of the melodic instrument. Unlike generic instruments such as synthesizers
[
8
,
10
]
, orchestral instruments are limited in their range and usually play in a specific register
[
17
]
.

To evaluate such playability, we generate five extracts from the same original piece without textural control attributes and assign the melody to an instrument.
Based on our pitch class-based tokenization, we let the model infer the
Octave
tokens of the melody notes, while the other components (pitch class and duration) are enforced.
As presented in Table
II
, the model manages to associate these instruments with their usual register.
However, while the difference between the generated average pitch and its middle-range note is below a fourth for woodwinds and trumpet, the average generated pitch for the cello and the violin are much lower, as much as a sixth lower than the midpoint note of the violin’s full register. Violin parts and, more generally, string parts, are indeed typically written below the extreme high register of the instrument
[
18
, p. 52]
.

V
Conclusion & Future directions

In this study, we present METEOR, a first step towards texture-based controllable multi-track style transfer with a focus on melodic fidelity.
The models can achieve melodic fidelity and playability, with control over texture attributes. We show that the melody constraints, as well as the choice of the instrumentation impact the controllability of the model.

Our study focuses on bar-level and track-level controllability, discarding piece-level controllability
[
1
]
. Future work towards multi-level multi-track style transfer may include a model able to perform style transfer at these three levels.
From a musical perspective, although the model succeeds in ensuring that melodic instruments fit their physical constraints, the playability of the accompaniment has not been thoroughly studied and is also overlooked in multi-track music generation studies. Ensuring accompaniment playability in relation to instrumental constraints, timbre effects, and instrument groupings
[
19
]
, would be a significant advancement towards automatic humanly playable orchestration.
