Respiratory syncytial virus positivity, seasonal trends and risk factors among children under five with acute respiratory infection in Western Democratic Republic of Congo, 2022–2023
Authors: Grace M Mufwaya, Edith Nkwembe, Marc K Yambayamba, Youdhie Ituneme, Leonie Manya, Saleh Muhemedi, Pelagie Babakazo, Steve Ahuka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), the influenza sentinel surveillance network established in 2007 has primarily focused on influenza and, more recently, COVID-19. However, limited data are available on the epidemiology of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), one of the leading causes of lower respiratory infections in young children globally. This study aimed to describe RSV positivity rates, seasonal patterns, molecular subtypes, and sociodemographic correlates among children under five years of age.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cross-sectional analysis using data from the Influenza Sentinel Surveillance System (ISSS) collected between 2022 and 2023. Children under five years presenting with acute respiratory infections were enrolled at six sentinel sites in Kinshasa and Matadi. Oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal swabs were collected and transported in viral transport medium to the National Institute of Biomedical Research (INRB) for molecular detection of RSV by real-time RT-PCR, followed by subtyping into RSV-A and RSV-B. Data were analysed using R software (version 4.0.4). Univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were used to assess associations between RSV infection and sociodemographic factors (age, sex, site, and year), with site included as a fixed effect.ResultsA total of 1,468 samples were analysed, of which 20.9% tested positive for RSV. Overall, 772 (53%) participants were males, the median age of RSV-positive children was 12 months (IQR: 18). Over the study period, RSV-B accounted for 52% of cases, predominantly affecting infants under nine months of age. RSV circulation exhibited a clear seasonal trend, with cases increased during the rainy season. We also noted a clear alternation between RSV-A and RSV-B subtypes across the two years. In the multivariable model, gender and year of data collection were significantly associated with RSV positivity: males were less likely to be infected than females (aOR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.59–0.98, p = 0.036), and samples collected in 2023 had reduced odds of RSV infection compared with those from 2022 (aOR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.53–0.91, p = 0.007).ConclusionRSV remains an important cause of acute respiratory infections among children under five in the DRC, with distinct seasonal variation and subtype alternation. Integrating systematic RSV testing into national surveillance and enhancing genomic monitoring will be essential to guide targeted prevention and support future vaccine introduction efforts.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12224-3.Keywords: Acute respiratory infection, Respiratory syncytial virus, Children, Democratic Republic of Congo

Background

Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) causes an estimated 33.1 million cases of lower respiratory infections globally, leading to 3.2 million hospitalisations and nearly 120,000 deaths. More than 95% of these deaths occur in low-resource settings [1]. Almost half of these deaths occur in infants younger than six months. This highlights the urgent need for stronger public health measures against RSV [2, 3]. RSV is an enveloped, non-segmented, single-stranded negative-sense RNA virus of the Pneumoviridae family [4], named after syncytia, multinucleate structures resulting from the fusion of infected respiratory epithelial cells [5–7]. RSV has several surface proteins that are essential in the infection process, including fusion protein (F), attachment glycoprotein (G) and SH [8, 9]. It is classified into two antigenic and genetic subgroups, A and B, differentiated mainly by the variability of the G protein [9]. Subgroup A comprises 22 genotypes and subgroup B includes 37 genotypes. RSV preferentially targets the ciliated epithelial cells of the respiratory tract, contributing to its pathogenicity [9, 10].

RSV is responsible for 15–40% of pneumonia and bronchiolitis cases in children [11, 12], and contributes significantly to the onset of bronchitis and other severe respiratory infections. The majority of infections occur during the first two years of life [2, 9], with greater severity in infants, the elderly and immunocompromised individuals [6, 7, 13]. RSV circulation is seasonal, occurring in autumn and winter in temperate regions, and during the rainy season in tropical areas [6, 14]. RSV symptoms are often non-specific and range from mild influenza-like illness (ILI) to severe acute respiratory infection (SARI). This makes it difficult to distinguish clinically from other respiratory pathogens [12, 14].

Recent data confirm that Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) remains a leading cause of acute lower respiratory infections (ALRI) and hospitalizations among young children across Africa. A systematic review and meta-analysis covering studies from 2002 to 2022 found a pooled RSV prevalence of ≈ 23% (95% CI: 20%–25%) among children < 18 years presenting with acute respiratory illness, with highest rates among inpatients (≈ 28%) and among those with lower respiratory tract disease [15]. The similar figures were reported for on children under five years in sub-Saharan Africa [16]. Although the DRC has had an influenza sentinel surveillance system (ISSS) since 2007, data on RSV remain limited because previous studies have focused primarily on influenza. The infection is largely under-diagnosed and frequently confused with other viral or bacterial respiratory diseases such as influenza, COVID-19 recently or bacterial pneumonia in clinical practice [17]. As a result, it is often treated inappropriately, through the systematic use of antibiotics as first-line treatment, thus contributing to antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [18, 19]. A previous study on a general population in Kinshasa found that RSV accounted for 13.6% of respiratory infections, especially in hospitalized patients [14]. However, no study has yet documented the frequency of RSV infections among children under five years of age, a population particularly vulnerable to severe respiratory illness. Furthermore, few studies have explored the seasonality of respiratory infections such as influenza, which is known to be more prevalent during the rainy season, typically occurring between September and May in the DRC [20]. In contrast, the seasonal pattern of RSV infection in the country remains largely unknown [21].

Using the ISSS data, this study aims to describe the frequency and the seasonality of RSV and identify the sociodemographic factors associated with the risk of acute respiratory infections (ARI) in children under five in the Western of DRC.

Methods

Study design and settings

We conducted a prospective cross-section study using the DRC influenza sentinel surveillance system (ISSS) data covering the period from January 2022 to December 2023. During the study period DRC ISSS covered six sites in two provinces. A detailed description of the system is available elsewhere [22]. All these sites located in Kinshasa and Kongo central province (Matadi) were included. Five sites were in Kinshasa, these included the Provincial General Reference Hospital (HPGRK) previously called Mama Yemo, the Kalembe-Lembe Paediatric Hospital (HPKLL), the Kingasani and Boyambi hospitals and the Selembao Reference Hospital (Fig. 1). However, the later opened in 2023 and was not fully functional. In Kongo central the Kinkanda General Reference Hospital located in Matadi, the capital city was also included.

Data collection and storage

The study included children under five years presenting with ILI or SARI, recruited from sentinel sites in Kinshasa and Matadi during the collection period. Inclusion criteria followed World Health Organization (WHO) case definitions [23]. ILI was defined as measured fever of ≥ 38 °C and cough with onset within the last 10 days. SARI was defined an acute respiratory infection with history fever or measured fever of ≥ 38 °C and cough with onset within the last 10 days and requiring hospitalization [23, 24].

The variables of interest for this study included the socio-demographic factors such as age, gender, data collection site, ARI syndrome, and RSV PCR results (sample positivity and RSV subtype detected). These were collected using the sentinel surveillance standardised form. The seasonality was determined using precipitation and temperature data from the World Bank climate knowledge portal [25], the rainy season was considered as the period from September to May each year, and the dry season from June to August (monthly precipitation and temperature figure are provided in supplementary material).

Nasal and oropharyngeal swabs were collected by trained nurses within 10 days of symptom onset from eligible patients and stored at + 4 °C in 3 ml of viral transport medium (Universal Transport Medium; COPAN Diagnostics Inc.) [26]. Samples were transported to the INRB laboratory under cold chain conditions at + 4 to + 8 °C, in compliance with biosafety standards, for detection of RSV by RT-PCR. At the laboratory, samples were immediately processed to detect, identify and characterise the virus. Aliquots were stored at -80 °C for further analysis. We included all the samples collected during the study period.

Laboratory analysis

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) detection was performed in two stages by real-time RT-qPCR, using standardised protocols from the CDC in Atlanta (CDC Respiratory Syncytial Virus Real Time Subtyping Panel (RUO) kit). The first step was to detect the overall presence of RSV (pan-RSV), followed by differentiation of RSV A and RSV B subtypes in positive samples. The protocol used for sample processing and interpretation is provided in supplemental materials.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel 2016 and analysed using R software (version 4.0.4). Age was summarised by median and IQR, while categorical variables were presented as percentage of the frequency of the outcome of interest over the total number of samples, we also used a graph for visualisation of the seasonal trends. Comparisons between groups were made using the chi-square test, with a significance level of 5%. We computed a univariable and fixed effects multivariable logistic regression model to adjust for all the socio-demographic factors included in our dataset (age groups, gender, data collection site, and year of data collection) with data collection site as a fixed effect informed by the literature [27]. Standard errors and confidence intervals were adjusted for clustering by site using a robust sandwich estimator [28]. We adopted a full model approach for consistency across analysis and to preserve interpretability [29].

Results

A total of 1,468 participants were included in the analysis, of whom 307 (20.9%) tested RSV-positive and 1,161 (79.1%) tested RSV-negative. The median age of the patients was 12 months (IQR: 18). The age distribution was comparable between groups (p = 0.30), 517 (35%) were aged 0–9 months, 459 (31%) aged 10–23 months, and 492 (34%) aged ≥ 24 months.

Overall, 772 (53%) participants were male and 691 (47%) females, with 5 participants missing data on gender. RSV-positive cases included a slightly higher proportion of females (52%) compared with RSV-negative cases (48%) (p = 0.046). Specimens were collected primarily at HPKLL (969; 66%), followed by CH Kingasani (344; 23,4%), HPGRK (88; 6.0%), Matadi (35; 2.4%), CH Boyambi (30; 2%), and Selembao (2; 0.1%). The distribution of RSV status differed significantly by collection site (p = 0.024). Most participants presented with influenza-like illness (ILI; 956; 65%) compared to severe acute respiratory infection (SARI; 512; 35%), with no significant difference between RSV-positive and RSV-negative groups (p = 0.14). Data collection occurred in 2022 (541; 37%) and 2023 (927; 63%), with a significant different in the proportion of RSV-positive cases in 2022 of 45% compared to 55% in 2023 (55%) (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Among 1,468 samples, 307 (20.9%) tested positive for RSV. Subtype B was the most frequently detected (160 cases; 52%), followed by subtype A (122 cases; 39.7%) and co-infections with both subtypes (25 cases; 8.1%). The highest positivity rate was in the 0–9 months age group (120 cases; 39%), followed by children aged 24 to 60 months (97 cases; 32%) and those aged 10–23 months (90 cases; 29%). In the 0–9 months group (n = 517; 35% of total samples), the positivity rate was 39%, with overall, 3.7% testing positive for RSV A, 4% for RSV B, and 0.5% co-infected. Among children aged 10–23 months (n = 459; 31%), RSV B was more common (3.5%) than RSV A (2.3%), and co-infections were rare (0.3%) overall. In the 24–60 months group (n = 492; 34%), detection rates were 2.3% for RSV A, 3.3% for RSV B, and 1% for co-infections. RSV detection rates were similar across genders. However, co-infection with both subtypes was slightly more frequent in girls (1.1%) than in boys (0.5%) (data not shown). The difference in positivity rates between age groups was not statistically significant (p = 0.3) (Table 1).

Between January and May 2022, RSV A predominated, accounting for 90.5% of positive cases (86/95), followed by RSV B (7.4%, 7/95) and co-infections (2.1%, 2/95). Between October 2022 and May 2023, a reversal in trends was observed: RSV B largely dominated with 89.9% of cases (134/149), while RSV A represented only 2.7% (4/149) and co-infections 7.4% (11/149). Between November and December 2023, RSV A became predominant (52.5%), followed by RSV B (27.2%) and co-infections (20.3%). Overall, RSV positivity significantly increased during the rainy season, reaching 27.2%, compared to 1.1% in the dry season.

In the multivariable model, gender and year of data collection were statistically significant predictors of RSV positivity. Male patients had lower odds of RSV infection compared with females (adjusted OR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.59–0.98, p = 0.036). Patients sampled in 2023 also had reduced odds of RSV positivity compared with those sampled in 2022 (adjusted OR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.53–0.91, p = 0.007). No significant associations were observed for age group, data collection site, or case type after adjustment (Table 2).

Discussion

RSV molecular subtyping is a key tool in the surveillance of acute respiratory infections, providing valuable information on the dynamics of transmission and the distribution of the virus in communities [27]. The aim of this study was to describe the positivity and the seasonality of RSV and identify the sociodemographic factors associated with the risk of acute respiratory infections (ARI) in children under five in the Western of DRC. The RSV positivity rate was 20.9% (95% CI: 18.8–23.2%). The median age of the children was 12 months (2–60 months, IQR: 18 months). Infants aged 0–9 months accounted for most RSV cases (39.4%, or 121 out of 307 infections). We noted that RSV B was the most frequently detected subtype, accounting for 52% of positive cases (95% CI: 46–58%), compared to 40% for RSV A. Regarding the severity of RSV infections, most participants presented with influenza-like illness (ILI; 956; 65%) compared with severe acute respiratory infection (SARI; 512; 35%), with no significant difference between RSV-positive and RSV-negative groups (p = 0.14). The study revealed co-circulation of RSV A and B subtypes, with alternating dominance depending on the period. RSV A predominated between January and May 2022 (90.5% of cases), corresponding to the rainy season, while RSV B became dominant from October 2022 to May 2023 (89.9%). In this study, gender and year of data collection emerged as significant predictors of RSV positivity, while age group, type of case, and collection site showed no meaningful associations.

The positivity rate in this study is higher than reported in other contexts. In the Central African Republic, Komoyo et al. observed a positivity rate of 8% (312/3,903), including 4.7% for RSV B, between 2015 and 2018 as part of sentinel influenza surveillance [9]. In the DRC, Kapandji et al. reported an overall positivity rate of 13.6% (23/169) in 2019, with a rate of 10.7% in children under five [14]. In China, Zhang et al. found a positivity rate of 17.3% (3,449/19,898), also with a dominance of RSV B (53.3%) over the period 2014–2018 [30]. The differences observed between the studies can be explained by various factors, in particular the post-pandemic impact, the study period, the duration of the data collection period, and the targeted population. Easing of COVID-19 public health measures, including lockdowns and social distancing, has been linked to a resurgence in respiratory infections such as RSV [24, 30]. This study covers a longer period and includes a larger sample than Kapandji et al. [14], allowing for better insight into RSV seasonality and positivity rate. The majority of confirmed cases were from the Kalembe Lembe Paediatric Hospital (HPKLL), followed by the Kingasani Hospital (CH KINGASANI), with 15.5% (227/1464) and 4.1% (60/1464) respectively. These two hospitals cover a region with low socioeconomic profile and high population density which are key risk factors [27].

Gender differences in RSV infection risk have been observed in multiple studies [31], although the direction and magnitude of the effect vary across populations. Our finding indicate that males were less likely to test positive for RSV compared to females is consistent with some reports. For instance, Ursin et al. reported sex-based differences in RSV susceptibility and immune responses, possibly linked to hormonal influences and differences in airway size or physiology [32]. Other studies, however, have reported higher hospitalization rates for RSV among males, highlighting the complexity of sex-related immune responses and social factors [33]. Cultural or behavioral patterns affecting health-seeking such as parental decisions on when and where to seek care may also contribute to the observed pattern in this setting. Further investigation into immunological and sociocultural mechanisms could help clarify these dynamics [33].

The decline in RSV positivity in 2023 compared to 2022 may reflect temporal or epidemiologic shifts in virus circulation. RSV activity can vary year-to-year depending on climatic conditions, population immunity, and changes in public health interventions or non-pharmaceutical measures. The residual effects of COVID-19 mitigation strategies (e.g., masking, physical distancing, and school closures) have been reported to alter RSV seasonality in multiple regions [34]. In Kinshasa and other sites, the easing of pandemic restrictions may have influenced patterns of virus transmission differently across years. Alternatively, local factors—such as differences in surveillance intensity, testing availability, or health facility attendance—might also explain part of the variation. Longitudinal monitoring over multiple seasons will be critical to determine whether this represents a temporary fluctuation or a sustained trend [35].

Children below 9 months of age accounted for more than one third of infections. Infants are especially vulnerable due to immature immunity, narrower airways prone to inflammation, and close-contact transmission. Social factors, such as promiscuity and low levels of parental education, can also increase the risk of exposure [36]. These results are consistent with those reported in other studies, notably in the Central African Republic (CAR) in children under six months of age [9], in the DRC in children under two years of age [14], and in China [30], all of which confirm the major role of RSV in severe respiratory infections in young children, particularly infants.

RSV B was the most frequently detected subtype in our study, accounting for 52% (95% CI: 46–58%) of cases, consistent with reports from the Central African Republic (49.7%) [9], China (53.3%) [30], and India [37]. RSV A predominated during the rainy season (January–May 2022; 90.5% of cases), whereas RSV B dominated from October 2022 to May 2023 (89.9%).

Subtype circulation varies by geography and season. In tropical regions such as the Central African Republic, the DRC [14], and China, RSV activity peaks during the rainy season [9, 30], while in India, RSV A predominated in 2016–2017 before being replaced by RSV B in 2018 [38–40]. In Nepal, RSV A was strongly predominant (94.3%) between September and December 2021, with minimal RSV B circulation [41]. These patterns reflect not only the influence of climatic factors humidity, temperature, and rainfall on viral transmission but also immuno-epidemiological dynamics [34]. Evidence from immunological and molecular studies suggests that infection with one RSV subtype induces homotypic immunity providing protection against reinfection with the same subtype for a certain period but only partial heterotypic immunity against the alternate subtype [42]. As a result, large-scale circulation of RSV A in 2022 would have reduced the pool of individuals susceptible to this subtype while leaving a partially immune population still vulnerable to RSV B, facilitating its dominance in the following season. This pattern of alternating subtype predominance has been consistently documented in multiple settings and is thought to result from this interplay between subtype-specific population immunity and viral transmission dynamics [42, 43]. The findings from the present study therefore align with global evidence, underscoring the role of transient, subtype-specific herd immunity in shaping RSV epidemiology across consecutive seasons [42, 44].

Limitations

The lack of genomic analysis of RSV in this study limits our understanding of the genetic diversity of circulating strains and their possible link with clinical severity. Conducting the study only in western DRC limits national generalisability, as environmental and epidemiological conditions vary regionally. Finally, the study is based on ISSS data, therefore not accounting for treated at home, asymptomatic cases or those treated outside the surveillance network. This selection bias could lead to an underestimation of the true positivity rate of RSV in the paediatric population and limit the statical representativeness of the results.

However, this study is the first in the DRC context to document the subtypes of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and their seasonal characteristics. Integrated into the sentinel surveillance system, it strengthens the capacity for early detection of epidemics and the orientation of public health interventions. By specifically targeting children under the age of five, it provides valuable data on a group at high risk of severe forms of the virus. Finally, the inclusion of both outpatients (ILI) and inpatients (SARI) provides a more complete understanding of the epidemiology of RSV in this population.

Conclusion

This is the first study in the DRC to detail RSV subtype circulation among children under five. RSV, especially subtype B, is a major cause of respiratory infections in young children, with cases peaking during the rainy season. The alternating seasonal dominance of RSV subtypes A and B underscores the need for continuous surveillance to track viral trends and inform public health interventions. Although co-infections are rare, their presence in older children calls for increased vigilance in the community settings. By targeting particularly vulnerable paediatric population of both outpatients (ILI) and inpatients (SARI), this study provides a solid basis for planning appropriate preventive interventions. It argues in favour of strengthening molecular surveillance, stopping misuse of antibiotic, extending geographical coverage and integrating RSV into national child health priorities including vaccination.
