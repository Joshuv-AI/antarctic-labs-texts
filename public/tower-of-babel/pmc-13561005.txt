Anti-CTLA-4 antibody potentiates the antitumor effect of armed oncolytic virus OVM18 via enhancing intratumoral IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells
Authors: Xueying Lin, Jifu Zhang, Zhen Fan, Jingyu Yang, Shiming Yi, Hanrui Wang, Huizhen Zou, Linyi Hu, Wenbo Zhu, Yuan Lin, Jun Hu, Jing Cai, Guangmei Yan, Jiankai Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOncolytic virus M1 encoding a mutant IL-18 decoy (OVM18) represents a novel virotherapy that integrates selective oncolysis with localized activation of the IL-18 pathway. However, the heterogeneity of therapeutic responses suggests that host immune determinants influence its efficacy.MethodsTo elucidate these determinants, we performed integrated immune profiling using bulk and single-cell transcriptomics, flow cytometry, and T cell receptor (TCR) repertoire analyses. The functional contribution of specific immune subsets was validated using Batf3⁻/⁻ mice and FTY720-mediated lymphocyte trafficking blockade. Potential synergistic immunotherapies were identified through transcriptomic database screening and confirmed in tumor-bearing mice receiving combined OVM18 and immune checkpoint blockade.ResultsWe identified intratumoral IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells as an important effector population, whose IL-18R1 expression and intratumoral abundance positively correlate with OVM18 efficacy. Integrated single-cell RNA-sequencing and cytometry analyses revealed that IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells are clonally expanded, tumor antigen-enriched, polyfunctional cytotoxic T cells. Their generation requires conventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC1)-dependent priming within tumor-draining lymph nodes, followed by trafficking into tumors to mediate antitumor responses. Notably, CTLA-4 blockade enhances cDC1-derived IL-12 and promotes IL-18R1+CD8+ T-cell expansion, thereby overcoming resistance to OVM18 therapy. Combination treatment with OVM18 and anti-CTLA-4 enhances intratumoral IL-18R1+CD8+ T cell infiltration, delays tumor progression, and prolongs survival in poor-responsive models.ConclusionsOur findings establish IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells as an important effector population in OVM18 therapy, thereby providing a strong rationale for combining OVM18 with CTLA-4 blockade to overcome therapeutic resistance and achieve durable antitumor responses.

Introduction

Immunotherapy has redefined the therapeutic landscape of oncology, delivering unprecedented and durable benefits in tumor patients. Landmark advances in immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting PD-1 and CTLA-4, which have been recognized by the 2018 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, have proved that activation of the immune system can achieve transformative clinical outcomes.1 However, despite their success, a large proportion of patients fail to respond or develop drug resistance after treatment, underscoring the need for complementary strategies.2 3

Oncolytic viruses (OVs) are emerging as a powerful class of immunotherapeutics that combine direct tumor lysis with systemic immune activation. By selectively infecting and lysing malignant cells, OVs release tumor-associated antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns, igniting both innate and adaptive antitumor immune responses.4 5 Furthermore, their engineering flexibility enables delivery of potent immunostimulatory payloads, exemplified by the herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1)-based talimogene laherparepvec, which locally expresses granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor to promote dendritic cell (DC) maturation and antigen presentation.6

The oncolytic virus M1 (OVM), a single-stranded RNA virus of the alphavirus genus, represents a new generation of tumor-selective virotherapeutics. Since its identification in 2014, OVM has been demonstrated to possess some exceptional properties, including robust tumor selectivity, rapid intratumoral replication, and efficient transgene expression.7–10 Based on this platform, we engineered M1-mutIL-18 (OVM18) by encoding a decoy mutant of IL-18 binding protein (mutIL-18) to achieve localized IL-18 signaling activation.11 OVM18 markedly drives clonal expansion of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and robust IFN-γ production, eliciting potent and durable tumor regressions. However, therapeutic responses differ substantially across tumor models, with complete remissions in some but refractory in others, highlighting the presence of host determinants that govern therapeutic efficacy.

IL-18 is a pleiotropic proinflammatory cytokine whose signaling via the IL-18R1/IL-18R2 heterodimer orchestrates broad immune programs, including Th1 polarization and cytotoxic T lymphocyte activation.12–15 Engagement of the IL-18 receptor recruits MyD88, triggering the IRAK-TRAF6 axis and NF-κB activation, culminating in transcription of effector cytokines such as Ifng, Tnf, Il4 and Il13. Consistent with its central role, genetic ablation of Il18r1 completely abolishes the antitumor efficacy of decoy-resistant IL-18 in vivo.16

In this study, we define IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells as a cytotoxic T-cell subset enriched for tumor antigen specificity, whose IL-18R1 expression and intratumoral abundance positively correlate with OVM18 efficacy. In responsive tumors, conventional type 1 DCs (cDC1s) in tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLNs) drive the priming of antigen-specific IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells, which subsequently infiltrate the tumor microenvironment (TME) to mediate antitumor effects. Strikingly, CTLA-4 blockade, but not PD-1 blockade under the conditions tested, overcomes OVM18 resistance by licensing cDC1s to generate IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells. Our findings reveal IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells as an important effector population in OVM18 therapy and establish a rational combination strategy to overcome resistance, offering a framework for precision deployment of oncolytic virotherapy.

Methods

Cell lines

RM-1, B16-F10, CT26, EMT6, LLC, BHK-21, and Vero cell lines were originally purchased from the American Type Culture Collection. MC38 and GL261 cells were kindly provided by Guangzhou Virotech. After acquisition, all cell lines were cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen at low passage numbers, and low-passage cells were expanded to establish working cell banks for subsequent experiments. All cell lines were verified to be mycoplasma-negative on cell bank establishment, and murine tumor cell lines were authenticated via short tandem repeat profiling.

For in vitro culture, B16-F10 and RM1 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Carlsbad, California, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco) and 1×penicillin–streptomycin solution (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, California, USA). The remaining cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) (Gibco, Carlsbad, California, USA). All cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ and used at low passages throughout the experiments.

Virus

The alphavirus OVM18 used in this study was kindly provided by Guangzhou Virotech Pharmaceutical Technology. For viral amplification, Vero cells were utilized for virus propagation. Briefly, Vero cells were seeded in T75 culture flasks and maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Before viral infection, the culture medium was switched to virus production-serum-free medium (VP-SFM) (Gibco, #11681020) supplemented with 10% minimum essential medium non-essential amino acid solution (NEAA) (Gibco, #11140050) and 20% GlutaMAX supplement (Gibco, #35050079). On reaching approximately 80% confluence, cells were infected with OVM18 at a titer of 100 CCID50. Infected Vero cells were cultured continuously for 48–72 hours until obvious cytopathic effects were observed.

Cell culture supernatants were subsequently harvested and centrifuged at 5000×g to eliminate cellular debris. After centrifugation, the collected supernatants were sterile-filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane to generate purified OVM18 viral stocks, which were preserved at −80°C for subsequent experiments. The functional titers of prepared viral stocks were quantified via a CCID50 assay using BHK-21 cells.

Quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA from tumor tissues was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The FastKing One Step Kit (TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China) was utilized to perform absolute quantitative RT-PCR following the manufacturer’s protocol. The copy number of OVM18 per μg RNA was calculated from the standard curve generated using serial dilutions of OVM RNA standards. The primer sequences used were as follows:

M1-NS1-F: GTTCCAACAGGCGTCACCATC

M1-NS1-R: ACACATTCTTGTCTAGCACAGTCC

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (TargetMol #C0005) following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, cells were seeded into multiwell plates and treated as indicated. After treatment, 10 µL of the CCK-8 solution was added to each well. The plates were then incubated at 37°C for 1–4 hours. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek).

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were conducted with approval from the Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Sun Yat-sen University. Wild-type C57BL/6 and BALB/c mice were obtained from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center, while Batf3 knockout mice were supplied by Shanghai Model Organisms Center. All animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with free access to standard diet and water.

For tumor inoculation, 1×10⁶ B16-F10, EMT6, CT26, or RM1 cells, or 2×10⁶ Lewis Lung Carcinoma (LLC) or MC38 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right hind flank of recipient mice. When tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm³, mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups. Oncolytic virus was administered via intravenous injection once daily for a total of five doses, at a titer of 3×10⁶ CCID50 per mouse, as indicated in the corresponding figure legends. For immune checkpoint blockade, anti-PD-1 antibody (clone RMP1-14, Bio X Cell) and anti-CTLA-4 antibody (clone 9H10, Bio X Cell) were administered intraperitoneally at 300 µg per mouse every 3 days in the RM1, LLC, and B16-F10 tumor models. FTY720 (20 µg per mouse, SML0700, Sigma) was injected intraperitoneally on a daily basis. Control groups received sterile PBS or isotype control antibodies.

Tumor growth was periodically monitored with a digital caliper, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: length×width2×0.5. Individual tumor size and body weight were recorded throughout the experimental period. In accordance with the guidelines approved by the Sun Yat-sen University Animal Ethics Committee, mice were euthanized when tumor volume reached 2000 mm³, or when other humane endpoints were met, including tumor ulceration, impaired mobility, severe body weight loss, or signs of distress. All survival analyses were performed based on these predefined ethical endpoints. Mice with no palpable tumor mass were regarded as fully cured.

Analysis of immunophenotype

Single-cell suspensions were isolated from harvested tumors, tumor draining lymph nodes (TDLNs), peripheral blood, and spleens following previously established protocols.17 18 Prior to immunophenotypic staining, all cellular samples were treated with Fixable Viability Stain 700 (BD, 564997) for dead cell exclusion, followed by Fc receptor blockade using commercial blocking antibodies (BD, 553142) to minimize non-specific binding. For lymphocyte cytokine detection, cell suspensions were stimulated with Leukocyte Activation Cocktail containing Golgi-plug protein transport inhibitor (BD, 550583) for 4 hours at 37°C. For detection of intracellular IL-12 in DCs, samples were treated with 1 µg/mL lipopolysaccharid (LPS) (Sigma, L4391) combined with Monensin (BD, 554724) for a 4-hour incubation at 37°C to facilitate intracellular cytokine accumulation. For intracellular staining, cell fixation and permeabilization were performed utilizing the Transcription Factor Buffer Set (BD, 562574).

Subsequent cell surface and intracellular staining was conducted with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies obtained from BD Biosciences or BioLegend, including CD45 (30-f11), CD3e (145-2C11), CD8α (53-6.7), CD4 (RM4-5), NK1.1 (PK136), IL-18Rα (A17071D), IFN-γ (XMG1.2), GZMB (QA18A28), TNF-α (MP6-XT22), CD44 (IM7), CD11c (HL3), I-A/I-E (2G9), XCR1 (ZET), CD80 (16-10A1), CD86 (GL1), IL-12 (C15.6), CD39 (Y23-1185), PD-1 (29F.1A12) and Biotin H-2K(b) OVA Monomer (SIINFEKL) (280051). All fluorescence signals were acquired on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). Undyed control samples and fluorescence minus one controls were applied for accurate and unbiased gating during postacquisition data analysis.

Bulk RNA-sequencing

All bulk RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analyses were performed using tumor tissues collected from multiple murine tumor models, including B16-F10, CT26, RM1, LLC, and EMT6. For sample grouping and biological replicate setting, the control groups consisted of three mice each for the RM1, EMT6, LLC, and B16-F10 models, while the CT26 control group included four mice. For the OVM18-treated groups, three mice were used for RM1, EMT6, LLC, and B16-F10 models, and four mice were included in the CT26 cohort.

Total RNA was isolated from the above tumor tissue samples, and cDNA library construction and high-throughput RNA sequencing were commercially performed by the Beijing Genomics Institute (BGI, China) using the BGISEQ sequencing platform. Low-quality sequencing datasets were strictly filtered and excluded from subsequent analyses to guarantee data reliability. Gene expression normalization and differential expression profiling were implemented via the DESeq2 R package. All bioinformatic analyses of transcriptomic datasets were conducted using the BGI Dr. Tom multiomics data mining platform (https://biosys.bgi.com/#/report/login). The RNA-seq data have been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE334005 and GSE334006 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo).

Single-cell RNA-seq and scTCR-seq

For single-cell transcriptomic and TCR profiling analysis, tumor-infiltrating CD45+ immune cells were sorted and purified from B16-F10 tumor tissues, with three independent biological replicate mice included per experimental condition. To ensure sufficient cell input and uniform sample preparation, equal numbers of cells from each individual biological replicate were pooled at the single-cell suspension stage prior to library construction. Isolated CD45+ cells were loaded onto the 10X Genomics Chromium instrument (Pleasanton, California, USA) to generate barcoded single-cell libraries. Both 5’ gene expression libraries and TCR-enriched libraries were constructed from the captured barcoded cDNAs, followed by high-throughput RNA sequencing for all prepared samples.

Initial data quality control and preliminary normalization were performed by NovelBio Co., Ltd. using the NovelBrain Cloud Analysis Platform (https://www.novelbrain.com/home/index). Subsequent standardized single-cell data processing, including cell-level normalization and data regression, was implemented via the Seurat R package (V.4.1.1, https://satijalab.org/seurat/). Specifically, unique molecular identifier counts and mitochondrial gene expression ratios of individual cells were incorporated to regress out technical variations and generate scaled transcriptomic data. Cell type annotation was determined by integrating the expression patterns of top variable genes and well-recognized canonical cell lineage markers. For single-cell TCR sequencing (scTCR-seq) data, sequence alignment and repertoire analysis were conducted using Cell Ranger VDJ V.7.1.0 with the official mouse VDJ reference database provided by 10× Genomics.

Notably, all scRNA-seq analyses presented in this study are purely descriptive. The pooling of biological replicates during sample preparation precluded the evaluation of biological variability between individual animals, and no inferential statistical comparisons based on inter-mouse replicates were performed for these datasets. The scRNA-seq data and scTCR-seq data have been deposited in the GEO database under accession number GSE295936 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo).11

Analysis of public transcriptomic datasets

To independently assess the impact of various anti-tumor therapies on intratumoral Il18r1 expression, we leveraged publicly available transcriptomic data and single-cell sequencing data. Gene expression datasets were retrieved from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). We systematically searched for datasets that profiled tumor tissues from mouse models treated with therapies of interest, including chemotherapy (5-fluorouracil (5-FU) and Cisplatin), targeted immunotherapy (anti-IL-17 therapy), ICI therapy (anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 therapy), molecularly targeted therapy (Olaparib and trametinib therapy), and CAR-T cell therapy.

The specific GEO dataset accession numbers analyzed in this study are GSE188481 (5-FU and cisplatin),19 GSE138306 (trametinib),20 GSE290828 (Olaparib),21 GSE165797 (CAR-T),22 23 and GSE146027 (anti-PD-1, anti-CTLA-4, and anti-IL-17).24 25 Raw data or preprocessed gene expression matrices were downloaded. For raw data, we performed background correction, normalization, and summarization using the robust multiarray average algorithm or a platform-specific standard pipeline. When preprocessed matrices were used, no further normalization was applied.

Subsequent analysis was conducted using R software (V.4.5.1) and Bioconductor packages. The Il18r1 gene expression value (probe-level data were collapsed to gene symbols by selecting the probe with the maximum variance) was extracted for each sample. Samples were grouped based on their treatment condition (eg, therapy vs control). Differences in Il18r1 expression between treatment and control groups within each dataset were assessed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. All statistical comparisons across treatment groups were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction to control for multiple testing. With nine comparisons performed, the corrected significance threshold (α) was set at approximately 0.0056 (0.05/9). Only therapies with P values below this adjusted threshold were considered to significantly upregulate Il18r1 expression. It should be noted that all analyses based on these public omics datasets are exploratory in nature, and further validation with independent experimental cohorts is required to confirm the observed associations.

ELISpot

B16-F10 tumor tissues were harvested from mice on day 7 after OVM18 administration. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes were isolated via Ficoll density gradient separation, followed by two rounds of washing with PBS supplemented with 2% FBS. Cell suspensions were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies targeting CD45, CD3, CD8 and IL-18R1 for 30 minutes. After washing, IL-18R1⁺CD8+ T cells and IL-18R1⁻CD8+ T cells were sorted using a BD FACSAria III cell sorter.

Prior to the co-culture assay, B16-F10 tumor cells were prestimulated with 50 ng/mL recombinant mouse IFN-γ protein (MCE, HY-P7071) in vitro for 24 hours. Excess soluble IFN-γ was thoroughly removed by extensive PBS washing, and treated tumor cells were then harvested via trypsin digestion. For IFN-γ ELISpot detection (Mabtech, 3321–4 APT), 1,000 sorted IL-18R1⁺CD8+ or IL-18R1⁻CD8+ T cells were seeded into each well, followed by the addition of 330 IFN-γ preconditioned B16-F10 tumor cells, establishing an effector-to-target ratio of 3:1. The co-culture system was incubated at 37°C under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ for 24 hours, after which IFN-γ-positive spot-forming units were quantified.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism V.9 (GraphPad Software). Statistical comparison procedures and animal numbers for each experiment are described in the corresponding figure legends. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), two-way ANOVA, two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, two-tailed paired Student’s t-test, Pearson correlation, or log-rank testing (see figure legends) were used to determine statistical significance, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparison testing where appropriate (*p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, ****p<0.0001). No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size.

Results

IL-18R1+CD8+ T cell predicts anti-tumor efficacy of OVM18 therapy

Previous studies have demonstrated that OVM18 can suppress the growth of multiple murine solid tumors (CT26, B16-F10, MC38, EMT6, GL261) and achieve complete tumor remission in a subset of mice.11 Consistently, intravenous OVM18 treatment significantly inhibited tumor growth in two additional murine models (RM1 and LLC), although no complete responses were observed (figure 1A,B). Across all models, OVM18 displayed robust antitumor activity, yet complete response rates varied substantially (figure 1C), prompting investigation into the determinants of OVM18 therapeutic heterogeneity.

Since viral replication efficiency and direct cytotoxicity are often considered key drivers of oncolytic efficacy, we first assessed whether these parameters correlated with therapeutic outcome. However, neither in vitro cytotoxicity nor in vivo intratumoral replication kinetics showed significant association with complete response rates (online supplemental figure 1A-D). These findings suggest that virus-induced cell death and virus replication are not the primary determinants of OVM18 efficacy, implicating the importance of immunostimulatory mutIL-18 payload. Given that IL-18 signaling requires interaction with the heterodimeric IL-18 receptor (IL-18R1/IL-18R2),15 we analyzed tumor transcriptomes from five murine models. Strikingly, intratumoral Il18r1 mRNA expression positively correlated with OVM18 complete response rates in both control and OVM18-treated groups, whereas Il18r2 mRNA expression did not (figure 1D, online supplemental figure 2A). Moreover, the same trend was observed for intratumoral Il18r1 transcript levels in individual mice (online supplemental figure 2B and C).

Single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) analysis and cytometry revealed that IL-18R1 expression was largely confined to T cells and NK cells (figure 1E, online supplemental figure 3), consistent with prior reports.11 26 To further identify the specific immune cell subsets associated with OVM18 efficacy, we examined IL-18R1 expression on CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells across six tumor models. Notably, IL-18R1 expression on intratumoral CD8+ T cells, evaluated in both PBS group and OVM18 groups (day 7), correlated strongly with complete response rates, whereas no such association was found for CD4+ T cells or NK cells (figure 1F–H, online supplemental figure 4A and C). Importantly, OVM18 treatment induced a significant enrichment of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells in the TME,11 and by day 7, this infiltration correlated with complete remissions (figure 1K). In contrast, basal IL-18R1+CD8+ T cell levels in untreated tumors showed no such correlation (figure 1I). No significant correlation was observed for the infiltration of IL-18R1+CD4+ T cells or IL-18R1+ NK cells (online supplemental figure 4B and D). Meanwhile, we also performed correlation analyses using IL-18R1 expression levels and IL-18R1+ T-cell frequencies from individual mouse tumors as independent data points. These analyses yielded overall trends that were consistent with those obtained from averaged data (online supplemental figures 5 and 6). Interestingly, the IL-18R1 expression level on NK cells and abundance of IL-18R1+ NK cells in individual tumor samples exhibited a negative correlation with the complete response rate of OVM18 therapy (online supplemental figure 7). Moreover, IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells in peripheral blood after OVM18 treatment also showed predictive value: expression of IL-18R1 on CD8+ T cells and the frequency of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells both correlated with tumor progression in the RM1 model, whereas the expression of IL-18R1 on CD4+ T cells and the frequency of IL-18R1+CD4+ T cells did not (figure 1L–O).

Together, these results suggest that surface IL-18R1 levels on CD8+ T cells may serve as a reliable pretreatment baseline indicator of OVM18 responsiveness, whereas the intratumoral frequency of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells at early treatment time points may provide a sensitive on-treatment pharmacodynamic readout for evaluating OVM18 therapeutic performance across multiple tumor models.

IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells represent a tumor antigen-enriched polyfunctional T cell subset

To further characterize the functional features of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells, we first selected a representative responsive tumor model for subsequent analyses. Although the CT26 tumor model features elevated IL-18R1 expression and abundant intratumoral infiltration of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells, the overwhelming predominance of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells results in a scarce IL-18R1⁻ CD8+ T-cell population, which prevents reliable flow cytometric comparison of the two subsets (online supplemental figure 8A and B). Moreover, OVM18 treatment achieves an extremely high complete response rate of nearly 90% in CT26 tumors.11 This strong therapeutic response greatly restricts the yield of intact viable tumor tissue and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes for subsequent functional assays. Therefore, we selected the B16-F10 model, which provides a more balanced distribution of IL-18R1+ and IL-18R1⁻ CD8+ T cells (online supplemental figure 8C and D), enabling more reliable comparative analyses between the two subsets.

Previous evidence has shown that OVM18 drives robust clonal expansion of CD8+ T cells in the TME;11 therefore, we investigated whether IL-18R1 serves as a marker of the expanded clones in the B16-F10 model. TCR sequencing revealed a positive association between Il18r1 expression and clonal size, with highly expanded (“big”) CD8+ T-cell clones exhibiting elevated Il18r1 levels compared with medium or small clones within the TME (figure 2A,B). Differential gene enrichment analysis between IL-18R1+CD8+ and IL-18R1⁻ CD8+ T cells further showed that genes upregulated in IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells were predominantly enriched in cell division-related pathways (figure 2C,D), implicating the elevated proliferation of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells. Consistently, scRNA-seq suggested that IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells expressed higher levels of cytotoxic effector genes (Ifng, Tnf) than their IL-18R1⁻ counterparts (figure 2E,F). Moreover, Il18r1 expression in CD8+ T cells positively correlates with the expression of effector molecules, including Ifng, Gzmb, and Tnf (online supplemental figure 9). In line with the transcriptional signatures, flow cytometry confirmed that IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells in B16-F10 tumors, both in PBS- and OVM18-treated groups, displayed elevated levels of cytotoxic mediators (IFN-γ, TNF-α, GZMB) and a higher frequency of polyfunctional CTLs compared with IL-18R1⁻ cells (figure 2G–N, online supplemental figure 10). Moreover, in the B16-F10-OVA model, IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells were enriched for OVA-specific SIINFEKL-tetramer+ cells as well as CD39+CD44+ cells, a phenotype associated with tumor antigen specificity27 (figure 2O–R). To further evaluate the enrichment of tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells within the IL-18R1+ subset, we sorted IL-18R1⁻ CD8+ T cells and IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells from B16-F10 tumors and co-cultured them with B16-F10 cells in an IFN-γ ELISpot assay. The IL-18R1+CD8+ T cell subset was enriched for tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells (approximately 20%), a frequency markedly higher than that observed in the IL-18R1⁻ CD8+ T cell subset (online supplemental figure 11). Collectively, these findings establish intratumoral IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells as a clonally expanded, functionally activated cytotoxic T-cell subset with enrichment of tumor antigen-specific T cells.

cDC1 drives the formation of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells in TDLN

To further define the origin of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells, we took advantage of the OVM18 treatment model, in which their frequency is significantly enhanced, to first examine whether they arise from local proliferation within tumors or recruitment from peripheral lymphoid organs. OVM18 treatment markedly increased the infiltration of lymphocytes in the TME, including IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells, whereas pharmacologic blockade of lymphocyte egress with FTY720 significantly reduced tumor infiltration of CD45+ immune cells, CD3+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells (figure 3A, online supplemental figure 12A). Notably, while IL-18R1 expression on CD8+ T cells was unaffected, the absolute infiltration of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells was markedly diminished (figure 3B,C), accompanied by a substantial loss of OVM18-mediated tumor control in both B16-F10 and MC38 models (figure 3D). These results indicate that IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells recruited from TDLNs may contribute to OVM18-mediated antitumor efficacy.

Consistently, the proportion of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells in TDLNs was significantly enhanced after OVM18 treatment, whereas IL-18R1+CD4+ T cells remained stable (figure 3E). Phenotypically, these lymph node-derived IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells, either in PBS or OVM18 group, mirrored their intratumoral counterparts by displaying heightened IFN-γ and TNF-α production, increased polyfunctionality, and enrichment of SIINFEKL-tetramer+ or CD39+CD44+ populations (figure 3F–O, online supplemental figure 12B-I). Given that TDLNs are the primary site of cross-priming, we hypothesized that DCs may drive IL-18R1 expression during CD8+ T-cell activation. Indeed, in Batf3⁻/⁻ mice, depletion of conventional type 1 DCs (cDC1s) completely abolished OVM18-driven IL-18R1+CD8+ T-cell expansion in TDLNs without affecting IL-18R1+CD4+ T cells (figure 3E,Q). Moreover, cDC1 deficiency markedly reduced the infiltration of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells into tumors and impaired OVM18-mediated tumor control in the MC38 model (online supplemental figure 12J, figure 3R–T), whereas IL-18R1+CD4+ T cells remained unaffected.

Previous studies have shown that IL-12 can potently promote IL-18R1 expression on T cells.28–30 Consistently, our in vitro experiments showed that IL-12 markedly upregulated IL-18R1 expression on CD8+ T cells (online supplemental figure 13A). Given that cDC1s are well recognized as an important source of IL-12 among DC subsets,31 32 we next examined whether OVM18 treatment affected IL-12 production by DCs, particularly cDC1s, in vivo. In the B16-F10 tumor model, OVM18 treatment significantly increased the frequency of IL-12-producing DCs in TDLNs, especially cDC1s (online supplemental figure 13B-D), whereas no comparable increase was observed in intratumoral DC populations (online supplemental figure 13E and F). This spatial pattern is consistent with our previous findings showing that FTY720-mediated blockade of lymphocyte egress from TDLNs markedly reduced intratumoral IL-18R1+CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and that cDC1 ablation largely abolished the OVM18-induced expansion of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells.

Together, these findings identify cDC1s as critical drivers of IL-18R1+CD8+ T-cell formation in TDLNs and support an IL-12-associated mechanism underlying this process. Once generated, these cells may infiltrate tumors, mediate potent tumor-specific cytotoxicity, and sustain OVM18 efficacy.

Anti-CTLA-4 increases intratumoral infiltration of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells via cDC1

To this end, we performed a literature survey and data mining of tumor single-cell sequencing data and transcriptome datasets from serial antitumor therapies. Comparative analyses revealed that anti-CTLA-4 antibody,24 25 5-FU,19 and CD8+ CAR-T therapy22 23 markedly upregulated intratumoral Il18r1 expression, whereas cisplatin,19 anti-PD-1 antibody,24 25 anti-IL-17 antibody,24 25 trametinib,20 olaparib21 and CD4+ CAR-T therapy22 23 showed little or no effect (figure 4A–G).

Interestingly, both ICIs did not show the same pattern, while anti-CTLA-4 treatment significantly enhanced Il18r1 expression, anti-PD-1 failed. We next interrogated scRNA sequencing datasets from mouse oral carcinoma MOC1esc133 models to further validate this tumor-intrinsic effect of CTLA-4 blockade. The analyses revealed that anti-CTLA-4 treatment significantly elevated Il18r1 expression in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells (figure 4H). Functionally, IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells in anti-CTLA-4-treated MOC1esc1 tumors exhibited enriched cell division-related pathways and displayed transcriptional programs associated with activation, cytotoxicity, and exhaustion compared with their IL-18R1⁻ counterparts (online supplemental figure 14).

Consistent with these findings, flow cytometry analysis confirmed that anti-CTLA-4 treatment increased both the expression of IL-18R1 on CD8+ T cells and the infiltration of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells within the TME (figure 4I–L). In contrast, under the conditions tested (12 mg/kg, every 3 days), anti-PD-1 antibody did not promote IL-18R1+CD8+ T cell accumulation in the RM-1 tumor model (figure 4I–L). Notably, expression of IL-18R1 on CD8+ T cells in TDLNs was decreased by anti-CTLA-4 therapy, indicating that CTLA-4 blockade regulates IL-18R1 expression in a TME-restricted manner (figure 4M–O). We further validated the effects of anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 therapy on IL-18R1+CD8+ T cell abundance in the LLC and B16-F10 models. In both models, anti-PD-1 treatment induced only a modest increase in the abundance of intratumoral IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells, whereas anti-CTLA-4 therapy exerted a more prominent promoting effect (figure 4P–T).

Anti-CTLA-4 blockade disrupts the competitive binding of CTLA-4 to CD80/CD86 costimulatory molecules on antigen-presenting cells, thereby enhancing activation of effector T cells.34 We hypothesize that anti-CTLA-4 also increases the infiltration of IL18R1+CD8+ T cells in the TME via cDC1. In accordance, anti-CTLA-4 significantly enhanced intratumoral DCs infiltration and the expression of co-stimulatory factor CD80/CD86 within RM1 tumor model (figure 5A–D). However, the same enhancement was not observed after anti-PD-1 treatment. Furthermore, IL-12, a cytokine previously reported to potently induce IL-18R1 expression,28 29 was significantly increased in both intratumoral DCs and cDC1s after anti-CTLA-4 treatment, accompanied by an increased frequency of IL-12-positive cells within these populations (figure 5E–H). To further confirm in Batf3-KO mice, we found that cDC1 deficiency abrogated the anti-CTLA-4-driven increase in intratumoral IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells but not IL-18R1+CD4+ T cells (figure 5I–L).

Anti-CTLA-4 enhances the antitumor efficacy of OVM18

Next, we evaluated whether CTLA-4 blockade could potentiate the therapeutic efficacy of OVM18 in the poorly responsive RM1 model. As expected, neither OVM18 nor anti-CTLA-4 monotherapy significantly altered the expression of IL-18R1 on CD8+ T cells or the abundance of infiltrating IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells in TDLNs (figure 6A–D). In striking contrast, the combination of OVM18 and anti-CTLA-4 markedly increased both IL-18R1 expression and the infiltration of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells within TDLNs (figure 6B–D). Consistently, compared with either monotherapy, the combination regimen further promoted intratumoral accumulation of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells, although IL-18R1 expression levels remained unchanged (figure 6E–G). Importantly, this enhanced T-cell infiltration translated into therapeutic benefit: the combination of OVM18 with anti-CTLA-4 significantly inhibited tumor growth and extended survival of tumor-bearing mice (figure 6H,I). In contrast, the combination of anti-PD-1 therapy did not confer further antitumor efficacy (figure 6J,K). We further validated the synergistic effect of anti-CTLA-4 on OVM18 therapy in the LLC and B16-F10 models. In both models, the combination of anti-CTLA-4 and OVM18 significantly increased the intratumoral abundance of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells, further suppressed tumor growth and prolonged mouse survival compared with monotherapy (online supplemental figure 15). Together, these results demonstrate that CTLA-4 blockade facilitates the recruitment of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells, enhancing the cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells, and ultimately driving robust antitumor responses.

Discussion

Our study identifies intratumoral IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells as an important effector population in OVM18 therapy. Primed by cDC1s in TDLNs, IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells display heightened cytotoxicity and enriched tumor antigen specificity, and their abundance in TME reflects treatment responses. Importantly, anti-CTLA-4 therapy synergizes with OVM18 by enhancing cDC1 activity and expanding intratumoral IL-18R1+CD8+ T cell infiltration, whereas PD-1 blockade fails to exert comparable effects under our experimental conditions. Together, these findings establish IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells as an important effector population associated with OVM18 efficacy and highlight CTLA-4 blockade as a rational combination strategy.

IL-18 exerts its potent antitumor effects primarily through IL-18 receptor-mediated induction of IFN-γ.35 Although IL-18R1 is expressed on multiple lymphocyte subsets in the TME, including CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, and NK cells, our data showed that only IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells are functionally associated with OVM18 efficacy. This subset represents a highly cytotoxic, tumor antigen-enriched population that undergoes robust activation and proliferation on IL-18 and viral stimulation. Interestingly, tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells accounted for approximately 20% of the IL-18R1+CD8+ T-cell subset, and only a fraction of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells were OVA-tetramer-positive. The antigen specificity of the remaining IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells that do not recognize OVA remains to be fully defined. These cells may recognize endogenous tumor antigens other than OVA, particularly in the B16-F10 tumor model, which contains multiple tumor-associated or neoantigenic epitopes.36 37 Alternatively, some of these cells may represent bystander-activated CD8+ T cells induced by the inflammatory TME after OVM18 treatment. Future studies using broader antigen-screening approaches, such as peptide-library stimulation, TCR cloning, or TCR-antigen pairing, will be needed to comprehensively define the antigen specificity of this population.

Our own data and previous studies on decoy-resistant IL-18 (DR18) provide substantial support for IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells as an important functional effector subset contributing to OVM18-mediated antitumor immunity. First, OVM18 expresses mutIL-18, whose sequence is identical to the previously reported DR18. In that study, DR18-mediated antitumor activity was shown to depend on CD8+ T cells expressing IL-18R1.16 Second, our previous study showed that the superior antitumor efficacy of OVM18 was primarily attributable to the incorporated mutIL-18 payload, highlighting the functional relevance of the IL-18/IL-18R1 axis in the enhanced activity of OVM18. Further depletion of CD8+ T cells markedly impaired the therapeutic efficacy of OVM18, whereas depletion of CD4+ T cells or NK cells had a much more limited effect, indicating that CD8+ T cells are a major effector population mediating OVM18 activity.11 Within the CD8+ T-cell compartment, our scRNA-seq and flow cytometry analyses showed that IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells exhibited stronger clonal expansion, higher expression of cytotoxic effector molecules, and greater enrichment of tumor antigen-responsive features compared with IL-18R1⁻CD8+ T cells. Third, Il18r1 expression in CD8+ T cells positively correlates with the expression of effector molecules Ifng, and a previously published study showed that blockade of IFN-γ markedly abrogated the therapeutic efficacy of OVM18.11 Therefore, the positive association between Il18r1 expression and Ifng expression in CD8+ T cells further supports the functional relevance of the IL-18R1-associated effector program. Together with the observation that FTY720 reduced the accumulation of intratumoral IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells and diminished OVM18 efficacy, these data provide converging evidence that lymph node-derived IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells are functionally linked to OVM18-mediated tumor control. However, Il18r1-deficient mice or validated in vivo IL-18R1 blockade would provide more direct evidence to establish the causal requirement of this pathway.

Notably, following OVM18 treatment, the correlation between intratumoral IL-18R1+CD8+ T cell abundance and therapeutic outcome was further strengthened. We attribute this to a feed-forward mechanism in which IFN-γ produced by IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells activates DCs, promoting the generation of additional tumor-specific IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells that subsequently reinfiltrate tumors to sustain cytotoxic activity. This cascade is supported by the marked clonal expansion of CD8+ T cells observed in the TME after OVM18 treatment. In contrast, NK cells and CD4+ T cells failed to establish a similar amplification circuit, with CD4+ T cells even declining on OVM18 treatment.

Given their important role in mediating OVM18 efficacy, a key question concerns how IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells are generated. Our data indicated that cDC1s significantly upregulate IL-18R1 expression on CD8+ T cells, thereby enhancing their capacity to respond to OVM18-derived IL-18 within the TME. cDC1s are professional antigen-presenting cells that prime tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T cell responses through cross-presentation in TDLNs.38 In addition to conferring antigen specificity, this priming process simultaneously induces the expression of critical cytokine receptors (eg, IL-2R, IL-12R) that license subsequent effector functions.39 40 Among the known regulators of IL-18R1 expression, IL-12 is particularly notable as the most potent inducer and is predominantly secreted by DCs after pathogen recognition and antigen presentation. Therefore, cDC1-derived IL-12 during TDLN priming may directly drive IL-18R1 expression on CD8+ T cells. Supporting this notion, we observed that OVM18 or anti-CTLA-4 treatment both significantly upregulated IL-12 expression levels in DCs. This was accompanied by a marked increase in the intratumoral infiltration of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells, suggesting that OVM18 or anti-CTLA-4-mediated enhancement of IL-12 production in cDC1 may contribute to the enrichment of this subset. Moreover, cDC1 activation may upregulate additional cytokine and chemokine receptors (eg, CXCR3, CCR5), creating a receptor landscape that favors IL-12 responsiveness and subsequent IL-18R1 induction.

Our results reveal that CTLA-4 antibodies markedly enhance tumor infiltration of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells, whereas PD-1 antibodies do not exert comparable effects. Mechanistically, CTLA-4 blockade disrupts the competitive interaction between CTLA-4 on Tregs and CD80/CD86 on DCs, thereby restoring effective CD8+ T cell-DC engagement. In parallel, anti-CTLA-4 relieves DC suppression, augments co-stimulatory signaling, upregulates chemokine receptors such as CCR7, and promotes DC migration into tumors.41 These changes facilitate efficient DC priming of CD8+ T cells and elevate IL-18R1 expression. Notably, the effects of anti-CTLA-4 were spatially restricted: both DCs and IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells were increased in TME but not in TDLNs. This spatial pattern differs from that observed after OVM18 treatment, which primarily promoted IL-18R1+CD8+ T-cell formation in TDLNs. We attribute this specificity to the high density of Tregs within tumors, sustained by TGF-β-driven differentiation, IL-10-dependent expansion, and hypoxia-induced HIF-1α signaling.42 Thus, anti-CTLA-4 predominantly reinvigorates DC-CD8+ T cell crosstalk locally within tumors, thereby amplifying the OVM18-induced immunostimulatory circuit. By contrast, PD-1 blockade primarily reverses T cell exhaustion but does not overcome DC dysfunction within the immunosuppressive TME, explaining the absence of changes in DC infiltration or IL-18R1 expression in vivo. Supplementary phenotypic analysis in both B16-F10 and RM1 tumor models showed comparable PD-1 expression levels and similar frequencies of PD-1-positive cells between IL-18R1+ and IL-18R1⁻ CD8+ T cells under baseline conditions (online supplemental figure 16). These data indicate that IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells did not exhibit preferentially elevated PD-1 expression. Although anti-PD-1 may act on pre-existing PD-1+ T cells, it did not increase the abundance of the IL-18R1+CD8+ T-cell population associated with OVM18 efficacy. This may partly explain why anti-PD-1 failed to further potentiate OVM18 therapy.

Combined treatment with anti-CTLA-4 and OVM18 significantly enhanced the infiltration of IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells within both TME and TDLNs, further suppressing tumor growth. However, this combination therapy failed to achieve complete tumor regression in RM1 tumor model, suggesting the persistence of additional resistance mechanisms. Intriguingly, despite the combination of anti-PD-1, anti-CTLA-4, and OVM18, no additive therapeutic benefit was observed, suggesting that the residual resistance may involve mechanisms beyond PD-1-mediated T-cell exhaustion. Future investigations should therefore prioritize elucidating the immunosuppressive factors following OVM18 treatment and developing targeted strategies to neutralize their immunosuppressive functions, which may substantially enhance the therapeutic efficacy of OVM18-based regimens.

Our study establishes IL-18R1+CD8+ T cells as an important effector population in OVM18 therapy. Anti-CTLA-4 treatment effectively expands this population, thereby providing a mechanistically grounded rationale for combination therapy.
