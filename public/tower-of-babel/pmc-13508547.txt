Pulmonary toxocariasis presenting as migratory pulmonary infiltrates and mediastinal lymphadenopathy: a case report and literature review
Authors: Fatemeh Ghasemi, Niloofar Khoshnam Rad, Bita Rashidinejad, Milad Nasrollahzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary toxocariasis, caused by the nematode Toxocara canis or T. cati, is an underdiagnosed cause of eosinophilic lung disease with highly variable radiological presentation that often mimics malignancy or other eosinophilic conditions.Case presentationA 53-year-old female smoker presented with progressive dyspnea and cough. Initial chest CT revealed right lower lobe consolidation with mediastinal lymphadenopathy (station 4R with short-axis diameters 12 mm), raising concern for lung cancer. Bronchoscopy with EBUS-TBNA was non-diagnostic. The patient was empirically started on dexamethasone (8 mg daily, tapered over 3 months) for suspected organizing pneumonia. During steroid taper, her symptoms recurred with delayed emergence of peripheral eosinophilia (670/µL; initial absolute eosinophil count on presentation was 90/µL), elevated IgE (456 IU/mL), and serial CT scans demonstrating migratory pulmonary infiltrates involving the right lower, right middle, and left lower lobes. A history of raw beef liver consumption prompted serological testing, which confirmed Toxocara canis infection. Treatment with albendazole alone (400 mg twice daily for 14 days, without corticosteroids) resulted in complete clinical and radiological resolution.Literature reviewWe searched PubMed and Scopus (January 2014 – February 2026) for English- and French-language case reports of pulmonary toxocariasis. Fourteen new cases were identified and analyzed alongside the 12 cases from Ranasuriya et al.‘s [1] review. These 26 cases demonstrate marked radiologic heterogeneity: multiple bilateral nodules (50%), consolidations (23%), pleural effusion (27%), and migratory infiltrates (8%). Pleural effusion has emerged as a distinct manifestation in seven recent cases. Delayed eosinophilia occurred in 12% of cases. Immunocompromised states (including primary ciliary dyskinesia, hematologic malignancies, and immunosuppressive therapy) were present in 23% of cases and may predispose to atypical presentations.ConclusionPulmonary toxocariasis should be considered in patients with migratory infiltrates, unexplained eosinophilic pleural effusion, or lung nodules with eosinophilia. A meticulous dietary and exposure history is essential. Diagnosis is confirmed by serology, and patients respond well to albendazole therapy.

Introduction

Human toxocariasis is a zoonotic helminthiasis caused by infection with larvae of Toxocara canis or Toxocara cati, the common roundworms of dogs and cats [2, 3]. Humans become accidental hosts through ingestion of embryonated eggs from contaminated soil, water, or unwashed vegetables, or through consumption of raw or undercooked tissues from paratenic hosts such as cattle, chickens, or sheep [2, 4]. After ingestion, larvae hatch in the intestine, penetrate the intestinal wall, and migrate via the bloodstream to various organs, including the liver, lungs, central nervous system, and eyes, where they incite an intense eosinophilic inflammatory response [5].

The global seroprevalence of toxocariasis is estimated at 19%, with wide geographic variation ranging from 2 to 5% in urban areas of industrialized countries to over 50% in some tropical regions [6, 7]. Despite this high prevalence, the disease remains underdiagnosed and neglected, largely due to its non-specific clinical manifestations and lack of physician awareness [8]. Pulmonary involvement in toxocariasis is common but highly variable in its presentation. The classic description is that of visceral larva migrans with transient pulmonary infiltrates, cough, dyspnea, and wheezing [9]. However, the radiologic spectrum has expanded considerably over the past decade to include multiple bilateral nodules, solitary consolidations, ground-glass opacities, migratory infiltrates, and increasingly recognized eosinophilic pleural effusion [10–12].

In 2014, Ranasuriya et al. published a review of 12 pulmonary toxocariasis cases, characterizing the clinical and radiologic features of this entity [1]. In the ensuing decade, numerous case reports have expanded our understanding by describing novel presentations in immunocompromised hosts, unique radiologic findings such as transdiaphragmatic larval migration, and the emergence of pleural effusion as a distinct manifestation. We report a case of pulmonary toxocariasis with striking migratory pulmonary infiltrates and mediastinal lymphadenopathy that initially mimicked lung cancer and review the literature published since 2014 to provide an updated perspective on the clinical and radiologic spectrum of this disease.

Case presentation

A 53-year-old female lawyer and active smoker with a 20-pack-year history presented to our pulmonary clinic with a one-month history of progressive exertional dyspnea and a persistent non-productive cough. She denied fever, weight loss, hemoptysis, or night sweats. Her medical history was unremarkable, and she took no regular medications. A detailed exposure history revealed frequent contact with household pets (dogs, cats, and birds) and a long-standing dietary habit of consuming raw beef liver several times per month.

Initial physical examination was unremarkable, with normal vital signs and oxygen saturation of 95% on room air. A non-contrast chest computed tomography (CT) scan (performed due to a history of mild contrast allergy) demonstrated a dense solitary consolidation in the right lower lobe (RLL) with mediastinal lymphadenopathy involving station 4R (short axis diameter 12 mm). Given her smoking history, the primary clinical suspicion was bronchogenic carcinoma with nodal metastases. The patient underwent bronchoscopy with endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) of the enlarged lymph nodes. Cytological examination revealed lymphocytic aggregation in a fibrinoid background, with no evidence of malignant cells. Microbiological studies, including bacterial, fungal, and mycobacterial cultures, were negative. The absolute peripheral eosinophil count at initial presentation was 90/µL (within normal limits).

Two weeks later, the patient presented to the emergency department with worsening dyspnea, a new productive cough, fever to 38.3 °C, wheezing, and hypoxemia with oxygen saturation of 90% on room air. She was admitted and started on empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics for suspected community-acquired pneumonia. A repeat chest CT scan showed complete resolution of the initial RLL lesion but the emergence of a new dense consolidation in the right upper lobe (RUL). Antibiotics were discontinued after 72 h due to lack of clinical response, and dexamethasone (8 mg daily) was initiated for suspected cryptogenic organizing pneumonia, leading to partial symptomatic improvement. The dexamethasone was tapered over three months.

Two months later (week 12 from initial presentation), during an attempted steroid taper (when the daily dose reached 1 mg of oral dexamethasone), her symptoms recurred with low-grade fever, cough, and sputum production. Laboratory investigations at this time revealed a normal leukocyte count but marked peripheral eosinophilia of 15% with an absolute count of 670/µL, which had been absent on admission (initial count 90/µL). The eosinophil count had been suppressed during the dexamethasone course (serial counts ranged from 110–210/µL) and rose only after steroid tapering below 2 mg daily. Total serum immunoglobulin E was elevated at 456 IU/mL (normal < 100 IU/mL). A third CT scan was performed at week 12, demonstrating yet another new area of consolidation, this time in the left lower lobe (LLL), confirming the persistently migratory nature of the infiltrates. Stool examination for ova and parasites was negative.

This radiographic pattern — fleeting, migratory lobar consolidations — coupled with newly emerged eosinophilia and a history of raw beef liver consumption — prompted serological testing for parasitic infections. Toxocara canis-specific IgG antibodies were strongly positive by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), confirmed by Western blot. Testing for other helminth infections, including Strongyloides stercoralis, Ascaris lumbricoides, and Fasciola hepatica, was negative. A diagnosis of pulmonary toxocariasis (Visceral Larva Migrans) was established.

The patient was treated with albendazole 400 mg twice daily for 14 days without corticosteroids (the dexamethasone had been discontinued one week prior to starting albendazole). Her symptoms resolved completely within one week of initiating therapy. A follow-up complete blood count four weeks later showed normalization of the eosinophil count to 2% (absolute count 90/µL). At her six-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, and a chest X‑ray obtained at that time demonstrated complete radiological clearance of all previous pulmonary infiltrates (Fig. 1).

Literature review

Search strategy

To identify relevant cases of pulmonary toxocariasis published since the review by Ranasuriya et al. [1], we conducted a literature search of PubMed and Scopus databases covering the period from January 2014 to February 2026, with language restrictions limited to English and French.

The PubMed search employed a combination of MeSH terms and keywords including “Toxocara,” “Toxocariasis,” “visceral larva migrans,” “lung,” “pulmonary,” “pleural effusion,” and “case report.” The Scopus search used similar terms in the title, abstract, and keyword fields. The complete search strategies are available in the supplementary materials.

The PubMed search yielded 42 results, and the Scopus search yielded 57 results. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, 34 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Studies were included if they reported confirmed Toxocara infection with pulmonary parenchymal or pleural involvement and provided sufficient clinical data. Review articles, studies without original case data, and cases without pulmonary involvement were excluded.

This process identified 14 new case reports of pulmonary toxocariasis published between January 2014 and February 2026, which were analyzed alongside the 12 cases from the Ranasuriya et al. [1] review for a total of 26 cases.

Summary of newly identified cases

The 14 cases of pulmonary toxocariasis published between 2014 and 2026, including the present case, are summarized in Table 1 alongside the 12 previously reported cases from Ranasuriya et al. [1]. These cases demonstrate the expanding clinical and radiologic spectrum of the disease, including presentations with pleural effusion, migratory infiltrates, and multi-organ involvement. Notable cases include the first documented instance of trans-diaphragmatic larval migration from liver to lung on serial MRI [13], the first pediatric case of pleural effusion caused by toxocariasis [14], and multiple cases in immunocompromised hosts including patients with hematologic malignancies, primary ciliary dyskinesia, and those on immunosuppressive therapy [15–19]. Risk factors identified among these cases included consumption of raw meat, particularly beef liver, contact with dogs, and ingestion of raw snails or slugs. Treatment with albendazole, with durations ranging from 5 days to 8 weeks depending on disease severity, resulted in complete clinical and radiologic resolution in all cases.

Analysis of published cases

Combining the 14 newly identified cases with the 12 cases previously reviewed by Ranasuriya et al. [1] yields a total of 26 published cases of pulmonary toxocariasis. The combined analysis reveals important patterns in demographics, risk factors, laboratory findings, radiologic presentations, and treatment outcomes.

Patients ranged in age from 5 to 79 years with a mean age of 46 years, and there was a male predominance with 63% of cases occurring in males. Geographic distribution was diverse, with 58% of cases reported in Europe, 33% in Asia, and 8% in North America. The most frequently identified risk factor was exposure to dogs or cats, reported in 54% of cases. Consumption of raw or undercooked meat was documented in 33% of cases, including raw beef liver in five cases, raw chicken liver in two cases, raw pork or chitlins in one case, and raw snails or slugs in three cases [21, 24, 34]. An immunocompromised state was present in 23% of cases (6/26), including patients with ulcerative colitis on azathioprine, hematologic malignancies, primary ciliary dyskinesia, and end-stage renal disease. Rural residence or poor sanitation was noted in 17% of cases, while no specific risk factor could be identified in 13% of cases.

Peripheral eosinophilia exceeding 500/µL was present in 88% of cases, with a mean peak eosinophil count of 6,540/µL and a range from 162 to 16,500/µL. However, in 12% of cases (3/26), eosinophilia was absent at initial presentation and developed only later in the disease course. Total serum immunoglobulin E was elevated in 90% of tested cases, with a mean value of 2,550 IU/mL and a range from 339 to 13,480 IU/mL. Bronchoalveolar lavage was performed in six patients and demonstrated eosinophilic alveolitis in all cases, with eosinophil percentages ranging from 11% to 96%. Pleural fluid analysis was performed in seven patients with pleural effusion (two from the Ranasuriya cohort plus five from the new cases), all of which were exudative with eosinophil predominance ranging from 15% to over 50%, and anti-Toxocara antibodies were detected in pleural fluid in five of the seven cases where this was tested.

Chest CT findings were remarkably heterogeneous. The most common pattern, observed in 50% of cases, was multiple bilateral pulmonary nodules, often surrounded by a ground-glass halo known as the halo sign. Solitary or migratory consolidations were seen in 23% of cases (6/26), including our own case and others with documented migratory infiltrates on serial imaging. Pleural effusion was present in 27% of cases (7/26), all of which were eosinophil-predominant. Interstitial or ground-glass opacities were seen in 8% of cases. Unique findings included direct visualization of trans-diaphragmatic larval migration from liver to lung on serial MRI [35], and mediastinal lymphadenopathy (present in our case and one other [22]).

All patients received anti-helminthic therapy. Albendazole was the most commonly used agent, administered to 85% of patients (22 of 26). The duration of albendazole therapy varied considerably, ranging from 5 days to 8 weeks, with the most common regimen being 400 mg twice daily for 10 to 14 days. Longer courses of 4 to 8 weeks were employed in patients with severe or multi-system disease, including those with cerebral vasculitis [24], cardiac involvement [15, 17], and trans-diaphragmatic migration [22]. Mebendazole was used in 15% of cases (4 of 26). Corticosteroids were administered as adjunctive therapy in 35% of patients (9 of 26) with severe inflammatory manifestations, including cerebral vasculitis, hypereosinophilic syndrome with myocarditis, and severe pneumonia with respiratory failure. Outcomes were uniformly favorable, with all 26 patients achieving clinical improvement or complete resolution of symptoms following treatment. Follow-up imaging, available in 23 patients, demonstrated complete or near-complete resolution of pulmonary abnormalities in all cases, and peripheral eosinophil counts normalized in all patients with follow-up testing.

Discussion

Our case illustrates several key features of pulmonary toxocariasis that are important for clinicians to recognize. The initial presentation with a solitary pulmonary nodule and mediastinal lymphadenopathy in a smoker was highly suspicious for lung cancer, leading to invasive diagnostic procedures. The subsequent migratory pattern on serial imaging, delayed emergence of eosinophilia, and history of raw beef liver consumption were critical clues that redirected the diagnosis toward a parasitic etiology.

In clinical practice, distinguishing pulmonary toxocariasis from cryptogenic organizing pneumonia (COP) or eosinophilic pneumonia (EP) can be challenging. Our literature review identified several imaging features that may aid differentiation. In toxocariasis, the “halo sign” (ground-glass attenuation surrounding a nodule or consolidation) was present in 42% of cases where detailed imaging was provided, reflecting eosinophilic inflammation around a migrating larva [10, 36]. This sign is uncommon in COP, which typically shows peribronchial consolidations or the “reverse halo” sign [37]. Eosinophilic pneumonia typically demonstrates diffuse, bilateral, peripheral ground-glass opacities rather than the migratory, often unilateral consolidations seen in toxocariasis [38]. Additionally, pleural effusion in toxocariasis is virtually always eosinophil-predominant (> 15% eosinophils), whereas eosinophilic pleural effusions are rare in COP or idiopathic EP. When migratory infiltrates are observed on serial imaging, parasitic infection (toxocariasis, paragonimiasis, or fascioliasis) should be strongly considered over COP or EP, which rarely demonstrate true migratory behavior [10, 26].

The migratory pattern observed in our case is a classic but infrequently documented feature of pulmonary toxocariasis. It represents the physical passage of Toxocara larvae through the lung parenchyma, inciting a local eosinophilic inflammatory response that forms transient consolidations ^28^. This pattern, while characteristic, is often not recognized due to the lack of serial imaging or the rapid resolution of individual lesions. Among the 26 reviewed cases, migratory infiltrates were documented in only 5 (21%), suggesting that this pattern may be underappreciated. The differential diagnosis of migratory pulmonary infiltrates is broad and includes cryptogenic organizing pneumonia, eosinophilic pneumonias including drug-induced and parasitic causes, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis formerly known as Churg-Strauss syndrome [29]. Our case underscores the importance of including parasitic infections in this differential, particularly when accompanied by eosinophilia or a suggestive history of exposure.

The literature review confirms that pulmonary toxocariasis is far more heterogeneous than traditionally described. While multiple bilateral nodules remain the most common presentation accounting for 50% of cases, pleural effusion has emerged as a distinct and increasingly recognized manifestation, accounting for 27% of cases (7 of 26). This is an important observation, as pleural effusion is rarely mentioned in reviews of toxocariasis and may be underdiagnosed. The seven cases of toxocariasis presenting with eosinophilic pleural effusion share several common features including unilateral predominance in six of seven cases, eosinophil-rich fluid with percentages ranging from 15% to over 50%, exudative nature by Light’s criteria, detectable anti-Toxocara antibodies in pleural fluid in five of the seven cases where this was tested, and excellent response to albendazole therapy.

The mechanism of pleural effusion in toxocariasis likely involves direct larval migration into the pleural space, triggering an eosinophil-mediated inflammatory response. This hypothesis is supported by the detection of anti-Toxocara antibodies in pleural fluid in several cases [18, 21] and by one case in which larvae were directly visualized in the pleural fluid [39]. The case reported by Kakimoto et al. [22] documenting trans-diaphragmatic migration of larvae from liver to lung on serial MRI provides direct imaging evidence supporting this mechanism and confirms that larvae can migrate through coelomic cavities in humans, a phenomenon previously demonstrated only in animal models [40].

Another notable case reported by Cheon and Yoo [35] described multiple cavitary lung nodules in a patient with colon cancer initially mistaken for metastatic disease, highlighting the potential for toxocariasis to mimic malignancy particularly in patients with known cancer. The case reported by Park et al. [19] of pulmonary toxocariasis mimicking invasive aspergillosis in a patient with ulcerative colitis illustrates another important diagnostic pitfall, with a false-positive serum galactomannan assay attributed to translocation of dietary galactomannan across the disrupted gut mucosal barrier leading to treatment with antifungal agents before the correct diagnosis was established.

Our patient’s history of raw beef liver consumption represents a well-recognized but underappreciated risk factor for toxocariasis. This transmission route, first described in Japan in the 1990s [30], has now been documented worldwide. Raw beef liver consumption was implicated in five of the cases in our review, highlighting the importance of obtaining a detailed dietary history in patients with unexplained eosinophilic lung disease. The three cases linked to ingestion of raw snails or slugs [24] are particularly noteworthy, as mollusks are not true paratenic hosts for Toxocara but can serve as transport hosts, ingesting embryonated eggs from contaminated soil and concentrating them in their tissues [41]. Both patients who ingested raw snails or slugs developed severe multi-system disease, suggesting that ingestion of transport hosts may result in higher larval inocula. Importantly, 13% of cases had no identifiable risk factor, and one patient’s infection likely dated back to dog contact more than 9 years before presentation 18, emphasizing that because Toxocara larvae can remain dormant in human tissues for years, the exposure history must extend back years or even decades.

Peripheral eosinophilia is a hallmark of toxocariasis but may be absent at presentation, as in our case and 12% of reviewed cases. This phenomenon likely reflects the time required for Th2-mediated immune responses to develop and underscores the importance of serial monitoring. The patient reported by Minamii and Nishioka [18] had only mild eosinophilia of 162/µL at presentation, which rapidly progressed to 9,000/µL within 10 days. Total immunoglobulin E is a more sensitive marker, elevated in 90% of tested cases, and may be particularly useful when eosinophilia is absent. In our case, elevated IgE at 456 IU/mL was present at the time of diagnosis and decreased following treatment.

An important finding of this review is the recognition that immunocompromised patients represent a population that may be at risk for atypical or severe presentations of pulmonary toxocariasis. Five cases or 23% occurred in immunocompromised hosts, including patients with ulcerative colitis on azathioprine, acute myeloid leukemia and Hodgkin lymphoma post-chemotherapy, primary ciliary dyskinesia, end-stage renal disease on hemodialysis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease with multiple comorbidities. In these patients, pulmonary toxocariasis presented with atypical features, including rapidly progressive disease, multi-organ involvement involving the heart, pleura, and lung, and delayed diagnosis due to attribution of symptoms to underlying disease. These cases highlight the importance of considering parasitic infections in immunocompromised patients with unexplained pulmonary infiltrates, particularly when accompanied by eosinophilia or elevated IgE.

Albendazole is the treatment of choice for toxocariasis, used in 85% of reviewed cases. However, the optimal duration of therapy remains uncertain and appears to depend on disease severity and extent. Short-course therapy of 5 days was used in only three cases, all with mild disease, and while effective in these cases, this regimen has been associated with treatment failure and recurrence in some studies [42]. Intermediate-course therapy of 10 to 14 days was used in the majority of cases (54%), including our own, and appears effective for most patients with moderate pulmonary involvement. Long-course therapy of 21 days to 8 weeks was used in 25% of cases with severe or multi-system disease, including cerebral vasculitis, cardiac involvement, trans-diaphragmatic migration, and hypereosinophilic syndrome. Corticosteroids were used as adjunctive therapy in 33% of cases with severe inflammatory manifestations to dampen the intense eosinophilic inflammatory response to migrating or dying larvae, which can cause significant tissue damage [43]. In patients with cerebral vasculitis, myocarditis, or hypereosinophilic syndrome, corticosteroids may be life-saving, though they should always be used in conjunction with anti-helminthic therapy and never alone.

Based on this review, we propose a diagnostic approach for suspected pulmonary toxocariasis: obtain detailed exposure history (dog/cat contact, raw meat consumption, travel, rural residence, raw snail/slug ingestion); laboratory evaluation including complete blood count with differential (with serial monitoring if initially negative), total IgE, Toxocara serology by ELISA with Western blot confirmation, and exclusion of other helminth infections; for pleural effusion, perform thoracentesis with cell count, differential, and anti-Toxocara antibodies; chest CT to characterize pulmonary involvement, with serial imaging if migratory pattern suspected; treatment with albendazole 400 mg twice daily for 10–14 days (extended to 3–8 weeks for severe disease), with consideration of corticosteroids for severe inflammatory manifestations.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, publication bias likely overrepresents atypical or severe cases, and the true spectrum of pulmonary toxocariasis may include milder, self-limited forms that go unreported. Second, the inclusion of cases from the 2014 Ranasuriya review relies on their data extraction, which we could not independently verify. Third, the heterogeneity in diagnostic testing (some cases using serology alone, others with biopsy confirmation) and treatment regimens precludes definitive recommendations on optimal therapy duration. Fourth, the absence of histopathologic confirmation in many cases, including our own, means that alternative diagnoses cannot be entirely excluded, though the clinical response to albendazole strongly supports toxocariasis.

Conclusion

Pulmonary toxocariasis is an underdiagnosed cause of eosinophilic lung disease with a remarkably heterogeneous radiologic presentation that includes multiple nodules, migratory infiltrates, and increasingly recognized eosinophilic pleural effusion. This review of 26 cases highlights that key diagnostic clues include peripheral eosinophilia (though delayed in 12% of cases), elevated total IgE, and a history of exposure to dogs or consumption of raw meat, particularly liver. In patients with pleural effusion, analysis of pleural fluid for eosinophil percentage and anti-Toxocara antibodies can provide rapid diagnostic confirmation. Immunocompromised patients represent a high-risk population for atypical or severe presentations and require a high index of suspicion. With appropriate anti-helminthic therapy, typically albendazole for 10 to 14 days, the prognosis is excellent, and patients can be spared unnecessary invasive procedures and prolonged immunosuppression. Increased awareness of this treatable infection is essential to ensure timely diagnosis and optimal outcomes.
