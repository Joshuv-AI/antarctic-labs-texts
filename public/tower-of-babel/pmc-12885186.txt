Promoter Methylation of MMP9 and RUNX3: Diagnostic Potential and Clinical Associations in Rheumatoid Arthritis
Authors: Amir Mohamad Mohamadizad Moghadam, Yousef Mohammadi, Shahrouz Khoshbakht, Ruhollah Mirjani, Ali Zarei, Mohsen Ghasemzadeh Soroush, Seyyed Mohamad Hoseini, Meysam Mosallaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune disease characterized by chronic inflammation and joint damage. Conventional diagnostic markers for RA have limitations, prompting the exploration of DNA methylation analysis as a potential biomarker. This study focuses on evaluating the methylation levels of two genes, MMP9 and RUNX3, in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) as potential biomarkers for RA.Materials and Methods:We assessed the methylation levels of the MMP9 and RUNX3 promoters in PBMCs from 72 RA patients and 72 healthy controls using the methylation-quantification of endonuclease-resistant DNA (MethyQESD) method. The data were analyzed for diagnostic utility and correlation with clinical features, including C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR).Results:MMP9 methylation levels were significantly lower in RA patients compared to controls (38.23 ± 28.62 vs. 62.27 ± 19.68, P < 0.001), indicating significant hypomethylation. RUNX3 methylation showed no significant difference between the groups (P: 0.295). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis revealed a sensitivity of 73.61% and a specificity of 72.22% with an area under the ROC curve of 0.756 (P < 0.001) for MMP9. MMP9 methylation was negatively correlated with ESR and CRP levels (P < 0.001), while RUNX3 showed positive correlations with ESR (P: 0.014) and CRP (P: 0.001).Conclusion:The study suggests that MMP9 promoter hypomethylation may serve as a promising diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for RA. In addition, RUNX3 promoter methylation showed potential for assessing disease activity. These findings have the potential to improve the diagnosis and prognosis of RA, contributing to improved clinical management of patients.

INTRODUCTION

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by continuous joint inflammation, leading to progressive joint damage and significant disability, greatly affecting the patients’ quality of life.[1] The core phenotypes in RA include the persistent autoimmune response, activated in synovial tissues, resulting in chronic pain, joint deformities, and systemic complications such as cardiovascular, pulmonary, renal, and neurologic involvement.[23] RA has a multifactorial nature, involving both genetic predisposition and environmental factors, which result in immune system dysregulation.[4]

Conventional diagnostic approaches for RA often rely on clinical assessments and biomarkers, such as rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPA). Despite their widespread use, these biomarkers have limitations in both sensitivity and specificity as RF and ACPA are not present in all RA patients and can also be found in other conditions.[567] This necessitates the exploration of more reliable biomarkers for accurate diagnosis and prognosis.

In recent years, novel molecular biomarkers have gained attention for their potential to improve RA diagnosis and management. Among these, epigenetic modifications, particularly DNA methylation, have emerged as crucial players in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases. Methylation alterations can affect gene expression, leading to the loss of immune tolerance and the onset of autoimmunity. DNA methylation has been emerged as a valuable biomarker for improving diagnostic precision and predicting disease progression.[89]

Matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9) and Runt-related transcription factor 3 (RUNX3) are two genes implicated in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases, including RA.[1011] MMP9, part of the matrix metalloproteinase family, is an enzyme that degrades various components of the extracellular matrix.[10] It plays a significant role in autoimmune diseases by regulating immune cell migration, modulating inflammation, and generating autoantigens.[1012] MMP9 is significantly upregulated in RA which is correlated with joint destruction and inflammation.[131415] RUNX3 is a transcription factor that regulates the expression of various genes involved in immune responses, cell growth, and apoptosis.[1617] RUNX3 plays a vital role in the regulation of immune cell differentiation, influencing the balance of Th1/Th2, subsequently impacting the production of interleukins and modulating inflammatory responses.[161819] Additionally, RUNX3 is implicated in the development of autoimmune-related diseases, including ankylosing spondylitis, Crohn’s disease, and psoriasis.[17] Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) are a suitable and clinically relevant source for measuring methylation DNA biomarkers. They contain leukocyte populations directly involved in RA pathogenesis and circulate systemically, interacting with inflamed tissues like the synovium. PBMCs act as a direct cellular reservoir of disease-relevant immune cells and provide insight into systemic and tissue-associated inflammatory processes.[2021]

This study is aimed to evaluate the promoter methylation levels of MMP9 and RUNX3 in PBMCs from RA patients in comparison with healthy controls. Additionally, we seek to explore the correlation between methylation levels of these genes and clinical characteristics such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) level to assess their potential utility as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers in RA.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

The study included two subgroups from the same population: 72 Iranian patients diagnosed with RA and 72 healthy controls. Blood samples (5cc) were collected from all participants recruited from rheumatology clinics and inpatient wards at Al-Zahra Hospital, Isfahan University of Medical Sciences. The patients met the diagnostic and classification criteria set by the American College of Rheumatology (2019). Additionally, all individuals in the study were unrelated, and the healthy controls exhibited no symptoms or family history of RA or any other autoimmune or immune-related disorders. A structured questionnaire was utilized to collect demographic information, including age, gender, height, weight, and blood pressure records. Family history of autoimmune diseases, as well as clinical and laboratory data such as ESR and CRP, were also recorded for all participants.

Isolation of PBMCs and DNA extraction

PBMCs were isolated from the obtained blood samples using the standard Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient centrifugation method, as previously described by Fuss et al.[22] Briefly, blood samples were carefully layered over Ficoll-Hypaque solution in a conical tube, followed by centrifugation at a specific speed and duration to effectively isolate the PBMCs from other blood cells. The PBMC layer was carefully collected and washed multiple times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove any remaining contaminants. Following PBMCs isolation, genomic DNA was extracted from the purified PBMCs using the Prime Prep Genomic DNA Isolation Kit (Roche). The extraction process was carried out according to the manufacturer’s protocol, which included cell lysis, removal of proteins and other contaminants, and purification of the DNA. The quality and integrity of the isolated genomic DNA were then assessed using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and a Nanodrop spectrophotometer device (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

MMP9 and RUNX3 methylation analysis

To analyze the methylation status of the MMP9 and RUNX3 promoter regions, we employed the Methylation Quantification Endonuclease-Resistant DNA (MethyQESD) technique. This technique, which relies on restriction enzyme digestion and quantification of methylated DNA using real-time PCR (RT-PCR), has been thoroughly described in two studies conducted by Ehtesham et al.[2324] The procedure consists of two sequential steps. First, the template DNA is digested with methylation-sensitive enzymes (Methylation Quantification Digestion, MQD) and then subjected to real-time PCR. Hin6I (G^CGC) cleaves unmethylated GCGC sites, while methylation-insensitive enzymes XbaI (T^CTAGA) and DraI (TTT^AAA), used for Methylation-Independent Calibrator Digestion (CalD), separately digest total DNA. The fraction of methylated DNA that remains uncleaved by Hin6I is quantified by RT-PCR and normalized against a reference DNA that is not digested. The forward and reverse sequence-specific primers used for amplifying region around the CpG sites were as follows: MMP9 forward: 5’-GAGTCAGCACTTGCCTGTCA-3’; MMP9 reverse: 5’-CTGCCAGAGGCTCATGGTG-3’ and RUNX3 forward: TCCCTCAACCACAGAACCAC; RUNX3 reverse: CGGGGAATGAATGAATGAGGC.

Statistical assessment

All data were analyzed using SPSS version 25 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). The statistical comparison between the two groups was assessed using Student’s t-test. The clinical characteristics of the participants were evaluated by the Mann–Whitney U-test and the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables, while the χ²-test was employed for categorical variables. For the gene that showed a significant difference in methylation levels between cases and controls, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated. These curves plotted the true positive rate (sensitivity) against the false positive rate (1-specificity) to determine the optimal threshold for differentiation. The diagnostic accuracy was assessed by examining the area under the ROC curve (AUC) and the 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Additionally, the Pearson correlation coefficient test was utilized to investigate the relationship between methylation levels and clinical characteristics. Data are reported as means ± SD, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

MMP9 and RUNX3 methylation

The mean methylation level of MMP9 in RA patients was 38.23 ± 28.62, significantly lower than that in the control group (62.27 ± 19.68), indicating hypomethylation of MMP9 in the patients’ group (P < 0.001). Although the methylation level of RUNX3 was slightly higher in RA patients compared to healthy controls (44.08 ± 16.67 vs. 40.44 ± 24.13), this difference was not considered as statistically significant (P: 0.295; Table 3, Figure 1). Through ROC curve analysis and using an optimal cutoff value of 47.30%, our results showed a sensitivity of 73.61% and a specificity of 72.22%. This demonstrates a good accuracy (AUC = 0.756, P < 0.001) for MMP9 as a biomarker in distinguishing individuals with RA from healthy subjects [see Figure 2].

DISCUSSION

The findings of this study underscore the diagnostic potential and clinical relevance of promoter methylation levels of MMP9 and RUNX3 in RA. Our analyses revealed significant hypomethylation of the MMP9 gene in patients with RA, when compared to healthy controls. This hypomethylation is thought to be a likely mechanism in increased expression of MMP9, which is consistent with previous research linking elevated MMP9 levels to joint destruction and inflammation in RA patients.[14] Moreover, the strong correlation between MMP9 methylation levels and inflammatory markers (ESR and CRP) further underscores its potential role in disease activity and progression.

It has also been demonstrated that MMP9 is upregulated in other autoimmune diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE),[1325] Sjogren’s syndrome,[2627] and multiple sclerosis,[2829] highlighting its role in immune regulation and inflammation. Gruber et al.[30] have demonstrated, for the first time, that MMP9 levels are significantly elevated in the sera and synovial fluid (SF) of patients with RA. This finding indicates the potential of MMP9 to be used as a valuable biomarker in diagnosing and monitoring disease activity. Zhou et al.[31] investigated the immunolocalization of MMP2 and MMP9 in human rheumatoid synovium. They found that both MMP2 and MMP9 were present in synoviocytes and vascular endothelial cells. Additionally, MMP9 was found in infiltrating CD68+ macrophages. This suggests that MMP9 are actively involved in the inflammatory processes and angiogenesis associated with RA. A study by Xue et al.[14] further supports the role of MMP9 in RA pathogenesis, indicating that endogenous MMP9 promotes rheumatoid synovial fibroblast survival, inflammation, and cartilage degradation. Suppressing MMP9 significantly reduced RA synovial fibroblast proliferation, migration, invasion, and inflammatory mediator production and promoted apoptosis. These findings highlight the critical role of MMP9 in RA and its potential as a therapeutic target.

Additionally, a study examining elevated circulatory MMP2 and MMP9 levels in patients with RA and SLE found that both MMP2 and MMP9 levels and activities from all patients were significantly higher than those from control subjects. Notably, MMP9 levels were 11-fold higher in RA patients and 4-fold higher in SLE patients compared to controls.[13] A study by Matache et al.[32] investigated the expression and secretion of MMP9 in PBMCs from SLE patients. The study found that fresh PBMCs from SLE patients expressed significantly higher activity of MMP9 and spontaneously released higher levels of MMP9 compared to healthy donors. In patients, subgrouped based on disease status, the most increased pro-MMP9 activity inside the PBMCs was identified for the relapse SLE subgroup. However, following culture, PBMCs from remission SLE patients secreted significantly higher MMP9 levels than those from relapse SLE patients. These observations suggested the multiple roles of MMP9 in the progression of inflammation and tissue damage or repair, depending on the clinical stages of SLE. In contrast, Liu et al.[25] found remarkedly reduced serum levels of MMP9 in SLE patients relative to healthy controls. Additionally, they observed that MMP9 levels were lower in patients with active disease and renal damage compared to those with inactive disease and without renal damage. Furthermore, another study highlighted an inverse correlation between MMP9 levels and anti-dsDNA antibodies in SLE patients.[33]

Regarding the methylation change of MMP9 in autoimmune diseases, Ehtesham et al.[24] reported hypomethylation of this gene in SLE patients compared to healthy controls, which may be linked to increased MMP9 expression. Additionally, they found that this methylation dysregulation in the patient group was associated with renal damage and laboratory findings, such as anti-dsDNA, C3, and C4 levels. Another study indicated that DNA methylation of MMP9 is correlated with elevated levels of MMP9 messenger RNA in periapical inflammatory lesions.[34]

Our study found that, while there was no significant difference in the methylation levels of RUNX3 between the case and the control groups, there was a notable positive correlation between the methylation level of the RUNX3 promoter and the inflammatory ESR and CRP markers. This suggests that higher methylation levels of RUNX3 are linked to increased levels of these inflammatory markers, which are typically elevated in RA patients. Studies have shown that RUNX3 plays a crucial role in the differentiation of T helper cells, as well as in the production of interleukins and the modulation of inflammatory responses.[16171819] Specifically, in a study conducted by Fu et al.,[35] it was demonstrated that RUNX3 is significantly overexpressed in CD4+ T cells obtained from patients with psoriasis. This overexpression was found to be associated with an increased number of Th17 and Th22 cells, as well as elevated levels of interleukins IL-6, IL-20, and IL-22. These cytokines are known to contribute to the inflammatory processes observed in psoriasis. Additionally, the study reported that suppressing RUNX3 can reverse these effects, thereby reducing the number of Th17 and Th22 cells and lowering the levels of the associated interleukins. However, research has demonstrated that RUNX3 is downregulated in plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDcs) of patients with systemic sclerosis (ssc), which was associated with hypermethylation of RUNX3, leading to increased severity of skin fibrosis.[36]

Generally, our findings are concordant with previous reports of gene-specific methylation alterations in PBMCs from RA patients. Multiple studies have identified differential methylation in immune- and inflammation-related loci that perform diagnostic or prognostic roles, including CDKN2A, IL6, IFI44L, and PARP9.[21373839] Hypomethylation or hypermethylation at these loci has been linked to dysregulated cell-cycle control, cytokine overproduction, interferon-driven responses, and altered DNA-repair or immune-signaling pathways, respectively. These PBMC-derived epigenetic signatures reflect immune-cell intrinsic changes and systemic inflammatory exposure, and in several cases, they correlate with clinical measures of disease activity, treatment response, or specific clinical phenotypes. Together with our observation of MMP9 promoter hypomethylation and the activity-associated RUNX3 methylation pattern, these data support a model in which peripheral blood methylation profiles capture both mechanistic drivers of joint pathology and actionable biomarkers for disease stratification and monitoring.

Overall, our findings highlight MMP9 hypomethylation as a promising biomarker for RA and suggest a complex relationship between RUNX3 methylation and inflammation in RA patients. Nonetheless, this study is not without its limitations, including the possible influence of ethnicity on gene methylation patterns and the relatively small sample size. Consequently, it is advisable to conduct similar research in diverse populations with larger cohorts to yield more robust results.
