Identification and expression validation of key genes of Xiaozhengtongluo formula in the treatment of diabetic nephropathy by Mendelian randomization
Authors: Zhao Liu, Moxixuan Liu, Haina Wu, Ying Zhang, Zhiqiang Chen, Xiaona Wei, Yun Ma, Xinai Wang, Hui Gao, Jinmei Zheng, Jing Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Xiaozhengtongluo formula (XZTL) has a positive effect on the treatment of diabetic nephropathy (DN), but its mechanism is not fully understood. Therefore, it is important to explore the key genes of XZTL in the treatment of DN. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between DN and control obtained from GSE96804, drug target genes of XZTL, and disease target genes of DN obtained from public databases were intersected. Genes of intersection were defined as candidate genes. Next, Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was used to ascertain the causal associations between candidate genes and DN. Afterwards, key genes were confirmed through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and expression validation. Subsequently, enrichment analysis, molecular regulatory network analysis, and molecular docking were conducted. Finally, experimental verification of the expression levels of key genes was performed through reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). Altogether, 29 candidate genes were screened via MR analysis, identifying APOD, IGFBP3, and LPL as significantly associated with DN. IGFBP3 and APOD were risk factors, whereas LPL was protective. Consistent expression trends across training and validation datasets defined them as key genes. All three were co-enriched in 26 pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation. Regulatory networks showed MIR497HG/hsa-miR-19a-3p regulated IGFBP3, and NEAT1/hsa-miR-29a-3p regulated LPL; IGFBP3 and LPL were co-targeted by SP3 and SP1. Molecular docking revealed APOD-baicalein, LPL-oleic acid, and IGFBP3-quercetin binding, suggesting therapeutic potential. RT-qPCR confirmed aberrant expression of these genes in DN, which was normalized by XZTL intervention. In this study, three key genes (APOD, IGFBP3, and LPL) of XZTL in the treatment of DN were finally obtained, providing mechanistic clues for understanding XZTL’s multi-target mechanism and providing experimentally tractable candidate targets for DN molecular subtyping, targeted therapeutic development, and precision medicine approaches in TCM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10142-026-02045-1.

Introduction

Diabetic nephropathy (DN), one of the most predominant microvascular complications of diabetes mellitus (DM)(He et al. 2026), has an incidence of approximately 20% to 40% (B. Li et al. 2023a, b). With the global surge in the prevalence of DM, DN has become the leading cause of hospitalization among patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), posing a severe threat to public health (X.-X. Zhang et al. 2020). DN progresses insidiously from glomerular hyperfiltration to microalbuminuria, proteinuria and progressive renal dysfunction (Reutens and Atkins 2011).

Although drugs recommended by the American Diabetes Association (ADA) guidelines, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin Ⅱ receptor blockers (ARBs), sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is), and novel non-steroidal selective mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), have been successively applied in clinical practice, they still fail to effectively halt the progression of DN (Kim et al. 2022). The pathogenesis of DN is associated with the synergistic regulation of multiple pathways, such as oxidative stress, podocyte injury, and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) dysregulation, yet the key molecular nodes remain not fully elucidated (Dwivedi and Sikarwar 2025). In recent years, with the continuous improvement of clinical research on traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), the role of TCM in the prevention and treatment of DN has gradually become a research hotspot (X.-J. Liu et al. 2022). According to TCM theory, the major pathological products of DN are the intermingling of phlegm and blood stasis. The Xiaozheng Tongluo formula (XZTL) is developed based on this theory, composed mainly of Astragali Radix, Atractylodis Macrocephalae Rhizoma, Angelicae Sinensis Radix, Chuanxiong Rhizoma, Salviae Miltiorrhizae Radix et Rhizoma, Carthami Flos and Fritillariae Thunbergii Bulbus. Modern pharmacological studies demonstrate that active components of these medicinal herbs target the core pathogenesis of diabetic nephropathy. Astragalus membranaceus and astragaloside attenuate renal oxidative stress via Nrf2, inhibit fibrosis through TGF-β1/Smad2/3, reduce proteinuria, and improve renal function (Zhang et al. 2024a, b). Salvia miltiorrhiza extract suppresses inflammation and fibrosis by activating PPAR-α/PPAR-γ and downregulating TGF-β1 and IL-1β (Hsu et al. 2024). Ligustilide from Chuanxiong relieves oxidative stress via Nrf2 and inhibits inflammation and apoptosis through p38/ERK MAPK (Qi et al. 2024). Angelica polysaccharides mitigate mesangial proliferation and inflammation by inhibiting NF-κB and RAGE and upregulating BMP-7 to preserve renal function (Sui et al. 2019). These findings indicate that ingredients of XZTL act synergistically against diabetic nephropathy through antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antifibrotic pathways. Although XZTL shows significant clinical efficacy, its precise molecular targets and mechanisms remain poorly defined. Therefore, identifying key genes responsible for XZTL-mediated renal protection is critically important.

Network pharmacology (NP) is a discipline based on systems biology theory, integrating multi-omics and bioinformatics (Zhao et al. 2023). It constructs a drug-component-target-disease interaction network from a holistic perspective to screen core targets and elucidate regulatory mechanisms, thereby providing support for the design of multi-target drugs. Mendelian randomization (MR) is a causal inference method that uses genetic variants (single nucleotide polymorphisms, SNPs) as instrumental variables, whose reliability relies on three fundamental assumptions: relevance (SNPs are strongly associated with exposures), independence (SNPs are independent of confounding factors) and exclusion restriction (SNPs affect outcomes only through exposures) (Ference et al. 2021). This method can effectively circumvent confounding factors and reverse causality bias. Both approaches have been widely applied in DN and related diseases: the pathogenesis of DN involves multiple pathways including inflammation, oxidative stress and renal fibrosis, which is highly consistent with the NP concept of multi-target and holistic regulation. NP is often used to screen active components of TCM herbs (e.g., astragaloside Ⅳ, salvianolic acid B) and compound formula targets for DN treatment, as well as to predict core regulatory pathways (Sun et al. 2022). MR is mostly employed to verify the causal associations between candidate genes/metabolic indicators and DN, resolve controversies in previous studies and fill the gaps in causal inference (W. Zhang et al. 2024a, b). Together, these two approaches provide important theoretical and experimental evidence for drug development and etiological investigation of DN. Despite its utility, conventional NP faces two key limitations: predicted compounds may not reflect actual herbal chemistry, and network associations do not prove causation. MR addresses this gap by using genetic variants to infer causal relationships, moving beyond correlation to causal prioritization.

Based on transcriptomic data from public databases, this study first screened differentially expressed genes between DN and control samples via differential expression analysis. Next, drug target-related genes were obtained from the chemical components of XZTL, and DN-associated target genes were retrieved from public databases. The intersection of the three gene sets yielded candidate targets of XZTL for DN treatment. MR analysis was then performed to further screen these candidate targets to identify key genes. A series of bioinformatics analyses were subsequently conducted to explore the key genes and potential regulatory mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of XZTL on DN. To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate NP with MR for identifying key genes of XZTL in DN. The novelty lies in three aspects: (1) multi-omics intersection (DEGs, drug targets, and disease targets) for candidate screening rather than relying on NP alone; (2) MR for causal prioritization of candidate genes, addressing the causal inference gap inherent in conventional NP; and (3) multi-layer experimental validation, including ROC curve analysis across two independent datasets and RT-qPCR verification in an animal model with XZTL intervention, providing a more robust evidence chain than typical NP studies. These integrated approaches establish a framework that transitions DN therapeutic target discovery from correlation-based prediction toward causality-informed prioritization and experimental validation, aiming to provide novel references for the clinical treatment of DN.

Materials and methods

Data collection

GSE96804 and GSE30528 datasets were retrieved in Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) database using the GEOquery package (v2.73.4) (Cao et al. 2026). Raw expression data were obtained as platform‑specific normalized expression matrices directly from GEO. This dataset was used as the training set for differential expression analysis, functional enrichment analysis, and network construction. The GSE96804 dataset was derived from the GPL17586 platform and included 41 diabetic nephropathy (DN) and 20 control glomerular tissue samples. The GSE30528 dataset was derived from the GPL571 platform, including nine DN and 13 control glomerular tissue samples, and served as an independent validation set for receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and expression trend verification.

The ebi-a-GCST90018612 (DN) was obtained from the Integrative Epidemiology Unit Open genome-wide association study (IEU OpenGWAS) database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/), including 132,984 samples of East Asian (220 DN samples and 132,764 control samples) and 12,447,074 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs).

Component of Xiaozhengtongluo formula (XZTL)

XZTL was the drug used in the present study, including Astragalus membranaceus (Fisch.) Bge., Salvia miltiorrhiza Bge, Atractylodes macrocephala Koidz., Angelica sinensis (Oliv.) Diels, Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort., Carthamus tinctorius L., and Fritillaria thunbergii Miq. The detailed purchasing information, batch numbers and origins of each herbal piece are as follows: Astragali Radix (Batch No. 24112721) and Chuanxiong Rhizoma (Batch No. 24112211) were obtained from Sinopharm Le Rentang Hebei Traditional Chinese Medicine Co., Ltd. (Anguo, Hebei); Atractylodis Macrocephalae Rhizoma (Batch No. 251102) from Hebei Yaoxing Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Anguo, Hebei); Angelicae Sinensis Radix (Batch No. 20240901) from Hebei Qixin Chinese Medicine Granule Decoction Pieces Co., Ltd. (Anguo, Hebei); Salviae Miltiorrhizae Radix et Rhizoma (Batch No. 241101) from Liandong Pharmaceutical (Hebei) Co., Ltd. (Anguo, Hebei); Carthami Flos (Batch No. 2411013) and Fritillariae Thunbergii Bulbus (Batch No. 2410151) from Anhui Puren Chinese Medicine Decoction Pieces Co., Ltd. (Bozhou, Anhui). All herbal decoction pieces were professionally identified and complied with the quality standards specified in the Pharmacopoeia of the People’s Republic of China, with reliable sources and qualified quality for experimental use.

The XZTL preparation was manufactured via a standardized traditional water decoction method. Briefly, the seven herbal materials were accurately weighed according to the prescription ratio, soaked in 10-fold volume of distilled water for 60 min at room temperature, and decocted at 95–100 ℃ under atmospheric pressure for 60 min. The first filtrate was collected, and the residual herbs were re-decocted with 8-fold volume of distilled water for 40 min to obtain the second filtrate. The two filtrates were combined and filtered through four layers of gauze to remove impurities. The mixed solution was concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator at a temperature below 60 ℃ to obtain a stock solution with a crude drug concentration of 1.0 g/mL. After centrifugation at 4000 r/min for 15 min at room temperature, the supernatant was collected, sterilized through a 0.22 μm aqueous filter, aseptically subpackaged, and stored at − 20 ℃ in the dark. Before administration, the stock solution was diluted to the required working concentration with sterile normal saline.

To ensure batch-to-batch consistency of XZTL preparations, the dry extract yield of each batch was determined with a predefined quality standard of 22% ± 5%. Batches beyond this range were discarded. A total of 3 batches of XZTL were prepared for animal experiments, and all batches met the established yield criteria with stable quality. Dry extract yield was used as the sole quality control index in this study, and quantitative detection of active ingredients was not performed.

To guarantee the batch-to-batch stability of XZTL preparations and the repeatability of animal experiments, the dry extract yield was detected as the key quality control index for each batch of XZTL. The qualified range of dry extract yield was set as 22% ± 5%, and unqualified batches beyond this range were discarded. A total of 3 batches of XZTL were prepared in this study, and all batches met the predefined quality standard with stable and uniform quality.

Identification and functional analyses of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

All expression data were log2‑transformed prior to analysis to satisfy the distribution assumptions of limma differential expression analysis. The “limma” (v 3.58.1) (Ritchie et al. 2015) package was applied to acquire differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the comparison between DN and control samples within GSE96804. For between‑array normalization, the normalizeBetweenArrays function in limma was used to remove inter‑chip technical variation and ensure comparability of expression values across samples. In addition, the following quality control steps were performed prior to differential analysis: (1) probe filtering—probes without successful annotation to official gene symbols or with ambiguous annotation information were excluded; (2) gene deduplication—for multiple probes mapping to the same gene symbol, the probe with the highest average expression level (rowMeans) across all samples was retained as the representative; (3) sample selection—DN and control groups were strictly defined based on the clinical annotations (source_name_ch1) provided by the original studies; and (4) low‑expression filtering—genes with detection signals below background noise levels in the vast majority of samples were filtered out prior to limma analysis, retaining only genes with sufficient expression abundance for downstream analysis. The threshold values were p < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| > 0.5. A volcano plot was utilized to visually represent the DEGs through “ggplot2” (v 3.5.1) (Zheng and Huang 2024) package, and the top 10 up- and down-regulated genes in the DN group, sorted according to Log2(FC), were labeled in the volcano plot. Additionally, the “ComplexHeatmap” (v 2.10.8) (Gu et al. 2016) package was used to draw the expression heat map of these 20 DEGs. To further control the false discovery rate, we re‑analyzed the differential expression using the Benjamini‑Hochberg (BH) method for multiple testing correction. We applied the threshold of adjusted P (adj.P) < 0.05 combined with |log₂FC| > 0.5. Following, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of DEGs were undertaken (p < 0.05) utilizing “clusterProfiler” (v 4.10.1) (Wu et al. 2021)package.

Acquisition of drug target genes and disease target genes

The active ingredients of XZTL were retrieved from the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology (TCMSP) database (https://tcmsp.91medicine.cn/#/home). Ingredients with an Oral Bioavailability (OB) of 20% or higher and a Drug-Likeness (DL) score of 0.1 or above were selected as the efficacious active components of XZTL, and the “related targets” module of TCMSP was utilized to search potential targets of efficacious active ingredients. Then, the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/uniprot/) was applied to obtain the gene names of these potential targets, which served as drug target genes.

To obtain target genes associated with DN, we searched for disease target genes in GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and Therapeutic Target Database (http://db.idrblab.net/ttd/) using the keyword “diabetic nephropathy”, with species limited to “Homo sapiens”. The genes obtained from the retrieval of the two databases were summarized, and the duplicate genes were removed. These genes were recorded as disease target genes of DN.

Identification and functional analyses of candidate genes

The overlapping of DEGs, drug target genes, and disease target genes was achieved through the “ggvenn” (v 0.1.10) (Zheng et al. 2022) package. These overlapping genes were recorded as candidate genes, and a network was constructed according to the correlation among disease, candidate genes, efficacious active ingredients, and drugs. Subsequently, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of candidate genes were also undertaken utilizing the “clusterProfiler” (v 4.10.1) package. All P-values from the enrichment analysis were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method to control the false discovery rate (FDR), with p.adjust < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Then, in order to explore the relationship among efficacious active components, candidate genes, and enriched pathways, the interaction network was constructed by combining efficacious active components, candidate genes, and the top five enrichment pathways ranked by count for each category of GO. The network was displayed by Cytoscape (v 3.9.1) (Shannon et al. 2003).

Data preparation for Mendelian randomization (MR)

Expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data for candidate genes (exposure factors) were obtained from the IEU OpenGWAS database. The exposure factors were read by the extract_instruments function in “TwoSampleMR” (v 0.6.4) (Hemani et al. 2018) package, and SNPs significantly linked to the exposure factors were identified under the condition of p-value less than 5 × 10− 6. To ensure independence among SNPs, SNPs in linkage disequilibrium (LD) were pruned using r² < 0.001 within a window of > 10 kb, consistent with the independence assumption of valid instrumental variables. Subsequently, the harmonise_data function was applied to align effect alleles between exposure and outcome GWAS summary statistics, ensuring consistent effect direction. To evaluate instrument strength, we calculated the F-statistic for each SNP using the approximation F = β² / SE², where β represents the estimated effect size of the SNP on the exposure and SE denotes its corresponding standard error. SNPs with F-statistic < 10 were excluded to avoid weak instrument bias. Exposure factors with fewer than three qualifying SNPs were also removed. Afterward, we excluded SNPs that were significantly associated with the outcome, as identified through GWAS data. The final obtained SNPs were used as IVs for MR analysis. The occurrence of DN was defined as the outcome.

MR analysis

The effect alleles and their corresponding sizes were standardized using the harmonise_data function within the “TwoSampleMR” (v 0.6.4) package. Then, MR analysis was carried out combining five algorithms [MR Egger (Xian et al. 2023), Weighted median (Liu et al. 2023), Inverse variance weighted (IVW) (Mounier and Kutalik 2023), Weighted mode (Xu et al. 2022), Simple mode (Dai et al. 2023) via the MR function to ascertain the relationship between exposure factors and outcome that indicated causation. The results of the IVW algorithm were mainly considered. The observed p-value and odds ratio (OR) value for IVW were noted, and p < 0.05 suggested a statistically significant causal link between candidate genes and DN. Forest plots, scatter plots, and funnel plots were used to further illustrate the MR consequence. Subsequently, to assess the credibility of MR findings, sensitivity analyses including heterogeneity test (Li et al. 2023a, b), horizontal pleiotropy assessment (Gan et al. 2023), and leave-one-out analysis (LOO) (Dong et al. 2023) were performed. In the MR heterogeneity test, the IVW Cochran’s Q test yielded a p-value exceeding 0.05, indicating an absence of heterogeneity among candidate genes and DN. Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the mr_pleiotropy_test function, with p > 0.05 indicating the absence of horizontal pleiotropy and thus no confounding bias. Leave‑one‑out analysis was conducted using the mr_leaveoneout function to sequentially remove each SNP and recalculate the meta‑effect of the remaining SNPs via IVW; if the overall effect did not show significant bias after excluding any single SNP, the results were considered reliable. Finally, to rule out reverse causation, the Steiger directionality test was performed using the directionality_test function; candidate genes with correct_causal_direction = TRUE and steiger_pval < 0.05 were considered to have passed the test and were retained as candidate key genes for further analysis.

Expression verification and ROC curve construction

In GSE96804 and GSE30528, to further evaluate the predictive performance of candidate key genes, the candidate key genes were selected to draw the ROC curve, which was performed using the “pROC” (v 1.18.5) (Robin et al. 2011) package. Meanwhile, the area under the curve (AUC) was determined. Genes with AUC > 0.7 and not equal to one in both datasets were delivered to the Wilcoxon test to analyze the expression levels between DN samples and controls in two datasets. To assess the robustness of the gene signature and rule out chance findings, we performed internal validation within the GSE96804 discovery set using repeated stratified cross-validation and permutation testing. A logistic regression classifier was constructed based on the candidate genes; the dataset was randomly split into training and test sets at a 7:3 ratio while preserving the original case-control proportion, and the model was fitted on the training set and evaluated on the test set by AUC. This procedure was repeated 100 times, and the mean AUC with 95% confidence interval was calculated. Additionally, 1,000 permutation tests were conducted by randomly shuffling the disease-status labels while keeping the expression matrix intact; the model was rebuilt for each permuted dataset and the AUC was recalculated. The empirical P value was defined as the proportion of permuted AUCs exceeding the observed AUC. Genes that consistently showed AUC > 0.7 in both datasets, along with significant differential expression (p < 0.05) and concordant expression trends, were further defined as key genes.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

We selected “h.all.V2023.2.Hs.Symbols.gmt” as the reference gene set and used “psych” (v 2.4.3) (Zein and Akhtar 2025) package to calculate the Spearman correlation coefficient of each key gene and all other genes in GSE96804. Then, all genes were sorted in descending order based on the correlation coefficients to obtain a list of correlated genes for each key gene. GSEA was performed using the “clusterProfiler” package (v 4.10.1) to explore the potential functions of key genes. |Normalized enrichment scores (NES)| > 1, false discovery rates (FDR) < 0.05 and p < 0.05 were the thresholds for significant enrichment. In order of p value, the top 10 enrichment pathways were selected for display using “enrichplot” (v 1.20.3) (Liu et al. 2024) package.

Chromosomal localization and functional similarity analysis

To explore the chromosomal localization of key genes, the “RCircos” (v 1.2.2) (Zhang et al. 2013) package was used for annotation and mapping, showing the distribution of key genes on chromosomes. Then, to identify the genes with similar functions to the key genes, the key genes were imported into the GeneMANIA (https://genemania.org/) database, and those genes that exhibited similar functionalities were identified.

Construction of molecular regulatory networks

To acquire a deeper insight into the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying key genes, the microRNA (miRNA) interacting with the key genes (mRNA) was predicted by DIANA-microT (http://diana.imis.athena-innovation.gr/DianaTools/index.php) and miRDB (https://mirdb.org/) database. The predicted miRNAs in the two databases were intercrossed using the “ggvenn” (v 0.1.10) package. Next, the starBase (https://rnasysu.com/encori/) and miRNet (https://www.mirnet.ca/) databases were utilized for predicting the long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) that interact with miRNA. In combination with the above prediction, Cytoscape (v 3.9.1) was used to establish the molecular regulatory network consisting of lncRNA, miRNA, and mRNA. At the same time, we identified the potential transcription factors (TFs) associated with the key genes through the miRNet database, and Cytoscape (v 3.9.1) was used to visualize the key gene-TF regulatory network.

Molecular docking

In order to verify the degree of binding between the key genes and the efficacious active ingredients of the XZTL, molecular docking was performed. Firstly, the efficacious active ingredients corresponding to each key gene were sorted (based on OB value), and the top one of the corresponding component of each key gene was selected for molecular docking. Then, in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (http://www.rcsb.org) and National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pccompound/) database, protein structure data of key genes and structure data of efficacious active ingredients were downloaded. The A chain of each protein was used as the receptor for docking. Finally, the CB-Dock2 website (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/php/blinddock.php) was utilized for molecular docking, with the binding pocket automatically predicted based on the cavity features of the protein structures; no manual pocket specification was applied. All docking parameters were set to the software defaults, and binding affinity was evaluated by binding free energy (kcal/mol).

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

To further elucidate the mechanism of action, assess the stability of the protein–ligand complexes, and characterize the dynamic binding behavior of the active ingredients, MD simulations were subsequently performed using GROMACS 2024.4 software. All simulations were conducted following the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field with the TIP3P water model in a cubic periodic box, maintaining a minimum distance of 1.0 nm between the box edges and the protein surface, and counterions were added to neutralize the system charge. Energy minimization was carried out using the steepest descent algorithm, followed by system equilibration through NVT (constant particle number, volume, and temperature) and NPT (constant particle number, pressure, and temperature) ensembles. During both equilibration phases, temperature coupling was regulated using the V‑rescale thermostat at 300 K with a time step of 2 fs for 100 ps each, and the production MD run was then performed for 20 ns. Finally, the simulation trajectories were analyzed to calculate the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of the complexes, root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) of protein residues, total system energy, and the number of hydrogen bonds formed between the active ingredients and the target proteins.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

To verify whether the in vivo expression changes of key target genes in renal tissues of diabetic nephropathy (DN) rats after Xiaozhengtongluo (XZTL) intervention were consistent with the bioinformatics prediction results, eight-week-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats with a body weight of 150 ± 20 g were used in this study. All rats were supplied by Sibeifu (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. This animal experiment was strictly conducted in accordance with the guidelines for laboratory animal care and was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Hebei Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Ethical Approval No.: 2026-KY-052-01). All experimental animals were housed under standard SPF conditions with a temperature of 22 ± 2 ℃, a relative humidity of 30%–70%, and a 12 h light/12 h dark circadian rhythm.

The DN rat model was established by a combination of high-fat diet feeding and a single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ). The high-fat diet with 35% fat energy, consisting of basal diet, sucrose, lard, cholesterol, sodium cholate, mineral mixture and vitamin mixture, was purchased from Boaigang (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Before modeling, the rats were fasted for 12 h with free access to water. STZ was freshly dissolved in 0.1 mol/L citrate buffer (pH 4.3), and administered via single intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 35 mg/kg to induce DN injury.

Random blood glucose was detected 72 h after STZ injection. Rats with random blood glucose ≥ 16.7 mmol/L were initially judged as diabetic model rats, while those with unqualified or excessively high blood glucose were excluded. At the 4th week after STZ injection, 24 h urine samples were collected to determine 24 h urinary protein quantity and urinary albumin/creatinine ratio (UACR). Combined with sustained hyperglycemia (blood glucose ≥ 16.7 mmol/L), rats with significantly increased urinary albumin excretion were finally confirmed as successful DN models. The qualified DN rats were randomly divided into the DN model group and XZTL intervention group, with the normal control group set simultaneously, and 5 rats were included in each group. Drug intervention was initiated immediately after successful modeling and lasted for 12 weeks. At the end of the 12-week intervention, all rats were sacrificed. Bilateral kidney tissues were rapidly isolated, renal capsules were stripped, and renal cortex tissues were collected. The tissue samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 ℃ for subsequent molecular biological detection.

Total RNA was extracted from frozen rat renal cortex tissues using RNA extraction solution (G3013) and chloroform substitute (G3014) (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The concentration and purity of total RNA were detected by a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer, and only qualified RNA samples were used for subsequent reverse transcription. Genomic DNA was removed, and cDNA was synthesized using the SweScript All-in-One RT SuperMix for qPCR (G3337, Servicebio). GAPDH was selected as the internal reference gene, and preliminary experiments confirmed that GAPDH exhibited stable expression levels among all experimental groups, which was suitable for the normalization of target gene expression.

Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using 2×Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (G3326, Servicebio). The amplification procedure was set as 40 cycles with an annealing temperature of 60 ℃, and melting curve analysis was performed to verify the specificity of amplified products. The primer sequences of APOD, IGFBP3, LPL and GAPDH were designed and synthesized by Servicebio (Wuhan, China), and detailed information is listed in Supplementary Table 1. The relative mRNA expression levels of target genes were calculated by the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. The normal control group was used as the baseline for normalization, and the relative expression levels of target genes in the renal cortex of each group were statistically analyzed.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were executed utilizing the R programming language (v 4.3.1). The Wilcoxon test was applied to compare differences between the two groups, with a statistical significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

DEGs were enriched in 1715 GO pathways and 79 KEGG pathways

A total of 2,693 DEGs between DN and control were obtained, including 1,290 up-regulated genes, such as FN1, and 1,403 down-regulated genes, such as HBA1, in the DN group (Fig. 1A-B). To further control the false discovery rate, we re-analyzed the differential expression using the BH method for multiple testing correction. With the threshold of adj.P < 0.05 combined with |log₂FC| > 0.5, 2,645 DEGs were identified, among which the key genes APOD, IGFBP3, and LPL were all retained in this FDR-corrected DEG list (Supplementary Table 2). To further understand the potential biological roles and pathways of DEGs, altogether 1386 biological processes (BPs), 105 cell components (CCs), 224 molecular functions (MFs), and 79 KEGG pathways (p < 0.05) were identified. DEGs were mainly significantly enriched in small molecule catabolic process (BP), apical part of cell (CC), sulfur compound binding (MF), and fatty acid degradation (KEGG). Only the top five functions ranked by count value were shown in each part of the GO analysis. The top five pathways with the lowest p value in KEGG enrichment analysis were labeled (Fig. 1C-D). To clarify the directionality of pathway alterations, we performed separate GO and KEGG enrichment analyses for up- and down-regulated genes. Down-regulated genes were mainly enriched in fatty acid degradation, tryptophan metabolism, and peroxisome, suggesting suppressed metabolic functions. Up-regulated genes were predominantly enriched in integrin signaling, ECM-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, and AGE-RAGE signaling pathway, indicating activation of extracellular matrix remodeling and inflammatory pathways. GO analysis similarly showed down-regulated genes enriched in catabolic processes, while up-regulated genes were enriched in extracellular matrix organization and immune-related processes. The full enrichment pathway information was shown in Supplementary Table 3.

Screening of candidate key genes by MR

To obtain the candidate key genes, MR was performed on the candidate genes. Through the screening of SNPs, the eQTL data of 18 candidate genes were finally screened for subsequent analysis. Results based on the IVW algorithm, IGFBP3, APOD, CAV1, and LPL were significantly associated with DN (p < 0.05) (Table 1). In the scatter plot, APOD and IGFBP3 showed a positive slope, indicating that they were risk factors. CAV1 and LPL showed a negative slope, suggesting that they acted as protective factors. In the forest plot, the overall effect of IVW for CAV1 and LPL was less than 0, indicating that they were protective factors. The overall effect of IVW for APOD and IGFBP3 was more than 0, indicating that they were risk factors. The findings of the forest plot aligned with those of the scatter plot (Fig. 3A-B). APOD and IGFBP3 were identified as risk factors (IVW effect > 0), while it is noteworthy that APOD expression was observed to be downregulated in DN tissue samples—a seemingly contradictory detail that will be further explored in the discussion. In the funnel plots, the SNPs of the APOD, IGFBP3, and LPL were basically distributed symmetrically, which was in accordance with the second law of Mendelian randomization. CAV1 had a poor distribution status and was eliminated at this step (Fig. 3C). To evaluate the strength and reliability of the MR findings, a sensitivity analysis was performed. The results of the heterogeneity test indicated that there was no heterogeneity in LPL, IGFBP3, and APOD (p > 0.05) (Table 2). The results of the horizontal pleiotropy test showed that there was no horizontal pleiotropy in LPL, IGFBP3, and APOD (p > 0.05) (Table 3). The LOO analysis showed that the gradual elimination of each SNP resulted in little change in the effect of the remaining SNPs on the outcome variables, indicating that the results of the MR analysis for LPL, IGFBP3, and APOD were reliable and stable (Fig. 3D), and these three genes passed the sensitivity analysis. Then, to verify that the analysis results were not affected by reverse causality effects, we performed the Steiger test. The Steiger test for these three genes was TRUE (p < 0.05) (Table 4). Therefore, LPL, IGFBP3, and APOD were validated by MR and were defined as candidate key genes.

LPL, IGFBP3, and APOD were identified as key genes

In order to evaluate the diagnostic value of candidate key gene therapy for DN, ROC curves were drawn in GSE96804 and GSE30528. LPL, IGFBP3, and APOD with AUC values > 0.7 in the two datasets indicated that these three genes have the ability to diagnose the occurrence of DN, and they were included in subsequent analyses (Fig. 4A-B). Meanwhile, LPL, IGFBP3, and APOD were significantly differentially expressed (p < 0.05), and the expression trend was consistent between these two datasets. APOD and LPL were down-regulated, while IGFBP3 was up-regulated in DN samples (Fig. 4C). To further evaluate the diagnostic robustness of this three-gene panel, we performed internal cross-validation and permutation tests within the GSE96804 discovery set. A logistic regression model was constructed using these three key genes, and the dataset was repeatedly split into training and test sets (7:3 ratio) for 100 rounds. The average AUC from this cross-validation was 0.934 (95% CI: 0.766–1.000). Additionally, 1,000 permutation tests with randomly shuffled disease labels were conducted; the observed AUC was significantly higher than the random expectation (empirical P < 0.05), confirming that the diagnostic performance was not attributable to chance. Therefore, LPL, IGFBP3, and APOD were identified as key genes for the diagnosis of DN (Supplementary Table 8).

RT-qPCR validation of APOD, IGFBP3, and LPL

To verify the differential expression of the core key genes (LPL, IGFBP3, APOD) and the interventional effect of the XZTL group, reverse transcription quantitative RT-qPCR was performed to detect the relative expression levels of these genes in the control group, DN group, and XZTL group. The results showed that compared with the control group, the relative expression levels of LPL (p < 0.01) and APOD (p < 0.05) were significantly downregulated in the DN group, while that of IGFBP3 was markedly upregulated (p < 0.01). Compared with the DN group, the XZTL group exhibited a significant regulatory effect on gene expression: the relative expression levels of LPL and APOD were notably increased (p < 0.01) and were closer to those of the control group, whereas the relative expression level of IGFBP3 was significantly decreased (p < 0.01). No statistically significant differences were observed in the relative expression levels of the three aforementioned genes between the XZTL group and the control group (p > 0.05) (Fig. 10).

Discussion

DN is a major microvascular complication of diabetes with complex pathogenesis involving genetic susceptibility, metabolic disturbance, oxidative stress and inflammation, while accurate diagnostic biomarkers and targeted therapies remain lacking clinically (Zhang et al. 2025). XZTL is a Chinese herbal formula based on the TCM theory of qi-yin deficiency and collateral stasis, which improves renal function in DN patients via replenishing qi and nourishing yin, resolving stagnation and activating blood circulation, with confirmed efficacy in early DN (F.-L. Wang et al. 2012). Using network pharmacology and bioinformatics, we identified LPL, IGFBP3 and APOD as hub genes from 2693 differentially expressed genes; these genes were enriched in 26 key pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and fatty acid metabolism, contributing to DN progression by regulating energy metabolism and renal fibrosis. Mendelian randomization provided evidence supporting that downregulated LPL and APOD were associated with protective effects, while elevated IGFBP3 was associated with increased DN risk, suggesting a potential causal association. RT-qPCR showed XZTL significantly normalized the abnormal expression of these three genes, and molecular docking demonstrated computational binding affinity (binding energy < − 6 kcal/mol) between the main active components (baicalein, quercetin, oleic acid) and their respective target proteins. This study identifies LPL, IGFBP3, and APOD as candidate genes potentially involved in DN and suggests potential mechanisms of XZTL, providing preliminary evidence for diagnostic biomarkers and candidate targeted therapeutic strategies for DN.

Screening logic and reliability validation of core key genes

First, transcriptome data analysis was performed in this study to identify 2693 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the DN and control groups. These DEGs were significantly enriched in biological pathways, including small molecule catabolism and fatty acid degradation, which were highly consistent with the core pathological features of metabolic disturbance and dysregulated lipid metabolism in DN. Furthermore, a cross-analysis of the intersection of DEGs, disease target genes, and drug target genes was conducted to pinpoint 29 candidate genes. Notably, these candidate genes were enriched in the oxidative stress response pathway and the pathway of fluid shear stress and atherosclerosis, both of which represent critical pathological processes underlying the initiation and progression of DN (Ma et al. 2021), thus verifying the functional relevance and disease specificity of the identified candidate genes.

To distinguish primary mechanisms from secondary associations among the 26 co-enriched pathways, we adopted a biology‑driven prioritization strategy. Specifically, we focused on pathways most directly linked to the established pathophysiology of DN, including metabolic dysregulation (oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid metabolism), allogeneic transplantation rejection. These pathways were prioritized as core pathways in the subsequent discussion, whereas other co-enriched pathways were considered secondary associations that may reflect indirect or compensatory biological processes. This prioritization strategy allowed us to focus the mechanistic interpretation on pathways with the strongest a priori relevance to DN pathogenesis.

Correlation of biological functions of core key genes with the pathogenesis of diabetic nephropathy

IGFBP3: a risk factor for DN onset and its regulatory pathways

IGFBP3, the most abundant IGFBP family member, regulates IGF-1 activity via binding/half-life modulation (mediating proliferation, apoptosis, metabolism) and modulates fibrosis/inflammation independently of IGF-1. The IGF-IGFBP axis is critical in DN (Oh 2012). Here, IGFBP3 was significantly upregulated in DN samples, and MR analysis confirmed it as a DN risk factor. Enrichment analysis linked IGFBP3 to allograft rejection and oxidative phosphorylation; disrupted oxidative phosphorylation-induced mitochondrial dysfunction exacerbates oxidative stress-mediated renal injury, a key mechanism of IGFBP3-driven DN (Higgins and Coughlan 2014). Regulatory network analysis showed IGFBP3 is regulated by the MIR497HG/hsa-miR-19a-3p axis and targeted by transcription factors (AR, HDAC1, SP3, SP1), with dysregulation of these regulators (e.g., upregulated HDAC1 (Noh et al. 2009), downregulated miR-19a-3p (Zhou et al. 2018) synergistically promoting IGFBP3 overexpression. RT-qPCR validated IGFBP3 upregulation in DN models, and XZTL intervention significantly normalized its expression, suggesting that IGFBP3 may represent a promising therapeutic target of XZTL warranting further investigation for clinical translational value.

APOD and LPL: regulatory roles in the homeostasis of lipid metabolism

Apo-D, encoded by the APOD gene and belonging to the lipocalin superfamily, participates in lipid transport and cholesterol homeostasis as an HDL component interacting with LCAT (Flores et al. 2019). APOD serves as a biomarker for acute renal transplant rejection (Dlouha et al. 2021), and our MR analysis identified it as a risk factor for DN, despite its downregulation in DN samples. This apparent contradiction—genetically elevated APOD as a risk factor yet downregulated in diseased tissues—reflects a critical distinction between genetic susceptibility and dynamic changes in gene expression during disease progression. The MR estimate captures the lifelong effect of genetically determined higher APOD expression on DN susceptibility, consistent with its role as a predictive risk marker. In contrast, the downregulation of APOD in established DN likely represents a secondary or compensatory response to the pathological milieu. Under this framework, XZTL-mediated restoration of APOD expression should be interpreted as reversal of this maladaptive downregulation, rather than augmentation of a risk-conferring genetic trait. This dual role—where a gene may be a genetic risk factor yet its disease-induced downregulation is therapeutically corrected—is recognized in other disease contexts, such as B-type natriuretic peptide in heart failure, where genetic variants predict risk while therapeutic elevation of the peptide improves outcomes. We propose that genetically higher APOD expression may increase DN susceptibility (Rossi et al. 2015), while its post-disease reduction represents secondary or compensatory changes. This apparent discordance—APOD being a genetically predicted risk factor (IVW effect > 0) yet downregulated in diseased kidney tissues—does not constitute a biological contradiction. Rather, it reflects a fundamental distinction between the lifelong genetic susceptibility conferred by APOD variants and the dynamic, stage-specific transcriptional response to established disease. Genetic association studies have similarly reported that APOD polymorphisms can modify disease risk (e.g., a 3-fold increased risk of early-onset Alzheimer’s disease associated with the APOD − 352G allele) (Helisalmi et al. 2004), while APOD expression is concurrently upregulated in diseased brain tissues (Shibata et al. 2013), illustrating that the direction of genetic effect and tissue expression change can diverge. In the context of DN, the downregulation of APOD in the diseased state may represent a compensatory or maladaptive response to chronic metabolic stress, oxidative injury, and inflammation—a pattern consistent with the observation that APOD upregulation exerts renoprotective effects by suppressing oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory cascades (Huang et al. 2026). Reduced APOD expression disrupts lipid metabolism, promotes renal lipid deposition, inflammation, and fibrosis (Han et al. 2024), aggravating renal injury. Regulatory network analysis showed APOD is exclusively regulated by transcription factor NOTCH3. XZTL significantly upregulated APOD, suggesting that restoration of lipid homeostasis may be one mechanism underlying its renoprotective effects in DN, although the causal role and stage-specific function of APOD in DN require further validation through functional studies.

The LPL gene encodes lipoprotein lipase (LPL), an enzyme expressed in the heart, muscle and adipose tissues (Knapp et al. 2023). LPL exerts dual functions as a triglyceride hydrolase and a ligand/bridging factor, and is essential for receptor-mediated lipoprotein uptake. As a rate-limiting enzyme in lipid metabolism, it primarily catalyzes the hydrolysis of triglycerides in the core of chylomicrons and very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL) into free fatty acids and monoglycerides (Talmud et al. 1998). The LPL gene has multiple polymorphisms that are closely associated with the development of dyslipidemias including hyperlipidemia and hypertriglyceridemia. Severe LPL gene mutations can cause lipoprotein lipase deficiency, thereby leading to type I hyperlipoproteinemia (Ng et al. 2006). Reduced LPL activity has been observed in patients with DN. Our study identified LPL as a protective factor for DN, with significantly decreased expression in DN samples—consistent with previous reports of reduced LPL activity in DN patients (Kashiwazaki et al. 1998). Downregulated LPL increases plasma triglyceride levels, exacerbates renal lipid deposition, oxidative stress, and fibrosis (Pei et al. 2020), which was confirmed by RT-qPCR in DN models. Enrichment analysis linked LPL to adipogenesis and, together with IGFBP3 and APOD, to pathways including oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid metabolism. Regulatory network analysis showed LPL is regulated by the NEAT1/hsa-miR-29a-3p axis and targeted by transcription factors (NFKB1, RELA, SP3, SP1); notably, SP3 and SP1 co-regulate both LPL and IGFBP3, suggesting synergistic transcriptional regulation. XZTL significantly upregulated LPL expression, suggesting its renoprotective effects might involve restoring LPL-mediated lipid metabolism, although this mechanistic link requires experimental confirmation. Collectively, these three hub genes may contribute to DN progression via coordinated regulation of lipid and energy metabolism, and XZTL’s simultaneous normalization of their aberrant expression provides a foundation for hypothesizing its multi-targeted therapeutic mechanism in DN, which awaits further experimental verification.

Clinical translational value and drug targeting potential of core key genes

The constructed drug-active component-core gene network reveals the multi-target mechanism of XZTL against diabetic nephropathy (DN). Network analysis showed that seven herbs act on 29 candidate genes including IGFBP3, APOD, and LPL via 150 bioactive ingredients, embodying the multi-component, multi-target, multi-pathway synergy of traditional Chinese medicine formulas. Molecular docking demonstrated predicted binding affinities with binding energies < − 6 kcal/mol: quercetin-IGFBP3 (− 7.5 kcal/mol), baicalein-APOD (− 6.1 kcal/mol), and oleic acid-LPL (− 6.5 kcal/mol). Quercetin, a flavonoid with known lipid-regulating, antioxidant, and renoprotective activities (Chen et al. 2022), may potentially attenuate oxidative phosphorylation disorder and renal fibrosis by inhibiting IGFBP3 overexpression, as suggested by the binding affinity observed in our docking analysis. Baicalein, a major antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antifibrotic component (Wang et al. 2015), may protect the kidney via targeting APOD to modulate lipid metabolism and oxidative stress based on the predicted interaction. Oleic acid improves lipid profiles and lowers triglycerides (Listenberger et al. 2003), and may enhance LPL-mediated lipid clearance to reduce renal lipid accumulation according to our computational prediction. Notably, IGFBP3, APOD, and LPL are co-enriched in oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition, suggesting XZTL might act through coordinated regulation of these pathways. Nevertheless, molecular docking results only indicate binding affinity at the computational level and should not be equated with in vivo efficacy or direct causal mechanisms. These in silico findings serve primarily as hypotheses-generating evidence and require rigorous experimental validation through cellular assays, animal models, and pharmacological studies to confirm target engagement and therapeutic relevance. Quercetin could theoretically inhibits IGFBP3 to restore oxidative phosphorylation, while oleic acid activates LPL and baicalein might regulate APOD to rebalance lipid metabolism, jointly reducing renal oxidative stress, lipid deposition, and fibrosis. This multi-component, multi-target, multi-pathway synergy highlights the advantage of XZTL and provides a scientific basis for its clinical application in DN.

In addition, the ROC curve analysis showed that the AUC values of the three core genes all exceeded 0.7, with their expression levels significantly altered in DN samples, indicating their potential as novel biomarkers for DN diagnosis. Compared with traditional indicators such as the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), genetic biomarkers offer higher specificity and sensitivity, and the combined detection of the three genes may further improve diagnostic efficacy, thus providing a novel technical approach for the early screening and disease monitoring of DN.

Study limitations and future perspectives

This study has certain limitations. First, CAV1 was excluded from MR analysis due to poor SNP distribution, which may have led to the omission of potential key genes; expanding the sample size or using multicenter data is warranted for future validation. Colocalization analysis was not performed due to data accessibility constraints—the eQTL and DN GWAS data differed in population background and platforms, and APOD had only 3 SNPs, limiting posterior power. Although we conducted multiple sensitivity analyses (heterogeneity, pleiotropy, leave-one-out, Steiger test) to support MR robustness, we acknowledge that the absence of colocalization precludes fully excluding distinct causal variants within the same region. Second, the lncRNA‑miRNA‑mRNA regulatory networks (e.g., MIR497HG/hsa‑miR‑19a‑3p/IGFBP3 and NEAT1/hsa‑miR‑29a‑3p/LPL) were constructed based solely on database predictions (DIANA‑microT, miRDB, starBase) and literature mining, without experimental validation in the original transcriptomic cohorts or biological samples; these regulatory axes remain hypothetical and require verification through cellular and animal experiments. Third, the diagnostic value of the three key genes should be interpreted with caution, as ROC analyses were conducted using only two public datasets with limited sample sizes, and no direct quantitative comparison with established clinical biomarkers (e.g., UACR, eGFR) was performed. Fourth, the public datasets lacked detailed DN pathological staging information (e.g., Mogensen or KDIGO stages), preventing stage-stratified analyses; future prospective cohorts with complete staging annotations are needed to assess stage-specific expression patterns. Molecular docking results indicate only computational binding affinity and should not be equated with in vivo efficacy; the proposed mechanisms require confirmation through biochemical assays and functional studies. Due to the absence of complete clinical covariates in the public datasets, we were unable to adjust for these confounders in the differential expression analysis; however, MR analysis inherently mitigates such confounding, and future prospective cohorts with full clinical annotations are required for multivariate-adjusted validation. Fifth, the STZ-induced rat model does not fully replicate human DN pathophysiology, and future studies using complementary models (e.g., db/db mice) are needed to confirm the findings. Sixth, the active components of XZTL were selected based on TCMSP database criteria (OB ≥ 20%, DL ≥ 0.1) rather than experimentally measured phytochemical composition, which may not fully reflect the actual chemical profile of the herbal decoction used in animal experiments. Future LC-MS/MS-based chemical fingerprinting is needed to bridge the gap between in silico predictions and experimental validation. Seventh, this study did not explore the specific regulatory patterns of the co-enriched pathways, and future studies can clarify these mechanisms using gene silencing and overexpression techniques. Eighth, the causal effects identified through Mendelian randomization analysis represent statistical associations between genetic variants and disease risk, and do not on their own demonstrate biological causation or mechanistic function. Although we performed RT-qPCR validation of key gene expression in DN models, further functional experiments are required to establish the mechanistic roles of these genes in DN pathogenesis. Ninth, the GSEA results are computational predictions requiring experimental validation. While we verified mRNA expression of APOD, IGFBP3, and LPL by RT-qPCR, the enriched pathways (e.g., oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism) need further confirmation via protein-level and functional assays. Tenth, this study did not directly investigate whether modulation of APOD, IGFBP3, or LPL affects renal fibrosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, or podocyte injury. While GSEA linked these genes to pathways relevant to these processes, their direct roles in these pathological events remain to be experimentally determined. Future studies will assess these phenotypes following gene manipulation in cellular and animal models. Eleventh, this study is a preclinical mechanistic investigation based on public databases and animal models; no patient cohort receiving XZTL therapy was analyzed to verify the expression changes of APOD, IGFBP3, and LPL in relation to clinical outcomes (e.g., UACR, eGFR improvement). Therefore, the clinical translation of these findings requires prospective observational studies or randomized controlled trials in DN patients to confirm the therapeutic relevance and target engagement of XZTL in the clinical setting. Twelfth, longitudinal validation is absent; all expression analyses were performed at a single time point, making it unclear whether APOD, IGFBP3, and LPL predict disease progression or therapeutic response over time. Thirteenth, this study did not evaluate potential interactions between XZTL and standard DN therapies (ACEIs, ARBs, SGLT2i, MRAs). Experiments were conducted with XZTL alone, without combination groups, so we cannot assess additive effects on blood pressure, volume status, potassium, or renal function, nor draw conclusions on combination safety or efficacy. Future studies will include monotherapy and combination arms to systematically evaluate pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and safety. Fourteenth, although we verified the mRNA expression of APOD, IGFBP3, and LPL by RT-qPCR, corresponding protein-level validation (e.g., Western blot, ELISA, or immunohistochemistry) is lacking; therefore, the correlation between transcript and protein abundance remains to be established. Fifteenth, although XZTL treatment restored the mRNA expression of APOD, IGFBP3, and LPL in DN models, these transcriptional changes do not establish these genes as direct therapeutic targets of the herbal formula. The absence of functional experiments—such as gene knockdown/overexpression followed by XZTL intervention—limits our ability to conclude whether the renoprotective effects of XZTL are causally mediated through modulation of these genes. Future cellular and animal studies with genetic manipulation are required to validate their functional roles as mediators of XZTL efficacy. Sixteenth, cellular mechanistic experiments (e.g., in podocytes or mesangial cells) and genetic intervention studies to establish causal relationships between XZTL efficacy and APOD, IGFBP3, or LPL are currently lacking; these are required to confirm target engagement and will be pursued in future work. Seventeenth, systematic toxicological evaluation of XZTL was not conducted, and its safety profile remains to be established through standardized preclinical toxicity studies.

Despite the aforementioned limitations, this study identified LPL, IGFBP3 and APOD as the core key genes of DN through multi-dimensional analyses, and clarified their diagnostic value, regulatory networks and drug targeting potential, thereby providing novel insights into the mechanistic investigation and clinical translation of DN. Building on the findings of this study, future research can focus on the development of novel drugs targeting these core genes or the construction of a multi-gene combined diagnostic model, which will offer more robust support for the precise prevention and treatment of DN. Future translational efforts should prioritize: (1) prospective validation of the three-gene diagnostic panel against conventional biomarkers; (2) time-series animal models and tissue-specific genetic manipulation to resolve APOD’s dual role as genetic risk factor yet downregulated in disease; (3) orthogonal biochemical assays and target perturbation studies to confirm computational docking predictions; (4) functional dissection of co-enriched pathways to determine whether these genes act synergistically or independently; and (5) longitudinal studies with multi-timepoint sampling (e.g., 2, 4, 6, 8 weeks post-modeling) to evaluate whether these genes predict disease progression and therapeutic response; (6) Colocalization analysis integrating large-scale kidney eQTL datasets (e.g., NephQTL, GTEx kidney tissues) with DN GWAS data, combined with transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS), to more precisely dissect the causal variants and regulatory mechanisms of these key genes; (7) integration of single-cell transcriptomic and spatial transcriptomic technologies to systematically map the expression distribution of APOD, IGFBP3, and LPL across different renal compartments (glomeruli, tubules, interstitium) and cell types, as well as prospective cohort studies with whole-kidney tissue and blood samples to validate their diagnostic value across type 1 and type 2 DN subtypes. These investigations will advance current findings from target discovery toward mechanistic elucidation and clinical translation for DN precision medicine.
