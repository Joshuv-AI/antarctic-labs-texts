Beyond the aerobic-first paradigm: Optimizing functional capacity through combined aerobic and resistance training in heart failure
Authors: Wenfei Zhu, Jiaming Yan, Yuliang Sun
Published: 2026

Heart failure (HF) remains a major global public health challenge, affecting more than 64 million individuals worldwide1 with considerable morbidity and mortality, and imposing a substantial and growing burden on healthcare systems.2,3 The condition is characterized by a progressive decline in exercise tolerance and quality of life, arising from complex interactions between central cardiac dysfunction and pervasive peripheral abnormalities. Although exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation is a Class I recommendation for improving functional capacity in patients with HF,4 the optimal composition of exercise prescriptions remains unresolved. For decades, aerobic training (AT) has served as the cornerstone of rehabilitation programs. However, the multifactorial pathophysiology of HF, particularly the prominent role of skeletal muscle dysfunction, suggests that an exclusively aerobic approach may be insufficient to address functional impairment fully.

In this issue of the Journal of Sport and Health Science, Terada and colleagues5 report a systematic review and meta-analysis comparing combined aerobic and resistance training (CT) with AT alone across the HF spectrum, including patients with reduced (HF with reduced ejection fraction, HFrEF) and preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). Their findings demonstrate that, particularly in HFrEF, CT confers significantly greater improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), 6-min walk distance, and upper-body muscle strength than AT alone. Notably, these superior gains in CRF were achieved despite a substantially lower aerobic volume in CT protocols. Across trials, the mean aerobic duration was 23.5 min per session in CT compared with 43.0 min in AT-only programs, and the advantage of CT persisted even when total session duration was matched between modalities. These results challenge the long-standing assumption that aerobic volume is the primary driver of functional adaptation in HF, highlighting instead the added value of resistance training. This insight is particularly salient given that “lack of time” remains the most frequently cited barrier to sustained exercise participation.6

From a mechanistic standpoint, the improvement in CRF with CT, despite the absence of meaningful changes in central cardiac indices, such as left ventricular ejection fraction, underscores the critical role of peripheral adaptations in HF-related exercise intolerance. Patients with HFrEF commonly exhibit skeletal muscle abnormalities, including reduced lean mass, a shift toward less oxidative fiber phenotypes, and diminished mitochondrial content.7,8 Resistance training directly targets these deficits by increasing muscle mass and enhancing skeletal muscle oxidative capacity and oxygen extraction. The findings of Terada et al.,5 therefore, provide strong support for a muscle-centric mechanism in which improvements in peripheral oxygen utilization partially offset fixed central hemodynamic limitations. This mechanism offers a plausible physiological explanation for clinically meaningful functional gains in the absence of demonstrable cardiac remodeling.

Furthermore, this meta-analysis highlights a distinct advantage of concurrent training in enhancing upper-body muscle strength, an area where AT alone provides minimal benefit. Terada et al.5 reported a significant mean difference of 8.3 kg in upper-body strength, favoring CT. Although the certainty of this evidence was graded as low due to risk of bias and imprecision, the clinical implications for functional autonomy may be substantial. Upper-body strength is a prerequisite for instrumental activities of daily living, such as lifting and carrying. Moreover, objective metrics such as handgrip strength and mid-upper arm circumference serve as independent prognostic markers, inversely associated with mortality in HF populations.9,10 By specifically targeting the upper extremities, CT offers a more comprehensive rehabilitative strategy that may help counteract the catabolic processes, physical disability, and loss of independence commonly observed in advanced HF.

A rigorous appraisal of the evidence also requires careful consideration of its limitations and real-world applicability. Generalizability remains a primary concern, as the available evidence is derived predominantly from male patients with HFrEF; women comprise only 17% of the study population, and HFpEF is represented by a single trial accounting for just 11% of the pooled cohort.11 Consequently, extrapolating these findings to women and HFpEF populations remains highly uncertain. In addition, the durability of the benefits is unclear, given that most trials were short-term and conducted under closely supervised conditions, leaving unresolved questions about the persistence of functional gains once structured programs conclude. Scalability presents a further challenge, particularly in home-based or resource-limited settings where access to equipment, supervision, and monitoring is constrained. Finally, key elements of the resistance training prescription, including optimal dosing, progression, and integration with aerobic exercise, remain insufficiently defined, limiting the translation of these findings into routine clinical practice.

Relatedly, the sequencing of exercise modalities warrants further investigation. Most trials implemented aerobic exercise before resistance training; however, the optimal order for minimizing concurrent training interference and maximizing skeletal muscle adaptation remains unclear. Addressing these gaps will require adequately powered, sex-stratified trials with longer follow-up, alongside pragmatic delivery models and efforts to identify dose-efficient CT protocols capable of sustaining peripheral adaptations outside controlled laboratory environments.12,13

In summary, the synthesis by Terada and colleagues5 provides strong evidence that CT is a safe and effective strategy for improving functional capacity in clinically stable HF, particularly in HFrEF. By explicitly targeting peripheral skeletal muscle adaptations alongside aerobic conditioning, CT offers a more functionally relevant approach to rehabilitation. The next critical step is to determine how broadly these benefits extend, especially among women and patients with HFpEF, to ensure that exercise prescriptions can be applied more equitably across sex and ejection-fraction phenotypes.
