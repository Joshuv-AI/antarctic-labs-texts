Enteral and Partial Parenteral Nutritional Support in Two Hospitalised Giant Anteaters (Myrmecophaga tridactyla)
Authors: Livia Perles, Camila Goloni, Brana Sanctos Alo Bonder, Fabio Alves Teixeira, Stephanie de Souza Theodoro, Aulus Cavalieri Carciofi, Karin Werther
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundInformation regarding enteral and parenteral nutritional support in hospitalised giant anteaters (Myrmecophaga tridactyla) is limited.ObjectivesTo describe the nutritional management and clinical outcomes of two giant anteaters receiving different forms of nutritional support following traumatic brain injury.MethodsClinical records of two free‐ranging giant anteaters admitted after traumatic accidents were retrospectively reviewed. Clinical presentation, nutritional interventions, administration procedures, complications and outcomes were evaluated. One animal received enteral nutrition through a nasoesophageal tube, whereas the other received partial parenteral nutrition (PPN).ResultsThe first anteater presented with a skull fracture and cerebral haemorrhage and was unable to feed voluntarily. Nasoesophageal feeding was provided for 7 days and was not associated with observed aspiration or nasal complications. Voluntary feeding subsequently resumed, and the animal was discharged after 2 months. The second anteater presented comatose following head trauma and received PPN after 4 days without voluntary food intake. Hyperglycemia occurred during administration and was managed by reducing the infusion rate. Voluntary food intake resumed on the second day of PPN. However, the animal died 15 days after admission from complications attributed to the head trauma.ConclusionsNasoesophageal feeding and PPN were technically feasible in these two hospitalised giant anteaters. These cases provide preliminary clinical information on alternative nutritional support in this species but do not establish the safety or efficacy of either technique.Trial RegistrationThis retrospective descriptive case series did not involve a prospective clinical trial requiring registration.Keywords: enteral nutrition, giant anteater, intensive care, nasoesophageal tube, parenteral nutrition

Introduction

The giant anteater (Myrmecophaga tridactyla) is a specialised insectivorous mammal widely distributed throughout South America and currently classified as Vulnerable by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) (Miranda et al. 2025). In Brazil, they are originally distributed across the nation, from rainforests to semi‐arid fields (Gaudin et al. 2018; Miranda et al. 2025). Owing to its highly specialised feeding anatomy, including an elongated rostrum, protrusible tongue and adhesive saliva, this species feeds almost exclusively on ants and termites in the wild (Gaudin et al. 2018; Miranda et al. 2025). Wild giant anteaters admitted to rehabilitation centres and veterinary hospitals frequently present traumatic injuries associated with road traffic accidents, wildfires or other anthropogenic causes (Miranda 2012; Miranda et al. 2025). These patients commonly exhibit pain, single or multiple fractures, neurological impairment, reduced consciousness or severe debilitation (Santos Zupirolli et al. 2021; Minto et al. 2021; de Siqueira et al. 2024), conditions that may prevent voluntary food intake for prolonged periods. In critically ill veterinary patients, nutritional support is recognised as an essential component of intensive care because prolonged fasting contributes to protein catabolism, impaired immune function, delayed tissue repair and poorer clinical outcomes (Brunetto et al. 2010). Although these principles are well established in domestic species, information regarding nutritional support in hospitalised giant anteaters remains limited. Consequently, maintaining adequate nutrition under hospitalised conditions represents a considerable challenge, since replicating its natural diet is difficult and often requires specially formulated diets (Valdes and Soto 2012; Clark et al. 2016; Cole et al. 2020; Morford and Meyers 2003; Steinecker‐Quast et al. 2024).

Whenever the gastrointestinal tract is functional, enteral nutrition is generally considered the preferred route because it maintains gastrointestinal integrity and is associated with fewer complications than parenteral nutrition (Brunetto et al. 2010). However, the anatomical characteristics of giant anteaters and the clinical condition of many hospitalised patients may limit the use of conventional enteral feeding techniques. Oesophagostomy has previously been described as a feasible technique for nutritional support in this species; nevertheless, the procedure requires general anaesthesia, which may not be appropriate for animals presenting severe neurological injury or unstable clinical conditions (Santos et al. 2011; Camargo et al. 2024). In these situations, less invasive enteral techniques or partial parenteral nutrition (PPN) may represent valuable alternatives, although published information describing their application in giant anteaters is scarce. Therefore, the present report describes the clinical application of nutritional support strategies in two hospitalised giant anteaters with traumatic brain injury, emphasising technical aspects, nutritional management and clinical outcomes.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective descriptive study reviewed the medical records of two free‐ranging giant anteaters (M. tridactyla) admitted to the Veterinary Hospital “Governador Laudo Natel” (HV), Jaboticabal, Brazil, following traumatic injuries. Information regarding signalment, clinical presentation, nutritional management, feeding protocols, complications and outcomes was extracted from the medical records.

Patient 1

An adult female M. tridactyla, weighing 37 kg, was referred to the veterinary hospital in 2014 after being found near a railway track. On admission, the animal exhibited a depressed level of consciousness but remained responsive to tactile and painful stimuli. To allow safe handling, complete physical examination and diagnostic imaging, the patient was chemically restrained with butorphanol (0.4 mg/kg IM) and midazolam (0.5 mg/kg IM). Clinical evaluation showed facial and skull scratches and radiography and computed tomography revealed a cranium fracture and cerebral haemorrhage, with no other fractures or lesions detected. Treatment for head trauma was initiated, consisting of mannitol (1 g/kg, IV, semel in die [SID], for 2 days), fluid therapy (Ringer Lactate, IV) and nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory medication (Meloxicam, IM, SID, 0.2 mg/kg) (Desai et al. 2019). Daily clinical evaluations performed during the first 4 days of hospitalisation showed no improvement in the neurological status. The animal remained depressed, exhibited nystagmus, was unable to maintain a standing position and absence of voluntary food intake. Because the animal remained unable to feed voluntarily despite supportive treatment, the multidisciplinary team elected to initiate enteral nutritional support after 4 days of hospitalisation through a nasoesophageal tube.

During the daily clinical evaluations, the swallowing reflex was assessed by gently moistening the tongue with water administered using a syringe. The animal consistently perceived the water stimulus and swallowed saliva, confirming preservation of the swallowing reflex and allowing enteral feeding. Owing to the patient's traumatic brain injury and depressed level of consciousness, general anaesthesia was considered contraindicated because of the potential risk of worsening the neurological condition. Therefore, the tube was placed without anaesthesia, using only topical lidocaine gel for lubrication. Before tube placement, thoracic radiographs were obtained to estimate the appropriate insertion length. Based on these radiographs, the tube was measured externally from the nostril to the level of the seventh intercostal space, corresponding to the thoracic oesophagus. A 6‐Fr nasoesophageal tube filled with iodinated contrast medium was then gently introduced through the nostril. When the tube reached the caudal nasopharynx, the animal was observed swallowing it, allowing its passage into the oesophagus. Because fluoroscopy was not available, serial contrast radiographs were obtained during tube advancement to confirm its progression and final positioning within the oesophagus (Figure 1A).

Nasoesophageal tube for enteral feeding in giant anteater (Myrmecophaga tridactyla). (A) Radiographic image of the tube filled with iodinated contrast. (B) Water administration through the nasoesophageal tube secured with adhesive plaster.

The patient was fed a high‐protein and high energy‐dense diet (0.96 kcal/mL; parameters compared to carnivores’ pets) through the nasoesophageal tube (nutrients per 100 g of dry matter: 33.4 g crude protein; 28.4 g fat; 1.5 g crude fibre, 0.7 g calcium, 0.55 g phosphorus and 0.55 g potassium). This diet, formulated by the veterinary nutritional services, included (on as‐fed basis) water (69.5%), soluble soy extract (15.3%), milk cream (11.4%), corn flour (1.1%), dextrose (1.1%), mineral–vitamin–amino acid supplement (0.8%), potassium chloride solution at 19.6% (0.3%) and taurine (0.03%) in a liquid consistency. The energy supplied was calculated using the resting energy requirements (RER) formula 70 kcal/BW0.75/day. Fluid therapy (Figure 1B) and medication administration (Piracetam, OR, 10 mg/kg) were also performed via the nasoesophageal tube. Routine feeding through the nasoesophageal tube did not require anaesthesia or sedation. Because the animal remained with a depressed level of consciousness, only minimal physical restraint was occasionally necessary during feeding.

The anteater was fed through the nasoesophageal tube for 7 days, with the RER proportion gradually, that is, 15% of RER on the first day, 25% on the second day, 50% on the third day, 75% on the fourth day and 100% from the fifth day onwards. The volume of diet administered via feeding tube was portioned into six meals per day, with each meal delivered at 3‐h intervals. After 7 days, the animal spontaneously regurgitated the nasoesophageal tube and gradually resumed voluntary feeding with the institution's routine diet for hospitalised giant anteaters, consisting of eggs, lactose‐free bovine milk, milk cream, calcium and a multivitamin supplement. No complications were observed during tube use (e.g., aspiration of food, nasal secretion and sneeze), though it was time consuming to administer the diet due to its consistency and the tube's small caliber. Body weight could not be monitored during enteral feeding because repeated transport to the hospital's large‐animal scale would have required chemical restraint, which was considered contraindicated in this patient. Hydration status was monitored daily, with no alterations observed during tube use. No faecal output was observed during the period of nasoesophageal tube feeding. Defecation occurred only after spontaneous expulsion of the tube and resumption of voluntary feeding, precluding evaluation of the effects of the enteral diet administered through the tube on faecal consistency. No gastrointestinal complications were recorded after the animal resumed voluntary feeding until discharge. The animal was discharged after a 2‐month hospitalisation period.

Patient 2

An adult female M. tridactyla, weighing 30 kg, was referred to the veterinary hospital in 2016 after being found in comatose status on a road. On admission, the animal exhibited a depressed level of consciousness. To allow safe handling, complete physical examination and diagnostic imaging, the patient was chemically restrained with butorphanol (0.4 mg/kg IM) and midazolam (0.5 mg/kg IM). Clinical examination revealed facial and body abrasions, and radiographic examination did not identify any fractures. CT was not available at the time of admission. The animal was treated for head trauma, and therapy consisted of mannitol (1 g/kg, IV), fluid therapy (Ringer Lactate, IV) and nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory medicine (meloxicam, IM, SID, 0.2 mg/kg) (Desai et al. 2019).

During the first 3 days of hospitalisation, the patient received supportive treatment for traumatic brain injury and underwent daily clinical evaluations until its neurological condition was considered sufficiently stable to receive nutritional support. During these evaluations, the swallowing reflex was assessed by gently moistening the tongue with water administered using a syringe. Unlike Patient 1, the animal did not respond to the water stimulus or swallow saliva, indicating absence of a functional swallowing reflex. Therefore, enteral nutrition through a nasoesophageal tube was considered unsafe, and the multidisciplinary team elected to initiate PPN. Since the patient remained markedly depressed neurologically, peripheral venous catheterisation was performed without additional sedation or general anaesthesia, requiring only gentle physical restraint. However, because the individualised PPN solution had to be prepared under aseptic conditions and the required components were not immediately available, an additional day was necessary before administration could be initiated. The individualised PPN formulation was compounded under aseptic conditions by the veterinary nutrition service. Consequently, PPN was started 4 days after admission through a peripheral venous catheter (left brachial vein on the first day and jugular on the second day) aiming to supply 60% of RER (70 kcal/BW0.75/day). The partial parenteral solution was constituted by 60% of energy as dextrose (50% dextrose solution) and 40% as lipids (20% lipid emulsion). Energy from protein were not considered. It was arbitrarily established, a supply of 3.4 g protein per 100 kcal of RER, which was supplied as a 10% amino acid solution. Vitamin B complex was supplied at 1 mL/100 kcal. Electrolyte composition was settled at: 20 mEq/L of potassium (as potassium chloride solution at 19.6% and potassium phosphate solution at 2 mmol/L), 40 mEq/L of sodium (as sodium chloride solution at 20%); 50 mEq/L of chlorine (as magnesium sulphate solution at 50%); 3.0mMol/L of phosphorus and 4.7 mEq/L of magnesium. The osmolality of the solution was calculated at 750 mOsm/L. The solution was prepared in a sterile environment, and its infusion was controlled by an infusion pump (ST550 T2, Samtronic Indústria e Comércio Ltda, São Paulo, Brazil). The rate of infusion was adjusted according to blood glucose level. The final volume infused on the first day was 35 mL/kg/day, and 20 mL/kg/day on the second day. Vital parameters (temperature, respiratory and cardiac frequency) were observed every hour. Blood glucose levels were measured by a portable glucose meter (Accu‐Chek Active, Roche Ltda., São Paulo, Brazil) every 30 min by collecting one drop of blood from the tail's vein until levels were stabilised.

On the first day of PPN administration, blood glucose concentrations showed marked variation, ranging from 135 to 303 mg/dL (i.e., 7.5–16.8 mmol/L), despite progressive reduction of the infusion rate. On the second day, better glycaemic control was achieved with a lower infusion rate, with blood glucose concentrations ranging from 115 to 207 mg/dL (i.e., 6.4–11.5 mmol/L), as shown in Figure 2. During PPN infusion, vital parameters remained within the expected range for the species (body temperature: 33°C–34°C; cardiac frequency: 50–70 bpm and respiratory frequency: 10–15 bpm). The patient showed clinical improvement with therapy for head trauma and nutritional support, and showed interest in food on the second day of PPN, beginning to eat and drink water gradually after 2.5 h of PPN infusion. From the third day onwards, the animal received only spontaneous feeding with the institution's routine diet for hospitalised giant anteaters. Body weight could not be monitored during PPN infusion because repeated transport to the hospital's large‐animal scale would have required chemical restraint, which was considered contraindicated in this patient, and it was also not possible to evaluate how PPN affected faecal consistency, since no faecal output was observed during the period of PPN. After 15 days of hospitalisation, the animal was found dead in the enclosure in the morning. Postmortem examination revealed extensive intracranial haemorrhage with blood clot formation, consistent with progression of the traumatic brain injury, and death was therefore attributed to complications of the head trauma.

Discussion

The use of alternative feeding routes for giant anteaters (e.g., nasoesophageal tube and PPN) showed to be an appropriate nutritional support for hospitalised patients. Alternatively, the use of the oesophagostomy procedure can be helpful (Santos et al. 2011; Camargo et al. 2024), but most trauma patients cannot undergo anaesthetic procedures due to possible complications (Desai et al. 2019). In Case 1, we opted to use a nasoesophageal tube due to evidence in other species that it is adequate to maintain feeding via the enteral route for better intestinal viability (Smith et al. 2019). In addition, oesophagostomy was deemed unsuitable due to potential side effects of anaesthetics that could increase intracerebral pressure (Desai et al. 2019). The process of introducing the nasoesophageal tube was a safe and effective alternative for the management of anteater patients who cannot undergo anaesthesia for placement of an oesophageal tube. The successful introduction of the nasoesophageal tube in this species showed that, despite the anatomical challenges, the procedure can be carried out without major complications. The technique of nasoesophageal tube placement in anteaters differs from other mammals (e.g., dogs and cats) because the nasal cavity is elongated, so the use of iodinated contrast during radiography is important to ensure correct placement. Clinical assessment alone, including observation of swallowing, was not considered sufficient to exclude inadvertent tracheal placement because of the patient's depressed neurological status and the small diameter of the feeding tube. A water‐soluble iodinated contrast agent was preferred over barium sulphate because inadvertent tracheal placement of the tube cannot be completely excluded during insertion. If aspiration occurs, iodinated contrast agents are generally considered safer than barium sulphate because they are less likely to cause persistent pulmonary complications (Thrall 2018).

The enteral diet applied was formulated by veterinary nutritionists based on recommendations for carnivore pets (NRC 2006). The giant anteater is insectivore for which nutrient requirements are not clearly established, making diet formulation in captivity challenging for this species (Nofs et al. 2018). We did not find specific nutritional profile recommendations for enteral diet of hospitalised anteater. It was decided to apply a similar complete enteral formula for the nutritional support of cats, which are routinely cared for at the veterinary hospital. The diet used was enriched with taurine because there is evidence that dietary taurine may be critical if protein intake is low or of poor quality (Nofs et al. 2018).

In Case 2, the possibility of surgically placing an oesophageal tube was initially considered, however due to anaesthetic difficulties that could increase intracerebral pressure, the procedure was deemed inappropriate (Desai et al. 2019). Also, the use of a nasoesophageal tube was not appropriate, since the absence of swallowing reflex meant the animal was unable to protect airways (Chan and Freeman 2015). Finally, PPN was chosen. It is an option for critical patients and presents less complications than total parenteral nutrition. Even being safer, PPN may present some complications, mainly of metabolic origin (Chan et al. 2002; Gosmanov and Umpierrez 2013). In PPN, hyperglycaemia was the most common metabolic complication in dogs, whereas lipemia and hyperglycaemia were equally common in cats (Chan et al. 2002). Blood triglycerides and cholesterol concentrations were not measured in the present case reported. The anteater presented hyperglycaemia on the first day of PNN infusion. The use of insulin is recommended to reverse the hyperglycaemic status and, in some cases, only the amount of dextrose infused can be reduced (Gosmanov and Umpierrez 2013). In the present case, the authors opted to gradually reduce the PPN infusion rate to keep the patient's blood glucose stable. Lack of information in the literature about safe insulin dosage administration in giant anteaters was a limiting factor for its use. At the time these cases were managed, no published reference values for blood glucose in M. tridactyla were available, and glycaemic values were therefore interpreted using reference intervals for healthy adult dogs. More recently, blood glucose concentrations of healthy M. tridactyla have been reported to be approximately 104 mg/dL in captive animals (Oliveira et al. 2018) and to range from 74.7 to 149.8 mg/dL in free‐ranging individuals (Alves et al. 2023). Compared with these currently available reference values, the patient remained hyperglycaemic throughout PPN administration, although blood glucose concentrations progressively decreased after reduction of the infusion rate. Although based on a single case, these findings suggest that blood glucose should be closely monitored during PPN administration in giant anteaters, allowing timely adjustment of the infusion rate when hyperglycaemia develops.

Metabolic complications associated with PPN are influenced primarily by infusion rate rather than solution composition (Gosmanov and Umpierrez 2013). In the present case, the infusion rate was adjusted according to serial blood glucose measurements and maintained below 20 mL/h, corresponding to a glucose infusion rate of 0.8 mg/kg/min, well below the maximum rate recommended for small animals (4 mg/kg/min) (Freeman and Chan 2012). Despite hyperglycaemia, we did not find other complications related to the PPN infusion period, maybe due to using a solution with less than 950 mOsm/L and keeping a low energy supply to avoid overfeeding (Chan and Freeman 2015). Another limitation of this case was that urinalysis was not performed; therefore, it was not possible to determine whether the renal threshold for glucose had been exceeded. Unlike Patient 1, enteral nutrition was contraindicated to patient 2 because of the absence of a swallowing reflex. At the time PPN was initiated, it was not possible to predict when voluntary food intake would resume. Based on our previous experience with Patient 1, in which spontaneous feeding resumed only after 7 days of nutritional support, prolonged anorexia was considered a possibility. Therefore, PPN was selected to provide partial energy support during a period in which enteral nutrition could not be safely administered, rather than relying exclusively on fluid therapy.

Some mechanical complications that may occur during the use of PPN include disconnection of intravenous lines or catheters, clogging of intravenous lines and thrombophlebitis (Chan et al. 2002; Thomovsky et al. 2007). We did not observe any of these complications in the present case. The main technical difficulty encountered during PPN administration was peripheral venous catheterisation. The robust forelimbs made effective tourniquet placement difficult, limiting venous distension and visualisation. In addition, the skin was sufficiently thick and resistant that the plastic intravenous catheter frequently kinked during insertion. To facilitate catheter placement, a small skin incision was made with a scalpel, allowing the catheter to pass through the dermis before advancing into the vein. This approach enabled successful catheterisation and administration of PPN.

Although animals from the family Myrmecophagidae are considered to have low metabolism in relation to other mammals, the recommendations of RER used in both cases were adapted from recommendations for carnivore pets (Smith et al. 2019). While the estimated maintenance requirements (NRC 2006) for dogs vary from 95 to 130 kcal of metabolisable energy/kg0.75/day, the estimated maintenance requirements for anteaters are 83 kcal of digestible energy/kg0.75/day (Stahl et al. 2012). Therefore, it is expected that RER, based on metabolisable energy, should be lower than 70 kcal/kg0.75/day. The use of RER estimated for dogs and cats was probably not a problem, since enteral nutrition supply was gradually increased in the first patient, and in the second patient only 60% of their daily RER were met.

Given the retrospective nature of this report, some information, including serial body weight measurements, complete faecal evaluation and additional details regarding nutritional monitoring, was unavailable. These limitations restrict the interpretation of some clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, these cases provide practical information on the nutritional management of critically ill giant anteaters and illustrate the challenges associated with providing nutritional support to neurologically compromised wild animals under clinical conditions.

Conclusion

The present report demonstrates that both nasoesophageal tube feeding and PPN can be successfully used as temporary nutritional support strategies for hospitalised giant anteaters with traumatic brain injury. The choice of nutritional route should be guided primarily by the neurological status of the patient and the presence or absence of a functional swallowing reflex. During PPN, careful blood glucose monitoring is recommended to allow timely adjustment of the glucose infusion rate. Additional studies are warranted to establish species‐specific nutritional recommendations for hospitalised giant anteaters.
