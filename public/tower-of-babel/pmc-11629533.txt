Impact of two arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi species on arsenic tolerance and accumulation in safflower (Carthamus tinctorius L.)
Authors: Hassan Salari, Rayhaneh Amooaghaie, Hossein Mozafari, Mansour Ghorbanpour, Ebrahim Sedaghati
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) can regulate metal(loid) tolerance in plants and their capacity for phytoremediation. These effects can vary depending on the host plant and the AMF species. The impact of different AMF species on the ability of safflower (Carthamus tinctorius L.) for arsenic (As) phytoremediation is still largely unknown. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the effect of two AMF species, Rhizophagus irregularis, and Funneliformis mosseae, on the tolerance and accumulation of As in safflower in soils spiked with varying arsenate concentrations (0, 25, 50, and 100 mg kg−1).ResultsThe results indicated that both AMF species established effective symbiotic relationships with safflower. However, plants inoculated with R. irregularis exhibited higher mycorrhizal dependency and root colonization, especially under 100 mg kg−1 As. Both AMF species significantly improved plant growth parameters, chlorophyll content, and phosphorus (P) nutrition, which resulted in increased P/As ratio and enhanced tolerance index in safflower plants. In addition, AMF inoculation reduced As-induced lipid peroxidation by enhancing catalase and peroxidase activity in leaves and roots. While the mycorrhizal symbiosis didn’t affect As availability in soils, it significantly reduced shoot As concentration and the translocation factor under all As levels. Furthermore, mycorrhizal inoculation, especially with R. irregularis, increased As concentration and modified-bioconcentration factor in the roots and enhanced total As uptake per plant.ConclusionsBased on the results and multivariate analyses, both AMF species, particularly R. irregularis, enhanced safflower's As tolerance by retaining As in roots, improving phosphorus nutrition, and increasing antioxidant enzyme activity, showcasing their potential to enhance phytostabilization in safflower plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05906-8.Keywords: Antioxidant enzyme, Arsenic tolerance, Mycorrhizal symbiosis, Oxidative stress, P/As ratio

Introduction

Arsenic (As) is a highly toxic metalloid that can harm all living organisms, including humans, animals, and crop plants [1]. In recent decades, human activities such as the use of As-based fertilizers and pesticides in agriculture, mining, industrial smelting, and coal burning have led to a significant increase in As entering the environment [1, 2].

Excess As in agricultural soils may cause toxicity symptoms in plants such as chlorosis, necrosis of leaf blades, reduction in the number and area of leaves, wilting, and growth retardation [3]. Arsenate [As(V) = AsO43−] is the dominant form of As in most soils and, due to its structural similarity to phosphate (Pi/orthophosphate), is often entered into the roots through Pi transporters of the plasma membrane [4]. In the cytoplasm, As(V) can be converted into AsIII, which interferes with phosphorus-dependent metabolic processes. The binding of AsIII to (-SH) groups of enzymes and important proteins also disorders the activity of many enzymes and the biochemical reactions in cells [1, 2, 4, 5]. Furthermore, As toxicity generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) and causes oxidative damage to proteins, lipids, and some biomolecules [2, 6, 7]. To combat metal(loid) toxicity, plants have developed mechanisms including the activation of antioxidant enzymes, the chelation of metal(loid) by phytochelatins and metallothioneins, vacuolar sequestering, and root exclusion of heavy metals [1, 7–10].

When crops are grown in As-polluted soils, arsenic easily enters the food chain and threatens human health. Therefore, the remediation of As contamination is of paramount importance [2, 11]. The remediation of As contaminated soils could be carried out with chemical and physical methods. However, these treatments are expensive and can alter the soil properties, reduce biodiversity and, in many cases, prevent plant growth [12]. Phytoremediation is an alternative to chemical and physical methods and exploits the natural ability of certain plants to remediate polluted soils. This approach does not cause adverse environmental effects and is more affordable than chemical techniques. Phytoremediation consists of several techniques, including phytoextraction and phytostabilisation [2, 13]. In the process of phytoextraction, proper plants called hyperaccumulators, extract metal(loid)s from the soil and concentrate in above-ground organs, thus allowing a permanent removal of metal(loid)s from polluted soil. While in the phytostabilization strategy, plants limit the transfer of metal(loid)s by leaching or erosion through their precipitation in soil or adsorption onto root cell walls or sequestration within vacuoles of root cells [11, 13]. So far, only a handful of arsenic hyperaccumulator plants have been identified, and the efficacy of phytostabilization has been restricted mainly due to low biomass and limited capacity of the uptake and tolerance of metal(loid)s in plants [12]. To deal with these disadvantages, the potential of metal(loid) absorption and tolerance of plants should be enhanced with appropriate treatments. Mycorrhizal symbiosis can increase the biomass and metal(loid) tolerance of plants. Therefore, myco-assisted phytoremediation has attracted much attention in recent years [12–17].

Mycorrhizal symbiosis is a mutualistic relationship that can occur between arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) and roots of over 80% of higher plants including oil crops. The extensive hyphal network of AMF increases the absorptive surface area of plant roots, leading to improved absorption of water and nutrients, especially phosphorus [16, 18, 19]. Additionally, AMF inoculation can enhance the tolerance of host plants to various stresses such as salinity and heavy metal toxicity [20–22].

Several studies showed obvious advantages of symbiosis between plants and AMF on As tolerance in plants [23–26]. This impact is often ascribed to the reduction of As uptake in mycorrhizal plants due to their effect on down-regulating Pi transporters [4]. In non-mycorrhizal plants, Pi is adsorbed by high-affinity Pi transporters (such as the PHT1 transporters) localized in the plasma membrane of epidermal cells [27]. In mycorrhizal plants, there is an alternative and crucial pathway in which fungi uptake Pi and translocate it along the hyphae and, finally, Pi efflux occurs in the apoplast of the root cortex. Then, the plant cells uptake it by specific Pi transporters that are preferentially expressed at the symbiotic interface of cortical cells [28, 29]. Christophersen et al. reported that AMF inoculation down-regulated transcripts of MtPhtl;1 and MtPhtl;2 genes in epidermal cells to decrease As adsorption, and increased expression of MtPht1;4 gene (that is encoding specific Pi transporters in colonized cells of root cortex) to improve P homeostasis in Medicago truncatula plant [30].

The increasing evidence has shown that inoculating plants with AMF can improve their tolerance to As by enhancing nutrient absorption, trapping As in fungal structures, participating in the sequestration and volatilization of As, converting inorganic arsenic to less toxic organic forms through methylation of As (III), and improving conditions in the rhizosphere soil [11, 31, 32]. Furthermore, AMF inoculation can boost gene expression and the function of antioxidant enzymes in plants under arsenic stress [17, 25, 27].

Experimental evidence has revealed that the impact of various AMF species on As accumulation in different plants is variable. For instance, AMF inoculation resulted in a higher accumulation of As in Pteris vittata L. [33] and Pisum sativum L. [17], whereas lowered As content in tissues of soybean [25] and lentil [34]. It is known that the secretion of a glycoprotein termed glomalin by AMF and the formation of glomalin–metal(loid) complexes in the soil immobilizes and thus can prevent metal(loid) uptake by plants [15, 35, 36]. On the other hand, AMF may enhance phytoremediation due to increasing the absorption surface area, chelating metal(loid)s, and preserving biomass in plants [12, 14]. The results of various studies indicate that the effects of mycorrhizal symbiosis on metal(loid) absorption by plants depend on different factors such as plant genotype and species, fungal ecotype and species, type of metal(loid), and soil characteristics [32, 37, 38].

Despite the considerable variety in studied combinations of AMF species, crop cultivars, and growth conditions, the impact of mycorrhizal inoculation on the tolerance and phytoremediation capacity of As in safflower (Carthamus tinctorious L.) remains largely unknown. Several characteristics such as a developed root system, high biomass, fast growth rate, high adaptability to environmental stresses including heavy metals, and also the possibility of converting biomass and the oil extracted from the seeds into biofuel suggest safflower may be a potential candidate for phytoremediation [39]. However, with particular regard to As, there is very little information about safflower. Just a study found that As accumulation in the above-ground biomass of safflower was negligible [40]. Another study also showed that high As concentration significantly decreased the grain yield of safflower [41]. However, these studies neglected to consider As content in the under-ground biomass. Ciaramella et al. [39] found that safflower concentrates heavy metals in the below-ground biomass, and due to the low concentration of heavy metals in the above-ground biomass, this crop can be used as a feedstock for bioenergy conversion. Previous studies have explored that the symbiotic association of AMF with plant roots growing in As-contaminated soil can improve P nutrition, mediate As(V) uptake, increase the P/As ratio in the shoots, and alleviate As toxicity in several plant species [4, 23, 24, 32, 42]. Therefore, we seek to gain insights into the beneficial potential of AMF in safflower plants under various levels of As in soil. We investigated the hypothesis that AMF inoculation could enhance the As tolerance and phytoremediation capacity of safflower plants. To address this, a greenhouse study was conducted to evaluate the efficacy of two AMF species, Funneliformis mosseae (syn. Glomus mosseae) and Rhizophagus irregularis (syn. Glomus intraradices), to mediate As phytostabilization in safflower. Furthermore, crucial attention was given to the physiological and biochemical mechanisms by which these AMF species mitigate the phytotoxic effects of As in plants.

The experiment was carried out in the greenhouse of Graduate University of Advanced Technology (Latitude 30°04′67.3''N, Longitude 57°24′59.4''E, and altitude 1920 m) located in Kerman, Iran.

The soil was sterilized by autoclaving at 120°C for one hour, and 4 kg of soil was dispensed into each pot. For contaminating soil with various concentrations of As (0, 25, 50, and 100 mg kg−1 soil), the required amounts of sodium arsenate (Na2HAsO4 0.7H2O) were dissolved in distilled water and were thoroughly mixed with the soil of each pot. For As equilibration in soil, wet-dry cycles with distilled water (at a controlled level to avoid As leaching) were performed for one month before cultivation [41]. The control pots also received similar wet-dry cycles with distilled water, and all the steps were identical to those for the As treatments.

Two AMF species were used in this study: Rhizophagus irregularis (DAOM181602, purchased from Utapan-Canada, Canada) and Funneliformis mosseae (BGCXJ03A, provided by the Microbial Laboratory of Plant Nutrition and Resources Research Institute, Beijing Academy of Agricultural and Forestry Sciences). Both AMF species were propagated by Organic Company (Hamedan, Iran) using corn as the host plant for four months. The mycorrhizal inoculum included sandy soil, AMF-colonized corn root segments (average 62% AMF colonization rate), AMF spores, and mycorrhizal hyphae.

Carthamus tinctorius seeds (Goldasht cv.) were provided by the Seed and Plant Improvement Institute (Karaj, Iran).

Cultivation, treatments, and experimental conditions

The study was performed in a factorial experiment based on a completely randomized design (CRD) with four As levels (0, 25, 50, and 100 mg kg−1 soil) and three inoculation treatments (non-mycorrhizal control and inoculation with R. irregularis or F. mosseae) with three replicates (n = 3). It is worth noting that previous studies have depicted that these two species are the predominant AMF species in As-contaminated soils [38, 43] and can colonize safflower roots [20]. Several studies also reported that the concentrations of 25, 50, and 100 mg kg−1 As in soil caused low, moderate, and severe toxicity in safflower and other crops [24, 25, 40, 41]. Therefore, these levels of As concentrations and AMF species were used in this study.

At the time of seed cultivation, 50 g AMF inoculum was placed approximately 3 cm below surface-sterilized seeds. The autoclaved mock inoculum was added to the un-inoculated pots same as the AM inoculum and 20 ml of microbial filtrate (0.45 μm pore size) was also added to provide a similar microflora except for the AM fungus [44].

The plants grew in a greenhouse at 14/10 h light/dark period, thermoperiod of 26/23 °C day/night, and 70% relative humidity for 45 days. Soil moisture was maintained at 70% field capacity in all pots to avoid As leaching due to drainage. To supply minerals, 200 mL of half-strength Hoagland’s nutrient solution was added to the soil every two weeks. The third leaf from the top of the plant was frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at –80 °C until the time of biochemical analysis.

Quantification of available As concentration in soil and As and P concentrations in plant

As and P concentrations in roots and shoots of safflower were measured by the method adopted by Campos et al. [50]. Dried samples were digested using HNO3 and HCl. After microwave heating and cooling at room temperature, the final solutions were diluted with deionized water. Then, As and P concentrations in samples were analyzed by an Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometer apparatus (ICP-OES, Varian, Australia).

Available As concentration in soil was determined using the DTPA/TEA method described by Karak et al. [51]. For this purpose, 5 g of each soil sample was weighed, and 10 mL of solution containing 0.005 M di-ethylene triamine penta acetic acid (DTPA), 0.1 M tri-ethanolamine (TEA), and 0.01 M CaCl2 was added and shaken for 2 h. After centrifugation and filtration by Whatman filter paper, the upper diluted solution was injected into the ICP apparatus (Varian, Australia).

Measurement of lipid peroxidation

Lipid peroxidation is a biomarker of oxidative damage to cell membranes, and malondialdehyde (MDA) is a final product of this process [53]. To measure MDA content, leaf or root homogenates were mixed with trichloroacetic acid and thiobarbituric acid and were incubated and centrifuged. Then, the amount of MDA was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 155 mM−1 cm−1 and subtracting optical absorbance at 532 and 600 nm, as described by Heath and Packer [54].

Assay of antioxidant enzyme activity

Enzyme assay was performed using the method adopted by Nabaei and Amooaghaie [55]. First, the root and leaf samples were pulverized and extracted with 3 mL of ice-cold phosphorus potassium buffer (50 mM, pH = 7.5) containing 1% (w/v) poly-vinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and 1mM EDTA. Then, homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was used for enzyme assay.

To measure catalase (CAT, EC 1.11.1.6) activity, 50 μl enzyme extract was added to 2950 μl potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH = 7.5) containing 15 mM H2O2 and the decrease in absorbance at 240 nm was monitored for 1 min. The activity of CAT was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 39.4 mM–1 cm–1.

To assay peroxidase (POD, EC 1.11.1.7) activity, 10 μl enzyme extract was added to 2990 μl potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH = 7.5) containing 9 mM guaiacol and 10 mM H2O2. Then, the increases in absorbance at 470 nm (related to the formation of tetraguaiacol) were monitored for 1 min. The POD activity was computed using the extinction coefficient 26.61 mM–1 cm–1.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were subjected to a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and the differences among the means were assessed using Duncan’s multiple-range tests, P < 0.05 (SPSS software version 24). To determine the relationship between variables, Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed using PAST (PAleontological STatistics) software (http://palaeoelectronica.org/2001_1/past/issue1_01.htm). Also, a heat map was generated using Morpheus online web-server (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus/).

Arsenic concentration in shoots and roots of both NM and M plants significantly elevated with increasing As concentration in the soil (Table 3). Inoculation by R. irregularis caused variable effects on As concentration of shoots and mBCF, i.e. a significant increment in the low level of As (25 mg kg−1), no significant impact in moderate As levels (50 mg kg−1), and a significant decline at high As concentration (100 mg kg−1) was recorded in R-inoculated plants. Inoculation with F. mosseae significantly lowered shoot As concentration and mBCF in F- inoculated plants under 50 and 100 mg kg−1 relative to NM counterparts (Table 3).

Inoculation with R. irregularis markedly augmented both As concentration and mBCF in roots at all As levels. In contrast, As concentrations and mBCF in roots decreased at 100 mg kg−1 in F-inoculated plants relative to the corresponding NM plants (Table 3).

Overall, in many cases, As concentration in shoots was significantly lower in M plants relative to NM plants under all As levels. However, As concentration and mBCF were higher in shoots and particularly in roots of R-colonized plants compared to F-inoculated plants.

With increasing As concentration in soil, total As uptake per plant increased in both M and NM plants. Total As uptake was higher in M plants compared to NM plants, and R. irregularis rather than F. mosseae enhanced total As uptake in safflower plants (Table 3).

With increasing As level in the soil up to 50 mg kg−1, TF increased, then reduced at 100 mg kg−1 and was equal to TF value at 25 mg kg−1 (Table 3). The values of TF were significantly lower in M plants than those in the corresponding NM plants at all As levels, with an exception for TF of F-inoculated plants at 100 mg kg−1 As in soil. Root-to-shoot As translocation was lower in R-inoculated plants compared to F-inoculated plants at 50 and 100 mg kg−1, whereas TFs did not show a significant difference at 25 mg kg−1. The lowest TF value was gathered in R-inoculated plants under 100 mg kg−1 As in soil (Table 3).

Discussion

Arsenic (As) is a carcinogenic and hazardous metalloid that poses a serious risk to human health by entering the food chain [1, 2]. Therefore, it is important to study how plants counterbalance As toxicity and find solutions to enhance As tolerance and phytoremediation ability in plants.

Tlustoš et al. [40] found that while safflower plants showed promising biomass in moderately As-contaminated soil (37.5 mg kg−1 of As), the biomass yield was notably reduced under 100 mg kg−1 of As. Similar results was observed in the present study, where the concentration needed for 50% inhibition of tolerance index (TI) was found to be higher than 50 mg kg−1 of As, whereas adding 100 mg kg−1 of As to the soil severely decreased growth attributes (Table 2). These findings suggest that safflower is relatively tolerant to As and could be considered for cleaning up mildly As-polluted soils. Therefore, it is desirable to enhance the phytoremediation capacity of this plant by ecofriendly methods. Inoculation with AMF can be an applicable method to increase As tolerance and phytoremediation efficiency in plants [12, 16]. Therefore, the impact of two AMF species on safflower’s tolerance and As phytoremediation ability was investigated in this study.

Both AMF species of R. irregularis and F. mosseae, successfully inoculated roots of safflower under all As levels (Figs. 1 and 2A). Although As toxicity can reduce AMF development and mycorrhizal colonization rate, the survival of different species of AMF varies in As-contaminated environments [12, 25, 32]. In the present study, the maintenance of AMF colonization, even in soils spiked with 100 mg kg−1 As (Fig. 2), points to a relatively high tolerance of both AMF species to As toxicity. It has been previously reported that F. mosseae and R. irregularis, are the predominant AMF species in As-polluted soils [38, 43]. These AMF species likely evolved some adaptation strategies similar to plants to enhance their survival in As-contaminated soils. However, the colonization rate of both AMF species decreased in safflower roots at 50 and 100 mg kg−1 As in soil (Fig. 2A). One reason for this decline might be that the exchange of essential signals (such as flavonoids and strigolactones by plant, and lipo-chito-oligosacharide by fungi) between the plant and the fungus for promoting symbiotic relations, is disrupted in metal(loid)-contaminated soils [56, 57]. A study showed that the reduction of soybean root colonization under As stress was related to the inhibition of spore germination and extraradical hyphal growth of R. intraradices in the pre-symbiotic stage [25]. Herein, root colonization with both AMF species improved the growth, Chl content and tolerance index (TI) of safflower at all As levels (Table 2). However, higher colonization rates by R. irregularis relative to F. mosseae (Fig. 2A) resulted in further improvement of Chl content and growth parameters in R-inoculated plants compared to F-colonized plants under all As stress levels (Table 2). A similar trend has been reported for root colonization and growth improvement of Robinia pseudoacacia inoculated with these two AMF species under Pb stress [22]. Conversely, Christophersen et al. [30] reported that increasing As concentration did not affect root colonization by F. mosseae, whereas slightly decreased root colonization with R. intraradices in Medicago truncatula. Our results and the above reports suggest that although both AMF species are relatively As-tolerant, maintaining the colonization rate might depend on the plant-AMF combination.

Interestingly, with increasing As concentration in soils, mycorrhizal dependency (MD) increased in plants inoculated with both mycobionts (Fig. 2B), confirming the critical role of mycorrhizal symbiosis in As tolerance of safflower. This further was supported by the positive correlation between MD and tolerance index (TI) with root colonization (Fig. 6), and higher TI (Table 2), MD and root colonization (Fig. 2) in R-inoculated plants compared to F-colonized plants. Similarly, Dong et al. [58] found that MD and surviving Lolium perenne L. and Trifolium repens in As-contaminated soils depended on the rate of mycorrhizal symbiosis. These effects are related to the ability of AMF to penetrate extraradical hyphal networks over a wider soil surface, providing plants with better access to water and minerals, particularly Pi [14, 18]. In addition, it has been reported that inoculation with AMF can reduce As uptake by plants, either by a down-regulation of As/Pi transporters due to Pi supply by the fungi [4, 32] or via immobilizing As in soil due to the secretion of the glomalin by AMF [35, 36].

In the current study, inoculation with both AMF species lowered As concentration and mBCF in shoots of safflower (Table 3). This was consistent with reports in Sophora viciifolia-R. intraradices [26] and pea-Glomus mosseae [42] associations. However, herein, mycorrhizal inoculation did not significantly affect As availability in soil, and increased total As uptake per plant (Table 3). Therefore, As immobilization in soil by glomalin or down-regulation of As/Pi transporters are not the mechanisms responsible for reducing As concentration and mBCF in shoots. Considering higher total As uptake in mycorrhizal plants (especially in R-inoculated plants), the lower concentration of As in shoots of safflower might be related to a “growth dilution effect”. This dilution reduces the toxic effects of As on shoot growth and Chl content in leaves. The negative correlation between growth parameters and Chl content with As concentration and mBCF in shoots (Fig. 6) also supports the above assumption.

Interestingly, mycorrhizal inoculation (especially by R. irregularis) increased As concentration and mBCF in the roots of safflower, whereas restricted translocation of As from the roots to the shoots in safflower as was indicated by reducing TF (Table 3). A mechanism responsible for increasing As retention in roots might be trapping metal(loid)s in the cell wall of fungal hypha and especially sequestering in vesicles of AMF that minimize metal(loid) translocation from the root to shoots [12, 16, 57]. The impact of AMF on inducing the gene expression of the phytochelatin synthase [26] and metallothioneins [31] in the roots of the host plant has also been reported as another mechanism that contributes to As immobilization in the roots. The inoculation with R. irregularis rather than F. mosseae increased mBCF in roots, and TF was lower in R-inoculated plants compared to F-inoculated plants (Table 3). Similar results have been reported by Yang et al. in Pb-stressed Robinia pseudoacacia L. [22]. Most likely, the higher colonization rate by R. irregularis resulted in providing a larger surface area for As uptake. At the same time, more robust chelation of As in fungal structures reduced root-to-shoot As transfer [59].

The analysis of phytoremediation indices in safflower tissues revealed that mBCF of shoots and roots and the TF, were less than 1 in all treatments. These findings indicate that safflower did not exhibit the characteristics of an As hyperaccumulator plant. However, higher mBCF in roots than shoots of non-mycorrhizal plants indicates that safflower can inherently store more As in the below-ground biomass and potentially be a suitable candidate for As phytostabilization. Our results depicted that mycorrhizal inoculation (especially by R. irregularis) could enhance the phytostabilization efficacy of safflower plants by providing a larger surface area for As absorption and maintaining plant biomass under As stress. Similar findings have been reported in Pisum sativum L., Medicago truncatula, and Lavandula angustifolia L. [17, 21, 60], where mycorrhizal inoculation improved the growth and enhanced phytoremediation capacity of these plants.

In the current study, higher biomass and Chl content (Table 2) concomitant with higher total As uptake (Table 3), particularly in R-inoculated plants, indicates that the mitigation of As toxicity in mycorrhizal plants is not necessarily due to lowering As uptake and other mechanisms should not rule out. Li et al. [61] reported that inoculation with R. irregularis improved As tolerance in alfalfa plants due to reduced shoot As concentration, increased As retention in roots, As detoxification, and the transformation of As(III) into a less toxic form of dimethylarsenic acid. Herein, the improvement of growth parameters and Chl content in safflower plants was positively correlated with P concentration. In addition, there was a negative correlation between As concentration and P concentration in shoots (Fig. 6). These findings point to the antagonistic effects of Pi and As in plants. Since As (V) is an analog of Pi likely, the competition between Pi and As(V) for entrance to the root via As/Pi transporters [2, 4, 32] reduced Pi concentration in roots and shoots of As-stressed safflower plants. However, Pi uptake by both AMF species and its transfer through fungal hypha contributed to increasing P concentration in both roots and shoots of As-stressed safflower plants (Fig. 3A, B). This was consistent with a positive effect of R. irregularis on P nutrition in As-stressed Medicago truncatula [29]. In mycorrhizal plants, the crucial pathway for Pi uptake overcomes competition between Pi and As for common transporters in epidermal cells and sustains the P concentration in plants [28, 30]. The high supply of Pi by AMF provides enough energy for survival and increases biomass production, leading to the relative dilution of As in plant tissues. Due to this “growth dilution effect” Rangel et al. [62] believe that the P/As ratio more favorably manifests the phytoprotective effect of AMF. Sharma et al. [24] stated that inoculation with Glomus etunicatum and Rhizoglomus intraradices lowered As accumulation and elevated P content, which resulted in a higher P/As ratio in wheat. A higher P/A ratio in mycorrhizal plants can prevent the replacement of the P by As during ATP synthesis and phosphorylation, thereby reducing the adverse effects of As toxicity on photosynthesis and plant growth [24, 32]. The present study also showed that As exposure decreased the P/As ratio in the shoots and roots of non-mycorrhizal plants. While efficient phosphate absorption by AMF improved the P/As ratio in the shoots and roots of mycorrhizal plants in all As levels (Fig. 3C, D). It is noticeable that inoculation with R. irregularis rather than F. mosseae increased the P/As ratio in shoots and roots of safflower, while As concentration was higher in shoots and particularly in roots of R-inoculated plants. Considering the positive and robust correlation between root colonization and P concentration in roots and shoots (Fig. 6), it can be concluded that higher root colonization by R. irregularis (Fig. 2A) led to taking up more Pi in these plants (Fig. 3A, B). Although AMF inoculation increased As uptake, a more significant increase in Pi uptake, especially in R-inoculated plants, resulted in a higher P/As ratio in these plants. These findings highlight the opinion of previous authors [32, 62], who stated the P/As ratio more realistically reveals interactions between P and As in soil and competition in plant uptake, especially in mycorrhizal plants. However, lower root colonization by F. mosseae could not preserve a high P/As ratio in F-inoculated plants, particularly under higher As concentrations (Fig. 3C, D).

The tolerance conferred by AMF inoculation may also result from more efficient defense responses in these plants. Our findings demonstrate a positive correlation between the concentration of As and MDA content in tissues (Fig. 6). Additionally, we observed higher levels of oxidative stress in roots compared to leaves (Fig. 4). It might be due to the fact that the conversion of As(V) to As(III) happened mainly in the roots and caused a high ROS generation and severe lipid peroxidation in the roots. However, inoculating with both AMF species increased the activities of CAT and POD (Fig. 5) and resulted in more effective ROS scavenging and reduced MDA content in the roots and leaves of safflower (Fig. 4). Previous studies have reported that increasing gene expression or antioxidant enzyme activity enhanced As tolerance in wheat-R. intraradices [24] and pea-G. mosseae [17, 42] associations. The results of HCA analysis (Fig. 7) also indicate that at high As concentrations (50 and 100 mg kg−1), the ability of AMF to stimulate the activity of antioxidant enzymes in roots and leaves is crucial for enhancing As tolerance in host plants.

In summary, contrary to previous studies that suggested the effect of AMF on increasing As tolerance was due to reducing As accumulation in the plant, the novel findings of the current study show that AMF inoculation, especially by R. irregularis, increased As absorption and phytostabilization in safflower plants. In other words, higher As tolerance in mycorrhizal plants was not result of reducing As absorption, but was related to different mechanisms such as increased biomass and As dilution in shoots, improved P/As ratio, boosted antioxidant enzyme activity, and enhanced As retention in roots (Fig. 8).

Conclusions

The current study showed that safflower can thrive in moderate As-polluted soils, retains As in roots and limits its translocation to the above-ground parts, showcasing the potential for As phytostabilization. However, high concentrations of As had a negative impact on safflower’s growth, interfered with phosphorus (P) nutrition, and caused oxidative stress in the roots and leaves. Inoculation with both R. irregularis and F. mosseae improved the attributes mentioned above and enhanced the As tolerance and phytostabilization capacity of safflower. Nonetheless, the extent of the impacts of the two mycobionts differed, and R. irregularis rather than F. mosseae influenced As tolerance and phytostabilization in safflower. Further in-depth studies using molecular analysis on As and Pi transporters as well as assessment of the impact of mycorrhiza on As speciation, detoxification and sequestration in plant and, soil characterizations such as pH, available P, … are needed to better understand the distinct mechanisms of these AMF species in regulating As tolerance and phytostabilization in safflower plants. Overall, the experimental evidence suggests that the association of R. irregularis and safflower could be beneficial for As decontamination programs in agricultural soils. It is recommended that the potential of native fungal strains isolated from As-contaminated soils and their association with various plants be further evaluated in field studies to aid in the ecological restoration of As-polluted areas.
