Tumor Microenvironment-Responsive Nanoparticles for Pancreatic Cancer Imaging and Treatment
Authors: Wenli Qiu, Jinghong Li, Yingying Cao, Hongguang Zhou, Hui Hu, Zhongqiu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly diagnosis and effective treatment of pancreatic cancer remain major clinical challenges, one of the most aggressive and lethal tumors. The tumor microenvironment (TME) in pancreatic cancer exhibits unique features, including high interstitial fluid pressure (IFP), abnormal blood vessels, hypoxia, acidosis, and excess reactive oxygen species (ROS), all favoring tumor progression and immunosuppression, meanwhile offsetting diagnostic efficiency and treatment efficacy. Thus, the TME of pancreatic cancer is considered as a target of profound theranostic significance. Nanoparticles (NPs) have shown a high profile in bio-imaging and targeted drug delivery. Here, we depicted the TME of pancreatic cancer and reviewed the designs and performances of TME-transforming NPs in the diagnosis and treatment of pancreatic cancer, as well as the obstacles to be overcome before their clinical translation. This review is expected to provide sparks for the early diagnosis and personalized treatment for pancreatic cancer.Keywords: tumor microenvironment, nanoparticles, pancreatic cancer, imaging, treatment

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer, the most aggressive and lethal tumor, develops as normal glandular epithelium differentiates into precursor lesions and ultimately invasive cancer, with an overall 5-year survival rate about 13% worldwide.1 Among them, approximately 95% of tumors arise from pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasias (panINs) (Figure 1).2 In the early stage, it usually presents no specific symptoms, but only mild abdominal bloating and loss of appetite, which often leads to missed or wrong diagnosis; upon the appearance of typical symptoms, such as jaundice, severe abdominal pain, and rapid weight loss, the disease has progressed to the late stage. Therefore, only 15–20% of patients diagnosed with early pancreatic cancer are eligible for surgical resection,3 which can increase the 5-year survival rate to 15–25%; however, the recurrence rate after resection is as high as 85%, and the overall prognosis remains dismal.4 Therefore, new approaches are still needed for the early diagnosis and effective treatment of pancreatic cancer.

The pathological progression from normal pancreatic tissue to pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN) and pancreatic cancer. Normal pancreatic tissue: cells are uniform in shape, duct structures are regular, no heterogeneity. PanIN: cell arrangement begins to show disorder and heterogeneity, nucleoli are enlarged. Pancreatic cancer: cell morphology is obviously irregular, arrangement is disordered, with significant heterogeneity, and a large amount of fibrous tissue proliferates in the stroma.

Despite their limited specificity and sensitivity, laboratory tests and imaging examinations remain conventional in the diagnosis, monitoring, and follow-up of pancreatic cancer.5–7 In laboratory tests, serum carbohydrate antigen (CA) 19–9 is routinely measured, but its relatively low specificity and sensitivity restrain its single use in early screening and diagnosis of pancreatic cancer.8,9 Current diagnostic imaging methods mainly include ultrasound (US), computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance (MR).10 These methods can display some morphological changes in pancreatic tissue, but each has its limitations in spatial resolution, imaging depth, and sensitivity. For example, EUS can detect lesions with a diameter as short as 2–3 mm, but its accuracy heavily depends on the experience of operator, and drops in a limited field of view.11,12 CT is not sensitive enough for lesions smaller than 1 cm in diameter, and may miss diagnoses.13,14 MRI examination can be interrupted by respiratory and intestinal motions, thereby reducing the spatial resolution on images. In summary, current diagnostic techniques cannot be relied on completely and solely to establish a definite diagnosis of pancreatic cancer.15

Over the past few decades, substantial advances have been observed in such treatment strategies as chemotherapy, radiotherapy and immunotherapy.16 Unfortunately, their potential is shallow in prolonging the survival of pancreatic cancer patients.17 A histopathological benchmark of pancreatic cancer is excessive fibrotic stroma that occupies more than 80% of the tumor volume and endows the tumor microenvironment (TME) with unique physical and chemical features, mainly including solid stress, high interstitial fluid pressure (IFP), abnormal vasculature, hypoxia, and acidosis.18,19 Among them, high IFP and low microvascular density prevent active pharmaceutical ingredients from infiltrating into and accumulating at the tumor site, foreshadowing a risk of drug resistance.20,21 Furthermore, the fibrotic stroma, as a barrier between the immune system and tumor, shapes the TME immunosuppressive.22–24 Therefore, the features of TME may be exploited to design ideal diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for pancreatic cancer.

Due to its inherent optical, magnetic and electronic properties, nanotechnology has become a central research tool for developing advanced diagnostic and therapeutic platforms for cancer.25 Utilizing nanotechnology, various TME-targeting strategies have been proposed, such as (1) TME-specific molecular receptors (active targeting) and biophysical delivery systems (TME-responsive),26,27 (2) physical alteration or physiological remodeling of the TME.28,29 Here, we reviewed the findings from recent studies on TME-responsive nanoparticles (NPs) for the diagnosis and treatment of pancreatic cancer.

Tumor Microenvironment Characteristics in the Pancreatic Cancer

Always established in a solid tumor, the TME contains cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), endothelial cells, immune cells, peripheral nerves, extracellular matrix (ECM), blood vessels, and signaling molecules, all interacting to promote the progression of pancreatic cancer (Figure 2).30,31 The TME exhibits unique physical and chemical features, including high IFP, abnormal blood vessels, hypoxia, acidosis, and excess ROS.32 These features are immunosuppressive and can discount the efficacy of treatments.33–35 Besides, excessive stroma, arising from the connective tissue proliferation, is the most prominent feature of pancreatic cancer TME, but rarely observed in other malignant tumors. As a defining histopathological feature of pancreatic cancer, this stroma is dense, fibrous and packed with cancer cells, accounting for more than 80% of the tumor mass, far higher than in other tumors.20 Owing to its dense interstitial fibers, the stroma form a natural physical barrier that prevents tumor cells from being attacked by conventional chemotherapy drugs, a mechanism that contributes to chemotherapy resistance. Moreover, the stroma sets up a barricade between the innate immunity and the pancreatic cancer, limiting the efficacy of immunotherapies. Additionally, the fibers in the stroma distort the arrangement of blood vessels in the pancreatic cancer, thereby putting out the action of anti-angiogenic therapies.21–24

The characteristics of the TME in pancreatic cancer, including cellular components, non-cellular components, and physiological conditions.

Cellular Components

Cancer-Associated Fibroblasts

CAFs, predominant in pancreatic cancer stroma, are generated from pancreatic stellate cells, resting fibroblasts, and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) through various activation pathways. Normal fibroblasts produce collagen subtypes to maintain the structure of the pancreatic gland connective tissue; in contrast, CAFs are large fusiform mesenchymal cells secrete cytokines to promote tumor development, invasion, and metastasis.36 These cytokines are represented by vascular endothelial growth factors (VEGF), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), platelet derived growth factor (PDGF), matrix metalloproteinase (MMP), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), sonic hedgehog (SHH), CXC chemokine ligand-12 (CXCL12), interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, and IL-10. Mounting evidence suggests that these bioactive molecules can induce tumor angiogenesis, fibrosis, and metastasis. Nanotechnology-based strategies, targeting or blocking the proliferation and activation of CAFs, are opening a chapter in the diagnosis and treatment of pancreatic cancer.

Pancreatic Stellate Cells

In 1982, Watarietal et al first described the star-shaped pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs), and Apte et al first isolated them successfully in 1998, thereby opening up the window into pancreatic fibrosis.37 Accounting for 15–20% of the total pancreatic stromal cells, PSCs are multipotent stromal cells of mesenchymal origin and homologous to hepatic stellate cells (HSCs). In the normal pancreas, PSCs present as quiescent cells (qPSCs), the cytoplasm of which contains abundant vitamin A-storing lipid droplets, a marker of qPSCs. PSCs play a crucial role in maintaining the normal composition of pancreatic norma, regulating exocrine and endocrine function, as well as immune responses. During the development of pancreatic cancer, qPSCs are activated under the stimulation of pathogenic factors (eg, chronic inflammation, smoking, and ethanol), cellular secretory factors (eg, TGF-β, hypoxia-inducible factor [HIF] 1α, IL-1, IL-6), and environmental factors (eg, hypoxia, hypoperfusion, oxidative stress). Then, these activated PSCs (aPSCs) lose vitamin A-storing lipid droplets, transform into myofibroblast-like phenotypes, and express the alpha-smooth muscle actin (a-SMA). The expression of α-SMA in aPSCs is directly related to the clinicopathological features of pancreatic cancer and is considered as an independent risk factor for the prognosis of pancreatic cancer.38 aPSCs disrupt the balance between ECM degradation and synthesis by producing abundant ECM components, such as collagen, fibronectin, laminin, and hyaluronic acid (HA), as well as dysregulate the expression of MMPs and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), thereby leading to desmoplasia.39 aPSCs can also oversecrete growth factors, such as VEGF, insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF1), PDGF, FGF, CTGF, and CXCL12, and cytokines (eg, IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10), promoting angiogenesis, along with the proliferation, migration, invasion, and even metastasis of cancer cells.40 Additionally, soluble factors secreted by PSCs, especially IL-6, have been shown involved in the transition from non-invasive to invasive pancreatic cancer, and the accumulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) through a STAT3-dependent mechanism, thereby turning the TME immunosuppressive.41

Tumor Endothelial Cells and Pericytes

During the growth of tumors, new blood vessels are generated to supply oxygen and nutrients in an uninterrupted manner.42 Tumor endothelial cells (ECs) and surrounding pericytes are essential components for the generation, stability and functioning of tumor blood vessels,43 and can be employed as a stromal target for treating pancreatic cancer.44,45 Compared with normal ECs, tumor ECs are heterogeneous, and their proliferation and migration elicit cancerous angiogenesis.46 A variety of pro-angiogenic and anti-angiogenic growth factor receptors are highly expressed in ECs, forming angiogenesis switches and binding to ligands to regulate angiogenesis by.47,48

Pericytes are contractile cells that located on microvessel walls and wrapped around the endothelial cells, providing structural supports for ECs and promoting vessel maturation by secreting large amounts of growth factors (such as VEGF, FGF, and epidermal growth factors [EGFs]).49–51 Unlike normal vascular in structural and functional aspects, the tumor blood vessels show irregular shapes, bends, dilation, chaotic branching, and leaky structure, all conducive to tumor growth and spread.52,53 The vascular ECs and pericytes are the first biological interfaces encountered by NPs when they enter the bloodstream, and as such, they are considered as one of the most promising targets of anti-tumor nanomedicine.54,55

Cancer-Associated Immune Cells

Local and systemic chronic inflammation can increase the risk of pancreatic cancer. Inflammation is interrelated with immunity. Cancer-associated immune cells, including T cells, B cells, dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, and MDSCs, are simultaneously involved in inflammatory and immune responses. These cells affect the status of TME by secreting inflammatory mediators. However, controversy still exists about the role of immune response, whether suppressive or promotive, during tumor growth.

T Cells

The T cells in the TME are diverse and can be divided into α-β T cells and γ-δ T cells according to the antigen recognition receptor.56 Each type of T cells has its own unique function, usually mediating immunity, in the occurrence and progression of pancreatic cancer.57 CD8+ T cells can recognize and destroy tumor cells, restricting the establishment of solid tumor.58 Due to the relative paucity of CD8+T cells in the TME, pancreatic cancer has been classically described as a “cold tumor”.59,60 However, new evidence shows significant heterogeneity in the T cells infiltrating into tumors, as well as a clear correlation between the density of effector T cells in tumors and the prognosis of patients, providing a theoretical basis for the design of immunotherapy for pancreatic cancer.61 Nonetheless, the intratumoral T cell populations in pancreatic cancer are dominated by suppressive CD4+ T cell lineages.62 Regulatory T cells (Tregs) present interstitial immune infiltration in tumors, and their abundance is associated with the probability of adverse clinical outcomes in many types of cancer.63,64 Therefore, Tregs are considered as the most effective inhibitor on anti-tumor immunity, and can reduce the activity of natural killer (NK), CD4+, and CD8+ cells.65 Treg cells suppress immunity in the TME through such mechanisms as direct eliminating or competing with effector T cells to enter antigen-presenting cells, releasing IL-10 and TGF-β, inhibiting IFN-γ production and CD8+T cell proliferation.66 These findings illustrate that pancreatic cancer may progress in a TME scarce in effector T cells and rich in inhibitory T cells.67

B Cells

B cells are tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) located in draining lymph nodes and lymphoid structures near the TCM.68,69 B cells are heterogeneous and can directly act on tumor behaviors or secrete cytokines to regulate other cell types to drive the development of tumors.70,71 Recent studies have observed that B cells can promote the development of tumors;72 however, their function in pancreatic cancer remains incompletely defined. For instance, B cells play a tumor-promoting role by secreting cytokine IL-35 to stimulate the proliferation of pancreatic cancer cells, or activating tumor-promoting macrophages.73,74 Pharmacological augmentation and depletion experiments and genetic analyses have also shown the effect B cells in accelerating the tumorigenesis of pancreatic cancer.75 HIF-1α, specifically produced by pancreatic ductal cells, can inhibit the infiltration of B cells into tumors,75 suggesting that HIF-1α may inhibit pancreatic tumorigenesis by restricting B cells’ recruitment.76 In addition, B cells can also recognize pancreatic cancer-specific antigens, regulate antigen processing and suppress T cell-related immune responses to promote tumor progression.77

Tumor-Associated Macrophages

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are a type of innate immune cells that originate from bone marrow mononuclear phagocytes and participate in chronic and acute inflammation.78 Under conditions of inflammatory damage and homeostasis, TAMs continuously regenerate in gastrointestinal tissues or organs, containing the pancreas.79 They clear debris from the infected site and present antigens to B and T cells, triggering the adaptive immunity.80 In the TME, TAMs affect tumor growth, angiogenesis, immunomodulation, metastasis, and chemotherapy resistance.81 TAMs can be activated into M1 and M2 phenotypes.82 The M1 phenotype is classically activated, and as anti-inflammatory cells, can be polarized by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and Th1 cytokines, such as interferon γ (IFN-γ) and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), to fulfil the anti-tumor and immunity-enhancing role.83 They repress detrimental reactions in tissues by producing tumoricidal molecules, such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, IL-23, TNF-α, and ROS.84 The M2 phenotype is alternatively activated, equipped with pro-inflammatory and immunosuppressive properties, and commonly polarized by Th2 cytokines IL-4 and IL-13. They promote tissue repair and tumor development by secreting cytokines, such as IL-10 and TGF-β.85 Although at a low abundance, both phenotypes appear and their ratio fluctuates in all stages of tumor development.86 In the early stage, TAMs are represented by the anti-inflammatory M1 phenotype to drive tumor progression, and in later stages, the pro-inflammatory M2 phenotype dominates to promote tumor growth and invasion.87 The infiltration of M2 TAMs into solid tumors is associated with a poor prognosis.88 In an analysis of CD68 as a marker of pancreatic M1 macrophages and CD204 of M2 macrophages, the level of CD204 is higher in pancreatic cancer patients than in chronic pancreatitis patients.89 Furthermore, the increase of M2 macrophages is related to a larger tumor size, an earlier local recurrence or metastasis, a shorter survival in pancreatic cancer patients.90–93

Dendritic Cells

As unique antigen-processing and presenting cells in the TME, DCs play an essential role in the initiation and regulation of immune responses.94 DCs act in tumor-specific immune response by inducting immune tolerance or immunogenicity. Activated, pro-inflammatory DCs can trigger the differentiation of auto-reactive killer clusters of CD4+ and CD8+ helper T cells by presenting self-antigen.95 Tolerogenic DCs (tDCs) enhance immune tolerance by inducing Treg cell generation, inhibiting T cell proliferation, and deactivating autoreactive CD4+ and CD8+ T cells.96 Tumor-associated DCs have shown tolerogenic features in multiple cancer types, such as the lean manufacture of pro-inflammatory cytokines, the weak competence to process and present antigens to T cells, and the low expression of co-stimulatory molecules.97,98 tDCs accumulate in the tumor as TME-derived immune-suppressive factors, such as IL-10, TGF-β, VEGF, and PGE2, which propel the maturation of DCs and promote the differentiation of tDCs, ultimately weakening T cell-dependent anti-tumor immunity.99

Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells

MDSCs are a group of immunosuppressive cells derived from bone marrow hematopoietic stem cells, which are the precursors of DCs, macrophages and granulocytes.100,101 Under a normal physical condition, MDSCs suppress inflammation by inhibiting the functions of T and NK lymphocytes. However, in the TME, MDSCs exhibit powerful immunosuppressive and tumor-promoting functions by inducing the production of immunosuppressive cells, blocking the homing of lymphocytes, boosting the production of ROS and nitrogen, depleting metabolites crucial for T cell function, expressing ectoenzymes that regulate adenosine metabolism, and activating negative immune checkpoint molecules.102,103 Studies have shown that MDSC levels rise in patients with pancreatic cancer compared to healthy subjects, which may be attributed to the enrichment of Tregs and CD4+ T cells.104,105

Pancreatic Cancer Stem Cells

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are defined as cancer cells with characteristics similar to stem cells from normal tissues, which have the ability to self-renew, proliferate and grow into a tumor.106 This definition has been doubted, but it is clear that these cells, also called tumor-initiating cells, function in tumorigenesis, metastasis, immune evasion, and treatment resistance.107,108

In 2007, Li et al first described CSCs in pancreatic cancer and analyzed the lesion tissues using cell surface markers CD24, CD44, and ESA. They have found that of all possible combinations, CD44+CD24+ESA+ pancreatic cancer cells carry stronger tumorigenic and aggressive potentials. Although CD44+CD24+ESA+ cells make up only 0.2–0.8% of the total population of pancreatic cancer cells, their tumorigenic potential increases by more than 100-fold relative to that of the unenriched cancer cells. Primary pancreatic adenocarcinoma grows in immunocompromised mice implanted with 100 positive cells, but not in those whereas implanted with thousands of CD44−CD24−ESA− negative cells. They also observe that the tumors formed in immunocompromised mice display the same histological characteristics to the isolated primary tumor.109 CD44+CD24+ESA+ pancreatic cancer cells exhibit the ability to produce stem cells that can differentiate and self-renew, and increase the expression of sonic SHH that contributes to the desmoplastic processes and intracellular carcinogenesis. During their development, CSCs undergo epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT) that predisposes them to metastasis, a typical hallmark of CSCs.110 Identifying pancreatic CSCs and further expounding the key signaling pathways that regulate their proliferation may provide a novel avenue for the treatment of pancreatic cancer.

Extracellular Matrix

The ECM, a dense network structure composed of fibronectin, collagen, proteoglycans, HA and enzymes, presents in all types of tissues and helps maintain tissue biochemical function and structure.111 The components in the ECM closely interact with other cells to form dynamic microsites in varying functional states.112 The ECM surrounding the cancer has long been associated with its progression, and abnormally overexpressed ECM components help maintain a microenvironment favoring cancer cell proliferation, metastasis and even invasion.113,114 In contrast to other tumors, the ECM overgrows in pancreatic cancer, immune cell infiltration is inhibited, and the drug delivery to the tumor core is also blocked,115,116 suggesting that remodeling the ECM within the surrounding stroma may be a new treatment option. Nevertheless, excessive and complete stromal ablation will remove the intrinsic tumor-suppressive confinement effect of stromal tissue, relieve growth inhibition on residual tumor cells, accelerate angiogenesis and distant metastasis. This stromal paradox indicates that crude full ECM degradation is accompanied by intrinsic safety hazards.

Hyaluronic Acid

HA, a major constituent of the ECM, is a hydrophilic glycosaminoglycan highly expressed in pancreatic cancer. It retains water, increases IFP, and impedes vascularization.117 High deposition of HA in pancreatic cancer builds up a high-pressure TME, which is related to poor prognosis in patients.118 The median survival time of pancreatic cancer patients with low-level HA deposition is 15 months longer than that of high-level patients.119 This suggests that reducing HA accumulation may bring prognostic benefits.

Hyaluronidase (HAase), a specific hydrolase enzyme, can rapidly degrade HA into oligosaccharides, thereby loosening the ECM skeleton. In 2013, a tumor-bearing mouse model of pancreatic cancer has shown that gemcitabine (GEM) in combination with human recombinant HAase PH20 (PEGPH20) can rapidly and sustainably consume HA, improve vascular permeability and tumor perfusion, thus achieving a stronger chemotherapeutic efficacy than GEM alone.120 These results suggest that degradation of HA can facilitate drug delivery to the core of the tumor. However, HAase may be degraded and inactivated in vivo, thereby restricting its accumulation in the TME.

Although recombinant human hyaluronidase PH20 (rHuPH20) facilitates stromal degradation and tumor penetration, multiple drawbacks restrict its clinical application. Tumor acidity suppresses its activity; free rHuPH20 induces off-target hyaluronic acid degradation and rapid renal clearance, requiring frequent high doses that increase systemic toxicity. Moreover, excessive stromal ablation mediated by rHuPH20 eliminates the intrinsic tumor-suppressive stromal barrier, thereby facilitating cancer cell invasion and distant metastasis. Such limitations demand nanoparticle delivery to improve tumor targeting, prolong circulation and reduce off-target effects. Consequently, nanocarriers have been recommended to protect HAase from degradation in the TME, improve its delivery efficiency, and balance penetration improvement and tumor-suppressive stromal functions. Zhou et al have embedded rHuPH20 into the poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid)-b-polyethylene glycol (PLGA-PEG) NPs, which are then coated with a relatively low-density PEG layer to reduce rHuPH20 exposure and prolong its blood circulation.121 Another study has shown that the combination with nanogels sulfhydryl enhances the tumor-oriented penetration of HAase.122

Matrix Metalloproteinases

Proteolytic enzymes, abundant in the ECM, maintain its physiological function and structure. Among them, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) play a critical role in the occurrence, growth and metastasis of tumors.19 MMP-1, MMP-2, MMP-7, MMP-9, membranous 1-MMP (MT1-MMP), membranous 2-MMP (MT2-MMP), and membranous 3-MMP (MT3-MMP) have been found overexpressed in pancreatic cancer, with MMP-2, MMP-7, and MMP-9 identified as promising biomarkers.123,124 Studies have shown a significant difference in the expression of MMPs and MMPs tissue inhibitors in pancreatic cancer tissues and untransformed pancreas, as well as a tight connection between the high expression of specific MMPs and the metastasis and poor prognosis.125,126 The expression of type IV collagenase MMP2 increases in the stroma of pancreatic cancer, and its aggressiveness is positively related to the degree of desmoplasia in vitro.127,128 These suggest that some MMPs can be downregulated to reduce desmoplasia of pancreatic cancer, thus increasing the accumulation of drugs inside. Preclinical studies have verified the effects of MMP inhibitors in melanoma,129 gastric cancer,130 and colon cancer131 xenograft mouse models, but not in patients with advanced pancreatic cancer,132,133 suggesting that non-specific inhibition on MMPs alone in pancreatic cancer produce an inadequate effect.

Collagens

As another major component in ECM, collagens contribute to the fibrosis in the pancreatic TME. Overproduction of collagens can limit drug absorption and lead to the development of drug resistance; therefore, a collagen-decomposing strategy may be used to ease drug delivery. Collagens are dynamically deposited in the ECM and regulate its homeostasis, conferring it with unique chemical and mechanical properties. This regulatory mechanism goes in two pathways: (1) an endocytic pathway through which collagens are hydrolyzed into fragments, internalized by the ENDO180 in fibroblasts and mannose receptor (MRC1) in macrophages, and degraded by lysosomal proteases, and (2) an extracellular pathway through which collagens are cleaved by soluble proteases produced by stromal cells and tumor cells. Collagen I increases the proliferation and invasiveness of pancreatic cancer cells by disrupting E-cadherin-mediated cell–cell contacts, activating c-Jun NH2-terminal kinase 1, and upregulating N-cadherin. Grzesiak et al have found that type I collagen promotes the proliferation, adhesion, and migration of pancreatic cancer cells, thereby arousing the occurrence of EMT and metastasis.134 Chen et al revealed that epigenetic COL1A2 silencing drives tumor cells to generate collagen I homotrimers, which activate α3β1 integrin, remodel intratumoral microbiota, suppress anti-tumor immunity and induce anti-PD-1 resistance, while ablation of these homotrimers inhibits tumor progression and restores immunotherapeutic responses.135

Integrins

Integrins are a class of transmembrane glycoprotein receptors widely expressed on cell surfaces. Composed of α and β subunits forming heterodimers, they prompt cells to react in response to stimuli and regulate their adhesion to the ECM, while constructing bidirectional signaling pathways. At least 18 α and 8 β subunits can combine to form more than 24 integrin types, such as αvβ3, α5β1, and αLβ2.136 Integrins determine the key cellular processes, such as growth, differentiation and motility, and integrin dysfunction may bring susceptibility to several pathological conditions, including malignancies.137 Certain integrin receptors have been found overexpressed in tumor tissues and capable of facilitating cancer spread.138 And integrins are associated with almost all events in tumor progression, such as growth, migration, metastasis, immune damage, angiogenesis, and drug resistance.139 For example, integrin αvβ3 can mediate the interaction between tumor cells and ECM proteins, thereby propelling the proliferation, migration, and invasion of tumor cells.140 At the same time, integrin αvβ3 is highly expressed on tumor cells, new vascular cells, and activated endothelial cells, but remains almost mute on most normal cells or quiescent endothelial cells, and has been considered as the marker of pancreatic cancer.141,142 Many functional nanocomposites have been designed to target integrins in pancreatic cancer.143

Soluble Factors

In the TME, soluble factors are secreted by tumor cells and stromal cells, overexpressed, and involved in a series of signaling pathways by binding to specific receptors.144 TGF-β plays a pivotal role in the immunosuppressive transformation of the TME. It promotes the differentiation of monocytes into MDSCs, inhibits cytotoxic T cell infiltration, induces Tregs differentiation, and downregulates the expression of activation receptors NKG2D and NKp30 on NK cells, thereby suppressing NK-mediated antitumor immune responses. Furthermore, TGF-β drives the transformation of fibroblasts and endothelial cells into CAFs, stimulates the production of fibrotic matrix, and induces immune cells to secrete immunosuppressive cytokines.145 VEGF, a leading regulator of angiogenesis in pancreatic cancer, induces the proliferation and migration of endothelial cell and activates VEGFR2 receptors on endothelial cells to increase vascular permeability.146 The resultant abnormal vascular system allows the rapid growth of pancreatic cancer, but its blood vessels are often twisted and dysfunctional, viciously leading to hypoxia and acidosis that further stimulate VEGF secretion.147 VEGF can also inhibit T cell infiltration and promote the recruitment of regulatory Tregs and TAMs through paracrine effects, forming an immunosuppressive microenvironment and weakening anti-tumor immune responses.148 Therefore, emerging drugs are expected to disrupt the interactions between soluble factors and their receptors in the TME, thus providing a new therapeutical approach for pancreatic cancer.

Tumor Microenvironment Features

Hypoxia

Hypoxia, a hallmark of solid TME, manifests as oxygen concentrations between 0.3 and 2.2% in tumors, compared to 4.6 and 9.5% in normal cells.32 Its occurrence is mainly due to abnormal tumor-associated angiogenesis, during which immature, leaky, and sparse small vessels supply inefficient blood.149 Meanwhile, extravascular tumor cells in rapid proliferation compress these vessels, obstruct blood flow, and aggravate local ischemia and hypoxia. In this hypoxic environment, tumor cells sustain their growth and proliferation by altering their metabolic pathways.150 At the same time, some genes, such as HIF-1α, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), endothelin-1 (EDN-1), and nuclear factor-κβ (NF-κβ), also change their expression profiles to adapt to hypoxia. Furthermore, hypoxia can also disrupt cellular behaviors, remodel the extracellular matrix, and increase the proliferation, migration and metastasis of tumor cells.151 Therefore, a hypoxic TME is closely related to the occurrence of drug resistance and the poor prognosis of patients.152 To transform the hypoxic TME, MnO2 NPs have been designed with a capability of decomposing H2O2 in TME into O2 and H2O, thereby oxygenating the tumors.153

Acidification

A high acidity is another feature of the solid TME. In the 1930s, Warburg first discovered that the extracellular pH of the tumor was in the range of 6.0~7.0, compared to the 7.4 in normal tissues.154 Mainly due to the high demand for energy to proliferate, glycolysis is abnormally enhanced in tumor cells, leading to the overproduction of lactic acid. Additionally, the intense fibrosis and space vascularization in the core region of the solid tumor establish a long-term hypoxic microenvironment, where glucose is converted into lactate through the Warburg effect, further reducing the extracellular pH. Meanwhile, carbon dioxide from the pentose phosphate pathway also contributes to the acidic TME. Furthermore, in the TME of pancreatic cancer, excessive ECM forms a physical barrier that impedes the diffusion and clearance of acidic metabolites, such as lactic acid, additionally exacerbating acidosis.19 Many studies have shown that the acidic microenvironment is associated with tumor metastasis, recurrence, and chemoresistance.155 Based on this feature, various pH active NPs that can sense and respond to the acidic microenvironment of tumors and improve tumor efficiency.156,157

Interstitial Fluid Pressure

A solid tumor develops with the overgrowth of new vessels, which are highly permeable due to abnormalities such as the incompleteness or absence of the endothelial cell layer and basement membrane.158 This abnormal vascular network shows a high resistance to capillary blood flow but a low resistance to lymphatic flow, leading to outflux of fluid into the tissue interstice surrounding tumor.159 The absence of functional lymphatics further increases fluid accumulation in the tumor, reduces fluid flow in the interstitium, and elevates the interstitial pressure. Overaccumulated high-molecular HA in PDAC stroma readily absorbs water to form a gel-fluid phase, sharply boosting IFP and compressing tumor blood vessels. In addition, the components of the stroma, such as collagen fibers, fibroblasts and macrophages, further increase IFP.160 Therefore, a higher IFP acts as a biological barrier to the delivery of therapeutic agents and allows tumor metastasis and invasion via lymphatic vessels.161 The IFP is only 0~3 mmHg in normal tissues, but 5~130 mmHg in tumors. In the TME, IFP may be modulated to improve the penetration and therapeutic efficacy of NPs.162,163

Reactive Oxygen Species Overproduction

ROS act as a secondary messenger to mediate redox signals required for a variety of cellular functions, including cell survival, death, proliferation, and immune response.164 At a level of ROS exceeding the scavenging capacity of the antioxidant system, ROS show a propensity to reacting with intracellular biological macromolecules, such as DNA, proteins, and lipids, thereby inducing oxidative damage.165 This damage can trigger cellular dysfunction, inflammatory responses, apoptosis, till the onset and progression of diseases. Therefore, maintaining the balance between ROS production and elimination is essential for cells to function normally. The ROS level increases in the TME, due to the increase of metabolic and peroxisome activity, accumulation of lactate, mitochondrial dysfunction, activation of growth factor signaling pathway, macrophage polarization, neutrophil infiltration.166 Meanwhile, the reduction of antioxidant molecules, such as glutathione (GSH) and catalase (CAT), impedes the clearance of ROS in tumor cells. It has been found that ROS benefit the survival and metastasis of various tumors by activating the NF-κB, MAPK, PI3K/AKT, HIF-1, and Keap1-Nrf2 signaling pathways and inactivating their negative regulators.167,168 To this end, redox-responsive nanoprobes have incorporated into strategies to combat cancers.169 For instance, Xiang et al have introduced an NIR-activated and ROS-sensitive polymeric nanoplatform into a combined photothermal therapy and chemotherapy for pancreatic cancer, which demonstrates an enhanced effect in in vitro and in vivo experiments.170

Tumor Microenvironment-Responsive Nanoparticles for Imaging-Based Diagnosis of Pancreatic Cancer

With abilities to prolong the retention of drugs in blood circulation, facilitate their penetration into tissues, optimize their distribution in bodies, augment image resolution, enhance probe sensitivity, TME-responsive NPs have been widely used in bioimaging, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography (PET), fluorescence imaging, photoacoustic (PA) imaging, and ultrasonography (Table 1).

TME-Responsive NPs for Imaging-Based Diagnosis of Pancreatic Cancer

Magnetic Resonance Imaging

MRI is being widely used in the clinical diagnosis of pancreatic cancer.197 NPs with TME-responsive behaviors have been introduced into magnetic contrast agents, such as iron oxide NPs and gadolinium (Gd) complexes, to improve the diagnostic efficiency of imaging for pancreatic cancer.

Zhang et al have constructed a GSH-responsive NPs by crosslinking biocompatible MnFe2O4-loaded carbonaceous nanoprobes (MFCs) to increase the accuracy of MRI for pancreatic cancer (Figure 3A).171 With a high magnetic susceptibility, MFCs enter the tumor and elevate the GSH in the TME to cleave disulfide bonds and expose the active groups of iron and manganese ions in situ. MFCs can enhance the performance of MRI in the detection of pancreatic cancer and enable simultaneous monitoring of treatments. Liu et al have engineered a pH-responsive nanoprobe (MR-CA) based on PEGylated polyanions and capable of controlling the self-assembly of Mn2+-doped CaP NPs.172 MR-CA can detect the acidity of the TME and trigger the release of contrast agent (ie, Mn2+) to boost molecular relaxivity (r1), thereby promoting the sensitivity of imaging to tumors. They find that BxPC3 pancreatic tumors cannot be distinguished by MRI in vivo, and after injection of MR-CA nanoprobes, ultra-small subcutaneous pancreatic tumors (1–5 mm) can be sensitively probed (Figure 3B). The enhancement of tumor-to-normal tissues (T/N) by MR-CA nanoprobes (>150%) is more significant than that by Gd-DOTA (111%) and can be maintained for at least two hours, thus increasing the longitudinal relaxation rate and accuracy of MRI, as well as raising a diagnostic threshold for ultra-small pancreatic cancer.

Extradomain-B fibronectin (EDB-FN) has been discovered as a biomarker highly overexpressed in the ECM of pancreatic cancer. A probe carrying EDB-FN-targeting Dex10-ZD2 peptide, biocompatible and diamagnetic dextran turns MRI highly sensitive in the early detection of pancreatic cancer173 (Figure 3C). Cao et al have designed a PEGylated recombinant hyaluronidase PH20 (PEGPH20), which can selectively degrade HA in the TME and improve intratumoral penetration. Dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) shows a higher accuracy in the mouse model injected with PEGPH20, and a marked decrease of the HA content in the tumor.174 Liu et al have designed multifunctional pH-responsive hybrid nanospheres SiO2-GAL@MnO2 that contain galangin (GAL, ROS-activating drug), SiO2, and MnO2. Once exposed to an acidic TME, MnO2 reacts with GSH and releases Mn2+ to convert endogenous H2O2 into ·OH and O2; together with the release of GAL, the level of ROS increases, which not only significantly inhibits the growth of pancreatic cancer (an inhibition rate of 62.7%), but also the diagnostic accuracy of USI and MRI for pancreatic cancer175 (Figure 3D).

Computed Tomography

Contrast-enhanced CT is currently the routine diagnostic tool for pancreatic lesions in clinical settings.198 However, iodine-based CT contrast agents have a short half-time in the blood circulation and a low X-ray attenuation coefficient, and have to be administered as a high dose for imaging, which increases the risk of adverse reactions.199,200 At the same time, conventional CT lacks sufficient sensitivity for lesions smaller than 1 cm in diameter, easily missing the diagnosis of early pancreatic cancer.201 Therefore, CT contrast agents with higher biocompatibility and attenuation coefficients are urgently needed.

RGD peptides are a class of short peptides containing arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (Arg-Gly-Asp) that targets integrin αvβ3 in the TME.202 Xu et al have developed redox-responsive R-G@ADP to increase the accuracy of CT/MR dual-mode imaging in detecting pancreatic cancer.176 To produce this composite, Michael addition reaction is initiated to cross-link the PAMAM amines with the BAC vinyl group, till the construction of PAMAM dendrimer NGs (DNGs). Then, Au NPs are entrapped within the DNGs and functionalized with Gd chelates and PEGylated RGD, sequentially. In the TME, high-level GSH cleaves the disulfide bonds, thereby inducing dissociation of DNGs and promoting the release of imaging agents. CT values and signal-to-noise ratio increase in the Panc-2 tumor-bearing mouse model injected with R-G@ADP NPs, indicating the enhancement of CT/MR contrast effect on the tumor (Figure 4A and B). Huo et al have reported a nanocluster bomb consisting of hypoxic microenvironment-targeted oxide NPs (WO NPs), as CT/PET contrast agents, that can respond to the MMP and target CCL-28 chemokines overexpressed in the TME. Once reaching the tumor site, WO NPs are destructed upon hypoxia to release small TME-targeting NPs (WOACC NPs) that penetrate deep into the tumor in orthotopic murine pancreatic cancer models177 (Figure 4C). Yang et al have designed a collagenase-functionalized Dox-loaded Au nanoplatform coated with tumor cell membranes.178 Executing a dual role of treatment and monitoring, this Col-M@AuNCs/Dox can efficiently target to degrade the dense ECM via collagenase, and simultaneously serve to guide and monitor the treatment course by CT imaging (Figure 4D). On the CT images of BxPC3 tumor-bearing mice, the study group shows a superior enhancement at the tumor site compared to the other groups, demonstrating the great potential of Col-M@AuNCs/Dox to control and monitor the progression of pancreatic cancer.

Photoacoustic Imaging

PA imaging has advantages of noninvasiveness, deep tissue penetration, high safety, and low cost, but its sensitivity and resolution are not ideal. Stimuli-responsive NPs are expected to overcome both limitations.

Darrigues et al have developed gold-based nanoassemblies functionalized with a biocompatible thiolated-polyethylene (HS-PEG-COOH) and a fluorescent dye (NH2-TR-BDP), according to the EDC/NHS coupling for PA imaging179 (Figure 5A). In their 3D pancreatic microtumor model, the release of fluorescence is triggered in the acidic TME, rendering an excellent performance of PA imaging (Figure 5B). Considering the acidity in the TME and overexpression of extracellular urokinase plasminogen activator (UPA), Gurka’s group have developed as a theranostic system for pancreatic cancer using UPA ligand-targeting, pH-responsive mesoporous silica NPs (MSNs) loaded with indocyanine green and GEM.180 Multispectral optoacoustic tomography (MSOT) shows that the accumulation of UPA-targeting MSNs is significantly higher than that of the untargeted MSNs in the tumor of orthotopic S2VP10 pancreatic cancer mice, while the untargeted MSNs are mainly accumulated within the spleen. Focusing on the acidic TME, Kimbrough et al have constructed pH-sensitive fluorescent probes (V7-750), which consist of pH low insertion peptide (pHLIP) and 750 NIR fluorescent dye.181 Using pH-insensitive fluorescent probes (K7-750) as untargeted control, multi-step order transmittal (MSOT) can detect tumors at four hours after intravenous injection of V7-750, while the K7-750 does not localize to the tumors in murine orthotopic pancreatic adenocarcinoma model. In addition, MSOT shows that V7-750 predominantly accumulates in the tumor bed with low off-target effects, while V7-750 preferentially accumulates within the kidney.

Ferroptosis modulation is an effective therapeutic approach for pancreatic cancer. Biothiols are suitable biomarkers for reflecting the status of ferroptosis. In the context of ferroptosis, their levels decrease dramatically as biothiols are depleted by ROS and the GSH synthesis pathway is inhibited. Li et al have reported HYD-DX, a biothiol-activated PA probe for imaging the status of ferroptosis, thus providing a new diagnostic tool for pancreatic cancer.182 In Panc-2 cells, HYD-DX demonstrates the ability to real-time visualize the concentration of biothiols during ferroptosis. Subsequently, in pancreatic cancer mice, HYD-DX offers PA bimodal images of ferroptosis (Figure 5C). Jia et al have reported a cationic liposome (DODAB/DOPE) and folic acid-modified DOPE, coated with reduced graphene oxide@gold nanostar (rGADA), for PA imaging-guided therapy of pancreatic cancer183 (Figure 5D). Having been injected into Capan-1 tumor-bearing mice, rGADA can actively target FA receptors on the surface of cancer cells, and shows a significant PA signal at the tumor site at 24 hours. Moreover, rGADA shows an outstanding efficacy against pancreatic cancer in vivo.

Fluorescence Imaging

Fluorescence has been widely used in biomedical engineering due to its unique ability to carry out real-time imaging with high sensitivity, cost-effectiveness, spatiotemporal resolution, and no need to use radioactivity materials. However, real-time fluorescence imaging remains challenging in clinical settings due to shallow penetration, low resolution and signal-to-background (SBR) ratio. Nanotechnology has shown potential to circumvent these shortcomings.203

Luo et al have developed a kind of metabolizable dextran-indocyanine green (DN-ICG) NPs in the second near-infrared (NIR-II) window for dynamically imaging TAMs in pancreatic cancer. The DN-ICG NPs are composed of ICG derivatives conjugated with carboxymethyl dextran, which could target highly expressed ICAM-3-grabbing nonintegrin related 1 (SIGN-R1) receptors in TAMs (Figure 6A). In orthotopic pancreatic tumor-bearing mice, DN-ICG NPs are gradually metabolized in the liver, but maintain a high concentration in the pancreatic tumor stroma, thus achieving dynamic NIR-II fluorescence imaging of TAMs with a high signal-to-background (SBR) radio (Figure 6B).184 Chen et al have designed supramolecular aggregation-induced emission (AIE) nanodots (α-CD-TPR-GEM-mmp) co-assembled by a matrix MMP-2-sensitive PEG-peptide (EK6), a functional α-cyclic sperm derivative, a far-red/near-infrared fluorescent rhodenin-3-acetate AIE luminogen and GEM.185 The AIE dots can respond to the overexpressed MMP-2 and high ROS levels in the TME, thereby increasing cancer cell uptake and GSH-triggered GEM release (Figure 6C). Compared with α-CD-TPRGEM-mmp(-) NPs, the tumor-bearing mice injected with α-CD-TPR-GEM-mmp(+) NPs exhibit a higher-intensity fluorescence at tumor site both in vivo and ex vivo. To detect pancreatic cancer at the early stage, Xu et al have synthesized I780BP-PEG12, an MMP-activatable near-infrared fluorescence (NIRF) NP, in which the fluorophores IR780 is labeled with MMP substrate peptide GPLGVRGKGG, and BHQ-3 is linked for fluorescence quenching.186 In the native state, I780BP-PEG12 emits a fluorescence signal at a very low intensity, because the fluorescence signal is excellently quenched by BHQ-3, while I780BP-PEG12 probe can be significantly activated by the high-level MMPs in the TME, and displays a high-intensity fluorescence signal. In orthotopic pancreatic cancer mice, the signal of the tumor presents enhancement as early as 0.5 hours after I780BP-PEG12 injection via the tail vein, and its intensity reaches the peak at 6 hours, which sustains for as long as 24 hours. The NIRF images of WT mice without pancreatic cancer show that the fluorescence signal in the abdomen is very low after the injection of probe (Figure 6D). In our previous studies, we have designed a GPC1-targeting and GEM-loaded multifunctional gold nanocarrier (GPC1-GEM-NPs) for NIRF/MRI and targeted chemotherapy against pancreatic cancer.187 In vivo studies have shown that the intensity of fluorescence signals peaks at 24 hours in the GPC1-GEM-NPs group and remains steady till to 48 hours, indicating that GPC1-GEM-NPs can be used as an ideal NIRF contrast agent for pancreatic cancer detection. Tu et al have covalently labeled GB-6 (bombesin analogues peptide) with NIR cyanine dye CyTE-78338 analogue (MPA) to develop a fluorescent probe (MPA-PEG4-GB-6), which can target the gastrin-releasing peptide receptor (GRPR) overexpressed in pancreatic cancer.188 In the SW1990 subcutaneous xenograft model and the pancreatic cancer orthotopic mouse model, the biodistribution analysis reveals that MPA-PEG4-GB-6 transmits a tumor-to-pancreas fluorescence signal ratio at 5.2 ± 0.3 and 7.66 ± 0.48, respectively, enabling clear tumor visualization and intraoperative navigation.

Ultrasonography

Compared to other imaging technologies, contrast-enhanced endoscopic ultrasound (CE-EUS) has several advantages, such as absence of ionizing radiation, safety, real-time observation, low cost, and simple operation. However, due to the low concentration and short half-life of current contrast agents in pancreatic tissue, CE-EUS may wrongly diagnose or miss small lesions, thus limiting its clinical application.204

PLGA-PEG-based nanoassemblies coated with perfluorooctane (PFOB) and conjugated to SHH antibodies have the ability to target high expression of SHH in the TME, and may serve as effective contrast agents for ultrasound imaging.189 In vitro experiments show that targeted nanosized ultrasound contrast agents can be significantly taken up by pancreatic cancer SW1990 cells with high SHH expression, but not by CFPAC-1 cells without SHH expression, indicating the high diagnostic value of this agent for pancreatic cancer. Luo et al have synthesized a collagenase-loaded hollow TiO2 NPs (Col-H-TiO2), which can degrade stromal barriers in the TME and produce sufficient ROS in a pancreatic cancer xenograft model, demonstrating a potential to be used in CE-EUS imaging and sonodynamic therapy (SDT) (Figure 7A).190 Compared to NPs carrying H-TiO2 (without collagenase), the NPs carrying Col-H-TiO2 are more abundant at 12 and 24 hours after injection, showing that Col-H-TiO2 can function as a CE-EUS contrast agent to better visualize pancreatic cancer by targeting to retain in the TME (Figure 7B). Recently developed, a graded zeolite (ZCM) nanocapsule loaded with catalase and methylene blue can sustain the decomposition of H2O2 in the TME and in situ production of O2, thus enhancing the ultrasound contrast in pancreatic cancer.191 In SW1990 pancreatic tumor-bearing mice, the intensity of contrast-mode ultrasound signals in ZCM nanocapsule-treated group is 4.8-fold higher than that in free catalase-treated group (group 1) at post-injection one hour (Figure 7C).

Positron Emission Tomography

PET is accurate, sensitive and non-invasive in the early diagnosis and treatment monitoring of diseases, including cancer.205 However, PET can just detect lesions larger than 5 mm in diameter, and for small pancreatic cancer lesions (less than 3 mm), which may be resectable and curable, PET may fall short.206

Watabe et al have prepared 225Ac-labeled and 64Cu-labeled FAP inhibitors (225Ac-FAPI-04 and 64Cu-FAPI-04) that can target to image pancreatic cancer in PET. In pancreatic cancer xenograft mice, dynamic PET images of 64Cu-FAPI-04 show slow washout from the tumors and rapid clearance through the kidneys. Delayed PET images of 64Cu-FAPI-04 show moderate uptake in the tumors. 225Ac-FAPI-04 exerts a significant suppression on tumor growth.192 Esfahani et al have developed a type I collagen (collagen-binding peptide, CPB8), as a probe specific to PET imaging (68Ga-CBP8), to target collagen in the TME for monitoring the response of pancreatic cancer to treatments in mouse models and patients with pancreatic cancer.193 In the PANC-1 and PDAC6 models treated with FOLFIRINOX, the signal of the tumor on 68Ga-CBP8 PET increases twofold, whereas in the FOLFIRINOX-resistant SU.86.86 model, no significant increase is observed. For pancreatic cancer patients, PET before neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy shows that 68Ga-CBP8 uptake is significantly higher in the tumor than in the normal pancreatic tissue, and after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, PET values in the tumors are significantly higher than those in untreated tumors (Figure 7D). Liu et al have used beta-emitter (177Lu) to label FAP inhibitors (FAPI-46) that can target FAP in the PET for pancreatic cancer xenografts.194 The [18F]FAPI‑74 PET imaging shows that [177Lu]FAPI-46 is rapidly cleared by the kidneys and can accumulate significantly in tumors at three hours after administration. Meanwhile, [177Lu]FAPI-46 also exhibits notable tumor-inhibitory effects with a high safety. Metzger et al have also designed 68Ga-labeled FAPI as a novel PET tracer for staging patients with locally advanced (LAPAC, n=15) or recurrent (LAPAC, n=12) pancreatic cancer after chemoradiotherapy.195 FAPI PET/CT modifies the stages in 23 of 27 patients, and accordingly, treatment strategies are altered in 14 patients. Wickstrom et al have constructed a KRAS PNA-peptide hybridization probe ([64Cu]KRAS‑IGF1) combining the insulin‑like growth factor 1 (IGF1) peptide analog and PET nuclide 64Cu, which provides specific genetic features for the staging and treatment selection of pancreatic cancer.196 In the PET images of human pancreatic cancer xenografts, the [64Cu]KRAS-IGF1 nanoprobe induces a strong contrast signal in the tumor tissue, with an intensity 8.6 ± 1.4 folds higher than that in the contralateral muscle at four hours post‑injection.

TME-Responsive NPs for the Treatment of Pancreatic Cancer

Chemotherapy

In the treatment of pancreatic cancer, current chemotherapeutics are limited by a low solubility, a lack of selectivity, and a short blood circulation time. TME-responsive NP-based chemotherapeutics show a longer retention in the blood circulation and a higher accumulation at tumor sites.258

Chen et al have prepared supramolecular aggregation-induced emission (AIE) nanodots (α-CD-TPR-GEM-mmp[+]) by conjugating MMP-2-sensitive peptide (PEG2000-RRRRRRRR [R8]-PLGLAG-EKEKEKEKEKEK [EK6]), rhodanine-3-acetic acid-based AIE luminogen, α-cyclodextrins (α-CD) derivatives, and chemotherapeutic drug GEM via a reduction-sensitive disulfide bond185 (Figure 6C). α-CD-TPR-GEM-mmp(+) NPs can target MMP-2 overexpressed in the tumor and respond to the acidic TME, thus increasing tumor cellular uptake and allowing selective GEM release within tumor cells. In orthotopic pancreatic tumor models, α-CD-TPR-GEM-mmp(+) NPs exhibit a strong tumor-inhibiting ability, which is further confirmed by the decrease in the tumor weight on ex vivo photographs.

Blocking the proliferation and activation of PCSs in the pancreatic TME is considered an effective strategy to deepen the penetration of therapeutic drugs. A recent study has employed this strategy to regulate PCSs, constructing a pirfenidone (PFD)-loaded and MMP-2-responsive peptide-hybrid liposome (MRPL-PFD) by co-assembling a tailor-designed MMP-2-responsive amphiphilic peptide with a phospholipid.211 In the MRPL-PFD, the PFD can be specifically released to target the MMP-2 overexpressed in the TME, thus downregulating the ECM expressed by PSCs, breaking the physical barrier during the penetration of GEM into tumor tissues, and enhancing the efficacy of GEM on pancreatic cancer (Figure 8A).

Given the difference in pH between the bloodstream and tumor core, the pH-responsive co-delivery system has been designed to efficiently deliver the toxic drugs to destruct tumor cells. In a recent study, a micelle co-loaded with paclitaxel (PTX) and phosphorylated GEM has been designed to enhance the effectiveness of chemotherapeutics against pancreatic cancer. In detail, the micelle (T-RKP) can co-deliver PTX and monophosphorylated GEM (p-GEM) based on the polyethylene glycol-polyarginine-polylysine (PEG-pArg-pLys) platform.212 In this design, PTX is linked through pH-sensitive 2-propionic-3-methylmaleic anhydride (CDM), p-GEM is combined through electrostatic interactions. The AE105 peptide (C-D-Cha-F-s-r-Y-L-W-S), anchored to the micelle surface, can target the urokinase type plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR) overexpressed on tumor and stroma cells. Upon entering the tumoral core, the lower pH there can rupture CDM, thus allowing micelle disintegration and simultaneous release of PTX and p-GEM that can destruct the tumoral internal stroma and cause cancer cell death (Figure 8B). In orthotopic pancreatic cancer tumor model, as anticipated, T-RKP increases cytotoxic T cells and decreases regulatory cells to transform the immunosuppressive TME, thereby presenting a higher anti-tumor efficiency. Regarding to the high level of ROS in the TME, Mallik et al have utilized PEG2000-S-S-PLA6000 to generate redox-responsive polymeric vesicles, encapsulated them with doxorubicin (DOX)/curcumin, and conjugated them with SV40 nuclear localizing signal peptide (PKKKRKV) via a short peptide containing the MMP-7 cleavage site.213 Three-dimensional (3D) cell spheroids studies have revealed that these polymersomes are more toxic to pancreatic cancer cells, compared to normal cells (a shrinkage rate of 49% versus 24% in spheroids). They have also constructed another tumor-penetrating hypoxia-responsive polymersome, consisting of PEG-azobenzene-PLA conjugated with iRGD peptide and coloaded with napabucasin (STAT3 inhibitor) and GEM; it can target the neuropilin-1 (NRP1) receptor overexpressed on pancreatic cancer cells, and upon reaching the hypoxic niche in the TME, its structure is decomposed to release two drugs. In the subcutaneous mouse model of pancreatic cancer, compared with that in the control group, the tumor growth rate in the polymersomes group is reduced by nearly 250%, and intratumoral necrotic rate increases by 60% (Figure 8C).214

Jia et al have synthesized dual-template molecularly imprinted polymer NPs (FH-MIPNPs) for targeted drug delivery to pancreatic cancer. Using bleomycin and glucose-modified FGF-inducible 14 (FN14) as templates, FH-MIPNPs target FN14-overexpressing pancreatic cancer cells, and in vitro studies show their superior therapeutic effects (Figure 8D).215 Cun et al have designed a size-switchable NP DGL/GEM@PP/GA capable of co-delivering GEM and 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid (GA). DGL is attached on polyethylene glycol-polycaprolactone (PEG-PCL) through the substrate peptide of MMP-2 (EGPLGVRGK) to form DGL-EGPLGVRGK-PEG-PCL, assembled with Me-PEG-PCL to form DGL@PP that further encapsulates GEM and GA to obtain DGL/GEM@PP/GA.216 In response to overexpressed MMP-2 in the TME, the DGL/GEM@PP/GA can release the GEM-loaded small DGL/GEM to penetrate into the deep tumor tissue and kill tumor cells; meanwhile, the residual GA-conjugated large PP/GA accumulates around the tumor vessels and is absorbed by CAFs to downregulate the damage response program (DRP), all contributing to a favorable antitumor efficiency (Figure 8E). In the Pan02 tumor-bearing mouse model, DGL/GEM@PP/GA presents a significant antitumor effect compared with other formulations. Shen et al have synthesized PTX-loaded monomethoxy (polyethylene glycol)-poly(D, L-lactide-co-glycolide)-poly(L-lysine)-cyclic peptide (arginine-glycine-asparticglutamic-valine acid) mPEG-PLGA-PLL-cRGD, an NP that can target the integrin αvβ3 overexpressed in the TME by cRGD. The NPs-Gels stay in the tumor for more than 50 days and exhibit a higher tumor inhibition rate than other preparations in vivo (Figure 8F).217 Huai et al have constructed a multifunctional endogenous GSH and MMP-2 dual-responsive liposomal-based nanovesicle, co-loaded with 5-FU and NLG919 (IDO1 inhibitor), named ENP919@5-FU, as a new component in the chemoimmunotherapy for pancreatic cancer. Under the high level of GSH in the TME, 5-FU and NLG919 are released from ENP919@5-FU to effectively induce tumor cell death and reverse the immunosuppressive TME.218

Gene Therapy

A gene therapy involves the deletion, modification, or insertion of specific genes in targeted cells and has shown a great potential in pancreatic cancer treatment.259 These genes (or nucleic acids) include short interfering RNA (siRNA), mRNA, short hairpin RNA (shRNA), plasmid DNA (pDNA), microRNA (miRNA), and antisense oligonucleotides (ASO). However, many cellular and tissue barriers in the TME can discount the effects of gene therapies.260 Nanocarriers have demonstrated as a resultful method to overcome these barriers that gene therapies confront in the fight against pancreatic cancer.261

In the hypoxic TME, many signaling pathways are activated. As one important transcription factor, HIF-1 is implicated in the growth, invasion, proliferation, and angiogenesis of tumor cells.262 Zhao et al have designed LENP-GEM-si-HIF1α by loading HIF1α siRNA (si-HIF1α) and GEM into biocompatible lipid-polymer hybrid NPs through ε-polylysine co-polymer (ENPs), amphiphilic copolymers PEG-PLGA, and lecithin (Figure 9A).219 As expected, LENP-GEM-si-HIF1α can effectively suppress HIF-1α expression and exhibit synergistic antitumor effects in the orthotopic murine models of pancreatic cancer. Innervation significantly increases during pancreatic tumorigenesis, and the progression of pancreatic cancer is highly dependent on the neural microenvironment.263 In pancreatic cancer, large nerve bundles form in proximity to tumor cells and perivascular areas, likely due to the expression of neurotrophic factors (NTFs), such as nerve growth factors (NGFs). Therefore, inhibiting the expression of NGFs may provide a treatment option for pancreatic cancer.264 Lei et al have developed a gold nanocluster-assisted delivery of NGF siRNA (GNC-siRNA) to silence NGFs for the treatment of pancreatic cancer (Figure 9B).220 The GNC-siRNA nanocomplexes potently downregulate the expression of NGFs, block the neurogenesis in the TME, and inhibit tumor growth in the subcutaneous model, orthotopic model, and patient-derived xenograft model of pancreatic cancer. Zhang et al have constructed iRGD-modified pluronic P123-PEI that can simultaneously deliver DTX and siAtg7 (autophagy-related gene 7 specific siRNA).221 The PP6iRGD/DTX/siAtg7 specifically binds to integrins overexpressed in the TME and silences Atg7 to inhibit autophagy in tumor cells, thereby enhancing the efficacy of DTX in pancreatic cancer treatment (Figure 9C). As a member of the TNF superfamily, tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) has a unique property to selectively kill tumor cells. Due to its denaturation, however, it exhibits a poor stability in the TME. To overcome this limitation, Han et al have synthesized the TRAIL gene (plasmid TRAIL, pTRAIL) with branched PEI (bPEI) into complexes, and via photochemical internalization (PCI), entrapped them along with pheophorbide A into human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) (Figure 9D).222 Consequently, in the Miapaca-2 xenograft mouse model, the PCI-pTRAIL/bPEI@hMSCs can significantly accelerate pancreatic cancer cell apoptosis.

The anti-angiogenic gene therapy can inhibit the growth of pancreatic cancer by disrupting neovascularization in the TME.265 VEGF, as a typical proangiogenic factor, is a hotspot target of anti-angiogenic gene therapies.146 Kataoka et al have prepared polymer/calcium phosphate (CaP)/VEGF siRNA hybrid NPs with a charge-conversional polymer (PEG-CCP) and pH sensitive block copolymer of poly (ethylene glycol) for treatment of pancreatic cancer (Figure 9E). The acidic TME can disrupt the equilibrium between calcium ions and the CaP complex, thereby triggering the release of entrapped VEGF siRNA to silence VEGF, which achieves excellent anti-tumor effects in subcutaneous pancreatic tumor (BxPC3) models.223 Xin et al have synthesized poly (ethylene glycol)-poly[aspartamidoethyl(pboronobenzyl)diethyl ammonium bromide] (PEG-B-PAEBEA) through electrostatic interaction and intense nitrogen-boron (N-B) coordination, aiming to co-deliver microRNA-34a (miR-34a) mimic and Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1) inhibitor volasertib (BI6727) to combat pancreatic cancer.224 Upon reaching the tumor site, the ROS in the TME induce the detachment of boric acid from PEG-B-PAEBEA micelles, and subsequent targeted release of miR-34a and BI6727, thereby reducing systemic toxicity and strengthening anti-tumor effect. In NSG mice bearing orthotopic pancreatic tumors, PEG-B-PAEBEA micelles retard tumor growth significantly.

Immunotherapy

In recent years, novel immunotherapeutic agents have reshaped the landscape of cancer treatment.266,267 However, the TME is always immune-quiescent and resistant in pancreatic cancer. The results are disappointing in the Phase I trial of mesothelin-specific CAR-T cells for chemotherapy-refractory metastatic pancreatic cancer268 and the Phase II trial of Ipilimumab for metastatic or locally advanced pancreatic cancer.269 Nanotechnology brings a hope to transform the highly immunosuppressive TME of pancreatic cancer.270

Hu et al have fabricated HA-coated cationic albumin NPs (HNPs) to concurrently deliver indoximod (IND) as an IDO inhibitor, 1-methyl-tryptophan (MT) as an immune adjuvant, and celastrol (CLT) as a chemotherapeutic agent for pancreatic cancer.225 The HA-mediated targeting to CD44 not only increases the accumulation but also deepens the penetration of HNPs into tumor tissues. Moreover, the combination of IND, MT and CLT can largely reverse the immunosuppressive TME. In in vivo studies, HNPs greatly enhance the anti-tumor effect in both xenograft and orthotopic pancreatic tumor models (Figure 10A). Lu et al have invented mesoporous silica NPs (MSNs) to deliver lipid-conjugated IND prodrugs (IND-PL) and oxaliplatin (OX) by synergistically interfering with the inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) and immunosuppressive indolelamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) pathway (Figure 10B).226 In Kras-derived pancreatic cancer models, the OX/IND-MSNs intensify the recruitment of CD8+ T cells and reduce the abundance of Foxp3+ T cells, resulting in significant tumor reduction.

(B) Schematic to illustrate how dual delivery of OX and IND may impact the anti-PDAC immune response. (C) Schematic illustration showing the procedure of LPR@CHG formulation and the underlying mechanism of reshaping the TME for postsurgical pancreatic cancer treatment. Cross, inhibited. (D) Illustration of size-adjustable HSA-BMS@CAP-ILTSL for combined treatment of immunotherapy and PTT. Cross, suppression.

Notably, TAMs are important immune regulatory components in the TME of pancreatic cancer. The M2 phenotype of TAMs can promote cancer growth and invasion via regulating the growth factors and cytokines in the TME,271 implying that an M2-to-M1 reprogramming may hold great promise for the treatment of pancreatic cancer.272 Inspired by this implication, Cao et al have synthesized nanoparticle complexes (LPR@CHG), a thermosensitive chitosan hydrogel containing lipid-immune regulatory factor 5 (IRF5) mRNA/C−C chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5) siRNA (LPR).227 In in vivo studies, LPR@CHG sustains and controls the release of LPR, promotes the M2-to-M1 shift and inhibits the recruitment of TAMs, downregulates the expression of CCL5 and upregulates the expression of IRF5, ultimately reestablishing an immune TME of pancreatic cancer and effectively reducing the risk of postoperative recurrence (Figure 10C). Su et al have developed CD44-targeting, HA-based, self-assembling NPs to deliver plasmid DNA expressing miR-155 and miR-125b-2, aiming to achieve anti-tumor effect through exosomes-mediated reprogramming. Transfection with LPR@CHG drives Panc-1 cells to produce exosomes that mediate the communication between Panc-1 cells and macrophages (J774.A1), and reprograms J774.A1 cells into the M1 phenotype.228 Based on the targeting capability of M2 peptides, Parayath et al have designed a pancreatic cancer immunotherapy with HA-PEG/PEI-based NPs encapsulating miR-125b.229 In the pancreatic cancer KPC genetically engineered mouse model, this therapy reprograms TAMs from a pro-tumor M2 phenotype to an anti-tumor M1 phenotype.

Enzyme-driven palmitoylation can modify cancer-associated proteins in a reversible manner. Zinc finger Asp-His-His-Cys-type palmitoyltransferases (ZDHHC) can mediate palmitoylation and is aberrantly elevated in pancreatic cancer tissues.273 Lin et al have developed ZDHHC9 siRNA NPs to potentiate the efficacy of immunotherapy against pancreatic cancer. In pancreatic tumor models, ZDHHC9-siRNA NPs silence ZDHHC9 expression, turn the TME from “cold” (immunosuppressive) to “hot” (pro-inflammatory), and efficiently suppress tumor progression.230 Yu et al have designed a strategy of immune checkpoint blockade treatment for pancreatic cancer with size-adjustable fibrotic matrix- and thermo-sensitive liposomes (HSA-BMS@CAP-ILTSL), a encapsulated with BMS-202-loaded human serum albumin (HSA-BMS).231 In response to the fibroblast activation protein-α (FAP-α) and NIR laser irradiation, HSA-BMS can be released from HSA-BMS@CAP-ILTSL to recover the activity of T cells and secrete IFN-γ and TNF-α, thereafter activating the immune microenvironment (Figure 10D).

Phototherapy

Phototherapy, represented by photodynamic therapy (PDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT), can locally destroy the tumor to delay disease progression, with fewer side effects on normal tissues.

Photodynamic Therapy

Under NIR irradiation in PDT, oxygen and photosensitizer (PS) produce ROS to induce cancer cells death. Based on a liquid metal nanoplatform, Hafiz et al have designed NPs through conjugating eutectic gallium indium (EGaIn) to HA and benzoporphyrin derivative (PS) via sonication.232 PS- and HA-coated EGaIn NPs (EGaPs) can release drugs as the pH value changes (Figure 11A). In the AsPC-1 pancreatic cancer nude mice, EGaPs induces a 2.3-fold higher degree of necrosis than that in controls. Hu et al have developed catalase- and methylene blue-loaded graded zeolite (ZCM) NPs, which can efficiently mitigate tumor hypoxia by sustainably decomposing a large amount of H2O2 in the TME, and realized oxygen self-sufficient PDT for pancreatic cancer. In SW1990 pancreatic tumor-bearing mice, the growth of tumor was completely inhibited for 18 days after exposure to ZCM and light.191 Yu et al have fabricated albumin-based NPs by conjugating pheophorbide-a (PPA) to HAS (P@HSA) and encapsulating GEM.233 Under NIR irritation, P@- GEM-has NPs produce optical fluorescence and sufficient high singlet oxygen (1O2), and release P@ and GEM that cooperate to combat pancreatic cancer with lymphatic metastases. Li et al have reported a TME dual cascade-responsive NP (GEM/Emo@NP@GHA) to enhance the effects of PDT and chemoimmunotherapy against pancreatic cancer. This NP is engineered from a guanidine-functionalized HA shell (GHA) and a GSH-responsive biopolymer core (GEM/Emo@NP), and encapsulated by two-photon-excited PS emodin (Emo) and GEM.234 The GEM/Emo@NP@GHA can specifically target tumor tissue and subsequently be degraded by HAase abundant in the extracellular matrix, while the high-level GSH within the TME breaks the disulfide bonds of GEM/Emo@NP, triggering the targeted release of GEM. Under laser irradiation, Emo generates 1O2 to further kill tumor cells (Figure 11B). In the Pan02 tumor model, the GEM/Emo@NP@GHA+light inhibits tumor growth in 97.65% of the mice, indicating its stronger antitumor activity (Figure 11C). Xu et al have designed M1@PAP that can transform the immunosuppressive TME of pancreatic cancer.235 The PPA is covalently connected to poly-L-arginine (Arg9), a nitric oxide (NO) donor, loaded with anti-PD-L1 siRNA (siPD-L1) via electrostatic adsorption, encapsulated with M1 macrophage membrane. Under laser irradiation, M1@PAP can produce O2−• and •OH to achieve an effect of PDT. Its M1 macrophage membrane confers M1@PAP an excellent tumor-targeting ability and repolarize M2 phenotype in the TME to M1 phenotype. Additionally, the ROS triggers the release of NO from Arg9, loosening the ECM and facilitating a deeper penetration of immune cells and NPs (Figure 11D).

Photothermal Therapy

In PTT, NIR laser induces photothermal agents to generate sufficient heat for killing cancer cells. Robert et al have proposed a strategy in which PTT and immunotherapy are combined to modulate the TME.236 The core-satellite PLA-b-PEG NP is coated with gold nanorods (AuNRs) and encapsulated by a potent PI3K-γ inhibitor (IPI-549). Under NIR laser, the drugs are released from AuNRs@PLA-PEG NPs. NIR radiance can be adjusted to generate varying degrees of hyperthermia, significantly reducing the viability of cancer cells, meanwhile maintaining that of macrophages. In addition, through the photo-stimulated release of IPI-549, these NPs can repolarize M2 macrophages into M1 macrophages to eradicate cancer cells (Figure 12A). This effect has been observed in the 3D co-culture of pancreatic cancer cells (KPC) and macrophage spheroids. Zhang et al fabricated a pH/GSH-responsive Janus-type manganese dioxide nanomotor (Au@H-MP@DOX NMs) by magnetron sputtering.237 In the synthesis of the Janus-type nanomotor, a crucial procedure is the asymmetric modification of Au NPs that serve as a key component of PTT nanodrugs. Once exposed to acidic TME, H-MnO2 reacts with H2O2 and GSH, and release Mn2+ and O2 to enhance T1-weighted MR imaging and provide propulsion direction for the nanomotor. Additionally, the dual response of the nanomotor to pH and GSH enables the controlled release of the loaded adriamycin hydrochloride (DOX) deep into tumor tissues, exerting anti-cancer effects by aggravating mitochondrial membrane destruction and modulating Bcl-2 family proteins (Figure 12B). In pancreatic cancer subcutaneous tumor models, Au@H-MP@DOX NMs show strong tumor-penetrating and tumor-homing capabilities. Yu has designed a CAF/NIR dual-responsive thermosensitive liposome (CAP-ITSL) loaded with IR-780 (CAP-ITSL). To produce this NP, FAP-α responsive peptide (CAP) is first assembled with phospholipids (DPPC). Then, albumin-PTX complex (HSA-PTX) is generated via self-assembly and encapsulated in the CAP-ITSL, resulting in HSA-PTX@CAP-ITSL.238 In response to the FAP-α and NIR laser, HSA-PTX is abundantly released from HSA-BMS@CAP-ILTSL into deep tumor tissues, inducing hyperthermia to perform effective PTT (Figure 12C). HSA-PTX@CAP-ITSL shows an excellent anti-tumor efficacy in Panc-02 subcutaneous/orthotopic tumor models.

Based on “offensive-defensive” maneuver, Wang et al have designed sNP@G/IR as dual-cascade responsive NPs. The core of sNP@G/IR is first formed using amphiphilic GSH-responsive polymer, encapsulated with GEM and IR1048 (photothermal agent), and then coated with an enzyme-responsive HA.239 Due to the CD44-targeting HA shell, sNP@G/IR preferentially accumulates at the tumor site. In addition, GSH in the TME can break disulfide bond and control the release of IR1048 and GEM. Under laser irradiation, IR1048 induces topical hyperthermia to eliminate tumor cells (Figure 12D).

In a recent study, Bian et al have designed a pancreatic cancer-specific AuNP-based phototheranostic modulator (AuHQ), which integrates AuNPs, aggregation-induced emission (AIE) luminogens, and Ala-Gly-Phe-Ser-Leu-Pro-Ala-Gly-Cys peptide (AGFSLPAGC).240 Once entering the TME, the AGFSLPAGC linkage can be specifically cleaved by overexpressed cathepsin E, triggering the self-assembly of AuNPs and AIE luminogens. Under NIR laser, the AuNPs aggregate to potentiate PTT and induce ICD, thus enhancing the diagnostic performance of PA imaging. Meanwhile, AIE luminogens assemble to generate AIE fluorescence, enabling an accurate imaging (Figure 12E). Hu et al have synthesized Au nanorings modified with HA and loaded with DOX (HA/DOX-AuNRs), which exhibits excellent photothermal efficiency, tumor-targeting ability, and acidic TME-responsive property. After irradiation with NIR-II (1064 nm, 8 min, 2.0 W/cm2), HA/DOX-AuNRs induce dramatic shrinkage in panc-1 pancreatic cancer xenograft models.241 Based on pH-sensitive liposomes (PSL), Zhu et al have designed a PTP-Lipo-GEM-ICG NPs, which are loaded with ICG (photothermolysis agent approved by the FDA) and GEM, and can target Plectin-1 through PTP peptide.242 In Pan02 tumor-bearing mice, the tumors are completely suppressed in the PTP-Lipo-GEM-ICG NPs NIR group, and no recurrence is observed throughout the monitoring period. Yilmaz et al have synthesized a liposome nanosystem (L2@AuNP@L1) that enables sequential release of FOLFIRINOX, a drug combination that can impart a synergistic efficacy on pancreatic cancer.243 Through the double-layer structure of L2@AuNP@L1 allows the sequential release of drug molecules. Leucovorin (LEU) and 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) are encapsulated in the inner liposome, irinotecan (IRIN) in the phospholipid bilayer, and oxaliplatin (OXA) in the outer liposome part. Core liposome (L2) is decorated by AuNP (L2@AuNP) to achieve the controlled spatiotemporal release of FOLFIRINOX components under NIR light. Irradiated by NIR laser, L2@AuNP@L1 shows a strong inhibition capacity (70.45%) on Panc-1 cells, compare to FOLFIRINOX components and PTT used individually (Figure 12F).

Radiotherapy

Through ionizing radiation, radiotherapy can generate substantial ROS at tumor sites to destruct DNA, proteins and lipids, leading to cell death.274 A high-dose ionizing radiation can better control tumors, but often cause severe damage to adjacent normal tissues.275 Smart NPs based on high-Z elements and radiosensitizers have been developed to overcome this obstacle.

Wang et al have designed collagenase-decorated transcytosis NPs (Col-TNPs) by programmed self-assembly of Au NPs, modification with pH-responsive polymer to obtain PAE@Au (Au5), and finally conjugation with collagenase.244 In response to the acidic TME, Col-TNPs can release collagenase and cationized Au5. Au5 is a radiosensitizer with a strong ability to scatter electrons and absorb X-rays. Collagenase can effectively degrade collagens in the TME, thereby enhancing the intracellular transcytosis of Au5 in pancreatic cancer tissues (Figure 13A). In BxPC-3 tumor mice, the Col-TNPs+5Gy X-ray irradiation shows a superior anticancer efficacy and a lower systemic toxicity than other treatments. As an effective Smoothened (SMO) inhibitor, cyclopamine (CPA) not only depletes CSCs and disrupts the tumor-associated stroma, but also enhances the sensitivity of cancer cells to ionizing radiation.276 Zhao et al have synthesized a core-crosslinked polymer micelle system loaded with CPA (M-CPA) to enhance the response of PSCs and pancreatic cancer cells to ionizing radiation.245 In Miapaca-2 cells and PSCs, M-CPA significantly boosts the response to ionizing radiation and impairs the DNA damage repair induced by ionizing radiation in tumor cells. Tirapazamine (TPZ) is a bio-reductive anticancer agent that can enhance the sensitivity to radiotherapy under hypoxic conditions.277 NAD(P)H dehydrogenase quinone 1 (NQO1), a two-electron reductase highly expressed in various tumors, is capable of catalyzing the conversion of quinones to hydroquinones, thereby generating substantial ROS.278 Yu et al have synthesized a hypoxia-responsive nanoscale, as a sensitizer for radiotherapy, by co-loading TPZ, MK-2206 (allosteric Akt inhibitor) and KP372-1 (potent NQO1 substrate) in a metronidazole (MN)-modified polymeric vesicle (V-K-T).246 By reshaping the distribution of hypoxic resistances, this NP not only mitigates endogenous hypoxia in the TME of pancreatic cancer, but also enhances the sensitivity to radiotherapy (Figure 13B). In subcutaneous Pan02 tumor-bearing mice, TPZ, KP372-1, Ves, V-K, V-T, and V-K-T treatments bring with tumor growth inhibition (TGI) rates of 47.7%, 66.5%, 16.4%, 69.4%, 68.8%, and 78.8%, respectively, demonstrating the superior antitumor effect of V-K-t. Johnson et al have reported a pancreatic cancer radiotherapy method based on dodecafluoropentane (DDFP) nano-emulsion (NVX-108), which can also increase the pO2 in the hypoxic TME and radiation response. In Hs-766T pancreatic tumor xenograft models, the intratumor pO2 concentration rises up to 400% and maintain for 2 hours in NVX-108 treatment group, greatly increasing the survival rate of radiated animals.247

Cerium oxide NPs (CONPs) possess both catalase-like and superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activities to regulate redox, and have been verified in pre-clinical trials as an adjuvant to radiotherapy.279 Wason et al have found that in an acidic TME, radiotherapy weakens the catalase-like activity and strengthens the SOD-like activity of CONPs, while in a neutral TME, these effects are not observed. By switching the oxidation states between Ce4+ and Ce3+, CONPs produce ROS to initiate the accumulation of free radicals, thus leading to selective apoptosis of tumors while protecting normal tissues from the toxicity of radiation. Depending upon the environmental acidity, CONPs serve as either radiotherapy sensitizers for tumors or protectants for normal tissues.248 In order to overcome the physical barrier of desmoplasia in pancreatic cancer, Schwartz-Duval et al have established a radiosensitization strategy based on intratumoral gold nanoclusters (GNCs) biosynthesized with ionic gold (Au3+), an atomic-scale agent.249 In vitro studies have shown that compared to normal cells, cancerous cells (PANC-1) present preferential Au3+ uptake and innate nuclear localization. In pancreatic cancer xenograft mouse models, these GNCs formed in situ have a strong ability to co-localize into the tumor, enhance radiosensitization, and minimize treatment-related toxicity. The GNCs+ radiation significantly prolongs the median survival versus radiation alone (235 vs 102 days). Pan et al have developed multifunctional HA@TPZMSN-AuNPs (HTMA), through loading HA and tirapazamine (TPZ) onto CD44-targeting and hypoxia-reversing MSN-AuNPs, to enhance the radiosensitization of pancreatic cancer and the therapeutic effect.250 In the PANC-02 tumor-bearing mice models, the tumor growth rate is suppressed most significantly in the HTMA+radiotherapy group, compared to other treatment groups.

Chemodynamic Therapy

Chemodynamic therapy (CDT) can induce severe oxidative damage to cancer cells by converting ROS into highly cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals (•OH) through Fenton reactions or Fenton-like reactions.280 In the TME with high levels of H+ and H2O2, nanomaterials can generate •OH by triggering the Fenton reaction, thereby inducing cell apoptosis and inhibiting tumor growth.281,282 However, due to its low efficacy and severe side effects, CDT should be modified with new nanoreactors.

TAMs are plastic and can be polarized into M1-like phenotype under the stimulation of Fe(III)/Fe(II).283,284 Ranji-Burachaloo et al have tailored out a nanocomplex (HFePQS) through the self-assembly of 4-(phosphonoxy)phenyl-2,4-dinitrobenzenesulfonate and Fe3+, followed by HA modification.251 Under the unique redox state in the TME, HFePQS can control to release SO2 and hydroquinone in a GSH-sensitive manner. SO2 reduces Fe3+ to Fe2+, generates sulfuric acid, and increases the level of ROS caused by hydroquinone, thus create a condition for the Fenton reaction to repolarize TAMs (Figure 14A). In orthotopic pancreatic cancer models, compared to GEM, HFePQS is more effective to inhibit tumor growth and extend the survival. To reduce the level of intracellular GSH and enhance the sensitivity of tumor cells to oxidative stress, Liu et al have prepared core-shell NPs composed of ZnxMn1-xS (ZMS) and upconversion NPs (UCNPs), and coated with BxPC-3 pancreatic cancer cell membranes (denoted as BUC@ZMS).252 In the acidic TME, the ZMS shell reacts with H2O2 to form cytotoxic •OH, and the synergistic depletion of intracellular GSH reduces the elimination of •OH, thereby enhancing the efficiency of Fenton-like reactions (Figure 14B). In in vivo studies, the BUC@ZMS specifically accumulates in the tumor and significantly inhibits the progression of pancreatic cancer without causing noticeable toxicity. Fan et al have developed a core-shell MnO2-based nanozyme (mSiO2@MnO2), composed of a mesoporous SiO2 core and a MnO2 shell, loaded with genistein (Gen), and modified with PEG.253 This mSiO2@MnO2 has unique GSH-activating and drug-releasing properties. Upon entry into the tumor cells, MnO2 is cleaved and Gen is released from the SiO2 mesopores for kinetic therapy. Simultaneously, Mn2+ can react with the high-level GSH in the TME, triggering Fenton-like reactions to generate •OH, thus achieving the effect of a CDT (Figure 14C). In PANC-1 cells, mSiO2@MnO2 disrupts the redox balance to induce the death of tumor cells. In PANC-1 tumor-bearing mice, mSiO2@MnO2 selectively accumulates at the tumor site to inhibit tumor growth and metastasis.

Based on the unique sensitivity of MnO2 to the TME,285 Liu et al have also produced lipid-based MnO2 nanozyme (MLP@DHA&Ce6) that can induce multisource ROS to enhance the efficacy of CDT/PDT in pancreatic cancer.254 MLP@DHA&Ce6 is obtained by decorating a MnO2 nano-shell on the liposome, and encapsulating Ce6 (photosensitizer) and dihydroartemisinin (DHA). In the TME, the MLP@DHA&Ce6 is gradually decomposed to release Mn2+ and O2. The conjugate generates multisource ROS through Mn2+-catalyzed Fenton reaction, Ce6-mediated PDT, and Fe2+ ions-mediated breakage of the endoperoxide bridge of DHA (Figure 14D). In BxPC-3 tumor mice, the MLP@DHA&Ce6+laser irradiation can effectively alleviate the hypoxia in the TME and repress tumor progression more significantly than other treatments. Zhao et al have reported a ginsenoside Rg3(Rg3)-sheltered dynamic nanocatalyst (Nano-FePt-Rg3, NFPR) with a hierarchical microstructure to improve the efficacy and safety of CDT for pancreatic cancer.255 (FePt)@(Fe1−xPtx)Oy(OH)z hybrid NPs (FePt NPs) are synthesized, then modified using (3-aminopropyl) trimethoxysilane (APTMS), and coupled with Rg3 by disuccinimidyl suberate (DSS). Due to the sensitivity of NFPR to acidity, Rg3 modification extends the circulation of NPs and increases the accumulation in the TME. Systematic NFPR inhibits the tumor growth in 86.6% of L3.6pl tumor-bearing mice, prolongs the survival time, and elicits no obvious toxicity. As a newly recognized mode of cell death, cuproptosis has shown a great antitumor potential.286,287 An inducer has been invented to simultaneously trigger cuproptosis to enhance the efficacy of CDT.288 Yu has constructed a Cu+ and DNAzyme-mediated nanocascade (ZIF-8-Cu2O-DNA),256 in which ZIF-8 supplies Zn2+ as DNAzyme and decomposes Cu2O to provide Cu+ for cuproptosis, and meanwhile DNA is loaded through electrostatic adsorption. In the weak acidic TME, Cu+, Zn2+, and DNA can be released from the nanocascade system. Among them, Cu+ can trigger Fenton-like reaction cuproptosis simultaneously. Zn2+ and DNA can form a DNAzyme that specifically recognizes and shears catalase-related RNA, leading to the accumulation of H2O2 (Figure 14E). In PANC-1 tumor-bearing mice, ZIF-8-Cu2O-DNA can efficiently and selectively accumulate in the tumor tissue to generate a prominent antitumor effect.

In tumor cells, glutamine restricts the effectiveness of CDT through metabolic reprogramming, so targeting the glutamine-metabolizing enzyme (GLS1) is expected to enhance the sensitivity of tumor cells to CDT.289,290 Herein, Dai et al have employed an assembly strategy to synthesize Fe-DBEF, a novel metal-polyphenol-based nanomedicine, to remodel the TME and enhance the sensitivity of pancreatic cancer to CDT.257 Fe-DBEF consists of Pluronic F127-stabilized and Fe3+-crosslinked epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), loaded with DOX and GLS1 inhibitor bis-2-(5-phenylacetamino-1,3,4-thiadiazole-2-yl) ethyl sulfide (BPTES). The weak acidity in the TME can trigger the dissociation of Fe-DBEF NPs to release Fe3+, EGCG, BPTES and DOX. Fe3+ and EGCG react with H2O2 to produce ROS for CDT, while BPTES inhibits GLS1 and blocks glutamine metabolism, amplifying the oxidative stress caused by ROS (Figure 14F). In all PANC-1 cells, human-derived organoids, and PANC-1 tumor-bearing mice, Fe-DBEF NPs exhibit favorable antitumor properties.

Conclusion and Prospects

TME-responsive NPs retain longer in the blood circulation, accumulate specifically at the tumor site, penetrate deeper into the ECM, and control to release drugs into the TME of pancreatic cancer. However, despite rigorous efforts to design TME-responsive NPs, the overall 5-year survival rate of pancreatic cancer patients remains extremely low.291 Therefore, many challenges need to be addressed before the clinical translation of TME-responsive NPs (Figure 15).

Challenges and future directions of TME-responsive NPs. The “?” symbol indicates “How to achieve clinical translation”. Yellow arrows, after application.

Firstly, most of current NPs present a poor delivery efficiency to solid tumors. Hence, how to increase their accumulation at the tumor site remains one big obstacle. PEG,292 zwitterionic,293 and cell membranes-coated materials have been designed to prolong their circulation and decrease the clearance in the blood.294 Due to abnormal vascular structures in tumor sites, the sizes of NPs can be optimized within 20~200 nm to enhance the EPR effect and increase intratumoral accumulation.295 Active targeting strategies, based on one or more target or stimulus-responsive targets, have been developed to increase accumulation in tumors.296,297 However, the effectiveness of the aforementioned strategies remains unsatisfactory.

Secondly, ECM-targeting strategies mainly rely on stroma ablation or CAFs depletion to break the physical barriers that prevent the diffusion of NPs.296,298 However, given that the TME is a highly intercorrelated system, stroma ablation, as a double-edged sword, may disrupt the internal balance, promote uncontrolled proliferation of tumor cells, and increase the risk of tumor dissemination.299 For example, the pancreatic cancer exhibits few desmoplastic stroma in the absence of epithelial-derived SHH, while its vascularization and proliferation are much prominent compared to the control group.146,300 Chiorean et al reported that the combination of pembrolizumab and PEGPH20 exhibited acceptable safety profiles yet failed to yield meaningful therapeutic benefits in patients with hyaluronan-high refractory metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (mPDAC). This disappointing clinical outcome can be partially attributed to the unrestrained tumor outgrowth triggered once the intrinsic stromal tumor-suppressive restraint is eliminated.301 Therefore, the NPs that transform the TME but cannot destroy the original stroma may provide tremendous therapeutic opportunities for pancreatic cancer.

Thirdly, these NPs have been analyzed in various mouse models, including genetically engineered mouse models and human xenograft models; however, human pancreatic cancer differs from that in mouse models in numerous aspects, such as tumor volume relative to the host, pharmacokinetic property, metabolism rate, and stromal cells within the tumor. A prominent translational discrepancy specifically relevant to early tumor imaging lies in the stark anatomical and microenvironmental gap between superficial subcutaneous mouse lesions and deep-seated human pancreatic tumors, which deserves special attention. Many preclinical studies reported successful visualization of tiny 1–5 mm subcutaneous xenografts using TME-responsive NPs, seemingly offering a promising solution to the clinical limitation that conventional radiological modalities cannot reliably identify human pancreatic lesions smaller than 1 cm. Nevertheless, such subcutaneous mouse tumors are situated just beneath thin skin layers with minimal overlying fat, muscle and gastrointestinal interference, free of severe respiratory or peristaltic motion artifacts; they also exhibit less dense desmoplastic stroma and relatively unobstructed blood perfusion, allowing sufficient NPs accumulation and high signal-to-background ratios for clear imaging. In sharp contrast, the human pancreas resides deep in the retroperitoneum, surrounded by abundant visceral fat, intestinal gas and peristaltic tissues that generate persistent imaging noise and signal attenuation. Tiny sub-centimeter human pancreatic lesions are wrapped in extensive fibrotic ECM accompanied by drastically elevated IFP and chaotic vasculature, which severely block NPs extravasation and intratumoral retention. Even NPs capable of detecting 1 mm superficial mouse tumors often fail to produce distinguishable contrast for equivalently minute deep pancreatic lesions in humans. Due to the impossibility of mouse models to completely replicate the TME of human pancreatic cancer, the clinic translation of many NPs is hindered.

Fourthly, most theranostic nanoplatforms covered in this review feature inorganic functional cores that resist complete in vivo biodegradation and metabolism, raising grave concerns over long-term biosafety and off-target toxicity. Following systemic delivery, residual inorganic nanoparticles persist within the reticuloendothelial system of the liver, spleen and kidneys, triggering chronic inflammation and irreversible organ injury. Non-specific uptake by healthy pancreatic, hepatic and splenic tissues further causes severe off-target adverse effects, precluding repeated long-term clinical administration. Fully degradable inorganic composite NPs need to be further developed to lower systemic toxic risks.

Fifthly, current NPs fabrication protocols remain confined to lab-scale small-batch synthesis. Scaling up to clinical-grade manufacturing triggers unregulated deviations in particle size distribution, surface modification density, residual impurities and TME-responsive functionality. Despite advantages, nanomedicines still bear notable limitations. The absence of unified industrial standards for NPs synthesis, purification and quality control, coupled with cumbersome GMP-compliant manufacturing workflows, costly raw materials, prolonged multi-phase clinical trials, and nanomaterial-specific rigorous regulatory oversight collectively lead to substantially elevated expenses for clinical development and patient administration costs. To strike a balance between therapeutic gains and economic/regulatory burdens, further efforts are needed to optimize TME-responsive nanoparticles and advance their clinical translatability.302

In conclusion, this review describes the therapeutic and diagnostic NPs that target to reshape the TME and kill tumor cells for countering pancreatic cancer. We hope that this review can inspire the design and fabrication of new functional nanomaterials tailored for the early diagnosis and personalized treatment of pancreatic cancer.
