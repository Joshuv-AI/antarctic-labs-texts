Box-Reachability in Vector Addition Systems
Shaull Almagor, Itay Hasson, Michał Pilipczuk, Michael Zaslavski

Abstract
We consider a variant of reachability in Vector Addition Systems (VAS) dubbed box reachability , whereby a vector 𝒗 ∈ ℕ d \boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d} is box-reachable from 𝟎 \boldsymbol{0} in a VAS 𝒱 \mathcal{V} if 𝒱 \mathcal{V} admits a path from 𝟎 \boldsymbol{0} to 𝒗 \boldsymbol{v} that not only stays in the positive orthant (as in the standard VAS semantics), but also stays below 𝒗 \boldsymbol{v} , i.e., within the “box” whose opposite corners are 𝟎 \boldsymbol{0} and 𝒗 \boldsymbol{v} . Our main result is that for two-dimensional VAS, the set of box-reachable vertices almost coincides with the standard reachability set: the two sets coincide for all vectors whose coordinates are both above some threshold W W . We also study properties of box-reachability, exploring the differences and similarities with standard reachability. Technically, our main result is proved using powerful machinery from convex geometry.

Introduction

Vector Addition Systems (VAS) are a well-established formalism for modelling and reasoning about concurrent systems, hardware and software analysis, biological and chemical processes, and many more.
In particular, VAS and Petri-Nets are equivalent (in a sense), with the latter being a long-studied model
[
17
]
.

Formally, a VAS is given by a finite set of vectors
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. A
trace
in
\mathcal{V}
is then a sequence of vectors obtained by summing vectors from
\mathcal{V}
, provided that all coordinates remain non-negative. The latter requirement is dubbed the
VAS Semantics
.
A vector
\boldsymbol{v}
is
reachable
if there is a trace that starts

Often a starting vector is also given, but the problem has the same “flavour” also when starting from

.
at
\boldsymbol{0}
and ends in
\boldsymbol{v}
.

The fundamental problem for VAS is
reachability
: given a vector
\boldsymbol{v}
, decide whether it is reachable in
\mathcal{V}
. This problem has a rich history, and its exact complexity has been only recently settled as
ACKERMANN-Complete
[
6

]
, with better bounds known for dimensions
1

2
.
A more involved problem is characterizing the set of reachable vectors. In general, there is no simple characterization (which is understandable, given the high complexity of reachability). However, in dimension up to five this set is
semilinear

[
10
]
.

From a modelling perspective, the coordinates of the vectors in a VAS typically correspond to some resources (e.g., memory locks, battery level, queue size, molecule concentration, etc.). Then, reachability amounts to the question of whether a certain configuration of the resources can be reached.
In the standard reachability question, no attention is paid to
how
the target configuration is reached. In particular, it may be the case that in order to reach a certain target configuration, the trace must go through configurations with a higher number of certain resources than the target.
Such traces may be less useful to the designer, or might not fit with their intent. For example, consider a chemical solution of three chemical compounds
A,B,C
, whose respective quantities are modelled as
(x_{A},x_{B},x_{C})
. Suppose we start at
(1,2,2)
and wish to reach
(10,10,10)
.
It may be the case that due to reactions between the materials, one first needs to flood the solution with compound
A
(going above
10
), and only then the reactions can lead down to
(10,10,10)
. In case
A
is expensive, dangerous or volatile, this trace might not be useful for actual reachability. Similar settings can occur with any types of resources, where one does not want to go over the amount of the resources in the target before reaching it.

To capture this notion, we introduce
box-reachability
: a vector
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
is
box-reachable
in a VAS
\mathcal{V}
if there is a trace from
\boldsymbol{0}
to
\boldsymbol{x}
such that throughout the entire trace, each coordinate
i

x_{i}
.

Example 1

Consider a 2
-VAS

\mathcal{V}=\{(-1,2),(2,-1),(10,10)\}

\pi_{2}:\boldsymbol{0}\xrightarrow{(10,10)((-1,2)(2,-1))^{11}}_{\mathbb{N}}{(21,21)}

However, both
\pi_{1}

\pi_{1}
is
(9,12)
(exceeding
(11,11)

\pi_{2}
is
(19,22)
(exceeding
(21,21)

It is not hard to see that
\boldsymbol{t_{1}}\notin\mathsf{BoxReach}(\mathcal{V})

\mathcal{V}
is
(10,10)
, and from there any added vector in
\mathcal{V}
exceeds the
(11,11)

However,
\boldsymbol{t_{2}}\in\mathsf{BoxReach}(\mathcal{V})

\pi_{3}:\boldsymbol{0}\xrightarrow{(10,10)(-1,2)(2,-1)(10,10)}_{\mathbb{N}}{(21,21)}
.
Moreover, a similar path can be taken to box-reach any vector
(k,k)

\boldsymbol{0}\xrightarrow{(10,10)((-1,2)(2,-1))^{k-20}(10,10)}_{\mathbb{N}}{(k,k)}

This example illustrates two central characteristics of the box-reachable set. First, we notice that the classical reachability set does not coincide with box-reachability. Indeed, as we show above,
(11,11)
is reachable but not box-reachable.
An even more obvious example of this is that
(30,0)
is reachable in
\mathcal{V}
(via the path
(10,10)(2,-1)^{10}

(10,10)
and in particular go above
0
in the
{\mathsf{y}}
-coordinate.

The second characteristic is that vectors that are “deep” enough in the positive quadrant, e.g.,
(21,21)
, allow us enough wiggle room along the path to apply all the “unsafe” vectors that exceed their box (e.g.,
(-1,2),(2,-1)
) without leaving the overall box. This suggests that perhaps the box-reachability set and the classical reachability set do coincide for vectors with large-enough entries. Indeed, this is the main contribution of this paper, for dimension 2.

Contribution.

We focus on dimension 2 (see
Examples

2
and
2

In
Theorem

3.1
we characterize the box-reachable set as follows: for every
2\mbox{\rm-VAS}
there exists a (polynomial size)
W\in\mathbb{N}
such that the reachability set and the box-reachability sets coincide on
[W,\infty)^{2}
.

Technically, the proof of
Theorem

3.1

uses insights on the geometry of the reachable set in the context of the cone it spans. We rely on nontrivial machinery from convex geometry (specifically, Steinitz’s lemma
[
8
]
and the Deep-in-the-cone Lemma
[
5
]
; both are introduced in
Section

4.1
).

Focusing on
2\mbox{\rm-VAS}
may seem limited. To justify this, we show that our result no longer holds neither for
3\mbox{\rm-VAS}
nor for
1\mbox{\rm-VASS}
(i.e., Vector Addition Systems with States).

In addition, we study the shape of the box-reachable set, showing that it is semilinear for
2\mbox{\rm-VAS}
as well as
1\mbox{\rm-VASS}
, and drawing a connection between box reachability in
d\mbox{\rm-VAS}
and standard reachability in
2d\mbox{\rm-VAS}
.

In a broader context, our work can be viewed as a form of
boundedness
constraint on the VAS, in the sense that box-reaching runs are bounded by their target. Unlike boundedness by a constant, this still allows counters to become arbitrarily large.

In terms of understanding the geometry of VAS, our contribution stands in an interesting contrast to standard methods of reasoning about VAS: the most common approach in reasoning about low-dimensional VAS (and VASS) is that of Linear Path Schemes, which intuitively allow to iterate a small number of cycles many times. However, LPS are “highly non-box safe”, in the sense that LPS typically reach their target by taking one cycle many times, making some counter very large, and then correcting this by iterating another cycle, and so on. Thus, LPS guide the path far outside the box and then back.
In our work, we need to exactly avoid this type of behaviors.

Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
denote the naturals and
\mathbb{Z}
the integers.
For

the interval between
i
and

. We extend the notation to

.
We denote vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
in bold (e.g.,
\boldsymbol{u}
), and refer to their components by subscripts:
\boldsymbol{u}=(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{u}_{d})
. We denote
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert=\max_{i\in[1,d]}\{|\boldsymbol{u}_{i}|\}

\boldsymbol{u}
.

For two vectors
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we write
\boldsymbol{u}\leq\boldsymbol{v}
if
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\leq\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
for all

.
For a set

we denote by
S^{*}
(resp.
S^{+}
) the set of finite sequences (resp. non-empty finite sequences) of elements of
S
.
For a vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we typically refer to its coordinates as
\boldsymbol{v}=(\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathsf{x}},\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathsf{y}})
.

A
vector addition system of dimension
d
(
d\mbox{\rm-VAS}
, for short) is a finite set of vectors
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. We denote by
\left\lVert\mathcal{V}\right\rVert=d\cdot\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{V}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert
.
A
path
of
\mathcal{V}
is a finite sequence of vectors
\pi=\boldsymbol{v_{1}},\boldsymbol{v_{2}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v_{n}}\in\mathcal{V}^{*}
(we sometimes omit the commas for brevity). The
length
of
\pi
is
|\pi|=n
.
For
i,j\leq n
we denote the infix
\pi[i,j]=\boldsymbol{v_{i}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v_{j}}
(if
j<i
the infix is empty). We also denote
\pi[j,\ldots]=\pi[j,|\pi|]
and
\pi[j]=\pi[j,j]
.

We introduce some notation for various properties of
\pi
: the
effect
of
\pi
is
\mathsf{eff}(\pi)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{v_{i}}
. Fix a coordinate
1\leq k\leq d
, we denote by
\mathsf{eff}_{k}(\pi)=\mathsf{eff}(\pi)_{k}
the effect in coordinate
k
.
The
drop
and
peak
of
\pi
in coordinate
k
are
\mathsf{drop}_{k}(\pi)=|\min_{j\in[0,n]}{\mathsf{eff}_{k}(\pi[1,j])}|
and
\mathsf{peak}_{k}(\pi)=\max_{j\in[0,n]}{\mathsf{eff}_{k}(\pi[1,j])}
. Observe that
\mathsf{drop}_{k}(\pi)\geq 0
and
\mathsf{peak}_{k}(\pi)\geq 0
, since for
j=0
we have that
\pi[1,0]
is empty, and thus has effect
\boldsymbol{0}
.

A starting vector
\boldsymbol{s}
and a path
\pi
as above induce the
trace

\boldsymbol{s_{0}},\boldsymbol{s_{1}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{s_{n}}
where
s_{0}=s
and for every
0<i\leq n
we have
s_{i}=s_{i-1}+\boldsymbol{v_{i}}
. We then write
\boldsymbol{s}\xrightarrow{\pi}\boldsymbol{s_{n}}
. If
\boldsymbol{s_{i}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
for all
i\in[0,n]
, we write
\boldsymbol{s}\xrightarrow{\pi}_{\mathbb{N}}\boldsymbol{s_{n}}
, and if we wish to emphasize that some coordinates may be negative, we write
\boldsymbol{s}\xrightarrow{\pi}_{\mathbb{Z}}\boldsymbol{s_{n}}
.

A vector
\boldsymbol{t}
is
reachable
in
\mathcal{V}
if there exists a path

such that
\boldsymbol{0}\xrightarrow{\pi}_{\mathbb{N}}\boldsymbol{t}
. We say that
\boldsymbol{t}
is

The

\mathsf{Reach}(\mathcal{V})=\{\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\mid\exists\pi\in\mathcal{V}^{*}\mbox{ s.t. }\boldsymbol{0}\xrightarrow{\pi}_{\mathbb{N}}\boldsymbol{t}\}

The fundamental problem regarding

\mathcal{V}

]
.
For general dimensions

[

3
Box Reachability

We start by introducing our main object of study, namely box-reachable vectors.
Consider a
d
-VAS

\mathcal{V}
and a vector
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
. We say that
\boldsymbol{t}
is
box-reachable in
\mathcal{V}
, denoted
\boldsymbol{0}\xrightarrow{\pi}_{\Box}\boldsymbol{t}
if there is a path
\pi\in\mathcal{V}^{*}
such that
\boldsymbol{0}\xrightarrow{\pi}_{\mathbb{N}}\boldsymbol{t}
and in addition, for every
1\leq i\leq|\pi|
it holds that
\mathsf{eff}(\pi[1,i])\leq\boldsymbol{t}
. That is, the trace induced by
\pi
from
\boldsymbol{0}
remains within the “box” whose opposite corners are
\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{t}
.
The
box-reachability set
of
\mathcal{V}
is then
\mathsf{BoxReach}(\mathcal{V})=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\mid\boldsymbol{v}\text{ is box-reachable in }\mathcal{V}\right\}
.

In contrast with the reachability problem for
VAS
, the corresponding
box-reachability problem
, namely deciding whether a vector
\boldsymbol{t}
is box-reachable in
\mathcal{V}
, is far less involved. Indeed, there are at most
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{t}\right\rVert^{d}
vectors that can be traversed in order to box-reach
\boldsymbol{t}
, and since a shortest path to
\boldsymbol{t}
does not visit the same vector twice, we can easily limit the search space.
However, as discussed in
Section

1
, given a VAS
\mathcal{V}
, we would like to understand the general shape of the box-reachable set of configurations.

We can now state our main result, which shows that for vectors with large-enough entries (i.e., deep enough in the first quadrant), reachability coincides with box-reachability (see
Section

4
).

Theorem 3.1

For every
2\mbox{\rm-VAS}

\mathcal{V}
, there exists an effectively-computable
W\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathsf{BoxReach}(\mathcal{V})\cap[W,\infty]^{2}=\mathsf{Reach}(\mathcal{V})\cap[W,\infty]^{2}
.

Our focus on
2\mbox{\rm-VAS}
may seem restrictive. However, as we now demonstrate (see also
Section

6
),
Theorem

3.1
cannot be extended to richer models, namely to
3\mbox{\rm-VAS}
or to
1\mbox{\rm-VASS}
(Vector Addition Systems with States).

Example 2

Consider the
3\mbox{\rm-VAS}

\mathcal{V}=\{(0,1,1),(1,2,-1),(1,-1,2)\}
and targets of the form
(2n,n+1,n+1)
. Note that these targets are arbitrarily “deep” in the positive octant, and are reachable via the path
(0,1,1),((1,2,-1),(1,-1,2))^{n}
.

However, these targets are not box-reachable: in order to start any path, the vector
(0,1,1)
must be taken first. Then, it is easy to see that the number of
(1,2,-1)
and
(1,-1,2)
used must be equal, and equal to
n
. Therefore, after taking
(0,1,1)
, the rest of any reaching path consists of vectors with negative entries, and in particular is not box-reaching.

For
1\mbox{\rm-VASS}
, it suffices to have two states
q_{0},q_{1}
such that
q_{1}
is only reachable via a negative number (but is reachable). Then any path ending in
q_{1}
cannot be box-reaching.

Reachability and Box Reachability Eventually Coincide

In this section we prove
Theorem

3.1
. The proof relies on two results regarding cones and lattices: the first is the
Steinitz Lemma

[
8
]
, which intuitively allows us to reorder paths so that they do not diverge wildly, and instead stay within some bounded “corridor”. The second is the
Deep-in-the-Cone Lemma

[
5
]
, which connects
\mathbb{N}
-reachability with
\mathbb{Z}
-reachability.
We start with some definitions and known results.

4.1
Reachability, Cones and Lattices

Consider a set of vectors
D=\{\boldsymbol{v_{1}},\boldsymbol{v_{2}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v_{n}}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(we do not think of it as a
VAS
at this point).
The
cone
spanned by
D
is the set of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
expressible as non-negative combinations of the vectors in
D
. Similarly, we define the
integer cone
where we restrict attention to non-negative integer combinations, and the
lattice
, where the coefficients are all integers:

\displaystyle\mathsf{cone}(D):=\left\{\lambda_{1}\boldsymbol{v_{1}}+\ldots+\lambda_{n}\boldsymbol{v_{n}}\ |\ \forall i.\ \lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\mathsf{intCone}(D):=\left\{\lambda_{1}\boldsymbol{v_{1}}+\ldots+\lambda_{n}\boldsymbol{v_{n}}\ |\ \forall i.\ \lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}

\displaystyle\mathsf{lattice}(D):=\left\{\lambda_{1}\boldsymbol{v_{1}}+\ldots+\lambda_{n}\boldsymbol{v_{n}}\ |\ \forall i.\ \lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}

The set
D
can be thought of as a representation of
\mathsf{cone}(D)
, typically called the
V-representation
(where V stands for “Vertex”). A classical result by Weyl
[
18
]
shows that we can compute from
D
a set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
2
2

2

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the standard inner product.

\mathsf{cone}(D)=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\forall\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{F}:\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle\geq 0\right\}.

The set
\mathcal{F}
is called the
H-representation
of
\mathsf{cone}(D)
(where H stands for “Halfspace”).
Intuitively, the set
\mathcal{F}
can be understood as comprising vectors that are perpendicular to the boundaries of the cone. Each vector
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{F}
defines a (positive) halfspace in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the inequality
\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle\geq 0
states that the vector
\boldsymbol{v}
lies on the “correct” side of this hyperplane.

In general, computing the H-representation from
D
may take exponential time.
However, for cones in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
an H-representation has at most two vectors
\mathcal{F}=\{\boldsymbol{f_{1}},\boldsymbol{f_{2}}\}
, and it can be computed in polynomial time.
Moreover,
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f_{1}}\right\rVert
and
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{f_{2}}\right\rVert
are polynomial in
\left\lVert D\right\rVert=\max\{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert\mid\boldsymbol{v}\in D\}
. Indeed, this follows by finding two “extremal” vectors that span the cone (or by first detecting that the cone is all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, a single halfspace, or a single ray – easier cases commented on in the following), which is in turn done by sorting
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
by their angle with the
{\mathsf{x}}
-axis, and then the facets are defined by normals to these two generating vectors. In particular, the entries of the normals are integers, and their description size is identical to that of the vectors, e.g., the normal we take to
(x,y)
is either
(-y,x)
or
(y,-x)
, depending on the required direction of the halfspace.

For an H-representation
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathsf{cone}(D)
, each
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{F}
induces a measure of how “far” a vector
\boldsymbol{v}
is from the respective facet of
\mathsf{cone}(D)
, namely the inner product
\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle
.
Specifically, given some
M\in\mathbb{N}
, we say that a vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{cone}(D)
is
M
-deep in the cone
if
\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle\geq M
for every
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{F}
.
Our first tool is the following lemma from
[
5
]
(which actually follows from existing literature, see references in
[
5
]
), which connects reachability in the integer cone with reachability in the lattice.

Lemma 1
(Deep-in-the-Cone, Lemma 16 in
[
5
]
)

Given a set of vectors
D=\{\boldsymbol{v_{1}},\boldsymbol{v_{2}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v_{n}}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and the H-representation
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathsf{cone}(D)
, there exists a constant
M\in\mathbb{N}
, depending on
D
and
\mathcal{F}
, such that if
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathsf{cone}(D)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is
M
-deep in the cone, then
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathsf{intCone}(D)
if and only if
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathsf{lattice}(D)
.

Moreover,
M
is polynomial in
\left\lVert D\right\rVert,\left\lVert\mathcal{F}\right\rVert
and exponential in
d
.

Since in our setting the dimension is
d=2
, and as mentioned above,
\left\lVert\mathcal{F}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\mathcal{D}\right\rVert
, we have that
M
in
Lemma

1
is polynomial in
\left\lVert D\right\rVert
.

Our second tool is the classical
Steinitz Lemma
.

Lemma 2
(Steinitz, as stated in
[
8
]
)

Let
\boldsymbol{v_{1}},\dots,\boldsymbol{v_{k}}
be a non-empty sequence of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{v}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{v_{j}}
and
I=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v_{j}}\right\rVert_{\infty}
. There exists a permutation
\sigma
of
\{1,\dots,k\}
such that for every
n\in\{d,\dots,k\}
, we have:

\left\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{v_{\sigma(j)}}-\frac{n-d}{k}\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq d\cdot I

In the context of 2
-VAS
, this lemma states that if a vector
\boldsymbol{v}
is reachable via some path
\pi
, then the vectors of
\pi
can be rearranged so that the resulting path, dubbed a
Steinitz path
does not stray too far from the straight line that connects the origin to
\boldsymbol{v}
. Specifically, the guaranteed bound (dubbed the
Steinitz constant
) is
d\cdot I=2\left\lVert\mathcal{V}\right\rVert
.
The form of Steinitz paths guarantees in particular that their drop and peak are not too large, which is particularly useful for us.
More precisely, we have the following (see
Section

0.A.1

5.1
From Box Reachability in
d\mbox{\rm-VAS}
to Reachability in

Having established the eventual-equivalence of
\mathsf{BoxReach}(\mathcal{V})

\mathsf{Reach}(\mathcal{V})

\mathbb{L}_{W}

6
Discussion and Future Work

In this work we define the notion of box-reachability.
Intuitively, the set of box-reachable vectors exhibit a “bounded” behavior that is more relaxed than a fixed, or even an existentially-quantified bound on some of the coordinates
[
7
]
.
Conceptually, this allows us to capture models where one wishes to reach a target without using more resources than the end goal.

We prove that despite the restriction this places on reachability, for
2\mbox{\rm-VAS}
the set of box-reachable configurations coincides with standard reachability for all configurations beyond some threshold.
As demonstrated in
Example

2
, extending
Theorem

3.1
is impossible for
3\mbox{\rm-VAS}
and
1\mbox{\rm-VASS}
.

We also show that box-reachability is semilinear for
2\mbox{\rm-VAS}
and
1\mbox{\rm-VASS}
. We leave open the question of whether this can be extended to
d\mbox{\rm-VAS}
for
3\leq d\leq 5
and
2\mbox{\rm-VASS}
, for which standard reachability is semilinear.

The introduction of box-reachability gives rise to several other natural definitions: one could consider
approximate
box reachability, where the box is allowed to be greater than the target by some fixed (additive or multiplicative) constant. Naive definitions of such extensions turn out to be not very interesting, in the sense that using the Steinitz path, even without our tools in
Section

4
, suffices to show that they coincide with reachability (since the Steinitz path already exceeds the target only by a little bit).
Nonetheless, it is possible that specific modelling needs may require some novel tweaks of box-reachability notions, and we hope this research serves as a basis for reasoning about such extensions.

x^{\circledcirc}\geq p_{3}(|Q|,\left\lVert T\right\rVert)
, then
\pi
visits large
{\mathsf{x}}
coordinates.
Fix some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we define a sequence of indices
\ell_{m}<\ldots<\ell_{1}
by setting
\ell_{1}=|\pi|
, and for every
1<j\leq m
define
\ell_{j}
to be the maximal index where
\pi
is to the left of its location at
\ell_{j-1}
. That is,
\ell_{j}=\max\{i\mid\mathsf{eff}(\pi[1,i])<\mathsf{eff}(\pi[1,\ell_{j-1}])\}
.

Observe that for every
1<j<m
we have by definition that
\mathsf{eff}(\pi[\ell_{j},\ell_{j-1}])>0
and for every
i>\ell_{j}
we have
\mathsf{eff}(\pi[1,i])\geq\mathsf{eff}(\pi[1,\ell_{j}])
(otherwise
\ell_{j}
would not be maximal).

Recall that
\left\lVert T\right\rVert
is the maximal entry in any transition of
\langle Q,T\rangle
.
In particular,
\mathsf{eff}(\pi[\ell_{j},\ell_{j-1}])\leq\left\lVert T\right\rVert
(otherwise
\ell_{j}
is not maximal) and thus
\mathsf{eff}(\pi[\ell_{m},\ell_{1}])\leq m\left\lVert T\right\rVert
. That is, the entire suffix of
\pi
within this sequence is “near”
x^{\circledcirc}
. However by the strict inequality of the
\ell_{j}
we have
\mathsf{eff}(\pi[\ell_{m},\ell_{1}])\geq m
.

Take
m=\mathsf{maxover}
. Since
x^{\circledcirc}>p_{3}(|Q|,\left\lVert T\right\rVert)>\mathsf{maxover}\cdot\left\lVert T\right\rVert
it follows that
\ell_{m}
is well-defined (in that the
\max
in the definition of
\ell_{j}
is over a nonempty set – we do not reach the “beginning” of
\pi
).

Note that
\mathsf{eff}(\pi[1,\ell_{m}])+\mathsf{eff}(\pi[\ell_{m}+1,\ldots])=x^{\circledcirc}
, so by the above we have the following properties:

1.

\mathsf{eff}(\pi[\ell_{m},\ell_{1}])\leq\left\lVert T\right\rVert\mathsf{maxover}
.

2.

\mathsf{eff}(\pi[\ell_{m},\ell_{1}])\geq\mathsf{maxover}
.

3.

\mathsf{eff}(\pi[1,\ell_{m}])\leq x^{\circledcirc}-\mathsf{maxover}
.

4.

\mathsf{eff}(\pi[1,\ell_{m}])\geq x^{\circledcirc}-\left\lVert T\right\rVert\mathsf{maxover}
.

Consider the configuration
(x^{\prime},q^{\prime})
reached by
\pi[1,\ell_{m}]
from
(0,q_{0})
, then
x^{\prime}\geq p_{3}(|Q|,\left\lVert T\right\rVert)-\left\lVert T\right\rVert\mathsf{maxover}
by Property 4 above.
By the exact same analysis as the “if” direction (here we used
p_{3}
in a tight manner, since we “lose”
\left\lVert T\right\rVert\mathsf{maxover}
), any induced path
\alpha\beta^{k}\gamma
of
\alpha\beta^{*}\gamma\in\mathsf{LPS}(|Q|,\left\lVert T\right\rVert)
that reaches
(x^{\prime},q^{\prime})
must satisfy
k\geq\mathsf{peak}(\alpha)
, and therefore
\mathsf{over}(\alpha\beta^{k}\gamma)\leq\mathsf{maxover}
.
Thus, by Property 3, we have

\begin{split}&\mathsf{peak}(\alpha\beta^{k}\gamma)=\mathsf{over}(\alpha\beta^{k}\gamma)+\mathsf{eff}(\alpha\beta^{k}\gamma)\\
&\leq\mathsf{maxover}+\mathsf{eff}(\pi[1,\ell_{m}])\leq\mathsf{maxover}+x^{\circledcirc}-\mathsf{maxover}=x^{\circledcirc}\end{split}

Denote
\theta=\pi[\ell_{m},\ell_{1}]
, then
\theta
is a box-safe suffix of
\pi
(by the construction of the
\ell_{i}
). We can assume without loss of generality that the trace
(x^{\prime},q^{\prime})\xrightarrow{\theta}(x^{\circledcirc},q^{\circledcirc})
does not repeat a configuration, otherwise an infix can be removed. All the configurations along this trace are in
[x^{\circledcirc}-\mathsf{eff}(\theta),x^{\circledcirc}]\times Q
(since
\theta
is box safe, and by the construction of the
\ell_{j}
), and by
Property 1 we have
\mathsf{eff}(\theta)\leq\left\lVert T\right\rVert\cdot\mathsf{maxover}
. By the pigeonhole principle, it follows that
|\theta|\leq\left\lVert T\right\rVert\cdot\mathsf{maxover}\cdot|Q|
, satisfying the length constraints.

Finally, observe that
\theta
closes
\alpha\beta^{k}\gamma
, since by Property 2 we have
\mathsf{eff}(\theta)\geq\mathsf{maxover}\geq\mathsf{over}(\alpha\beta^{k}\gamma)
. Thus,
(0,q_{0})\xrightarrow{\alpha\beta^{k}\gamma\theta}_{\mathbb{N}}(x^{\circledcirc},q^{\circledcirc})
.
∎
