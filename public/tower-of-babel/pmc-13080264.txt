The Impact of Acid‐Free Reflux on Vocal Cord Granuloma
Authors: Xiao‐Hong Liu, Yin Qiang, Meng Xie, Nan Cao, Yu‐Xi Yan, Jia‐Wen Zhao, Jia‐Le Ren, Hua‐Nan Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo examine the types and characteristics of acid‐free reflux in vocal cord granuloma (VCG).MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the VCG patients and those with benign unilateral vocal cord lesions between October 2017 and January 2025. Prior to treatment, all patients underwent 24‐h hypopharyngeal‐esophageal multichannel intraluminal impedance‐pH (HEMII‐pH) monitoring, high‐resolution esophageal manometry assessment, and electronic laryngoscopy examination. Furhermore, granuloma tissue samples from the VCG patients were obtained for detection of pepsin, pepsinogen A, pepsinogen C and trypsin, using immunohistochemical analysis.ResultsThe proportion of the VCG group experiencing acid‐free reflux was significantly higher than that observed in the benign lesion group (65.57% ± 23.26% vs. 30.43% ± 18.53%, t = 4.134, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) length in the VCG group was significantly shorter than that in the benign lesion group (2.25 cm vs. 2.60 cm, Z = −1.977, p = 0.047). Additionally, no pepsin expression was detected in any granuloma tissue samples. However, both pepsinogen A and pepsinogen C were positively expressed. Notably, the percentage of pepsinogen A‐positive cells in granuloma tissue exhibited a significant positive correlation with the size of the granulomas in the VCG patients (r = 0.691, p = 0.027).ConclusionAcid‐free reflux could potentially play a significant role in the development of granulomas in VCG patients.Level of Evidence4.Keywords: 24‐h HEMII‐pH monitoring, acid‐free reflux, pepsinogens, vocal cord granuloma

Introduction

Vocal cord granuloma (VCG) is characterized by inflammatory granulation tissue formation at the vocal process of the arytenoid cartilage [1]. Stimulation of refluxed substances is identified as a primary etiological factor for VCG, as it could compromise the integrity of the pharyngeal mucosa, leading to inflammation and subsequent granulation tissue proliferation [2]. Research by Guo et al. [3] demonstrated that 75.8% of individuals with VCG tested positive for the reflux symptom index (RSI), and 89.6% were positive for the reflux finding score (RFS), underscoring a significant association between VCG and reflux. Current therapeutic approaches for VCG predominantly include acid suppression therapy, local corticosteroid injections, and surgical intervention [4]. For cases of refractory VCG unresponsive to conservative management, intralesional steroid hormone injections represent a promising alternative, exhibiting minimal adverse effects [5]. Despite evidence suggesting the efficacy of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) combined with prokinetic agents in the management of VCG and postoperative care, there remains a paucity of literature analyzing the specific reflux characteristics in VCG patients [6]. The impact of different reflux including acid reflux, weak acid reflux, and non‐acid reflux on the pathogenesis of VCG remains unclear. Consequently, there is a degree of uncertainty in the application of acid‐suppressive treatments for VCG. Additionally, the assessment of reflux in VCG patients predominantly relies on subjective measures, such as the RSI and RFS, which lack an objective evaluative framework [7]. This study aims to employ 24‐h hypopharyngeal‐esophageal multichannel intraluminal impedance‐pH (HEMII‐pH) monitoring to examine the characteristics of reflux types in VCG patients and to investigate the correlation between different reflux types and granuloma size in these patients. This research is of considerable importance for the etiological analysis, precise treatment, and prognosis improvement in VCG patients.

Materials and Methods

Clinical Data

A retrospective analysis was conducted on VCG patients and those with benign vocal cord lesions (both unilateral) who presented to the Department of Otolaryngology‐Head and Neck Surgery at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi'an Jiaotong University between October 2017 and January 2025. Prior to treatment, all participants underwent 24‐h HEMII‐pH monitoring, high‐resolution esophageal manometry, and electronic laryngoscopy examination, with a minimum 2‐week washout period before testing.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) The experimental group comprised patients clinically diagnosed with VCG, while the control group included patients pathologically diagnosed with benign vocal cord lesions; (2) participants were required to be 18 years of age or older and to have provided informed consent before and after the testing; (3) participants agreed to undergo complete 24‐h HEMII‐pH monitoring and high‐resolution esophageal manometry; (4) participants possessed complete basic clinical data. Exclusion criteria include: (1) patients who have incomplete or failed 24‐h HEMII‐pH monitoring procedures; (2) patients who have received radiation therapy targeting the cervical region; (3) patients who have used PPI, H2 receptor antagonists, prokinetic agents, or gastric mucosal protectants within the preceding 2 weeks; and (4) patients with significant or chronic medical conditions.

24‐h HEMII‐pH Monitoring

All subjects underwent 24‐h HEMII‐pH monitoring using a multichannel probe catheter with a diameter of 2.3 mm. The catheter has four pairs of esophageal impedance electrodes, two pairs of pharyngeal impedance electrodes, and two proximal‐distal‐pH sensors (pH was monitored in the hypopharynx [proximal] and esophagus [distal]). Prior to monitoring, patients were instructed to fast and refrain from water intake for 8 h. During the examination, patients were required to lie on their side while the catheter, lubricated with paraffin oil, was gradually inserted through the nasal cavity. When the blue band, 1 cm below the proximal pH sensor, was near the upper esophageal sphincter (UES), the patients performed the Valsalva maneuver to expand the post‐cricoid area and expose the UES. The catheter was fixed when the blue band disappeared from the proximal edge of the UES. Throughout this process, careful observation was maintained to assess patient tolerance and monitor for any serious adverse reactions. Finally, it was connected to an external ambulatory recording device for 24 h at the exact position where the blue band was 1 cm below the proximal pH sensor to the proximal edge of the UES [8].

Assessment of Hypopharyngeal Reflux

The assessment of hypopharyngeal reflux episodes was detected by 24‐h HEMII‐pH monitoring [8]. The hypopharyngeal reflux includes liquid reflux and mixed reflux. Liquid reflux was defined as a retrograde 50% decrease in impedance starting distally and propagating to at least two more proximal impedance segments [9]. Mixed reflux was defined as a combination of gas reflux and liquid reflux patterns. A gas reflux was defined as a rapid (3 kV/s) increase in impedance by > 5000 V, occurring simultaneously in at least two esophageal impedance‐measuring segments in the absence of swallowing. A proximal reflux episode (liquid reflux + mixed reflux) was considered when the reflux reached the proximal pH sensors, and hypopharyngeal reflux episodes were characterized by reflux reaching proximally to 1 cm above the UES and were classified as acid reflux (pH < 4), weak acid reflux (pH between 4 and 6.5), and acid‐free reflux (pH > 6.5) [10]. Subsequently, relevant indicators were collected for statistical analysis.

High‐Resolution Esophageal Manometry

The high‐resolution esophageal manometry system utilized in this study was manufactured by the MMS company in the Netherlands, as referenced by Wang [11]. Prior to undergoing the examination, all participants were required to fast for a minimum of 6 h. The parameters assessed in this study included the UES length, the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) length, the UES mean resting pressure, the LES mean resting pressure, the distal contractile integral (DCI), the contractile front velocity (CFV), the peristaltic interruption length, the maximum interruption, and the distal latency (DL).

Immunohistochemical Analysis for the Detection of Pepsin Expression in VCG Tissue

Granuloma tissue samples from the VCG patients were obtained via laryngoscopy and subsequently fixed in 10% formalin at room temperature for a duration of 12 to 24 h before being embedded in paraffin. The procedure for immunohistochemical detection is based on previous relevant research [12]. It should be noted that the primary antibodies, including pepsin (Abcam, ab312852, 1:1000), pepsinogen A (San Ying, 17330‐1‐AP, 1:100), pepsinogen C (San Ying, 28532‐1‐AP, 1:50), and trypsin (Abcam, ab200997, 1:2000), were used in this experiment. Initially, the stained sections were examined under a 10× objective lens. Subsequently, using a 40× objective lens, five non‐repetitive and representative fields of view were selected for each section to assess the experimental outcomes. In cases where multiple repeated gradings occurred within the five fields, they were consolidated into a single judgment. ImageJ software was utilized to quantify the average percentage of positive cells.

Ethical Statement

All the patients have provided written informed consent before and after the testing. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi'an Jiaotong University (no. 2023049).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing SPSS software version 26.0. To compare baseline characteristics, 24‐h HEMII‐pH monitoring outcomes, and esophageal pressure measurement results between the VCG patients and those with benign vocal cord lesions, Fisher's exact test, the independent samples t‐test, and the rank sum test were employed. Furthermore, Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationship between the percentage of pepsinogen A positive cells and granuloma size. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered a statistically significant difference.

Results

Comparative Analysis of Baseline Data Between the VCG Patients and Those With Benign Vocal Cord Lesions

The study comprised a total of 26 patients, with 15 diagnosed with VCG and 11 presenting with benign vocal cord lesions. Among the latter, there were 6 cases of vocal cord leukoplakia, 2 cases of vocal cord polyps, 2 cases of vocal cord nodules, and 1 case of vocal cord keratinization, all exhibiting unilateral onset. Statistical analysis revealed no significant differences between the two groups in terms of gender distribution (male/female), age, body mass index (BMI), smoking history (present/absent), alcohol consumption history (present/absent), with all comparisons yielding p values greater than 0.05 (Table 1). These findings suggest that the baseline characteristics of the two groups are comparable.

Examination of 24‐h HEMII‐pH Monitoring Outcomes in the VCG Patients

The 24‐h HEMII‐pH monitoring results indicated that, among the 15 VCG patients, 2 cases (13.33%) were predominantly characterized by weak acid reflux, while 13 cases (86.67%) exhibited primarily acid‐free reflux. In contrast, among the 11 patients with benign vocal cord lesions, 1 case (9.09%) was primarily associated with acid reflux, 8 cases (72.73%) with weak acid reflux, and 2 cases (18.18%) with acid‐free reflux. The number and proportion of weak acid reflux were both significantly lower in the VCG patients compared to those with benign vocal cord lesions (27.87 ± 22.93 vs. 71.09 ± 45.41, t = −2.898, p = 0.012; 24.58% ± 18.91% vs. 54.42% ± 16.78%, t = −4.164, p < 0.001). Conversely, the proportion of the VCG patients experiencing acid‐free reflux was significantly higher than that observed in the patients with benign vocal cord lesions (65.57% ± 23.26% vs. 30.43% ± 18.53%, t = 4.134, p < 0.001). Furthermore, no significant differences were found between the VCG patients and those with benign vocal cord lesions in terms of the total reflux time, the upright total reflux time, the supine total reflux time, the number of reflux episodes, the reflux episodes lasting longer than 5 min, the longest reflux duration, the DeMeester score, the total reflux frequency, the acid reflux frequency, the acid reflux ratio, and the acid‐free reflux frequency (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Analysis of High‐Resolution Esophageal Pressure Measurement Outcomes in the VCG Patients

The results of high‐resolution esophageal pressure measurements indicated that the LES length in the VCG patients was significantly shorter than that in the patients with benign vocal cord lesions (2.25 cm vs. 2.60 cm, Z = −1.977, p = 0.047). This finding suggests that, compared to the patients with benign vocal cord lesions, the VCG patients have a reduced LES phase, making them more susceptible to gastroesophageal reflux and laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR). Furthermore, no significant differences were observed between the VCG patients and those with benign vocal cord lesions in terms of the DCI, the CFV, the peristaltic interruption length, the maximum interruption, the DL, the UES length, the UES resting pressure, and the LES resting pressure (p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Correlation Analysis Between the Expression Levels of Pepsin in Granuloma Tissue and the Size of Granulomas in the VCG Patients

Immunohistochemistry was employed to assess the expression of pepsin, pepsinogen A, pepsinogen C, and trypsin in granuloma tissues from the VCG patients, comprising a sample size of 10 cases. The findings indicated an absence of pepsin expression across all the granuloma tissues. In contrast, both pepsinogen A and pepsinogen C were positively expressed, whereas trypsin was positively expressed in only two cases (Figure 1). Furthermore, the proportion of pepsinogen A positive cells within the granulomatous tissue exhibited a significant positive correlation with the size of the granulomas in the VCG patients (r = 0.691, p = 0.027).

Discussion

Ylitalo et al. [13] employed 24‐h dynamic pH monitoring and observed that VCG patients experienced more frequent pharyngeal acid exposure compared to a healthy control group, so in this study we used 24‐h HEMII‐pH monitoring to assess hypopharyngeal reflux in the VCG patients. Our findings indicated that the VCG patients predominantly experienced acid‐free reflux (throat pH ≥ 6.5), whereas individuals with benign vocal cord lesions primarily exhibited weak acid reflux (throat pH 4.0 ≤ pH < 6.5), which diverges from previous research outcomes. Ding's study further suggests that the anatomical location of VCG may influence the therapeutic response to PPI [2]. Specifically, VCG located at the edges of the vocal cords and vocal processes respond well to PPI therapy, whereas granulomas on the surface of the vocal cords and arytenoid cartilage exhibit minimal therapeutic response to PPI. Moreover, extending the duration of PPI treatment does not enhance the complete remission rate, and our results may explain why using PPI alone may not be effective under certain conditions for VCG patients.

Under normal physiological conditions, the contents of the stomach and duodenum must traverse four barriers to reach the throat and beyond: the LES, esophageal peristalsis along with its acid resistance function, the upper esophageal sphincter, and the acid resistance function of the pharyngeal mucosa [14]. Consequently, individuals with normal physiological function are less likely to experience hypopharyngeal reflux due to the presence of these barriers. The length and functional integrity of the LES are crucial for maintaining normal esophageal function. Any abnormalities in the length or function of the LES could lead to symptoms such as gastroesophageal reflux, as the LES serves as a critical barrier against such reflux. This study found that the VCG patients exhibited a significantly shorter LES compared to the patients with benign vocal cord lesions, suggesting that the VCG patients are more susceptible to gastroesophageal reflux and LPR.

Shimazu et al. [15] demonstrated through experiments using the LPR rat model that VCG may arise from the synergistic effects of mechanical injury and LPR. Pepsin, a primary component of LPR, requires activation from pepsinogen in an acidic environment with a pH of less than 6.5 and plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of VCG [16]. In this study, we detected positive expression of pepsinogen A and pepsinogen C in the granulomatous tissue of the VCG patients, but did not observe positive expression of pepsin, which may be attributed to the predominance of non‐acidic reflux in these patients. The average pH of the throat is 6.8; when the pH decreases, pepsinogen may be converted into pepsin upon exposure to an acidic environment. This study utilized 24‐h HEMII‐pH monitoring and revealed that the VCG patients predominantly experience non‐acidic reflux (throat pH ≥ 6), which does not satisfy the pH conditions necessary for pepsinogen activation. Consequently, positive expression of pepsin was not detected in the granulomatous tissue of the VCG patients. Moreover, our study identified a positive expression of trypsin in the granulomatous tissue of two VCG patients, suggesting the presence of acid‐free reflux in these patients, a finding that aligns with the results from 24‐h HEMII‐pH monitoring. Additionally, a significant positive correlation was observed between the percentage of cells expressing pepsinogen A and the size of granulomas in the VCG patients, underscoring the crucial role of pepsinogen A in promoting granuloma formation. Notably, pepsinogens (PGs) exhibit heterogeneous expression across various cancer tissues, are implicated in multiple carcinogenic pathways, and play a role in immune regulation beyond the gastric mucosa. This heterogeneity positions PGs as potential biomarkers and targets for prediction, diagnosis, and prognosis assessment [17]. Furthermore, research by Stabenau et al. [18] demonstrated the carcinogenic potential of PGs and proton pump expression in Barrett's esophagus. Consequently, we hypothesize that the prolonged impact of reflux stimuli, as indicated by PGs, induces inflammatory responses in the mucosa of the vocal cord process and the membrane of the arytenoid cartilage. Reflux not only facilitates the development of VCG, primarily evidenced by granuloma enlargement, but also contributes to reactive congestion and infectious alterations in the cartilage membrane at the granuloma base. These changes may lead to the calcification of the arytenoid cartilage, thereby further promoting granuloma proliferation [19].

Conclusion

Acid‐free reflux could potentially play a significant role in the development of granulomas in VCG patients.
