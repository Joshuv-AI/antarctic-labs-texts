Empowering dental students: A quasi-experimental study on disability awareness through the EQUALISED program
Authors: Maryani Mohamed Rohani, Lily Azura Shoaib, Ninuk Hariyani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Persons with disabilities (PWDs) face significant barriers to accessing equitable dental care, primarily due to dentists’ insufficient training. The Equality in Special Care Dentistry (EQUALISED) program aims to empower dental students with better understanding in providing dental care for PWDs. This study evaluates the program’s impact on dental students’ knowledge and awareness toward PWDs.MATERIALS AND METHODS:Using a quasi-experimental pretest–post-test design, knowledge and feedback among students at Universitas Airlangga were assessed after the program. A total of 86 fifth-year dental students participated in a 1-day program, which included interactive seminars, experiential learning with PWDs, video campaigns assignments, and reflective discussions. Pre- and post-EQUALISED was assessed using a validated 10-item true/false quiz. Program feedback was collected using a structured online questionnaire with Likert scale and an open-ended item. Collected data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and Chi-square tests. The significance level was set at. 05.RESULTS:The EQUALISED program resulted in significant improvements in students’ post-test performance. The failure rate decreased from 1.2% to 0%, while the proportion of students achieving excellent scores increased from 34.1% to 90.6%. Statistical analysis demonstrated a significant difference (P < 0.001) with a strong effect size (Cramér V = 0.81). Students also provided positive feedback, particularly valuing the real-life experiences shared by PWDs.CONCLUSIONS:The EQUALISED program significantly improved students’ disability knowledge and awareness, supporting inclusive care goals aligned with health equity policies. Embedding such training into dental curricula can reduce oral health disparities and foster empathetic dentists.

Introduction

The United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities emphasizes equal rights. Yet, persons with disabilities (PWDs) continue to face substantial challenges in obtaining oral healthcare, including high treatment costs, inaccessible dental clinics, limited provider training, and communication barriers.[12] Globally, approximately 50% of PWDs struggle to access dental care, with disparities particularly pronounced in Southeast Asia due to limited infrastructure and inadequate professional preparation.[34] For instance, individuals with physical impairments may encounter clinics without wheelchair ramps, while those with intellectual or sensory disabilities often experience poor communication and stigma from providers. Negative attitudes among dental professionals further perpetuate oral health inequalities and reinforce exclusionary practices. These barriers underscore the urgent need for comprehensive, disability-focused training within dental education. The Equality in Special Care Dentistry (EQUALISED) program was a novel teaching method, developed as a targeted educational intervention to address these gaps by enhancing dental students’ understanding and empathy for practical competencies in special care dentistry (SCD).[56] This study aimed to evaluate the implementation of EQUALISED in improving disability knowledge and awareness among dental students, with the broader goal of fostering empathy towards managing PWDs in SCD [Figure 1].

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study utilizes a quasi-experimental pretest–post-test design, focusing on a single group of dental graduates at Universitas Airlangga, Indonesia (UNAIR). By comparing pre- and postintervention assessments, the study aims to measure the impact of the program on students’ awareness and attitudes through their understanding of disability and their readiness to deliver care to PWDs. Although the design did not include a control group, the comparison of pretest and post-test data allowed for an analysis of the program’s effectiveness. Students’ feedback was explored using online questionnaires.

Study participants and sampling

Following approval from the Dean of UNAIR to conduct the EQUALISED, a total of 86 fifth year dental students who attended the program were invited to take part in this study. No exclusion criteria were imposed. Participation in the study was voluntary, with informed consent obtained from all student students prior to their involvement. Students were assured of their right to withdraw from the study at any time.

Sample size

This study employed a nonprobability convenience sampling method involving all 86 dental students who attended the program, which was appropriate given the defined target population and logistical constraints of the scheduled intervention.

With an adequate sample size, a power analysis was performed using G*Power version 3.1. For a paired-sample t-test, assuming a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), a significance level of P = 0.05, and a power of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 34 students. A 10%–15% buffer was added to account for potential attrition, increasing the target sample size to approximately 40 students.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the change in students’ knowledge and understanding of disability-related care, measured using pre- and postintervention scores from a validated 10-item true/false quiz.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes included students’ satisfaction with the program, perceived achievement of learning outcomes, and qualitative feedback regarding the content and delivery of the EQUALISED intervention. These were evaluated through a structured online questionnaire incorporating Likert scale and an open-ended item.

Data collection tool and technique

Data were collected through self-administered online questionnaires, completed by students both before (pretest) and after (post-test) the EQUALISED intervention. The questionnaire consisted of two key sections: a demographic profile (age and gender) and a 10-question true–false section. This study used a previously validated and reliable tool; therefore, retesting reliability was not performed.[6] A total percentage was calculated for the pre- and postprogram scoring, with a passing score set at 50%. Scores between 50% and 79.99% were considered average, while scores of 80% and above indicated excellent performance.

Students’ feedback on the program was collected at the conclusion of EQUALISED using a self-administered online questionnaire comprising 20 questions. The first 15 items employed a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (extremely dissatisfied) to 5 (extremely satisfied). Two questions assessed the achievement of learning outcomes and alignment with course expectations, using response options of “yes,” “no,” or “not sure.” Additionally, two questions evaluated the instructor’s teaching performance and the likelihood of recommending the course, using a ten-point scale (1 = highly unlikely, 10 = highly likely). The questionnaire also included an open-ended item: “Tell us how we can improve this program.”

Intervention

Overview of the equality in special care dentistry program in UNAIR context

The program, adapted from University Malaya’s Disability Equality Training (DET) in Malaysia, was tailored to fit the educational and cultural context of Indonesian dental students.[6] As the first disability-focused dental education initiative ever conducted at the University, this pioneering 1-day program introduced a novel approach to bridging gaps in dental care for PWDs, through interactive and experiential learning activities, including real-life engagement with PWDs, the program aimed to raise awareness and enhance students’ empathetic competencies in SCD.

The EQUALISED distinguishes itself from existing models through cultural and linguistic adaptations tailored for Indonesian dental students. It was delivered in both English and Bahasa Indonesia to ensure accessibility and enhance engagement, particularly for students who more fluent in the national language. The program uniquely involved local representatives from diverse disability groups. It was structured as a 1-day intensive session combining seminars, group work, and discussions, designed to accommodate logistical and financial constraints. It fostered open dialog between students and PWDs, promoting a more participatory and reflective learning environment that aligns with the cultural emphasis on respect for authority.

EQUALISED was designed to strengthen students’ competencies in providing oral healthcare for PWDs by enhancing their knowledge, clinical skills, empathy, and awareness of disability-related barriers. It emphasized an inclusive, patient-centred approach to care by cultivating the attitudes and capabilities required to deliver compassionate and competent treatment. To achieve this, the program integrated experiential learning strategies including role-play and real-life interactions with PWDs, which were instrumental in fostering empathy and promoting deeper, applied understanding of the complex challenges faced by PWDs in accessing dental services.

Intervention components

Phase 1: Students attended an interactive seminar led by a certified DET trainer with visual impairment, focusing on the principles of disability equality and the barriers faced by PWDs in accessing healthcare. This was followed by group discussions and presentations on the barriers faced by PWDs with visual, physical, hearing, learning, and speech impairments, along with ways to overcome them. The activity continued with a forum featuring representatives from various PWD groups (individuals with physical, sensory, visual, speech, and hearing impairments, as well as intellectual disabilities), where students engaged in open dialog and reflected on real-world experiences

Phase 2: Students worked in groups to create a short video campaign using smartphones, designed to raise disability awareness in dental practice. Students were provided with tools and props to simulate PWDs, such as wheelchairs, walking sticks for people with visual impairments, and blindfolds, allowing students to experience firsthand the challenges faced by PWDs when seeking dental care. This exercise encouraged creative thinking, and the practical application of knowledge gained from the seminar. After presenting the videos, a reflection session was held with the EQUALISED trainers and facilitators, during which students shared their insights and received feedback on their assignments.

Ethical consideration

Ethics clearance was granted from the Faculty of Dental Medicine Health Research Ethical Clearance Commission, Universitas Airlangga (Certificate No: 680/HRECC.FODM/VI/2023). This study was not registered in a clinical trial registry as it employed a quasi-experimental educational design without randomization or clinical intervention.

Statistical analysis method

Data from the questionnaires were coded and entered IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA SPSS Statistics for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequency distributions and percentages, were used to summarize demographic characteristics, pre- and postintervention responses, and student feedback. A Chi-square test was conducted to compare the distribution of scores across grades (fail, average, excellent) between the pre- and post-EQUALISED interventions. The significance level was set at. 05. Open-ended responses to the question “Tell us how we can improve the program?” were analyzed using thematic analysis.

Results

Of the 86 students, 85 completed both pre- and post-EQUALISED assessments (response rate, 98.8%), with a female majority (n = 64; 75.3%). The students’ ages ranged from 20 to 27 years, with a mean age of 21.7. The sample size exceeded the minimum requirement, thereby strengthening the study’s validity. Academic outcomes post intervention

Following the intervention, the mean EQUALISED score increased from 6.88 (SD = 1.03) to 8.89 (SD = 1.05), representing a 29.2% improvement. Table 1 and Figure 3 provide a visual comparison of student performance before (pre-EQUALISED) and after (post-EQUALISED) the program. Prior to the intervention, 34.1% of students achieved an “Excellent” score, while one student (1.2%) failed. Post intervention, the failure rate was eliminated, and the proportion of students attaining “Excellent” scores rose to 90.6%, accompanied by a reduction in “Average” grades.

Figure 4 summarizes student satisfaction scores across various aspects of the course, rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The highest satisfaction was reported for knowledge gained (mean = 4.66, standard deviation [SD], 0.57). High ratings were also observed for the effectiveness of instructional materials (mean = 4.41, SD = 0.50) and learning activities (mean = 4.35, SD = 0.68). Lower mean scores were recorded for the use of technologies and instructor time management (both mean = 3.99; SDs, 1.26 and 1.25, respectively).

Several students recommended increasing interactive and engaging activities, such as incorporating more group discussions and games to enhance participation and enjoyment. Notable comments included, “meeting people with disabilities directly opened my mind more than any lecture could”. “Using the wheelchair and blindfold helped me understand their struggles in a real way.”

Student feedback also highlighted the need for improved accessibility and learning resources, particularly through the inclusion of more training and materials related to sign language. Suggestions regarding the program’s structure and reach included extending its duration to multiple days to reduce fatigue and offering it more regularly or to a broader audience. A few responses addressed content relevance and delivery, recommending better alignment of speakers with the program’s core objectives to ensure consistency and depth in topic coverage.

Discussion

The EQUALISED program had a significant positive impact on students’ performance, particularly in enhancing their knowledge and competencies in SCD. The elimination of failures and substantial improvement in student scores underscore the program’s effectiveness, as evidenced by a marked increase in excellent scores from 34.12% to 90.59% (P < 0.001, Cramér’s V = 0.81). This rise in the number of students achieving excellent scores highlights the program’s role in strengthening understanding and competency in SCD. These findings align with prior research that emphasizes the value of targeted educational interventions in addressing knowledge gaps in disability-related care among healthcare professionals.[78910]

The strong association between program participation and improved student performance evidenced by a highly significant difference between pre- and post program results demonstrates the practical impact of the EQUALISED initiative. These findings affirm the program’s effectiveness in elevating students’ proficiency from average to excellent. Overall, students perceived the program as informative, engaging, and beneficial in raising disability awareness. These outcomes align with existing literature that underscores the importance of inclusive education and competency-building into professional development to improve care for individuals with disabilities.[1112]

Overall, the results suggest that the EQUALISED successfully enhanced students’ understanding of disability-related care, leading to improved academic performance and better preparedness for serving individuals with special care needs in clinical practice. Many dental professionals lack adequate training in this area, with most dental schools offering limited education on SCD.[13] This education gap contributes to ongoing oral health disparities faced by PWDs, who encounter significant barriers to accessing dental services, including clinic inaccessibility, communication challenges, insufficient specialized training among dental professionals, and societal stigma.[14] Educational programs like EQUALISED aim to address these disparities by promoting disability awareness, fostering inclusive dental practices, and promoting competency among graduates in providing comprehensive care to PWDs, thereby helping to reduce oral health inequities in this vulnerable population.[15]

Strengths and limitations

In comparison with other studies, the impact of the EQUALISED was consistent with research demonstrating the benefits of disability education in healthcare training. For instance, Smith et al. found that disability-related educational interventions significantly enhanced healthcare students’ knowledge and empathy toward PWDs.[7] Both studies reported improvements in competency and a shift toward patient-centered care following the intervention, mirroring the outcomes observed in the EQUALISED.

A notable strength of this study lies in its comprehensive approach, which combined theoretical knowledge with experiential learning and clinical application. The inclusion of real-life interactions with PWDs, culturally relevant adaptations, and bilingual delivery in English and Bahasa Indonesia enhanced student engagement and accessibility. The structured video campaign and reflective discussions also helped to consolidate learning outcomes.

However, while earlier studies focused primarily on attitudinal shifts, the EQUALISED program emphasized practical skills, evidenced by the marked improvement in quiz scores and elimination of failures. This positions the EQUALISED as more comprehensive, combining theoretical knowledge with clinical application. Despite these positive outcomes, the need for long-term follow-up, as highlighted in both this and previous studies, remains a critical factor for ensuring sustained improvements in professional practice and SCD curriculum for undergraduates.[16] Furthermore, the sample was drawn from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other dental schools with differing curricular structures and cultural contexts. The single-center design also introduces the possibility of information contamination as students from the same cohort may have shared details about the intervention, potentially influencing post-test responses and inflating the observed effects. While the EQUALISED program was culturally and institutionally tailored, its modular and experiential design allows for flexible adaptation across various educational contexts. Nonetheless, differences in curriculum, institutional resources, and student demographics should be carefully considered when implementing the program in other settings. The study’s focus on pre- and post-test scores without follow-up assessments limits the ability to evaluate the long-term impact of the EQUALISED program on knowledge retention and clinical application. Additionally, self-reported data may be influenced by biases such as social desirability, recall inaccuracies, and response tendencies, which could inflate perceived knowledge or attitudes. Students may provide favorable responses due to the sensitive nature of disability awareness or the educational setting. These potential biases should be considered when interpreting the findings as they may not accurately reflect actual behavioral or cognitive changes.

Future research directions

Future research should reassess the reliability of evaluation tools within the target population as cultural and contextual factors may influence item interpretation and response accuracy. Longitudinal studies are needed to assess knowledge retention and the transfer of learning into real-world practice. Incorporating control groups would enhance internal validity, while multi-institutional studies could improve the generalizability of findings. Additionally, qualitative inquiry into students’ reflective experiences may offer deeper insights into how disability-focused education shapes empathy, professional identity, and clinical readiness.

To further enhance the program, expanding it to include interdisciplinary collaboration with professionals in occupational therapy, speech-language pathology, and social work could foster a more holistic approach to care. Incorporating diverse pedagogical strategies, such as problem-based learning, case discussions, and role-play, may deepen engagement and support the development of clinical reasoning. Embedding structured peer and mentor feedback mechanisms would provide personalized guidance, promote reflective learning, and support student development. Additionally, future studies should investigate the long-term impact of the program on clinical behavior and service delivery for PWDs.

Conclusion

The findings of this study have important implications for dental education, curriculum development, and health equity policy. Within the study’s limitations, the EQUALISED program significantly enhanced dental students’ knowledge, empathy, and preparedness to care for PWDs. Its modular, experiential design presents a scalable and adaptable model for integrating SCD into diverse curricula. Embedding such training into core dental education and national oral health strategies can promote inclusive healthcare and better equip future dentists to serve underserved populations. Strengthening practical components and feedback mechanisms may further enhance the program’s long-term impact.
