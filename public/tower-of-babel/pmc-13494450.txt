An Optimized Diagnostic Approach for Adults With Suspected Inherited Metabolic Disorders
Authors: Machteld M Oud, Elise A Ferreira, Clara D M van Karnebeek, Robin Wijngaard, Nicole I Wolf, Marc Engelen, Mareen Datema, Sacha Ferdinandusse, André B P van Kuilenburg, Laura Steinbusch, Saskia N van der Crabben, Mirjam Langeveld, the ZOEMBA Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInherited metabolic disorders (IMDs) arise from defects in metabolic pathways essential for normal biochemical function. Although the pathogenic genetic variants are present at birth, clinical manifestations may not emerge until adolescence or adulthood. Symptoms may be more subtle compared to those of childhood onset IMDs and may have overlap with signs and symptoms of acquired disorders. This makes diagnosing IMDs in adulthood challenging. To identify how routine diagnostic care for adults suspected of having an IMD could be improved, we conducted a reanalysis study in a cohort of 31 adult participants. The participants, who remained undiagnosed after standard diagnostic care, were seen by a clinical geneticist, adult metabolic specialist and neurologist for deep phenotyping and data collection. Subsequently, exome sequencing data were reanalyzed and possible disease‐causing variants were prioritized for further evaluation by a multidisciplinary team including both clinicians and geneticists (biochemical and molecular). This approach led to a (partial) diagnosis for nine participants from six families, giving a 25% diagnostic yield. We could distinguish four possible reasons why the identified disease‐associated variants were not detected during routine diagnostic care. Beyond the expected impact of technical advancements and emerging knowledge of disease genes, we found that the most impactful factor was of organizational nature. Our findings underscore the value of reviewing all strong genetic variants within a multidisciplinary team setting, facilitating a bidirectional exchange of expertise. This approach enhances diagnostic accuracy, particularly when the underlying cause of the disease is not immediately apparent.Keywords: adults, diagnostics, genetics, inherited metabolic disorders

Introduction

Inherited metabolic disorders (IMDs) are conditions in which impairment of a biochemical pathway is intrinsic to disease pathophysiology. For approximately half of the individuals diagnosed with an IMD this diagnosis is established in adulthood [1]. This is in part due to the fact that in a large proportion of these individuals the onset of symptoms is in adulthood, even though the underlying genetic cause is already present from birth. With the recognition that these genetic disorders can be dormant in childhood and only clinically manifest themselves later in life, the number of individuals that are potential candidates for broad metabolic and genetic diagnostics has increased significantly.

IMDs that have a later onset are often caused by less deleterious genetic variants compared to those observed in the severe early childhood onset IMDs and result in residual enzymatic or transporter activity [2]. This leads to a less prominent disease phenotype, with more subtle signs and symptoms, that can be difficult to distinguish from the consequences of common acquired disorders. In addition, the phenotype of a less deleterious variant in a gene may lead to a different phenotype compared to that of deleterious variants. For example for mucopolysaccharidosis type III apart from the well‐known childhood onset progressive neurodegenerative phenotype a late onset phenotype with retinopathy and cardiomyopathy as cardinal features exists [3]. Finally, signs and symptoms of IMDs may show overlap with other disorders that are generally considered non‐metabolic genetic disorders, for example cardiac hypertrophy in Fabry disease and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy caused by sarcomere mutations. This raises the question of what the best diagnostic approach is to an adult suspected of having an IMD based on clinical, biochemical, functional and/or imaging features but in whom standard diagnostics (including biochemical and genetic testing) have not yielded a diagnosis. In a previous study we concluded that metabolic and genetic diagnostics should be offered to any adult presenting with an objective sign or symptom involving one or more organ systems, in whom common causes are ruled out [4]. The likelihood of an IMD as an underlying cause is higher if the nervous system is involved, if multiple organ system are involved and if there are relatives with the same signs and symptoms. A challenging factor in adult IMD diagnostics is that adults often exhibit a combination of acquired and genetic pathologies. Distinguishing these two etiologies requires experienced physicians from different disciplines (e.g., neurology, cardiology, etc.) with knowledge of both common and rare disease mechanisms.

Besides the clinical examination the diagnostic trajectory of adult individuals suspected of having an IMD may consist of metabolite and/or enzyme activity studies as well as genetic analysis. The use of these methods is dependent on method availability and the experience of the team and/or hospital. Currently, the genetic analysis often comprises gene panel analyses, confining the search for a causative gene to a specific disease group, limiting the chances of a diagnosis if the disease falls outside of this defined subset. Moreover, in case of open exome analysis in adults it can be more challenging or even impossible to obtain DNA from both parents for trio analysis of sequencing data, making it more difficult to find the causative gene. If these techniques fail to establish a diagnosis, there is limited evidence on the best follow‐up diagnostic test in individuals with a high pre‐likelihood of an IMD.

In this study we performed reanalysis of exome sequencing (ES) data to find a diagnosis for adults suspected of having an IMD in whom no diagnosis was found in routine diagnostic care, including metabolic screening of plasma and urine, enzyme analysis if indicated and genetic testing. Our findings show that the likelihood of reaching a diagnosis in this adult cohort significantly increases with a multidisciplinary approach to deep phenotyping and genetic data interpretation. Finally, we would like to share our insights on the preferred diagnostic trajectory for adults suspected of having an IMD.

Materials and Methods

Study Approach

The ZOEMBA study was approved by the local ethics committee of the Amsterdam UMC (NL67721.018.19) and registered at https://clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06200142). All participating individuals were referred to and included at the Amsterdam UMC, Amsterdam, the Netherlands and they or their legal guardians signed informed consent for the study.

The study includes a cohort of adult individuals suspected of having an IMD from different IMD expertise centers in the Netherlands. Individuals were referred to the study by their treating physician (neurologist, clinical geneticist, internist, etc.) if a genetic metabolic disorder was suspected and standard diagnostic procedures had not yielded a diagnosis (see inclusion criteria below). Common causes for the presented disease signs and symptoms were ruled out. After referral, eligibility of the individual was assessed by the study team, taking into account the completeness of previous examinations (if standard laboratory genetic or imaging tests were not performed, this was advised) and the likelihood of a genetic versus an acquired disorder. The goal was to enrich the study population for those individuals with a high likelihood of an IMD. Upon study inclusion, the individual was seen by the study team (clinical geneticist, adult metabolic specialist, coordinator/PhD student and neurologist) for deep phenotyping using HPO terms and sample collection. ES data were obtained for reanalysis. Results were discussed in a multidisciplinary setting by a core team of experts including a coordinator/PhD student, adult metabolic specialist, neurologist, and clinical and molecular geneticists in collaboration with other specialists involved in data generation, analysis and interpretation.

Inclusion Criteria

Individuals were eligible for inclusion in the study if they had at least one of the clinical criteria AND no diagnosis despite extensive clinical, metabolic and genetic testing. Clinical criteria:

Unexplained metabolic phenotype (i.e., neurological symptoms, abnormalities on physical examination suggestive of a genetic metabolic disorder (e.g., splenomegaly)).

Medical Data Retrieval and Examination

At the first study visit individuals were examined by the clinical geneticist, the adult metabolic specialist and the neurologist (the latter if neurological symptoms were present). A full medical history and clinical examination were collected and, if stored, fibroblasts were retrieved. In addition, patient electronic records were obtained as well as available historical files to retrieve medical data, social history (developmental milestones, level of education, neuropsychological testing), and detailed and extensive family history was taken. In some cases clinical data from other (similar) affected relatives were obtained to help with clinical diagnosis. Diagnostic and laboratory data including brain MRI, skeletal X‐rays and ECGs were collected (raw data/images as well as the reports). All relevant medical data were pseudo‐anonymized and documented in standardized Case Report Forms.

Genetic Data Analysis

For the majority of participants (n = 26/31), ES was performed in a diagnostic setting prior to study inclusion. Five participants were included in the study via a previously included family member with whom they shared similar clinical signs and symptoms (P12, P22, P26, P27, and P29). For four of these participants genome sequencing (GS) data were generated in the study because ES data were not available and for one, P12, Sanger sequencing was performed. Upon inclusion, raw sequencing data were collected or generated and reanalyzed using an in‐house established research pipeline. In brief, single nucleotide variants (SNV) were called by the GATK haplotype caller and short‐tandem repeats (STR) using Expansion Hunter. Copy number variants (CNVs) in ES data were called using Conifer and ExomeDepth and in GS data using Canvas Copy Number Variant Caller. See Supplemental methods for more a more detailed description. The following variant types were analyzed from ES/GS data; SNVs (exonic and splice donor/acceptor +/−30 bases) and STRs for all participants. Where possible copy number variants (CNVs) were analyzed as CNV analysis requires a reference pool of technically matching samples and this was not always possible. Variant filters were applied, SNVs with an allele frequency > 1% and/or > 20% homozygosity counts in gnomAD (version 2.1) were filtered out as well as CNVs with a frequency > 1% and > 90% overlap with the 1000 Genomes database or an internal database. Data interpretation was performed using two strategies: first all coding, noncoding and splice variants in known IMD genes were selected (strategy 1), followed by an open exome/genome analysis including protein‐coding and noncoding transcripts (strategy 2). Genes included in strategy 1 were part of the IEMbase gene list (1556 genes, www.iembase.org, update April 2022) [5] or part of the Radboudumc & Maastricht UMC+ gene panels for metabolic disorders (1073 genes, www.radboudumc.nl/en/afdelingen/genetica/about‐us/genomediagnostics, version DG3.4). All selected variants from strategies 1 and 2 were prioritized based on a CADD score > 20, phyloP > 2.7 and/or spliceAI score ≠ 0. Subsequently, for each prioritized disease gene the inheritance pattern and associated HPO terms were interpreted with regard to the phenotype of the participant. Potential candidate genes were prioritized based on the function of the gene and when available findings from in vitro and animal model studies.

Data Interpretation

For all individuals a list of potential candidate genes/variants was composed as described in Section 2.3 and discussed within the study team. For strong candidates that required validation a plan was made. This plan could include segregation analysis, functional studies (e.g., functional tests in fibroblasts), (re)measuring specific metabolites, reverse phenotyping with regard to specific clinical findings (e.g., on imaging) and/or the use of matchmaking tools to find a second affected individual. Progress was evaluated in regular follow‐up meetings until a final conclusion was reached, being: a diagnosis explaining the full phenotype, a diagnosis explaining part of the phenotype, a possible diagnosis or no diagnosis.

Phenotype Similarity Analysis Between Gene Panels

In addition to the analysis of participant‐derived data, we investigated the similarities between gene panels in terms of clinical features associated with the included genes. Six different Radboudumc diagnostic gene panels were used, including METAB/OXPHOS (referred to as IMD, n = 1018 genes), intellectual disability (ID, n = 1161 genes), movement disorders (n = 243 genes), epilepsy (n = 225 genes), muscle disorders (n = 130 genes), and ciliopathies (n = 176 genes). To assess phenotype similarity independent of shared genes, all IMD genes were removed from the other panels prior to analysis. Genes were annotated with Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms using OMIM gene–phenotype associations (Human Phenotype Ontology; accessed 16 December 2025). For each gene panel, HPO frequencies were calculated as the proportion of genes associated with each HPO term. Analyses were restricted to HPO terms with a frequency ≥ 10% in at least one panel. Phenotype similarity between panels was quantified using cosine similarity and visualized using heatmaps generated with the pheatmap v1.0.13 R package.

Exome Sequencing Reanalysis Results

ES reanalysis was performed for 26 participants (21 families) and GS analysis was performed for four participants (3 families), consisting of 16 singleton analyses (only the affected participant) and 13 analyses with multiple family members. Of the prioritized variants on average seven variants (range 0–21 variants) in known or candidate IMD genes and 17 variants (range 1–69) in all other genes were identified. From the prioritized variants we found the causative variant in six families (F2, F5, F6, F8, F9, F10) leading to a diagnosis for nine participants. In addition, in two individuals (P17, P21) new variants of unknown significance (VUS) favoring pathogenicity were found resulting in a possible diagnosis and in another two individuals (P1, P7) variants in a candidate gene were identified. In two cases the VUS of interest had already been detected in the diagnostic setting prior to the study analysis but without conclusive evidence (P7: TCHP and P17: CACNA1A). Gene Panel Versus Open ES Approach

ES data can be analyzed for a specific set of genes, so called gene panels, or as an open analysis looking at variants in all genes. Prior to this study 29/31 participants received diagnostic ES analysis, of whom 12 datasets were analyzed for one or more gene panels. For seven participants an open ES analysis was performed after a gene panel analysis and for 10 datasets an open analysis was performed immediately. The selection of the most suitable gene panel for ES analysis is based on the phenotype of the individual. However, in many cases the phenotype is less specific or comprises clinical features that overlap multiple gene panels, for example, global developmental delay, seizures and hypotonia, making it more difficult to determine the most suitable gene panel for analysis. To illustrate the latter, we compared the HPO terms associated with genes from different gene panels, relevant to an IMD cohort and found substantial similarities between the IMD gene panel and three other gene panels, namely intellectual disability, epilepsy, and movement disorders (Figures 1 and S1). From the eight genes that led to a (possible) diagnosis in this study, four genes are present in the IEMbase (v.2.0.0) and are considered a metabolic gene (Table 3), while the other four genes belong to different disease groups, incl. epilepsy, muscle and movement disorders.

Deep Phenotyping Improves Variant Interpretation

ES analysis for a recessive disease model generally requires the interpretation of many rare variants when the phenotype of the individual is not taken into account. This number can be reduced by incorporating phenotypic information into the assessment of the prioritized variants. Moreover, once a candidate variant is identified, reverse phenotyping can be employed specifically looking for signs and symptoms expected to be present based on the gene involved. We encountered two families, F8 and F10, where a diagnosis was made based on reverse phenotyping for the prioritized variants. In the case of family 8 (P8 and P12), only after identification of the APOE variants upon ES analysis, the severe dyslipidemia in the individual and affected family members was identified (see Case vignette 1 and Ferreira et al. [6] for more clinical details) [6]. Family 10 consisted of three affected family members (P10, P13, and P19) with overlapping clinical features. ES analysis of only the variants that were shared by all three affected family members led to a faster identification of the probable causative gene, EGFR (see Case vignette 2 and Ferreira et al. [7] for more clinical details) [7]. In a third individual, P6, ES analysis revealed the causative gene, FGFR3, for only part of the individual's phenotype suggesting more than one underlying etiology. Additional studies are required to find the underlying cause of the remaining phenotypic features. Taken together, these three families highlight the importance of using phenotypic data for the interpretation of variants.

Case Vignette 1 Family 8

Participant 8 presented to the emergency department at age 37 years with acute abdominal pain that, after investigations, turned out to be due to an infarction in a grossly enlarged spleen. There were no neurological signs or symptoms. Further workup pointed towards a lysosomal storage disorder with foam macrophages in a bone marrow biopsy, elevated levels of N‐palmitoyl‐O‐phosphocholine‐serine (PPCS; 547 nmol/L, reference range 1.3–32.2 nmol/L) and increased chitotriosidase activity (901 nmol/h/mL, reference range 0–90 nmol/h/mL). ES analysis identified two homozygous APOE variants (see Table 3). The combination of reverse phenotyping, segregation analysis and functional tests confirmed the APOE variants as cause of the familial dyslipidemic splenomegaly (this case is published previously in more detail by Ferreira et al.) [6].

Case Vignette 2 Family 10

Participant 13 was admitted to the ICU at age 61 years with a hypercapnic coma and turned out to have a complex disorder of which the most prominent feature was cognitive dysfunction caused by temporal lobe epilepsy. Family history turned out to be positive for both epilepsy as well as the cognitive dysfunction suggestive of an autosomal dominant disorder. Inclusion of two other affected family members (P10 and P19) and combined ES reanalysis of all three affected family members led to the identification of a candidate variant in EGFR. Segregation analysis in other family members and reanalysis of MRI‐scans confirmed the probable pathogenicity of the variant in EGFR [7].

Improvement of ES/GS Analysis Methods

The 26 diagnostic ES datasets were generated in various ways using different platforms and analysis pipelines. In order to interpret all datasets in a standardized manner we reanalyzed each dataset using the same analysis pipeline. In addition to the standard variant calling for SNVs, this pipeline included methods to detect STR and CNV variants not previously analyzed in the diagnostic ES setting. This led to the detection of a causative STR variant in CNBP in participant 2. The availability of GS data for participant 5 allowed for CNV analysis and the detection of an exon deletion in DMD. This deletion was also present in the ES data of the participant and may have been picked up if a CNV analysis could have been performed on the ES data. These findings show that the continuous improvement of ES/GS analysis pipelines leads to higher diagnostic yields.

New Disease Gene Or Mechanism

In the ES data of participant 9 we detected a heterozygous variant in CAD (see Table 3). CAD encodes a multienzymatic protein that catalyzes the initial reactions of the de novo biosynthesis of pyrimidine nucleotides, a pathway essential for nucleotide homeostasis, cell growth and proliferation [8]. Currently only biallelic loss‐of‐function variants in CAD have been described, underlying a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE50, MIM# 616457) amenable with uridine treatment [9]. Functional studies in fibroblasts derived from participant 9 showed an impaired feed‐back regulation of CAD by uridine triphosphate (UTP) and a continuously active pyrimidine de novo pathway (data not shown). These findings indicate that the detected monoallelic variant in CAD leads to a gain‐of‐function pathomechanism [8].

The ES data of participant 21 revealed biallelic variants in ACACB (or ACC2, see Table 3) that are currently not associated with disease. ACACB encodes the enzyme acetyl‐CoA carboxylase 2 that is involved in the regulation of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation and is primarily expressed in heart and skeletal muscle tissue. Participant 21 presented with severe ketotic fasting hypoglycemia (with low insulin levels) in early childhood. In vivo functional studies, including stable isotope tracers, demonstrated an immediate shift to glyconeogenesis at the onset of fasting, without evidence of glycogenolysis. In the fed state, there was a reduced transition from fatty acid to carbohydrate oxidation. Plasma cholesterol and triglyceride levels were below P0 for age and sex. These results are compatible with observations seen in ACACB knockout mice [10, 11]. Additional studies are required to fully elucidate the disease mechanism.

Clinical Management and Treatment Upon Molecular Diagnosis

For five study participants the possible diagnosis led to changes in disease management with important health implications (see Table 3, P1, P8, P9, P12, P21). This consisted of changes in prescribed drugs or advice on a metabolic diet. For some participants the diagnosis had important implications for family planning and counseling as the condition is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner or, in the case of family 8, being a carrier of a recessive disease allele leads to specific health risks (in this case the associated dyslipidemia).

Standard Metabolic Screening

Beside genetic testing the majority (26/31) of participants received metabolic screening as standard‐of‐care in plasma only (7/26) or in plasma and urine (19/26). Standard‐of‐care metabolic screening includes analysis of plasma amino acids, acylcarnitines and urine organic acids and depending on the clinical presentation and the laboratory also other markers of peroxisomal disorders, purines and pyrimidines, bile acids, sphingolipids, isoelectric focusing of transferrin, sterols, glycosaminoglycans, oligosaccharides and other more specific markers. In 14/26 participants the standard metabolic screening in plasma and/or urine revealed abnormalities. In participant 30 the analysis of cerebrospinal fluid revealed an abnormality. From the 13 participants for whom a diagnosis was made in this study, metabolic abnormalities were found in 7/12 (P12 did not receive metabolic screening). In two cases the detected metabolic abnormality was also suggestive for the genetic defect that was found (P1 and P9), while this was not true for participants 2 and 8. Based on the metabolic findings in urine from participant 2, the differential diagnosis was a mitochondrial disorder, while the actual diagnosis turned out to be myotonic dystrophy (MIM# 602668). Similarly, the suspected diagnosis for participant 8 was Niemann pick type C, which turned out to be inherited dyslipidemic splenomegaly.

Discussion

In this study we performed ES data reanalysis for a cohort of adults suspected of having an IMD following a standardized study design. The primary aim of the study was to find a diagnosis for these adults, and we succeeded in nine participants from six families (25% of the included families). Half of these diagnoses are considered to be an IMD while the other half are inherited neuro(muscular) disorders. The substantial diagnostic yield was facilitated by the chosen study design focused on organizational integration, ES reanalysis and multidisciplinary interpretation of genetic variants. We could distinguish four possible reasons explaining why the disease‐causing variants were identified in the study, but not in the prior diagnostic trajectory: (i) identification of a new disease mechanism, (ii) the use of a novel data analysis method in the study, (iii) a potential new disease gene, and (iv) discussion of the variant list in a multidisciplinary team led to identification of additional clinical features (reverse phenotyping). These four reasons are consistent with the results from previous reanalysis studies, where the main contributors to a higher diagnostic yield were: novel disease‐gene associations, reclassification of variants based on latest genetic or functional evidence, and improved systematic filtering and analysis tools [12, 13, 14, 15].

Pathogenic variants can result in a clinical phenotype based on various underlying mechanisms of disease, for example, haploinsufficiency, dominant negative, or gain/loss of function. For an increasing number of genes multiple modes of inheritance, with variant types resulting in different phenotypes, are recognized. This complicates variant interpretation, as genes can now have multiple genotype–phenotype correlations. For participant 9, we identified a different disease mechanism then previously associated with CAD [9] and in family 10, a previously reported somatic variant in EGFR was detected in germline DNA that is likely‐pathogenic. This exemplifies that careful consideration of strong variants in combination with additional functional testing can lead to the detection of novel pathomechanisms and inheritance patterns, and ultimately result in a diagnosis for the affected individual.

In addition, the use of novel analysis methods (e.g., algorithms to detect STR and CNV variants) led to the discovery of the causative variant in two participants (P2 and P5) highlighting the importance of reanalysis using the continuous improvements of bioinformatic algorithms and analysis pipelines. Technical advancements as well as increasing opportunities for data sharing (genetic matchmaking) also contributed to new disease mechanisms and new disease genes identified in this study. Apart from the focus on ES data reanalysis in this study, it is important to note the sequencing advancements in the field. Recent publications have shown that the use of long‐read genome sequencing (LR‐GS) in diagnostic care could increase the diagnostic yield by 7%–12% [16, 17]. The potential benefit of LR‐GS lies mainly in the detection of SVs, coverage of regions of high sequence similarity, and variant phasing, among others. Another important reason for the relatively high diagnostic yield was not of a technical but of an organizational nature. In a standard diagnostic setting clinicians provide phenotypic information to the molecular geneticist who uses the input for variant assessment and a report is generated that is usually dichotomous (likely pathogenic variant identified yes or no). However, discussion of a list of prioritized variants in a team setting provides the opportunity for reverse phenotyping, looking for signs and symptoms that should be present if the identified variant is causative of the disease. A clear example of this in our study cohort is participant 8. Reverse phenotyping in this case led to accurate interpretation of the detected APOE variants and resulted in a diagnosis.

Other learning points from this study concern the ES analysis approach. Where possible a trio‐ES analysis will be preferred over a singleton as reliable detection of de novo variants and compound heterozygosity often depends on genetic information from both parents. However, in this study most reanalyses (71%) were performed on singleton ES datasets and reflect the practical limitation that in adult genetic care, parental DNA cannot always be obtained to enable trio‐ES analysis. Nevertheless, five participants were successfully diagnosed using singleton ES data, and thus, the absence of material from parents or siblings should not prevent ES analysis.

Another important aspect is related to the use of gene panels. We noticed that 66% (19/29) of the participants received restricted diagnostic genetic testing in the form of specific gene panel analyses (e.g., muscle, epilepsy, metabolic or custom). In ~26% of these participants (5/19) we were able to detect the (possible) causative gene and only in one case had the gene been part of the previously analyzed gene panel. In the latter case, the variant was missed due to technical limitations (no CNV analysis was performed). To select the most fitting gene panel(s) it is essential to have an accurate overview of the individual's phenotype. Certain clinical characteristics, for example, elevated lactate in mitochondrial disorders, may strongly indicate a specific disorder or a group of disorders, thereby guiding the selection of the most appropriate gene panel for ES analysis. On the other hand, many clinical features (standardized in the HPO terms) are shared between gene panels, as we showed by a cosine similarity analysis including six gene panels. A substantial degree of similarity was observed between the IMD, ID, epilepsy and movement disorder gene panels, indicating that the phenotypes associated with the selected genes exhibit substantial overlap, despite the different genes involved. All participants in our study were suspected of having an IMD, however, from the eight (possible) diagnoses only half are considered IMDs. This illustrates the challenge of diagnosing IMDs based solely on clinical presentation, despite clinical signs reliably reflecting an underlying genetic etiology. Based on these findings we recommend to request multiple gene panel analyses, including CNV and STR analyses, to ensure a comprehensive yet targeted assessment of relevant disease genes, prior to proceeding with an open ES analysis. Furthermore, we strongly advise interpreting these tests in a multidisciplinary team setting, to address the increased number of VUS resulting from open ES analysis and, more importantly, to enable reverse phenotyping. In addition to the challenging task to find the most appropriate gene panel, probands may also harbor more than one genetic disorder or show symptoms that are not genetic (nature) but are acquired during life (nurture). Especially in adults it can be challenging to distinguish between nature and nurture derived symptoms as they come to the clinic at an older age. For example, participant 2 presented with polyuria and polydipsia (most likely habitual) which are not part of the myotonic dystrophy phenotype that he was diagnosed with, or participant 5 with unexplained delayed puberty that is not related to his subsequent Becker muscular dystrophy diagnosis. Taken together, our findings highlight the importance of careful phenotypic evaluation, as not all symptoms are necessarily genetic in origin and individual genetic variants may account for only a part of the observed phenotype.

Prior to the reanalysis of ES data, the majority of participants received standard metabolic screening and in half of them metabolic abnormalities were detected. Interestingly, in two cases the metabolic findings pointed towards a different disorder than the actual diagnosis. The metabolic findings in participant 2 included elevated 2‐methyl‐3‐hydroxy‐butyric acid and tiglylglycine, findings that are strongly associated with a mitochondrial disorder. Therefore the primary focus during the diagnostic odyssey was on mitochondrial disease genes, rather than myotonic dystrophy which turned out to be the diagnosis. Intriguingly, recent publications suggest that myotonic dystrophy may indeed lead to secondary mitochondrial defects, potentially explaining the metabolic findings in participant 2 [18, 19]. In another example, standard metabolic screening in plasma revealed elevated lysosphingomyelin in participant 8, which, in combination with the splenomegaly, led to a differential diagnosis of Niemann Pick type C. In this case an additional analysis of the lipid profile would have shown the dyslipidemia and led to a faster diagnosis of inherited dyslipidemic splenomegaly. Therefore, we suggest including a standard lipid profile in all patients with an unexplained ‘storage’ phenotype (hepatosplenomegaly) in addition to the current routine metabolic screening.

Furthermore, we would like to address the importance of a diagnosis for clinical management and counseling. For five participants the (possible) diagnosis directly led to changes in their treatment plan resulting in a lower treatment burden (discontinuation of nightly tube feeding, discontinuation of protein restriction) and/or symptom/health improvements (normalization of liver enzymes, improvement of lipid profiles, see Table 3). In addition, even though in participants 1 and 21 no conclusive diagnosis could be reached, the possible diagnosis already led to changes in management which significantly improved their well‐being. For participant 1 discontinuation of estrogen containing anticonception led to normalization of plasma liver enzyme values and in participant 21 an oral pre‐nighttime feed rich in fat instead of carbohydrates made it possible for the patient to discontinue overnight tube feeding. Besides the benefits for the included participants themselves there can be substantial benefits for the siblings and children of the included adult participants since we identified a relatively high proportion of autosomal dominant conditions in this study, in line with our findings from our previous systematic review on adult IMD diagnostics. These diagnoses have far‐reaching consequences, both for family members as well as reproductive choices of the patients themselves.

Conclusion

The aim of this study was to find a diagnosis for adults suspected of having an IMD by making use of deep phenotyping, genetic analysis and importantly, by interpretation of the results with a multidisciplinary team. In almost a third of this carefully selected adult cohort, a genetic diagnosis could be established, identifying both metabolic disorders as well as other genetic disorders. Our results show that it can be difficult to distinguish IMDs from genetic neurodevelopmental or movement disorders based on the clinical phenotype. Furthermore, we showed that reanalysis of ES data using a renewed analysis pipeline and an improved interpretation approach may already lead to a substantial number of diagnoses without the need of newly generated data. To conclude, we recommend evaluating all strong/suspicious genetic variants in a multidisciplinary meeting, as the cause of the disease may not always be obvious at first glance.
