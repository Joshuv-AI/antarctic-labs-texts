PERL: Pinyin Enhanced Rephrasing Language Model for Chinese ASR N-best Error Correction
Junhong Liang

Abstract
ASR correction methods have predominantly focused on general datasets and have not effectively utilized Pinyin information, unique to the Chinese language. In this study, we address this gap by proposing a P inyin E nhanced R ephrasing L anguage Model (PERL), specifically designed for N-best correction scenarios. Additionally, we implement a length predictor module to address the variable-length problem. We conduct experiments on the Aishell-1 dataset and our newly proposed DoAD dataset. The results show that our approach outperforms baseline methods, achieving a 29.11% reduction in Character Error Rate (CER) on Aishell-1 and around 70% CER reduction on domain-specific datasets. Furthermore, our approach leverages Pinyin similarity at the token level, providing an advantage over baselines and leading to superior performance.

Introduction

Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) technology has widespread applications, including voice interaction and searching. However, although the widely available End-to-End structure of ASR
[
1
]
, the effectiveness of ASR models is often influenced by various factors, such as the number of speakers, accents, and background noise, errors in ASR model outputs can negatively impact downstream tasks, highlighting the importance of employing post-correction modules to enhance the accuracy of ASR outputs.

Pinyin is a Latin-based transcription system for Chinese characters, representing their pronunciation. Pinyin errors are prevalent in daily writing, not only among language learners
[
2
,
3
]
but also among native speakers
[
4
]
, making the integration of Pinyin information crucial for distinguishing characters with identical or similar pronunciations. Recent advancements in Chinese Spelling Correction (CSC) research indicate the potential of adapting text correction tools to address possible Chinese spelling errors in ASR and OCR outputs. Several methods have been proposed to effectively incorporate phonetic information, such as selectively integrating different embeddings of Chinese characters
[
5
]
, predicting Pinyin tokens
[
6
]
, and applying Pinyin integration methods in grammar error correction tasks
[
7
]
. Meanwhile, large language models (LLMs) have been widely utilized in various generative tasks, demonstrating robustness in CSC across different datasets
[
8
]
. However, the decoder-only structure of LLMs cannot maintain fixed output lengths and may introduce unwanted errors
[
9
]
. To address these limitations, methods such as training models at the character level to meet length and Pinyin constraints
[
10
]
or using LLMs as next-token predictors with length reward mechanisms
[
11
]
have been proposed. In this study, we leverage the advantages of the Encoder-Decoder model in the CSC task, incorporating a Pinyin encoder to address frequent Pinyin errors and extend its application to ASR N-best error correction tasks.

Most ASR errors are phonetic
[
12
]
, and
[
13
]
highlights that the Pinyin error rate in ASR outputs is lower than the character error rate. Utilizing Pinyin information in ASR systems could also enhance the overall performance
[
14
]
. Previous methods use Pinyin tokens as a source to detect variable length errors
[
15
]
. Recent research advancements explore the potential of LLMs, leveraging their text understanding ability to assist in error correction in a generative style, combined with phonetic information in the pre-training process
[
16
]
or during the instructed fine-tuning
[
17
]
.

Previous research has focused on variable-length error correction in generative tasks such as grammatical error correction, where autoregressive models are employed to address unequal-length corrections
[
18
,
19
]
. However, variable-length error correction in tasks like CSC and ASR correction encounter the “hard-constraint” problem, where most tokens in the input sequence remain unchanged
[
5
]
.

In the field of OCR correction, several studies have identified issues related to token splitting in English
[
20
]
, Chinese
[
21
]
, and mixed English-Chinese texts
[
22
]
. Similarly, ASR outputs exhibit variable lengths, making error detection crucial. Prior research has proposed improved alignment algorithms for phonetically similar tokens, often incorporating duration predictors to estimate token lengths
[
23
,
24
]
. Additionally, some approaches have employed fine-tuned T5 models with constrained decoding spaces to manage output length
[
25
]
effectively. Furthermore, selection modules have been introduced to balance between the 1-best result and corrections generated by large language models (LLMs)
[
26
]
. Our research addresses the variable-length challenge using a pre-trained length predictor that leverages the extensive knowledge embedded in pre-trained language models, such as BERT, to determine the actual sequence length based on N-best inputs.

Method

2.1
Pretrain a Pinyin encoder

A Pinyin encoder is designed to represent information about Pinyin. The Chinese pinyin system represents the Pinyin information and contains rich information about the language itself.

Each input Chinese sentence will be translated to Chinese Pinyin using pypinyin
1
1

1

https://pypi.org/project/pypinyin/
. Suppose the character of sentence containing
n
characters is
x=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})
and therefore the pinyin token for
i_{th}
character is
p_{i,1},p_{i,2},...,p_{i,l_{i}}
, where
l_{i}
represents the length of Pinyin of
x_{i}
. The Pinyin encoder contains four parts.

•

Pinyin Embedding: Each Pinyin token
p_{i,j}
will firstly be embedded into a vector
\mathbf{p}_{i,j}
.

•

Sequential Embedding: A Uni-directional GRU network captures the sequential embedding. Therefore the encoding of
\mathbf{p}_{i,j}
is

\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{i,j}=\text{GRU}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{i,j-1},\mathbf{p}_{i,j})
.
After that, only the last hidden state of the last Pinyin note of each sentence is extracted. Therefore, the Pinyin embedding after sequential embedding is

\mathbf{P_{0}}=(\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{1,l_{1}},\hat{\mathbf{p}_{2,l_{2}}},...,\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{n,l_{n}})
.

•

Global Attention Embedding: Transformer layers will be employed to construct the global information, denote
L_{p}
as the number of transformer layers; therefore,

\mathbf{P_{l}}=\text{Transformer}(\mathbf{P_{l-1}}),l\in[1,L_{p}]

•

Character Prediction: The embedding
\mathbf{P}_{L_{p}}
will then be used to predict the exact Chinese token of each position.

\hat{y}_{i}=\text{Softmax}(\mathbf{W}^{p}\mathbf{p}_{L_{p},i}+\mathbf{b}^{p}),\mathbf{p}_{L,i}\in\mathbf{P}_{L}

where
\mathbf{W}^{p}
and
\mathbf{b}^{p}
are learnable parameters.

Then cross-entropy loss is employed to calculate Pinyin loss
\mathcal{L}_{p}
during pre-training.

\mathcal{L}_{p}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\log(\hat{y}_{i})

where
y_{i}
is the ground truth of
i_{th}
token.

2.2
Pretrain a length predictor

A length prediction module is necessary to tackle the variable length issue in ASR N-best Correction. First, we obtain the N-best sentences. Let these sentences be denoted as
S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{n}
, where
n
is the total number of sentences in the N-best list. These sentences are then concatenated using the delimiter
|
to form a single concatenated sentence, denoted as:

S_{\text{concat}}=S_{1}\ |\ S_{2}\ |\ \dots\ |\ S_{n}
.
The label is the length of the gold truth sentence.
Then we use a BERT model to obtain the semantic embedding
\mathbf{E}_{s}
of
S_{\text{concat}}

After that, a linear projector is applied to give the output length.

\hat{l}=\text{MLP}(\mathbf{E}_{s})
.
Similarly, a cross-entropy loss is applied in this process.

2.3
Pinyin Enhanced Rephrasing Language Model

Our model can be divided into two stages: i) input processing and 2) encoding and predicting.

Input processing
We obtain the N-best sentences. Let these sentences be denoted as
S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{n}
, where
n
is the total number of sentences in the N-best list. These sentences are then concatenated using the delimiter
|
to form a single concatenated sentence, denoted as

S_{\text{concat}}=S_{1}\ |\ S_{2}\ |\ \dots\ |\ S_{n}
.

The predicted length
\hat{l}
of the output sentence is obtained by the length prediction module mentioned in Section
2.2

After that dynamic masking strategy
[
27
]
is employed by randomly setting some tokens as
[M]
, obtaining
S_{\text{masked}}

Encoding and predicting
The concatenated sentence is processed through Pinyin encoder
\text{Enc}_{s}
and semantic encoder
\text{Enc}_{p}
.

\mathbf{E}_{s}=\text{Enc}_{s}(S_{\text{masked}})
and
\mathbf{E}_{p}=\text{Enc}_{p}(S_{\text{masked}})
.

We obtain the sentence embedding by averaging
\mathbf{E}_{s}

\bar{\mathbf{e}}_{s}=\frac{1}{l_{max}}\sum_{i=1}^{l_{max}}\mathbf{E}_{s}[i]

(1)

where
l_{max}
is the maximum length of the sentence and
\mathbf{E}_{s}[i]
is the
i_{th}
token embedding of the sentence, and the weight of semantic feature and Pinyin feature for each token is calculated by

\displaystyle g_{s,i}

\displaystyle=\text{MLP}_{s}(\mathbf{E}_{s}[i],\mathbf{E}_{p}[i],\bar{\mathbf{e}}_{s})

\displaystyle g_{p,i}

\displaystyle=\text{MLP}_{p}(\mathbf{E}_{s}[i],\mathbf{E}_{p}[i],\bar{\mathbf{e}}_{s})

Where
g_{s,i}
and
g_{p,i}
are the weights of the semantic feature and Pinyin feature for the
i_{th}
token, respectively.

Then, the fused embedding is obtained by

\mathbf{E}_{f}=\mathbf{g}_{s}\odot\mathbf{E}_{s}+\mathbf{g}_{p}\odot\mathbf{E}_{p}

Finally, the masked token is predicted using BERTForMaskedLM. Cross-entropy loss is applied in this process to obtain the final loss. Figure
1
shows an overview of our model.

4.2
Pinyin Similarity

To assess the importance of the Pinyin encoder in our model, we evaluate the embedding similarity of tokens with similar pronunciations, as shown in Figure
2
. Using embeddings from BERT and our pre-trained Pinyin encoder (Section
2.1
), we compare pairs of characters whose Pinyin (including tones) have an edit distance of less than 2. The Pinyin encoder achieves an average similarity score of 0.77, significantly higher than BERT’s 0.42. This improvement stems from the Pinyin encoder’s use of pinyin tokens for each character, effectively grouping characters with similar pronunciations into the same phonetic space.

Discussion & Conclusion

This paper proposes a Pinyin-enhanced rephrasing language model to address the N-best ASR error correction problem. Our method effectively mitigates the impact of Pinyin errors seen in previous approaches. Additionally, we introduce a length prediction module to handle length inconsistencies in error correction datasets. We also created a domain-specific ASR dataset to extend research in this area. Extensive experiments demonstrate the efficiency of our proposed modules. Notably, this work shows that N-best ASR errors can be corrected using text-based correction methods, bridging different research domains. Future work could explore a more profound integration of Pinyin information into the model.
