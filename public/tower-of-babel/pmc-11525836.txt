Applicability of attribution methods for identifying runoff changes in changing environments
Authors: Tingting Huang, Yu Liu, Zhifeng Jia, Jie Zou, Peiqing Xiao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnder the dual impacts of global climate change and human activities, how to accurately and quantitatively assess and identify changes in watershed runoff is crucial to the rational development and utilization of water resources. Different methods of identifying runoff change attribution are applicable to different environments. In this study, we used five different methods, including the double mass curve (DMC), the soil and water assessment tool (SWAT), and the three Budyko hypotheses based on Fu, Choudhury‒Yang, and Milly‒Zhang formulas to explore the impact of human activities and climate change on runoff change in the Jinghe River basin (JRB). The results show that annual runoff of the JRB decreased considerably, and human activities accounted for more than 90%. Specifically, the results of the SWAT model and the three Budyko hypotheses are highly consistent except for the DMC method, which is not recommended in basins with significant changes in factors other than precipitation. More importantly, the Budyko hypothesis based on the Milly‒Zhang formula uses the vegetation water utilization coefficient to consider the impact of vegetation change on runoff, which is most consistent with the simulation results of the SWAT model and is recommended for the attribution analysis of changes in the Loess Plateau represented by the JRB.Keywords: Changing environment, Runoff, Quantitative identification, Land use and cover change, Jinghe River basinSubject terms: Climate change, Hydrology

Introduction

Due to global climate change, the hydrological cycle processes in the basin will change to some extent, which poses a challenge for water resources development and utilization1–3. The direct human activity (e.g., water withdrawal and water resource management) and indirect human activity (e.g., agricultural practices and underlying surface variations) can affect runoff generation and confluence in river basins, leading to changes in river discharge4,5. Under the combined influences of climate change and human activity, the effect of a changing environment on hydrology and water resources in a river basin has become a key research topic, especially in the arid and water-deficient Loess Plateau of China6. However, the hydrological cycle has complex nonlinear, abrupt, and random characteristics7. Thus, determining suitable methods to quantitatively identify and analyze the attributions of runoff change in a changing environment has become a research hotspot to understand the hydrological cycle and water resource changes in river basins8,9. To address this problem, researchers have proposed different quantitative methods to identify the attribution of changes in runoff.

Attribution identification methods of watershed runoff changes usually categorized as empirical relationship, semi-empirical formulae, and hydrological model methods10. For instance, empirical relation method establishes a statistical relationship between precipitation and runoff for a baseline period, uses this relationship to restore the runoff during the variation period, and compares it with measured runoff data to obtain the contribution of human activity to changes in runoff11. The semi-empirical formula method uses hydrological and meteorological theories to establish a relationship with a specific physical basis, inputs different variables under a changing environment, and estimates the influence of climatic factors and human activity on changes in runoff12. Hydrological model method can reasonably reflect the physical processes in the hydrological cycle within the river basin and estimates the contributions of meteorological factors and human activity to changes in runoff by changing the input conditions13.

The empirical relationship method, such as double mass curve (DMC) method, it is widely used because of it require only a time series of precipitation and streamflow data, however, it assumes a linear relationship between precipitation and discharge, which is not applicable in all situations. The hydrological model method can identify the attribution of changes through runoff restoration, and the calculation results are relatively reliable, however, it requires high data accuracy and involves complicated operations14. In contrast, the semi-empirical formula method can better reflect basic runoff variation mechanisms and provide a better solution for the attribution identification of runoff changes15,16. Nonetheless, there is a slight drawback in that these formulas usually do not consider climatic zoning or spatial heterogeneity of land cover, thus limiting their applicability in different landscapes.

In recent years, there has been an improvement in global vegetation cover17. Vegetation can alter river runoff by changing hydrological processes18,19. For example, vegetation absorbs water from the soil and releases it back to the atmosphere through transpiration, vegetation recharges more surface water to groundwater through infiltration. However, the empirical relation and semi-empirical formula methods only consider few changes in vegetation cover. In contrast, the hydrological model methods can consider the impact of vegetation changes on runoff changes to a certain extent by constructing land use and cover change (LUCC) databases in different periods20. In addition, the Zhang formula in Budyko hypothesis can achieve this purpose by using hydrometeorological and LUCC data21. Thus, suitable methods are necessary to consider the impacts of dominant vegetation cover changes in the quantitative identification of runoff change attribution.

In this study, we selected the Jinghe River basin (JRB) as the study area, which located on the Loess Plateau and has a considerably degrading ecological environment in recent years. We first systematically analyzed the meteorological, hydrological, and subsurface elements of the JRB, and then used the DMC, soil and water assessment tool (SWAT) model, and three Budyko hypotheses to quantify the impacts of climate change and human activities on the runoff evolution, as well as the heterogeneity and applicability of various attribution identification methods. The applicability and accuracy of runoff attribution methods based on the different Budyko hypotheses are discussed. Our findings can be used to guide the selection of methods for quantitative identification of runoff change attribution in changing environments and advance the current understanding of the mechanisms governing hydrological changes in the Loess Plateau represented by the JRB.

Quantitative identification of runoff change attribution

In this study, the study period was divided into a baseline period and a variation period based on the year of runoff mutation. The empirical relation method, the semi-empirical formula method, and the hydrological model method are respectively used to quantitatively calculate the relative contribution of climate change and human activities to the runoff change, and the quantitative identification effect of different methods is studied. In this study, the widely used DMC, Budyko hypothesis, and SWAT model simulation were selected as the representatives of the above three methods.

DMC was proposed by Merriam in 1937 to analyse the consistency of precipitation data23, and then it was applied to hydrometeorological data verification, mutation test, human activities on runoff and other aspects by Searcy et al.24. The DMC methodology is mainly divided into four main steps:

(1) calculates the cumulative values of precipitation and runoff, and constructs the mass curve for these two variables; (2) tests whether the slope of the curve changes abruptly; (3) calculates the amount of runoff change after the abrupt year; and (4) analyses the influence of meteorological factors and human activities on the runoff change25,26.

Study area

The Jinghe River originates east of Liupan Mountain in Jingyuan County, Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, China. It is the largest tributary of the Weihe River and a secondary tributary of the Yellow River. The JRB (34°46’–37°19’ N, 106°14’–108°42’ E; Fig. 2a and b) is located in the high-intensity soil and water erosion area of the Loess Plateau, which has a drainage area of 45,421 km2 and a total river channel length of 455.1 km (Fig. 2c). The soil in the basin is dominated by primitive soil, accounting for approximately 79.95% of the basin area (Fig. 2d). The JRB has a temperate continental climate, with characteristics of a single period of rain and heat, as well as distinct seasons. The annual mean precipitation in the basin ranges from 339.6 mm to 590.7 mm and gradually decreases from south to north (Fig. 2e). The annual mean potential evapotranspiration (ET0) ranges from 869.9 mm to 1060.6 mm and gradually increases from southwest to northeast (Fig. 2f).

Data collection

Daily meteorological data from 14 national meteorological stations in and around the JRB from 1951 to 2018 were obtained from the China Meteorological Data Network (http://data.cma.cn). The locations of the meteorological stations are shown in Fig. 2c. To address the missing meteorological data, we based this study on the standard sequence method33 and selected the stations with the best correlations for the data interpolation. In addition, the Penman‒Monteith formula recommended by the World Food and Agriculture Organization was used to calculate the ET0 at each station34.

The runoff data used herein were the monthly runoff amounts from 1951 to 2018 measured at the Zhangjiashan hydrological of JRB control stations, and were obtained from the Yellow River Water Conservancy Commission.

The digital elevation model (DEM) data with a 30 m × 30 m resolution in the JRB were obtained from the Geospatial Data Cloud (http://www.gscloud.cn). LUCC and soil type data were obtained from the Data Center for Resources and Environmental Sciences at the Chinese Academy of Sciences (http://www.resdc.cn). The Krasovsky_1940_Albers projection coordinates were used for these data.

Results

Characteristics of annual runoff changes

Trend analysis of runoff changes

From 1951 to 2018, the runoff depth in the JRB decreased at a rate of 0.41 mm/y. The maximum and minimum annual runoff depths were 90.44 mm (1964) and 14.62 mm (2015), respectively, and the extreme ratio was 6.19 (Fig. 3). The multi-year average runoff depth in the JRB was 35.23 mm, which was lower than the mean prior to the 1990s, and higher than the mean after the 1990s. The 10-y average runoff depth in the JRB was the highest (50.79 mm) in the 1960s and the lowest in the 2000s (22.30 mm). Overall, the multi-year average runoff depth from 1990 to 2018 was 26.18 mm, which was 37.58% lower than the multi-year average runoff depth from 1951 to 1989. In contrast, the multi-year mean precipitation decreased by only 2.35%, indicating that human activity likely had a larger impact on runoff changes in the JRB.

Variations in runoff depth in the Jinghe River basin: (a) annual runoff depths and (b) multi-year runoff depths.

Annual runoff mutation test

The M‒K test, moving t-test, and DMC were used to identify the abrupt years of annual runoff depth at JRB (Fig. 4). The M‒K test results indicate that the annual runoff depth changed abruptly in 1996. According to the moving t-test with a step size of five, three abrupt changes were observed (0.01 significance level) in the annual runoff depth: 1996, 1997, and 2009. The DMC results indicate that abrupt changes in the annual runoff depth occurred in 1996, and the R2 of the segments were all higher than 0.99. These results revealed that the annual runoff depth in the JRB changed abruptly in 1996 at the 0.01 significance level.

Results of the annual runoff depth change test using different methods: (a) Mann‒Kendall test, (b) moving t-test, and (c) double mass curve.

Before 1996, due to factors such as population and economy, the impact of human activities on JRB runoff was relatively weak. However, after 1999, the “Green for Grain” project was implemented in most parts of the JRB. The area of cultivated land in the JRB decreased and the area of forested land increased, with a consequent increase in the vegetation water use coefficient. In recent years, with the continuous increase in population and irrigation area of the combined circular economy zone, the supply of surface water has also increased from 249 million m3 in 1990 to 402 million m3 in 2015, which is increased 61.45%. Considering the runoff variations in the JRB and impact of human activity on runoff, we set the baseline period as 1951‒1996, accordingly, the runoff variation period was set as 1997‒2018.

In general, the calculation error of the Choudhury‒Yang formula (0.19%) is smaller than that of the Fu formula (0.69%), which can be attributed to the fact that the Yang formula is based on data from the Yellow River basin and it has strong applicability in JRB. SWAT model

In order to calibrate and validate the applicability of the SWAT model in JRB, we divided the baseline period into three stages: the warm-up period (1951–1960), the calibration period (1961–1980), and the validation period (1981–1996). The R2, NS, and RE values of JRB’s observed annual runoff depth and simulated annual runoff depth during the validation period were 0.90, 0.89, and 3.33%, respectively, and 0.73, 0.56, and −7.68% during the validation period (Fig. 7a and b). The results of each evaluation index confirmed that the SWAT model can accurately simulate the annual runoff change in the JRB and has good applicability in this basin.

Without changing the calibration parameters of the SWAT model, natural runoff during the variation period was restored using meteorological factors from 1997 to 2018 as input variables (Fig. 7c). The observed multi-year mean runoff flow rate during the variation period decreased by 25.16 m³/s, and the contribution rates of climate change and human activity to the runoff changes were 9.65% and 90.35%, respectively (Table 6). This result is most similar to that obtained from the Milly‒Zhang formula, with a difference of only 0.08%. In summary, based on the results of the five attribution methods, human activities are the primary driver of the reduction in runoff from the JRB.

Discussion

Comparative analysis of results from different attribution methods

The analysis results of the five methods show that the results of the five methods are basically consistent, with human activities accounting for more than 90% of the impact on runoff changes and climate change having a relatively mild impact. Among them, the results based on the three Budyko hypotheses are basically consistent with the results of the SWAT model, but it is relatively larger in the DMC results. In the three Budyko hypotheses, the Milly formula and Zhang formula are combined to consider the influence of vegetation cover change on runoff change to some extent, and the result has only a 0.08% error compared with the SWAT model. This result indicates that the Milly‒Zhang formula has higher accuracy in quantitatively identifying the attribution of runoff change, and it also recommended for the attribution analysis of runoff change in the Loess Plateau represented by the JRB. According to the difference in the attribution results of runoff change obtained by the Fu, Choudhury‒Yang, and Milly‒Zhang formulas, it can be inferred that the change in land planting structure has minimal effect on runoff change. Therefore, soil and water conservation measures such as terraces and sand dams in JRB are the main human activities that affect their runoff changes.

Among these five methods, the anthropogenic contribution to runoff estimated by the DMC method was the largest at 105.76%. This is mainly because the DMC method only considers the contribution of precipitation to the runoff change while ignoring the influence of other climatic factors. Therefore, the DMC method may overestimate the impact of human activities on runoff. This study showed that the SWAT model is suitable for runoff simulation in the JRB. The model can quantitatively identify the contribution rates of climatic factors (e.g., precipitation and temperature) and underlying surface elements (e.g., LUCC and reservoir regulation) to runoff changes in more detail35.However, hydrological models require more detailed data inputs. To ensure the accuracy of a hydrological simulation, using the hydrological model method to quantitatively identify the attribution of runoff changes can achieve good results36. However, SWAT models require precise data inputs, including high-resolution climate data, soil properties, and land use information. In addition, there is a risk of uncertainty in the model outputs due to the sensitivity of the model parameters. These uncertainties may affect the reliability of the results. The Budyko hypothesis could completely utilize hydrometeorological data and has a better identification effect for the attribution of runoff changes37–39. It can fully utilize hydrological and meteorological data, considering the vegetation water use coefficient in the Milly‒Zhang formula. Accordingly, it has been effective in identifying the attribution of runoff changes. It is worth noting that the Budyko assumption is less effective when applied to smaller watershed management.

The main human activities that affect runoff in JRB

All the three methods (empirical formula, semi-empirical formula, and hydrological model) considered in this study indicate that human activity is the main driving factor for runoff changes in the JRB. The mean annual water consumption in the JRB in Shaanxi Province is 476 million m3. The annual mean agricultural water consumption is 387 million m3, which accounts for more than 80% of the total water consumption. Both agricultural and industrial water consumption have increased recently (Fig. 8a). The surface water supply in the JRB is 270 million m3, accounting for 56.72% of the total water consumption, and 65.83% of the surface water is supplied in the form of diverted water (Fig. 8b). Thus, agricultural irrigation is the largest source of water use in the JRB, and agricultural irrigation water is mainly obtained from surface water. This shows that the increase in agricultural irrigation water use has the most direct impact on the decrease in river runoff40.

Statistics of human activity in the Jinghe River basin: (a) water consumption, (b) surface water supply, and (c) terraces and silt arresters.

The construction of terraces and silt arresters in the JRB began comparatively late. By the end of 1970, the terraced area covered 214 km2 and four large and medium-sized silt arresters were installed. Recently, terraces and silt arresters have become key measures for comprehensively controlling water and soil losses in the JRB41. By the end of 2015, the terraced area covered 8,161 km2, and 633 large and medium-sized silt arresters were installed in the JRB. In the 1990s, the terraced area increased substantially (to 4,456 km2), accounting for 54.60% of the total area. Furthermore, in the 2000s, construction of large and medium-sized silt arresters occurred rapidly. During this period, 443 silt arresters were built, accounting for 69.98% of the total (Fig. 8c). Therefore, increases in the terraced area and number of silt arresters changed the underlying surface conditions in the basin, thereby affecting the runoff yield and concentration and playing a decisive role in driving the changes in the watershed hydrological cycle42.

The response of runoff to vegetation

Since 1999, the “Grain for Green” project has been actively implemented in the JRB, resulting in considerable improvement in vegetation cover43. According to an analysis of the NDVI in the JRB from 1998 to 2018, the vegetation cover in the basin has improved substantially, with 98.67% of the region exhibiting an increasing trend of NDVI and 84.09% exhibiting a very significant increasing trend. Only 0.39% of the region, which is concentrated in the lower reaches of the basin, exhibited a very significant decrease in NDVI (Fig. 9). The increase in vegetation cover changed the depression detention, infiltration, and soil water storage capacity in the runoff generation process and surface roughness coefficients, surface water storage, and river convergence paths in the runoff concentration process. Thus, changes in these hydrological cycle processes lead to increases in canopy interception and conversion of the proportion of precipitation into evapotranspiration. However, the empirical formula, semi-empirical formula, and hydrological model methods use first-order approximation calculations when considering the contributions of climate change and human activity to runoff changes. Therefore, the impact of vegetation changes on runoff is attributed to human activity and, to a certain extent, leads to a larger contribution of human activity to the identification attribution of runoff changes44. Currently, few studies have addressed the mechanism by which changes in vegetation cover drive changes in hydrological processes; thus, this topic requires further investigation in the future.

Conclusions

In this study, we analyzed the hydrological, meteorological, and vegetation cover change characteristics in the JRB and used multiple methods to quantify the effects of climate change and human activity on changes in runoff. The main conclusions are as follows: (1) the annual precipitation, ET0, and temperature in the JRB increased, whereas the sunshine duration, wind speed, and relative humidity decreased. The most obvious changes in LUCC in the JRB were the decrease in farmland and increase in forest land areas. In addition, the annual runoff depth decreased considerably at a rate of 0.41 mm/y. (2) The attribution analyses of the DMC method, the SWAT model, and the three Budyko hypotheses based on the Fu, Choudhury‒Yang, and Milly‒Zhang formulas revealed that human activities were the predominant factor driving changes in runoff, contributing 105.76%, 90.35%, 93.84%, 93.05%, and 90.41%, respectively. (3) Between the three Budyko hypotheses, the Budyko hypothesis based on the Milly‒Zhang formula uses the vegetation water utilization coefficient to consider the impact of vegetation change on runoff, which is most consistent with the simulation results of the SWAT model.

The DMC method estimated the largest relative contribution of human activities, which is due to his consideration of the effect of precipitation on runoff only, while ignoring the effect of other climate elements on runoff, resulting in a low relative contribution of climate to runoff. The hydrologic model is more applicable in this paper and can be used to study the effects of climate and human activities on hydrologic processes in the watershed with more adequate input data. In this study, the Budyko hypothesis based on Milly‒Zhang formula was simulated best.

Our findings can facilitate the selection of methods for quantitative identification of river runoff change attribution in changing environments at basin scale. Moreover, our findings can advance the current understanding of the mechanisms governing hydrological changes in the JRB and other regions with similar environmental conditions. However, we only considered the impact of LUCC change when quantitatively identifying the attribution of runoff change. In future studies, the impact of vegetation changes on runoff should be further studied in combination with more vegetation indices.
