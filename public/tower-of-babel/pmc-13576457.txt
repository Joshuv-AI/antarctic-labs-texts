Sustainable biocatalysis via lipase immobilization: pyrolyzed lignocellulosic bamboo supports in batch and continuous flow systems
Authors: Giorgia Albonetti, Gabrielle Alves Ribeiro da Silva, Caio M. Pacheco, Matheus Candido da Silva, Alessandro Pellis, Alessandro Massi, Omar G. Pandoli, Rodrigo O. M. A. de Souza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bamboo-derived biochars obtained via fast and slow pyrolysis conditions were used as supports for the immobilization of Candida antarctica lipase B (CALB) through physical adsorption. The FP- and SP-based materials were then evaluated in esterification and hydrolysis reactions under both batch and continuous-flow conditions. In all cases, high immobilization yields were obtained, although the catalytic performance of FP-derived systems was consistently higher. In batch esterification of long-chain fatty acids, FP-CALB achieved high conversions, reaching up to 97% under optimized conditions (50 °C, fatty acid/ethanol molar ratio 1 : 1.5). The system also showed good reproducibility over four consecutive cycles. Only a limited decrease in activity was observed after several reuses, while overall stability remained high. The catalytic system was also tested on different substrates, including saturated and unsaturated fatty acids as well as industrial mixtures, giving high conversions throughout. Under continuous-flow operation, FP-CALB maintained high activity even at short residence times. Altogether, these observations indicate a strong effect of the pyrolysis conditions on the properties of the carbon supports, with fast pyrolysis biochars providing the most effective systems for repeated use.

Introduction

Carbon materials obtained from lignocellulosic biomass are increasingly considered as alternatives to conventional fossil-based supports in heterogeneous catalysis and biocatalysis. The reason is not limited to sustainability claims. When lignocellulosic precursors are subjected to thermochemical treatments such as pyrolysis, their internal organization is altered in ways that depend strongly on how the treatment is carried out.1,2 Variations in temperature profile or residence time can already shift the balance among devolatilization, carbon rearrangement, and pore formation. As a result, the carbon obtained after pyrolysis does not entirely lose the structural imprint of the original plant. Elements of the initial hierarchical organization can still be detected in the final material, even though the matrix has undergone substantial chemical and thermal transformation.3,4 This continuity between precursor and carbonized product contributes significantly to the interest in biomass-derived carbons for catalytic applications.5–7

In biocatalysis, immobilization is usually introduced for practical rather than theoretical reasons. Recovering the enzyme after the reaction, using it again over several cycles, or adapting the system to continuous-flow operation are considerations that tend to shape the process design from the beginning.8–10 Once experiments move past the exploratory stage, aspects such as long-term stability and mechanical robustness become difficult to ignore. Under these conditions, physical adsorption is often preferred, not because it is sophisticated, but because it is straightforward and requires no additional chemical functionalization of either the enzyme or the support. At the same time, this apparent simplicity means that the outcome depends heavily on the surface onto which the enzyme is deposited.3,11

Lipases are well suited to immobilized configurations. They tolerate reaction media that would inactivate many other enzymes and can catalyze a broad spectrum of transformations, including esterification, transesterification, and kinetic resolution.12,13Candida antarctica lipase B (CALB) has therefore become a commonly adopted reference system. Its reputation rests on a combination of high selectivity and the ability to remain active in organic solvents and at moderately elevated temperatures.2,14 After immobilization, however, the situation becomes more complex. The observed activity reflects not only the intrinsic properties of CALB but also its interactions with the surrounding solid matrix.

A wide variety of lignocellulosic feedstocks has been considered for the preparation of carbon supports, ranging from agricultural by-products to woody materials and nutshells.15,16 Bamboo occupies a somewhat particular position within this group.17 It grows rapidly, is available in large quantities, and exhibits a vascular organization characterized by hierarchical porosity.18,19 In the natural bamboo, its structural arrangement supports fluid transport over multiple length scales.7,20–24 Following pyrolysis, elements of the original organization may still influence the architecture of the carbonized material, particularly regarding pore connectivity and distribution.17

Pyrolysis, however, does not produce a single, clearly defined material that can be described independently of the processing conditions. Its outcome depends strongly on how heat is delivered and maintained over time.

Surface chemistry, hydrophobicity, and pore architecture influence how CALB is arranged at the interface and the conditions for substrate diffusion within the porous structure.12,25 This interplay largely explains the continued interest in carbon-based materials. Their surfaces can be modified by thermal treatment, and their hydrophobic character is often associated with stabilization of catalytically competent lipase conformations.26,27

Heating rate, final temperature, and residence time determine the extent of carbonization, shape the development of porosity, and affect the nature and distribution of oxygen-containing surface groups.2,14,28 Rapid heating conditions tend to produce more open and accessible pore systems, whereas slower treatments may favor further structural rearrangement and partial densification of the carbon matrix.29 These variations are expected to influence enzyme immobilization and catalytic behavior, although explicit correlations between pyrolysis parameters and biocatalytic performance remain relatively scarce.

This work is a continuation of our previous study of limmobilization of lipases on lignocellulosic bamboo powder for biocatalytic transformation in batch and continuous flow,30 here in a bamboo-derived biochars produced under controlled pyrolysis conditions were used as supports for CALB immobilization via physical adsorption. The study focuses on how differences in the thermal history of the precursor translate into measurable changes in surface characteristics, enzyme loading, and catalytic response.

Results and discussion

Bamboo powder was used as a precursor to prepare the carbon supports used in this study. The material was subjected to two distinct pyrolysis regimes to obtain biochars with different structural characteristics. Fast pyrolysis was conducted in a mixed N2/CO2 atmosphere, with final temperatures ranging from 300 to 800 °C.31 Samples obtained at 300, 400, 500, 600, 700, and 800 °C are indicated as FP-X, where X corresponds to the final treatment temperature. In parallel, a separate series of biochars was produced by slow pyrolysis under a N2 atmosphere.32 In this case, the temperature window was extended up to 1000 °C (300–1000 °C), and the resulting materials are referred to as SP-X throughout the manuscript.

CALB immobilization onto the carbon supports was carried out via physical adsorption at room temperature. Experimental details are provided in the SI. In most experiments, enzyme adsorption proceeded efficiently. However, when the immobilization procedure was repeated on independently prepared batches, minor fluctuations in the measured yields were detected, even though the experimental conditions were nominally the same. Such variability is not unexpected for adsorption-based immobilization on heterogeneous carbon supports, where small differences in surface hydration state or local chemical composition may affect protein uptake. A complete overview of all immobilization experiments, including replicate runs for both FP and SP supports, is provided in the SI (Tables S1 and S2).

Overall, these results highlight the potential of bamboo-derived biochars produced under both fast and slow pyrolysis conditions as promising supports for CALB immobilization. However, a high immobilization yield does not inherently translate into superior catalytic performance. To further assess the practical relevance of these materials, the catalytic activities of the resulting FP-CALB and SP-CALB systems were subsequently evaluated and directly compared in esterification and hydrolytic reactions under identical conditions, as presented in the following section.

Esterification of long-chain fatty acids was selected as the primary benchmark reaction, as it represents the main target application of the immobilised biocatalyst, while hydrolytic activity toward a complex triglyceride substrate was investigated to verify whether the observed trends were reaction-specific or reflected a more general difference between the two classes of carbon supports.

The esterification activity of the FP-CALB and SP-CALB materials was first evaluated in batch mode using palmitic acid and ethanol as model substrates under optimized reaction conditions (50 °C, fatty acid/ethanol molar ratio of 1 : 1.5, and heptane as solvent). Control assays carried out with enzyme-free FP and SP biochars under the same conditions showed no detectable conversion, demonstrating that the observed esterification activity originated exclusively from immobilized CALB. As shown in Table 2, FP-supported biocatalysts consistently achieved higher esterification conversions than the corresponding SP-supported materials across all biochars investigated, regardless of final pyrolysis temperature. Part of this behavior was anticipated, as different supports retained different amounts of enzyme, particularly when FP and SP materials were compared. However, the catalytic differences cannot be attributed solely to variations in enzyme loading. This is clearly illustrated by FP-800 and SP-800, which displayed similar immobilization profiles but markedly different esterification conversions. Such a result indicates that pyrolytic conditions affect not only the extent of enzyme adsorption, but also the nature of the biochar surface and the microenvironment surrounding the immobilized enzyme, ultimately impacting catalytic performance. A previous full chemical characterisation by Raman Spectroscopy (RS), electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and X-ray photoemission spectroscopy (XPS) analysis denotes the different chemical evolution of the lignocellulose biomass depending on inert (N2) and oxidant (N2/CO2) pyrolysis atmospheres.31,32

To clarify the structural and surface basis of the performance gap between the two families of supports, a representative subset of biochars was examined before and after immobilization by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and by thermogravimetric analysis (TG/DTG). Four supports were chosen: FP-400, FP-800, SP-400, and SP-900. These cover both pyrolysis regimes at the lower and upper ends of the temperature range applied to each series, and therefore span the structural extremes of the present work, from the less carbonized, oxygen-richer chars obtained at 400 °C to the more graphitic, oxygen-poorer materials produced at 800–900 °C. Full micrographs and datasets are provided in the SI.

By SEM, the free supports differed mainly in surface texture: smooth, massive fragments at 400 °C and rougher, more fragmented or glassy surfaces at higher temperature. After immobilization, all four supports carried surface deposits that their free counterparts lacked, from a continuous coating on FP-400 and a thick layer on FP-800 to sparser globular features on SP-400 and SP-900. EDS detected sodium and phosphorus on the immobilized samples that were essentially absent from the free supports. Because these elements come from the sodium phosphate buffer that accompanies the enzyme, they mark the presence and rough extent of a deposit rather than the protein itself. Read this way, FP-800 retained the most surface material and SP-900 the least, with FP-400 and SP-400 in between. The amount of deposit did not track the surface oxygen content, which argues against a simple oxygen-driven adsorption and points instead to a broader influence of the pyrolysis route on the support surface.

Thermogravimetry was consistent with this picture. Under nitrogen, the well-carbonized FP-800 and SP-900 supports were almost inert up to 800 °C, whereas the 400 °C chars still lost about a third of their mass through residual devolatilization. In every pair, the immobilized sample lost more mass than its free support and developed a new decomposition event between roughly 190 and 280 °C that the free support did not show. A mass loss in this range is typical of an organic solid and is consistent with an enzyme-derived layer; the effect was clearest for FP-800 and SP-900, whose inert supports leave no overlapping signal from the carbon.

Taken together, SEM, EDS, and TG confirm that material was deposited on both FP and SP supports after immobilization and that it decomposes as expected for an organic layer. We read these results qualitatively: the extra mass loss also reflects co-deposited buffer and adsorbed water, so it is not used to quantify the bound protein, which was measured independently by the Bradford assay and corroborated by the catalytic activity of the preparations. The characterization is nonetheless coherent with the catalytic data, in that the surface and structural features imparted by fast pyrolysis accompany the more effective FP-CALB systems.

The consistency of the observed performance difference across two chemically distinct reactions: esterification in organic solvent and hydrolysis in aqueous medium, indicates that the superior behavior of FP-derived biochars is not reaction-specific. Rather, it reflects more favorable enzyme–support interactions and/or improved substrate accessibility associated with the surface and structural features imparted by fast pyrolysis. On this basis and given the systematically higher catalytic efficiency observed for FP-CALB systems, further investigations were focused exclusively on biochars obtained by fast pyrolysis, as discussed in the following sections.

Initial esterification experiments were carried out using palmitic acid and ethanol in a 1 : 1 molar ratio, under otherwise identical reaction conditions, chosen as a reference to probe the intrinsic behavior of the FP-CALB system. Under these conditions, satisfactory conversion levels were obtained. In addition, a post-immobilization washing step with heptane was introduced to remove residual aqueous phase and homogenize the surface conditions of the FP-CALB biocatalysts prior to reaction testing. Then immobilized enzymes were subjected to a kinetic evaluation by monitoring the esterification conversion at different reaction times. The resulting data, reported in Table 4, shows a regular increase in conversion over time and overlapping values for all FP-derived supports within experimental error, confirming the stable and reproducible behavior of the FP-CALB system under batch conditions.

Within the FP series, no clear trend in catalytic performance was observed as a function of the final pyrolysis temperature. This is somewhat unexpected, as fast-pyrolysis biochars are known to undergo significant structural evolution with increasing temperature, ranging from lignocellulosic-like features at lower temperatures to progressively more graphitic domains at higher temperatures. Despite these differences, catalytic behavior remained largely comparable under the investigated batch conditions, suggesting that the resulting surface properties are sufficiently suitable to ensure effective accessibility of the catalytic sites and mass transfer.

Once a stable and reproducible catalytic response had been established, the effect of the fatty acid to ethanol molar ratio was evaluated to shift the reaction equilibrium toward ester formation. After defining the best reaction conditions, the catalytic performance of the immobilized enzyme was evaluated toward a broader range of fatty acid substrates. This investigation aimed to assess the versatility of the FP-CALB materials and to verify whether the high activity observed for palmitic acid could be extended to substrates differing in chain length and degree of unsaturation. Batch esterification reactions were carried out using stearic acid, oleic acid, and linoleic acid as representative C18 fatty acids, under the same optimized conditions adopted for palmitic acid. Conversion was monitored as a function of reaction time to compare the kinetic behavior of the system across the different substrates. The resulting time-dependent conversion profiles are summarized in Fig. 2.

Across the fatty acids tested, FP-CALB systems showed a steady rise in conversion as the reaction proceeded. Stearic acid (C18 : 0) displayed conversion values comparable to those obtained for palmitic acid throughout the monitored interval. The longer saturated chain therefore did not introduce any evident restriction in substrate diffusion or access to the active site under the applied conditions.

A somewhat different pattern was observed for the unsaturated substrates. The presence of one or more carbon–carbon double bonds increase conformational flexibility, which can affect how the molecule interacts with the active pocket of the enzyme. In the early stages of the reaction, this was reflected in slightly lower conversions compared to the saturated counterparts. As the reaction progressed, however, the gap narrowed and high final conversions were still obtained. Overall, the results indicate that the FP-CALB materials remain effective even when processing structurally more flexible fatty acids. Within experimental uncertainty, no clear performance gap was observed among the different FP-derived supports. Their conversion profiles were largely superimposable for all substrates considered, pointing to a reproducible immobilization outcome across the investigated fast-pyrolysis temperature range.

After establishing the performance toward individual fatty acids, attention was shifted to more complex feedstocks of industrial relevance. Palm fatty acid distillate (PFAD) and a related PFAD-derived fraction, hereafter referred to as PFAD*, were selected for this purpose. Although both materials originate from the same industrial process, they differ in their internal composition. PFAD is predominantly composed of palmitic and oleic acids, with smaller fractions of stearic acid, whereas PFAD* contains a higher proportion of oleic acid together with palmitic, linoleic, and stearic acids. The compositional variability between these mixtures makes them suitable test systems for probing the behavior of the immobilized biocatalyst under conditions that more closely resemble practical applications.

Esterification reactions were carried out under the same optimized batch conditions previously established for pure fatty acids, namely post-immobilization washing of the biocatalyst, a fatty acid to ethanol molar ratio of 1 : 1.5, heptane as solvent, and a reaction temperature of 50 °C. To focus on the overall efficiency of the process rather than on the behavior of individual components, PFAD and PFAD* were treated as single multicomponent substrates. Accordingly, conversion values were calculated as average conversions, obtained by averaging the conversions of the individual fatty acids measured at each sampling time.

The time-dependent esterification profiles obtained for the two industrial mixtures are reported separately in Tables 6 and 7, corresponding to PFAD and PFAD*, respectively. In both cases, FP-CALB systems remained highly active despite the increased compositional complexity of the substrates, affording steadily increasing average conversions over time.

For the PFAD mixture, high average conversions were achieved within a few hours, closely matching the performance observed for the corresponding pure fatty acids under identical conditions. Efficient esterification was also obtained for the PFAD* mixture, which contains a higher fraction of unsaturated fatty acids, although slightly lower average conversions were observed at shorter reaction times. Nevertheless, in both cases the esterification proceeded efficiently, confirming that the FP-CALB system retains high catalytic competence even in the presence of complex industrial feeds.

Importantly, within experimental error, the different FP-derived supports displayed overlapping catalytic behavior for both PFAD and PFAD* mixtures. This observation indicates that biochars obtained by fast pyrolysis provide a robust and reliable immobilization platform for CALB, enabling efficient esterification of multicomponent fatty acid feeds without the need for substrate pre-fractionation. Overall, these results further strengthen the applicability of FP-CALB systems in process-oriented esterification of industrial fatty acid mixtures.33

The heterogeneous nature of the FP-CALB system enables straightforward recovery of the biocatalyst from the reaction medium, a key requirement for assessing its applicability to repeated batch processes. On this basis, the reusability of FP-CALB materials was investigated by performing four consecutive esterification cycles under batch conditions. Reusability tests were carried out using palmitic acid as a model substrate, under the optimized reaction conditions previously established. After completion of each reaction cycle, the biocatalyst was recovered by simple phase separation and subjected to a cleaning procedure consisting of two washing steps with heptane, aimed at removing residual reactants and products without introducing additional drying or reconditioning treatments. The recovered catalyst was then directly reused in the subsequent cycle under identical conditions.

The esterification conversions obtained over consecutive reaction cycles are reported in Fig. 2. Conversion values remained essentially constant across the investigated cycles, with only a slight decrease observed in the fourth cycle (to below ∼20%). This minor variation does not indicate a significant loss of catalytic activity and is likely related to small variations during catalyst recovery and handling. Within experimental uncertainty, no measurable decline in activity was detected. To address enzyme leaching directly, the supernatant recovered after each reuse cycle was analysed by Bradford assay. No protein was detected in the filtrates of any of the four cycles, indicating that CALB remained adsorbed on the support throughout the reuse experiments. Together with the stable conversions in Fig. 3, this shows that neither leaching nor irreversible deactivation contributes appreciably under the adopted conditions.

Reusability of CALB immobilized on bamboo-derived biochars obtained by fast pyrolysis in batch esterification. Reactional conditions: consecutive esterification cycles were carried out using palmitic acid under optimized conditions (50 °C, fatty acid to ethanol molar ratio of 1 : 1.5, heptane as solvent). The stable catalytic performance observed over multiple cycles demonstrates that physical adsorption of CALB onto fast-pyrolysis biochars provides sufficient enzyme retention to sustain repeated batch operation. Given the essentially unchanged activity across consecutive cycles, extended reuse experiments were not pursued, as the system showed no indication of progressive deactivation within the investigated operational window. Overall, these results confirm the good operational stability of the FP-CALB system and further support the suitability of fast-pyrolysis biochars as robust supports for reusable biocatalysts in esterification processes.

After demonstrating the activity, robustness, and reusability of FP-CALB systems under batch conditions, the catalytic performance of the immobilized enzyme was further evaluated under continuous-flow operation. The transition to flow was aimed at assessing the behavior of the FP-CALB system under intensified conditions and at exploring its suitability for continuous processing. Flow esterification experiments were carried out in a packed-bed stainless steel reactor containing a physical mixture of silica and FP-CALB biocatalyst, selected to ensure adequate bed permeability while maintaining enough immobilized enzyme. Throughout this period, the system maintained a constant output, and no productivity decay or conversion loss was observed, demonstrating the excellent robustness and steady-state stability of the method.

Conclusions

The present study examined bamboo-derived biochars obtained through both fast and slow pyrolysis as supports for the immobilization of Candida antarctica lipase B, with particular attention to how the production route of the carbon material affects the behavior of the resulting heterogeneous biocatalyst. Efficient immobilization was achieved on both types of biochar. However, when the two materials were directly compared under identical conditions, those produced by fast pyrolysis repeatedly showed higher catalytic efficiency than their slow-pyrolysis counterparts.

A more detailed evaluation of the fast-pyrolysis systems confirmed that the corresponding CALB-based biocatalysts operate stably and reproducibly under batch conditions. Once the main reaction parameters were identified and carefully controlled, the FP-CALB materials proved capable of catalyzing the esterification of various long-chain fatty acids, including industrially relevant mixtures, while preserving activity over four consecutive reuse cycles. These observations indicate that fast pyrolysis biochars constitute a consistent and mechanically reliable platform for enzyme immobilization in heterogeneous setups.

The behavior observed in batch experiments was not limited to that configuration. When transferred to continuous-flow operation with residence times ranging from 4 to 30 minutes, the FP-CALB systems maintained significant catalytic activity and enabled efficient esterification even at relatively short residence times. From a practical standpoint, this suggests that the materials can be integrated into continuous processing schemes without substantial loss of performance.

The results also make it evident that the way the biochar is produced plays a decisive role in shaping the final catalytic response. Differences in the thermal protocol, beyond the nominal pyrolysis temperature alone, translate into measurable variations in the efficiency of the immobilized enzyme. In this respect, fast pyrolysis biochars emerge as particularly suitable supports for CALB in esterification processes.
