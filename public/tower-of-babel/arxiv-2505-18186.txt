Discovering Interpretable Concepts in Large Generative Music Models
Nikhil Singh, Manuel Cherep, Pattie Maes

Abstract
The fidelity with which neural networks can now generate content such as music presents a scientific opportunity: these systems appear to have learned implicit theories of the structure of such content through statistical learning alone. This could offer a novel lens on theories of human-generated media. Where these representations align with traditional constructs (e.g. chord progressions in music), they demonstrate how these can be inferred from statistical regularities. Where they diverge, they highlight potential limits in our theoretical frameworks—patterns that we may have overlooked but that nonetheless hold significant explanatory power. In this paper, we focus on the specific case of music generators. We introduce a method to discover musical concepts using sparse autoencoders (SAEs), extracting interpretable features from the residual stream activations of a transformer model. We evaluate this approach by extracting a large set of features and producing an automatic labeling and evaluation pipeline for them. Our results reveal both familiar musical concepts and counterintuitive patterns that lack clear counterparts in existing theories or natural language altogether. Beyond improving model transparency, our work provides a new empirical tool that might help discover organizing principles in ways that have eluded traditional methods of analysis and synthesis.

1
Introduction

When humans create, we draw upon a rich vocabulary of concepts. Some of these we can name and explicitly reason about; terms like “symmetry” or “gradient” in the visual arts, for instance. Others shape our media long before we have language for them: consider how the “Hero’s Journey” narrative structure infused myths, legends, and epics across cultures for millennia before Joseph Campbell famously theorized it in the 20th century
[
1
]
. This gap between practice and theory is emblematic of a more fundamental pattern: our ability to recognize and use structure often precedes our ability to describe it.

This poses an interesting puzzle for machine learning. Deep generative models have for some time now flaunted the ability to generate remarkably convincing content across domains including images, text, music, and code, through not much more than statistical learning from raw data. Their success at doing so suggests they may have learned internal representations of the building blocks that make such content coherent. If so, how do we bridge the gap between the raw statistical horsepower of these models and the structured conceptual vocabulary we humans use?

Consider what it means for a model to “understand” a musical concept like a chord progression. In its extensive network of neural activations, there must be some pattern that responds to and enables generation of chord progressions. Yet, this pattern might not correspond neatly to our theoretical description. It might combine elements we typically think of as separate, or be sensitive to distinctions we typically ignore. Indeed, when we formalize concepts to describe such patterns, we are articulating hypotheses about the underlying organization of that medium. Connecting these hypotheses to the opaque internal mechanisms of generative models could open up useful new interactions, by establishing a shared vocabulary for both analysis and synthesis.

Why music? Music offers a particularly compelling domain for investigating this. It combines a rich theoretical vocabulary developed over centuries with patterns that still resist verbal description. It exhibits striking statistical regularities and enormous cultural diversity. Importantly, it lacks the kind of large-scale high-quality paired data (like image-caption pairs) that might guide concept discovery in other domains. Yet, many efforts have sought to control music generation models to better align their behavior with human intent and expectation
[
2
,
3
]
. These characteristics make it a challenging, yet useful, test case for concept discovery and alignment in generative models.

This paper introduces a method for doing exactly this. Using sparse autoencoders, we extract features from the residual stream activations of a transformer-based music generation model. We demonstrate how these features reveal both familiar patterns that align with traditional concepts and novel regularities that elude precise formal description despite being perceptibly homogeneous. We develop methods for mapping discovered concepts to full pieces of music, and examine the properties of these features under various conditions. To support interpretability in this empirical sense, we propose a pipeline for discovering and labeling the model’s internal concepts without imposing a pre-defined theory or formal structure.

We emphasize that our work operates at a
proto-theoretical
level. It surfaces latent operational concepts from within a model’s internal representations. These concepts may support coherent generations, but they lack the formal structure, completeness, or normative grounding required of a formal musical theory. Our goal is to investigate the kinds of distinctions that emerge when large generative models are trained to produce music. These latent regularities may eventually serve as a substrate for developing new formal theories.

2
Related Work

2.1
Statistical Learning of Musical Structure

Computational approaches to modeling music date back several decades, encompassing rule-based systems, probabilistic approaches, and modern neural architectures. Early Markov models for music
[
4
]
could capture local dependencies (e.g. chord transitions) but struggled to represent longer-range structure like phrasing or form. Other prior work has applied information-theoretic analysis to music corpora to study the emergence of harmonic categories
[
5
]
. Modern transformer-based music generators, such as Music Transformer
[
6
]
, extend the notion of statistically modeling music, but have found greater success by relying on self-supervised learning on large corpora of symbolic music with a highly flexible model. In parallel, models like WaveNet
[
7
]
and SampleRNN
[
8
]
have shown that neural networks can learn effective audio-domain representations. Recent efforts
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
push these ideas further by modeling music at the audio level with transformer-style or diffusion models for audio. This empirical success raises deeper questions: as these models generate increasingly sophisticated music, what kinds of musical knowledge are they actually acquiring? The contrast between their generation capabilities and our limited understanding of their internal representations arguably warrants deeper examination. Indeed, the journey toward better modeling of musical structure has also been a journey toward greater opacity in how that structure is encoded. Understanding what musical concepts these models learn is the challenge that we tackle in this work.

2.2
Interpretability Through Feature Extraction

Interpreting internal computations in neural networks has been a major research focus for several years. Recent work has demonstrated that the distributed representations in LLMs often encode concepts in surprisingly localized ways
[
13
]
. This is reminiscent of classic work on sparse coding, which showed how mammalian visual cortex could be understood as implementing efficient codes for natural images
[
14
]
. The broader lesson here—namely, that sparse latent structure can be biologically and computationally advantageous—has also been leveraged for interpretability. One line of very recent work trains
sparse autoencoders
(SAEs)
[
15
,
13
]
on internal activation vectors from specific transformer layers, enforcing an L1 penalty to discover a basis of interpretable components. Formally, given an encoder
E
and decoder
D
, one may optimize:

\min_{E,D}\mathbb{E}_{x}[\|x-D(E(x))\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|E(x)\|_{1}]

(1)

This sparsity pressure is thought to promote discovery of reusable “atomic” concepts rather than relying on reconstruction by memorization. Once learned, concepts can be named or described via automated interpretability methods (e.g. using other LLMs to synthesize features based on sequences that strongly activate them). While such methods have been successful for LLMs, music generators pose unique challenges due to the hierarchical temporal structure and mix of discrete and continuous features in music. As a result, our work implements several adaptations to produce and describe interpretable musical features.

2.3
Interpreting Music Generation Models

Given a vocabulary of known concepts and example data to match, one strategy toward interpreting the internal representations in music generation models is
probing
. While probing has a rich history in NLP, researchers have recently begun to apply it to music models. Prior work
[
16
]
introduced the
SynTheory
dataset to systematically probe music foundation models (Jukebox
[
17
]
and MusicGen
[
10
]
) on core music theory concepts like tempo, intervals, and chords, revealing that certain layers capture these concepts more strongly than others. Similarly, other work investigates chord and pitch representations in MusicGen and find that musical concepts become more evident in deeper layers, yet may not be linearly separable without additional interventions in the hidden space
[
18
]
. Beyond these direct probing and manipulation techniques, recent work
[
19
]
uses the
DecoderLens

[
20
]
method to interpret intermediate activations, offering an auditory perspective on what each layer “hears” and how it evolves through the network. Such approaches exemplify an emerging trend: there is increasing interest in understanding how these models do what they do. However, these approaches have primarily focused on probing for known concepts, an important strategy but one not without its limits. For us, this raises one key question: what structures might we be missing by focusing only on concepts we already know to look for?

2.4
Automated Concept Discovery and Evaluation

A central question in interpretability is how to discover concepts that a model has organically learned, rather than imposing concepts
a priori
. Several frameworks have recently been proposed for automated discovery and evaluation of concepts in neural networks across domains. For instance, InterPLM
[
21
]
introduces a pipeline where sparse autoencoders learn thousands of latent features from a protein language model (ESM-2). They show that many features align with known and human-understandable concepts, while others appear novel. Complementary approaches called Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs)
[
22
]
aim to constrain or interpret a network’s intermediate layers in terms of discrete, named concepts, but they typically rely on a hand-specified concept set. Recent variants of CBMs invert this paradigm by using sparse autoencoders to first
discover
a dictionary of latent concepts and then
name
them through automated or semi-automated means
[
23
]
.

Though much of this work has been conducted on non-musical data, the same underlying principles might apply to neural music models: these models may encode a wide array of musical concepts without an explicit labeling scheme. Automated approaches to discovering and evaluating these latent musical features thus hold promise for deeper insights into how generative music models “understand” music, and for ultimately enabling fine-grained control or editing of generated content.

3
Methods

3.1
Dataset and Activation Extraction

The first step in our pipeline is to obtain a dataset of music clips for which we can obtain activations from the generative model. We use the
MusicSet

[
24
]
dataset for this, which is a collection of around 160,000 samples, most of which are
\approx
10 seconds long. MusicSet is a combination of data from MTG-Jamendo
[
25
]
, MusicCaps
[
9
]
, and MusicBench
[
26
]
. All the source datasets are Creative Commons-licensed (though we should note that only MTG-Jamendo explicitly states that this applies to the music clips). We chose this dataset because it offers a comparatively diverse range of genres, instrumentation, and musical characteristics at a larger scale than many alternative publicly available datasets. We also found the data to be more stylistically diverse and high-quality than FMA
[
27
]
, another popular option. We produce activations by feeding the music into two pre-trained MusicGen
[
10
]
models: MusicGen-Large (henceforth, MGL) and MusicGen-Small (henceforth, MGS). For each model, we extract activation vectors from five layers in the residual stream: the early layer (layer 2), late layer (second-to-last), middle layer, and layers at 25%, 50%, and 75% depth. This corresponds to
\textrm{{Layer}}_{\textrm{MGS}}\in\{2,6,12,18,22\}
, and
\textrm{{Layer}}_{\textrm{MGL}}\in\{2,12,24,36,46\}
. Activation dimensionality is 1024 for MGS and 2048 for MGL.

3.2
Training the Sparse Autoencoders

We train a series of SAEs to extract interpretable features from the residual stream activations of MusicGen. Each is trained to reconstruct the original activations
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from a learned sparse latent representation
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{\epsilon\cdot d}
, where
\epsilon
is an expansion factor (note: this means the latent dimension is typically
larger
than the input dimension for such SAEs).

The SAE architecture generally consists of an encoder (
\mathbf{h}=\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_{e}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{e})
) and a decoder (
\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{W}_{d}\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{b}_{d}
), both implemented as single linear layers where
\mathbf{W}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{\epsilon\cdot d\times d}
,
\mathbf{W}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\epsilon d}
,
\mathbf{b}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{\epsilon d}
, and
\mathbf{b}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the learned weights and biases. To enforce explicit sparsity, we apply a
k
-sparse projection operator
P_{k}:R^{\epsilon\cdot d}\rightarrow R^{\epsilon\cdot d}
to
h
, yielding the sparse latent code
z=P_{k}(h)
. These are typically called
k
-sparse autoencoders
[
28
,
29
]
. Where
S_{k}
is the set of indices of the top-
k
values
\in
h:

z=P_{k}(h)=\begin{cases}h_{i}&\text{if }h_{i}\in S_{k}(\mathbf{h})\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(2)

We minimize the mean squared reconstruction error, subject to the sparsity constraint. We experiment with expansion factors
\epsilon\in\{4,32\}
and sparsity levels
k\in\{32,100\}
, all of which are values encountered in prior SAE literature
[
29
,
21
]
.

3.3
Feature Mapping and Filtering

After the SAE has trained, its latents presumably correspond to meaningful musical features. Of course, the model itself does not reveal what these are. We need some way to work backwards from these latents to objects whose content we can perceptually interpret, in relation to a particular latent dimension learned by the SAE. A conventional approach is to use
max activating examples
. In the case of language models, these are token sequences (prompts, perhaps) that most significantly activate a given feature. In the case of music, it gets a little trickier. Unlike text, tokens here represent an almost imperceptible chunk of music: about 20 milliseconds. Even with a handful of these, interpreting a feature becomes quite challenging.

To characterize the relationship between learned features and input tracks, we compute a per-track summary statistic for each feature based on its activation profile over time. Let
f_{i}
denote the
i
-th learned feature, and let
\mathbf{\alpha}_{i,j}=(\alpha_{i,j,1},\dots,\alpha_{i,j,T_{j}})\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{j}\times d}
represent its activation time series over the
T_{j}
time steps of track
j
. The
mean activation
of feature
f_{i}
in track
j
is:
\mu_{i,j}=\frac{1}{T_{j}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{j}}\alpha_{i,j,t}

This gives us a scalar estimate of the feature’s average prominence over the duration of the track. We additionally considered a
temporal prevalence
measure, defined as the fraction of time steps in which the feature is active (
\pi_{i,j}=\frac{1}{T_{j}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{j}}\mathbb{I}(a_{i,j,t}>\tau)
) where
\tau\geq 0
is a sensitivity threshold. In practice, we found
\pi_{i,j}
to be strongly correlated with
\mu_{i,j}
, and thus focus on the mean activation
\mu_{i,j}
.

To support downstream interpretation and analysis, we then identify a subset of features whose activation patterns suggest both selectivity and relevance. The goal is to exclude features that are either inactive, overly common, or too rare to support generalizable interpretation. Let
\delta_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
denote whether feature
f_{i}
exhibits nonzero average activation, which corresponds to nonzero activation at any time step in track
j
:
\delta_{i,j}=\mathbb{I}\left(\max_{1\leq t\leq T_{j}}\alpha_{i,j,t}>\tau\right)
where
\tau\geq 0
is a small activation threshold (we set
\tau=0
). We define the corpus-level activation rate of feature
f_{i}
as
r_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{i,j}
where
N
is the number of validation tracks.

We discard feature
f_{i}
if any of the following conditions hold:
(a) Inactive
:
r_{i}=0
; the feature never activates in the validation set.
(b) Excessively Ubiquitous
:
r_{i}>\theta_{\text{max}}
, with
\theta_{\text{max}}=0.25
; the feature activates in more than 25% of tracks, suggesting diffuse or polysemous behavior that limits interpretability.
(c) Excessively Obscure
:
r_{i}<\theta_{\text{min}}
, with
\theta_{\text{min}}=0.01
; the feature activates in fewer than 1% of tracks, indicating insufficient coverage to support reliable interpretation. This filtering step seeks to ensure that only features with non-trivial, non-saturated, and sufficiently frequent activation patterns are retained for subsequent analysis. Thresholds are heuristic and informed by prior work in neural feature sparsity and interpretability
[
21
]
.

3.4
Selecting Examples for Labeling

One challenge of labeling concepts relates to
typicality
. Extreme values (e.g., the maximum activating example) might not represent the typical set of the distribution of examples that activate a given feature. Let
E_{j}=\{x\mid f_{j}(x)>\tau\}
denote the set of all inputs
x
that activate feature
j
with sufficient strength
f_{j}(x)
(as before, we set
\tau=0
as a conservative threshold). Instead of relying on a single max-activating example, we select the top 10 highest-activating examples,
S_{j}\subset E_{j}
, and infer the feature’s label from these. The choice of
|S_{j}|=10
is motivated by a balance between statistical robustness and noise from weakly activating examples. A larger activating subset improves the likelihood of capturing a representative sample from
E_{j}
, but diminishing returns arise as activation strength decays, especially under the sparsity constraint wherein the distribution might be truncated. Our heuristic aims to approximate the mode of the feature’s natural stimulus distribution.

3.5
Automated Interpretability

Now that we can represent a feature using the max activating examples, we must label them. The simplest method for this is arguably human interpretation. A typical listener may be able to quickly identify a concept that reproduces some known principle, such as a particular pitch or rhythmic pattern. An experienced musician with relevant experience may be able to reason about the properties of an ambiguous or emergent feature. Unfortunately, this process quickly hits a capacity limit. With sufficient feature volume, it can be infeasible to recruit enough of the right annotators for this task. Consider the case of ML. For any given layer, with expansion factor 32x, there may be 65,536 features. If even
\sim
1% of these activate, this is in the high hundreds for this SAE alone. Given 10 activating examples of 10 seconds each, this then corresponds to 18 hours of raw listening, in addition to any time needed to deliberate. Multiply this by multiple layers, or a larger share of activating features, and the need for a more scalable solution becomes quite evident.

As noted earlier in this paper, automated interpretability methods use language models to explain linguistic concepts. However, musical concepts present in more varied ways, across much longer sequencies, and lack very strong music-language models to use in such a setup. As such, to generate descriptive, meaningful labels for the discovered features, we use a multi-step pipeline:

1.

A
generative label proposal
strategy. We query a large multimodal model (in this case, Gemini Flash 1.5) with concatenated audio of the top-10 max activating examples for each feature. We carefully instruct the model to produce a set of concept tags, confidence scores for each, and an overarching label and description for the feature.

2.

A
classifier-based
strategy where we extract a set of tags for each feature using pre-trained Essentia
[
30
,
31
]
audio classifier models. We consider multiple top predicted tags from each model as candidate labels.

3.

We use CLAP
[
32
]
to compute semantic alignment between suggested labels and activating examples. This provides a quantitative measure of how well each concept label aligns with the audio content of the feature.

3.6
Human Validation

Ultimately, given the complexity of the musical signals we are working with and the possible ambiguity of some features, it’s important to get a robust empirical sense of the performance of these labeling approaches. For the Gemini and Essentia labels, we run a human validation study (IRB approved). Participants are presented with 3 audio examples for each feature randomly subselected from a couple of SAEs with significant valid feature counts, alongside proposed labels. They are then asked to rate (1) which of the proposed labels best captures the common features among the given examples, and (2) how confident they are that their selection represents these common features well. As an extension, we ask participants to give us their own intuitive label for the feature.

Figure

Human Evaluation

We sought to evaluate which of our two labeling pipelines produces better aligned labels. To do this, we selected 400 features for each labeling method (Gemini vs. Essentia), and recruited 80 participants (40 per method) to each review 10 randomly subselected features. We asked participants to select from the top label options, rate their confidence that this is the true label for the feature, and enter an alternate label. Overall, participants expressed greater confidence in Essentia labels (3.96/5 avg. confidence, 71% or 278 features
>
4) than Gemini labels (3.19/5 avg. confidence, 47% or 181 features
>
4). This suggests that though multimodal LLMs are promising as a way to label features in a more open-ended way (compared to Essentia’s classifiers which select from preset labels), their current accuracy at doing so may inhibit their utility for interpretability.

5
Conclusion

Our results demonstrate the feasibility and potential of using sparse autoencoders to discover interpretable concepts in large music generative models, i.e. using models for
musical discovery

[
34
]
. We have shown that these models learn a rich set of musical patterns, ranging from well-established concepts to novel and potentially ambiguous regularities that warrant further investigation. Through future work in this area, we believe it is possible to build a comprehensive understanding of how neural networks represent and generate music, ultimately bridging the gap between the raw statistical power of these models and the structured conceptual vocabulary we use to understand and create music and other media.

Appendix A
Additional Limitations

Our concepts are necessarily shaped by the data we use to train the SAEs, which is again shaped by the availability of data in this domain. As such, we expect the features to be biased towards Western popular music concepts, based on our examination of the results. Future work should aim to recruit more representative datasets.

Appendix D
Connections to Music Theory

This work is conceptually related to several core threads we are aware of in music theory, cognitive musicology, and computational modeling of music structure. Here, we clarify how our approach relates to existing work by seeking to uncover “operational” structures (what we refer to as features or concepts) in generative models.

Statistical foundations of music theory

Temperley
[
35
,
36
]
argues that listeners develop an understanding of many core elements of Western music (such as meter, harmony, and voice leading) by internalizing probabilistic regularities encountered in musical corpora. From this perspective, foundational concepts within music theory, rather than being seen solely as elements of a formal axiomatic system, can be understood as principles that often reflect this inductively learned, tacit knowledge of musical likelihoods. Temperley’s computational cognitive models are themselves compact and idealized representations which help explain why these music-theoretic constructs are perceptually and musically relevant.

Our approach parallels this in an AI setting. Rather than analyzing human musical corpora directly to infer cognitive principles, we seek to “reverse-engineer” generative models
trained
on such data to recover the internal distinctions and abstractions they have formed. The concepts discovered in our pipeline reflect these model abstractions, many of which correspond to statistical regularities observed in music (e.g. genre, texture, instrument timbre, harmonic style).

Operational concepts vs. formal theory

Gjerdingen’s schema theory
[
37
,
38
]
discusses the role of learned patterns in shaping musical fluency. His analysis of Galant phrase structure proposes that composers relied on a rich set of prototypical configurations (or
stock musical phrases
in particular), acquired through exposure and practice rather than via formal rule systems. These schemata were typically only formalized retroactively through pedagogical and analytical discourse, as we imagine concepts of the type our pipeline discovers might be in the future. As such, our work focuses on
proto-theoretical regularities
: we extract features that appear to be functionally meaningful to the model regardless of whether they map cleanly to any established theoretical category. Some features do align with known “schemata” but others do not, yet still display internal coherence. In this sense, our pipeline seeks to unearth raw material from which a theory (either of a model’s music understanding, or of some aspect of music itself) could potentially be formed.

Meta-theoretical implications

This empirical approach may also serve as a testing ground for meta-theoretical questions about music theory. If large-scale generative models learn internal representations that resemble certain theoretical concepts but diverge from others, this may point to systematic gaps or biases in how theory abstracts from musical practice. Conversely, the emergence of coherent but non-canonical features suggests that models may encode distinctions that are musically significant but neglected in prior analyses. In this way, our work does not propose a theory of music, but can be seen as contributing a method for investigating the gap between musical practice and theoretical formalism. We propose that such discovered features features be treated as
hypotheses
about musical structure that may be refined, validated, or rejected through future research.

Prompt for Gemini’s Labeling

Listen very carefully to this set of audio clips, which consists of song snippets concatenated in random order.

You need to discover common musical patterns across the whole set, to identify what musical feature is shared across all clips. You will need to listen carefully.
For each potential concept you identify, output a name, a confidence score between 0 and 1 (where 1 is highest confidence), and a concise description of the concept.

At a higher level, describe the overall concept shared across the set, give it a suitable name, and provide an overall confidence score (0 to 1).

Describe the *underlying concepts* not the specific audio snippets (e.g. your description could say "the concept" but not "the audio snippets"). However, try to avoid such verbiage altogether and concisely describe the musical concept’s main attributes.

Include NO FILLER text.

Focus on being specific.
