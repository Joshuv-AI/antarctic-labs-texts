Single-Injection Liposomal Bupivacaine Adductor Canal Block for Pain Control and Recovery After Total Knee Arthroplasty: A Randomized Controlled Double‑Blinded Study
Authors: Jiajun Wen, Haobin Peng, Ying Jiang, Manli Chen, Jianxin Ou, Weitao Huang, Peixing Li, Jun Zhou, Xianping Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe efficacy of the adductor canal block (ACB) in multimodal analgesia following total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains controversial. This study investigates the effects of local anesthetics single-injection ACB with different analgesic durations on TKA patients.MethodsThis randomized controlled double‑blinded trial enrolled 90 patients undergoing primary TKA. Participants were randomized to receive ACB with either liposomal bupivacaine (LB), ropivacaine (15 mL of 0.3%), or in the control group, with 30 patients in each group. The primary end points included the static and dynamic visual analog scale (VAS) scores at 24 hours postoperatively. The secondary end points examined the static and dynamic pain scores at 6,12,48, and 72 hours postoperatively; the knee range of motion (ROM); quadriceps muscle strength; duration to first ambulation; opioid consumption; and related adverse events.ResultsPatients administered a LB single-injection ACB (LB-ACB) demonstrated significantly reduced VAS scores at static and dynamic phases at 24 hours, relative to the ropivacaine single-injection ACB (R-ACB) and control group patients. We recorded a median interquartile range [IQR] of 1 (0.0–2.0 [0–4]) vs. 3 (2.0–4.0 [0–5]) vs. 3 (2.0–4.0 [0–6]) for static pain scores and 2.5 (1.0–3.25 [0–5]) vs. 4 (4.0–5.0 [2–7]) vs. 5 (4.0–6.0 [3–7]) for dynamic pain scores (P<0.001). Both LB-ACB and R-ACB groups showed lower pain scores at 6 and 12 hours postoperatively versus control (P<0.05). Time to first rescue analgesia was prolonged in the LB group, and LB-ACB also improved early quadriceps strength and knee ROM. No significant adverse events or motor blockade occurred.ConclusionAmong patients undergoing TKA, LB single-injection ACB offers a significant analgesic advantage compared to ropivacaine, lasting up to 24 hours. After 48 hours, there is no difference in analgesic effects when compared to the control group.Keywords: arthroplasty, replacement, knee, liposomal bupivacaine, nerve block, pain, postoperative

Introduction

In an alarming 2021 report, China announced a stark increase in osteoarthritis (OA) incidence from 53.35 million to 152.85 million cases during 1990–2021. This upward trend is particularly evident among patients suffering from knee joint OA.1 Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is the gold standard intervention for treating end-stage osteoarthritis of the knee, with a rapidly increasing utilization rate.2 Recent epidemiological evidence indicates that the number of TKA procedures remained consistently high during the COVID-19 pandemic and is now steadily returning to pre-pandemic levels.3 However, TKA patients exclusively experienced moderate to severe postoperative pain, which considerably impacted postoperative rehabilitation, patient satisfaction, and overall outcomes.4,5 Prior studies established that an optimized postoperative analgesic regimen can effectively reduce opioid consumption, related adverse events, hospitalization duration, medical costs, all while enhancing patient satisfaction of OA patients undergoing TKA.6,7

Peri-articular injection (PAI) is the classical postoperative analgesia for TKA patients. Unfortunately, its analgesic efficacy is suboptimal. More recently, the International Consensus on Anesthesia-Related Outcomes after Surgery (ICAROS) recommended peripheral nerve block (PNB) for improved outcomes among TKA patients, unless contraindications exist.8 Therefore, PNB is increasingly used in the multimodal analgesia regimen for TKA patients. In particular, the adductor canal block, relative to the femoral nerve block, provides highly effective analgesia with minimal quadriceps muscle functional impairment.9 However, some studies indicate that adding peripheral nerve blocks (PNB) to the postoperative analgesic regimen for total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is ineffective. In a study by Laoruengthana et al10 PAI in combination with other peripheral nerve blocks, such as adductor canal block (ACB) and femoral nerve block (FNB), confers no substantial benefit to patients undergoing TKA. This controversial result may stem from the insufficient analgesia duration that typically exists with ropivacaine or bupivacaine usage.11,12 The network meta-analysis conducted by Hussain et al further reinforces this perspective.13 The findings indicate that in total knee arthroplasty, a continuous ACB, but not single-injection ACB, combined with local infiltration analgesia (LIA), demonstrates statistically superior analgesic efficacy compared to the use of LIA alone.

Theoretically, this strategy of LB Single-injection ACB can potentially overcome duration insufficiency of ropivacaine action. Liposomal bupivacaine (LB) delivers prolonged analgesia using DepoFoam technology, and features an immediate 3% drug release rate and continuous slow release over 72 hours.14,15 The latest meta-analysis conducted by Ye et al demonstrates that LB single-injection ACB for TKA can offer short-term pain relief and decrease opioid consumption in comparison to commonly used local anesthetics.16 However, it is noteworthy that none of the four studies included employed a double-blind design.

Hence, the gap in the literature that this study aims to fill—whether it is the lack of high-quality double-blind randomized trials comparing LB single-injection ACB (LB-ACB) with ropivacaine single-injection ACB (R-ACB) and control group for TKA. The primary endpoint of this study was static and dynamic pain scores at 24 hours postoperatively. Secondary endpoints included pain scores at additional time points, knee function, time to first ambulation, opioid consumption, and adverse events.

Methods

Study Participants

This study strictly followed guidelines from the Declaration of Helsinki, and is registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (website: https://www.chictr.org.cn/bin/project/edit?pid=202284; identification number: ChiCTR2300074254; registration date: August 2, 2023). All study participants were made aware of the study details, and have provided informed consent prior to the initiation of the research. Additionally, this work received ethical approval from the participating Shunde Hospital, Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine, China. Patients were included based on the following criteria: those undergoing primary unilateral TKA under single-shot spinal anesthesia; age ≥18 years; American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III; body mass index (BMI) between 18 and 30 kg/m2. The excluded participants included those who failed to provide informed consent; exhibited infection at the puncture site; coagulopathy; had allergy to local anesthetics; history of psychiatric disorders or long-term usage of psychotropic medications. Patients with Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores below 24; with revised intra-procedure anesthesia methods; and those assessed by anesthesiologists as having contraindications to surgical anesthesia were also eliminated from the study. Finally, this study reporting adhered to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Randomization, Blinding, and Study Interventions

This is a double-blind, randomized controlled clinical trial. We employed a random number table to arbitrarily place study participants into one of three study groups, namely, the LB-ACB group: preoperative adductor canal block was performed using 15 mL (199.5 mg) of liposomal bupivacaine; the R-ACB group: preoperative ACB was performed with 15 mL of 0.3% ropivacaine;17 and the control group: no ACB was performed, but all other procedures were consistent with those of the block group. The participant treatment regimen was selected via a computer-generated randomization sequence, then placed in sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes opened only by the medical care team treating the individual. All pre-surgical nerve blocks and neuraxial anesthesia procedures were performed in the anesthesia preparation room by anesthesiologists who did not participate in other aspects of the study. Participants, orthopedic surgeons, anesthesiologists, and follow-up personnel were fully blinded to the group allocation. The nerve block was provided by an anesthesiologist, who was solely responsible for the immediate local anesthetic toxicity and other unexpected event management. For emergency purposes, such as, initiation upon respiratory depression (respiratory rate <8 breaths/min) or other life-threatening emergencies, the medical team was unblinded to patient grouping.

Our ACB protocol followed the standard practice in most institutions, as is described by Kon et al18 All patients underwent nerve block in the preoperative anesthesia preparation room. In short, a high-frequency linear ultrasound probe was transversely positioned along the longitudinal axis of the mid-thigh, at the midpoint between the anterior superior iliac spine and the patella. Subsequently, using ultrasound imaging, the femoral artery and vein were located beneath the sartorius muscle in short axis, with the saphenous nerve, appearing as a hyperechoic structure, lateral to the adductor canal artery. Once puncture was achieved via a 22-G × 80-mm nerve block needle (BBraun, UniPlex NanoLine, Germany, Melsungen), the ultrasound-guided ACB was administered by anesthesiologist (YJ) with over 10 years of experience. Considering that the onset time of LB may be prolonged, the range and effectiveness of the block are tested 20–30 minutes after ACB. Spinal anesthesia is performed routinely after successful blockage.

Standardized Management and Analgesic Rescue

All patients were given standard anesthesia and intraoperative periarticular injection (PAI), as detailed in the following interventions: (1) preoperative spinal anesthesia with 15–18 mg of ropivacaine; (2) during the procedure, a total of 50 mL of the mixed solution was used for PAI after the implant was placed and before the incision was closed: (a) 20 mL of 0.4% ropivacaine without epinephrine; (b) 2 mg (2 mL) hydromorphone; (c) 1 g (5 mL) tranexamic acid; (d) 23 mL normal saline. The injection sites comprised soft tissues, including the posterior joint capsule, medial and lateral collateral ligament attachments, the quadriceps tendon, the patellar ligament, and the edges of the resected meniscus. Post-operation, all patients received a daily oral dosage of 200 mg celecoxib, alongside routine postoperative rehabilitation protocol under the care of the same orthopedic specialty department. Participants were informed that they can request additional analgesics (tramadol 10 mg per dose) if experiencing intolerable pain (ie, Visual Analog Scale (VAS)≥4). Finally, the total opioid consumption, time to first opioid usage, and related adverse effects were recorded in the patient medical records.

Outcome Measures

Patient demographics, age, sex, namely, BMI, opioid-related parameters, and hospitalization duration were collected from electronic medical records. The primary study end points were the static and dynamic pain scores at 24 hours postoperatively, measured using the VAS on a scale from 0 to 10, assessing pain intensity, where 0 indicates no pain and 10 indicates the most severe pain. Among the secondary outcome measures were (1) static and dynamic pain scores at 6,12,48, and 72 hours postoperatively; (2) knee joint range of motion; (3) quadriceps muscle strength; (4) total opioid consumption and time to first opioid usage. For those using tramadol, administration quantity was determined via conversion to oral morphine milligram dosage (for instance: 100 mg of tramadol equals 10 mg of oral morphine), using ratios defined by the Pain Medicine Society of the Australian and New Zealand College of Anaesthetists;19 (5) postoperative related adverse events: dizziness and somnolence, nausea and vomiting, delirium, pneumonia, and lower limb motor block. We also recorded the knee joint range of motion (ROM) and quadriceps muscle strength at 24,48, and 72 hours postoperatively to assess functional recovery outcomes. Knee joint ROM was measured using a goniometer, and quadriceps strength was assessed by manual muscle testing with the Oxford Scale (grades 0–5). The patient was seated with both legs hanging naturally, while the examiner stabilized the thigh and instructed the patient to extend the knee actively. Grading was defined as follows: Grade 0, no visible or palpable muscle contraction; Grade 1, contraction present without joint movement; Grade 2, movement possible only with gravity eliminated (eg, sliding in the horizontal plane); Grade 3, full movement against gravity (complete knee extension); Grade 4, movement against moderate resistance; and Grade 5, movement against strong resistance, indicating normal muscle strength. Motor block was described as the inability to flex the hip joint in the absence of pain, and was assessed using the modified Bromage scale.20

The time-weighted Sum of Pain Intensity Difference (SPID) is used to summarize the pain difference at various time points for each group. It was calculated as , where ti=specific time interval, n=number of intervals, and PIDi=pain intensity difference for the specific time interval.21

Sample Size Calculation

The calculation of sample size was based on the primary hypothesis of the study, which proposed that patients receiving LB-ACB would exhibit a superior analgesic effect at 24 hours postoperatively. We anticipated a median difference of 2 points in static pain scores when compared to the control group. Adhering to the methodology outlined by Pascarella et al,22 we assumed a mean pain score of 2 with a standard deviation of 1.5, utilizing a truncated Gaussian distribution model. With a two-sided α = 5% with 90% statistical power (β = 0.10), while factoring in an estimated drop-out rate of 10%, a minimum of 30 participants per group was required. Consequently, the total sample size was calculated to be 90 participants.

Statistical Analysis

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA for normally distributed variables with homogeneous variances, and when appropriate, followed by the Least Significant Difference test for post hoc pairwise comparisons. Welch’s ANOVA was planned when the assumption of homogeneity of variance was violated; however, for variables requiring post hoc pairwise comparisons, homogeneity of variance was met, and standard one-way ANOVA was therefore used. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (Q1, Q3) and were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis H-test; when the overall difference was significant (P < 0.05), pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U-test with Bonferroni correction. Categorical variables were expressed as number (percentage) and compared using the χ2 test. All tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Time to first opioid use was analyzed as a time-to-event outcome. Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed, and group differences were assessed using the Log rank test. Patients who did not require rescue analgesia were treated as censored observations. To evaluate the longitudinal changes across groups and time points, linear mixed-effects models were used for pain scores and ROM, whereas quadriceps strength was analyzed using a cumulative logit mixed-effects model. The ordinal model included group, time, and the group-by-time interaction as fixed effects, with participant-specific random intercepts to account for repeated measurements. The control group and 24 hours were used as the reference categories. The ordinal outcome categories were coded in ascending order, with higher scores indicating greater quadriceps strength. The cumulative logit mixed-effects model was fitted using the clmm function in the ordinal package in R. Data analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., 2017, IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.) and R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Patients who underwent LB for ACB displayed markedly reduced 24-hour static and dynamic pain scores, relative to the R-ACB and control groups patient (P < 0.001). Notably, no marked differences were noted between the R-ACB and control groups in the static and dynamic pain scores at 24 hours postoperatively (P > 0.05).

As presented in Table 2, significant differences were observed among the three groups in static pain scores at 6 hours (P = 0.029), 12 hours (P = 0.003), and dynamic pain scores at 6 hours (P = 0.002) and 12 hours (P < 0.001). No significant intergroup differences were presented at 48 or 72 hours for either static or dynamic pain scores, except for dynamic pain at 48 hours, which was lower in the LB-ACB group than in the control group (P2 = 0.020). Secondary outcomes, including rescue analgesia requirements, morphine consumption, adverse events, and recovery parameters, were shown in Table 4. The mean time to first opioid was longer in the LB-ACB group compared with the R-ACB group (log-rank P = 0.013; Figure 4). No significant differences were observed among the groups in terms of rescue analgesia rate, total morphine consumption, incidence of adverse events such as pneumonia, nausea and vomiting, dizziness, delirium, or lower extremity venous thrombosis, first ambulation time, or length of hospital stay. Notably, no cases of lower limb motor block were reported in any group. Furthermore, no significant differences were identified in first ambulation time or length of hospital stay across the three groups. This finding suggests that although LB-ACB provides superior early analgesia and extends the time to first rescue analgesia, this benefit does not translate into reduced total opioid consumption when a standard multimodal analgesic regimen is used, including fixed-dose celecoxib. Lastly, no participants experienced lower limb motor blockade.

Sensitivity Analysis

As shown in Table S1, significant main effects of group were observed for static pain scores, with both the LB-ACB group [estimate = −0.800, 95% confidence interval (CI): −1.36 to −0.24, P = 0.006] and the R-ACB group (estimate = −0.700, 95% CI: −1.26 to –0.14, P = 0.015) exhibiting lower scores compared with the control group. Significant time effects were exhibited at 12, 24, and 48 hours relative to the 6‑hour baseline (all P < 0.05). A group-by-time interaction was observed for the LB-ACB group at 24 hours (estimate = –1.167, 95% CI: −1.88 to −0.45, P = 0.002), indicating a reduction in static pain scores over time compared with the control group. Additionally, the LB-ACB group showed a significant interaction at 72 hours (estimate = 0.900, 95% CI: 0.18 to 1.62, P = 0.014), suggesting a differential temporal pattern relative to the control group (Figure S1).

For dynamic pain scores (Table S2), both the LB-ACB group (estimate = −1.100, 95% CI: −1.71 to −0.49, P < 0.001) and the R-ACB group (estimate = −0.900, 95% CI: −1.51 to −0.29, P = 0.004) demonstrated lower scores compared with the control group. Time effects were significant across all post‑baseline assessments (all P < 0.01). A group-by-time interaction was identified for the LB-ACB group at 24 hours (estimate = −1.467, 95% CI: −2.22 to −0.72, P < 0.001), indicating a more significant reduction in dynamic pain scores over time relative to the control group (Figure S2).

For quadriceps strength, the predicted probabilities derived from the cumulative logit mixed-effects model are shown in Figure S3. In the control group, the predicted probability was primarily observed at lower quadriceps strength scores at 24 hours, and gradually shifted toward higher scores at 48 and 72 hours. In the LB-ACB group, the probability distribution was shifted toward higher quadriceps strength scores as early as 24 hours, and a greater probability of higher scores was observed at 72 hours. In the R-ACB group, the predicted probabilities also showed a gradual shift from lower to higher scores over time. Overall, the predicted probability patterns suggested time-dependent improvement in quadriceps strength in all groups, with the LB-ACB group showing a relatively more favorable distribution of higher quadriceps strength scores.

Regarding knee ROM (Table S3), a significant main effect of group was found for the LB-ACB group relative to the control group (estimate = 10.500, 95% CI: 3.20 to 17.80, P = 0.005). Time effects were significant at 48 and 72 hours compared with 24 hours (both P < 0.001). A group-by-time interaction was observed for the LB-ACB group at 72 hours (estimate = −8.233, 95% CI: −15.29 to −1.18, P = 0.023), indicating that the initial improvement in ROM in the LB-ACB group attenuated over time relative to the control group (Figure S4). No significant interactions were observed for the R-ACB group at any time point.

Discussion

ACB is rapidly gaining popularity as a critical component of multimodal analgesia protocols following TKA.23 Unlike FNB, which causes considerable quadriceps muscle weakness and late mobilization, ACB selectively targets the saphenous nerve and femoral nerve branches to provide highly efficacious analgesia while preserving quadricep motor function.24,25 Prior investigations revealed that ACB decreases quadriceps muscle strength by 8% only, delivers analgesic effects comparable to FNB, and preserves quadriceps function, while enabling earlier ambulation and accelerated early rehabilitation.26 Based on the results of the current study, patients receiving ACB experienced reduced static and dynamic pain scores at 6 and12 hours postoperatively, relative to the control group-receiving patients. This result corroborated findings by Rhatomy et al, whose randomized controlled trial established that a single ACB shot significantly diminishes postoperative pain and analgesic consumption in TKA-undergoing patients.27 Given these evidences, it is obvious that ACB, in combination with standardized PAI and postoperative multimodal analgesia, produces a synergistic effect that effectively manages moderate to severe early postoperative pain in TKA patients. Additionally, ACB does not impede early mobilization or joint ROM exercises, which successfully deters joint stiffness and optimizes long-term functional recovery. As a result, in this study, none of the ACB-administered patients experienced lower limb motor blockade.

LB single-injection ACB delivers a time-dependent analgesic advantage during the critical postoperative 12–24-hour window, likely due to its unique DepoFoam sustained-release technology. Prior investigations reported that LB usage can prolong analgesia duration.28,29 Herein, we demonstrated that, at 24 hours postoperatively, a single LB-ACB injection significantly diminished the static and dynamic pain scores, relative to the ropivacaine and placebo blocks. Moreover, the LB-ACB administration was associated with improved quadriceps muscle strength and knee ROM. These functional improvements may result from superior analgesic influences, whereby reduced pain intensity encourages more active participation in early rehabilitation.30,31 Concurrently, we observed that TKA patients receiving a single dosage of ropivacaine ACB demonstrated a progressively declining analgesic effect within 24 hours postoperatively, whereas those receiving LB maintained stable analgesia with the time to first analgesic rescue extended to 35.56 hours. This difference may be due to the limited analgesia duration linked to ropivacaine usage.32

Notably, the analgesic advantage of a single LB-ACB injection gradually waned after 24 hours, with no significant differences in pain scores or functional outcomes between the groups at 48 and 72 hours postoperatively. The comparison results of SPID at each time point among the groups provide further evidence, as shown in Figure 3. This outcome may be the result of the anatomical features of the adductor canal block itself. The Tran et al’s cadaveric proximal ACB staining displayed pigmentation in the saphenous nerve, the posterior medial branch of the medial femoral cutaneous nerve, the superior genicular nerve, and the obturator nerve articular branch only.33 Hence, the ACB technique does not reach the nociceptive afferents originating from the posterior and lateral regions of the knee joint.34 Given this limitation, as the postoperative pain progresses, the nociceptive input from the unblocked regions may gradually counteract the residual analgesic effect of LB. Furthermore, the pharmacokinetic characteristics of LB produce a gradual decline in drug release after 24 hours of administration, which further reduces its efficacy in managing breakthrough pain during the late postoperative period.35

Simultaneously, routinely using celecoxib within the multimodal analgesia protocol after surgery may mask the pain rebound that occurs following the decline of the ACB effect, thereby no significant differences in total opioid consumption among the groups. Moreover, this fixed-schedule celecoxib protocol may have produced a “ceiling effect”, which could further explain the absence of variation in opioid use.36 Future studies incorporating as-needed NSAID administration are warranted to evaluate the opioid-sparing potential of LB-ACB more accurately. In their study, Hubler et al compared the analgesic impacts of LB and bupivacaine in ACB among TKA patients, and demonstrated an early postoperative advantage with LB usage.37 Their finding closely mirrors that of a retrospective study by MA et al evaluating LB efficacy in delivering postoperative analgesia following total hip arthroplasty.38 The study demonstrated that LB offered superior analgesia within 24 hours, relative to bupivacaine. However, no obvious differences were recorded in the pain scores at 48 and 72 hours postoperatively or in the total morphine consumption within 24 hours. However, the meta-analysis by Ye et al suggests that compared to commonly used local anesthetics, LB single-injection ACB can reduce opioid consumption in patients undergoing TKA.16 The inconsistency of the results may be related to the sample size. In addition, Hussain et al reported that, among adult patients undergoing transversus abdominis plane block, LB versus conventional local anesthetics usage results in no marked difference in the area under the curve of static pain severity scores between the 24- to 72-hour period postoperatively.39 Peng et al further indicated that variations in surgical approaches, anesthesia techniques, and ceiling effect of multimodal analgesia may account for the LB efficacy inconsistencies in postoperative analgesia.15 In conclusion, a single LB-ACB injection can produce substantial gains during the early postoperative pain period, as was demonstrated by the significantly prolonged time to analgesic rescue in this study.

The clinical significance of this study lies in the use of a double-blind randomized control trial to validate the role of ACB under multimodal analgesia following TKA, as well as the influence of local anesthetics with varying analgesic durations. Our findings indicate that the conventional single-injection ropivacaine ACB may achieve limited analgesic control over an extended postoperative period (beyond 12 hours), possibly due to the ability of the traditional combined regimen of “cocktail” local infiltration and oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs in delivering satisfactory analgesic effects.40,41 A study by Youm et al demonstrated that during early postoperative analgesia following TKA (0–8 hours), PAI efficacy is superior to FNB, yet patients receiving PAI experience rebound pain at 24 hours.42 This is likely due to the insufficient duration of PAI-induced analgesia provided by the commonly used local anesthetics, specifically ropivacaine or bupivacaine. Effective early pain control can significantly reduce chronic pain incidence.43 Meanwhile, the postoperative 12 to 24-hour period represents a critical window for preventing pain-associated complications, while facilitating early mobilization and reduced opioid usage.44 Prior research reported that additional ACB offers limited benefits for TKA patients, with progressively increasing early postoperative pain scores, potentially due to insufficient analgesic duration of commonly used local anesthetics.10 In the current study, TKA patients receiving a single administration of LB-ACB experienced a prolonged time to first analgesic rescue, averaging 35.56 hours, along with significantly reduced 24-hour static and dynamic pain scores relative to ropivacaine, while demonstrating comparable safety. Given this evidence, we consider a single LB-ACB injection regimen to have remarkable clinical significance. Considering the potentially high cost-effectiveness of LB, it is recommended for use in patients at a high risk of experiencing pain.

It is imperative to acknowledge some limitations of our research. Firstly, the sample size was relatively small, which may affect generalizability, although the statistical power was sufficient to detect clinically significant differences in the primary outcomes. Secondly, this study was a single-center trial, and our findings may be influenced by the surgical techniques and rehabilitation protocols specific to our institution. Thirdly, this investigation did not examine long-term functional outcomes or chronic pain, which may be pertinent to the evaluation of sustained effects of early pain control on long-term recovery. Finally, although manual quadricep muscle strength testing is both clinically practical and convenient, it lacks the objectivity of quantitative measurements and may, therefore, be subject to subjective bias.45 Additional investigations are warranted to address these limitations using large sample size and multicenter trials. Moreover, combining LB-ACB with posterior knee blocks (such as, IPACK) can theoretically extend analgesic coverage to the posterolateral region of the knee joint, which may enable further exploration of more comprehensive analgesic effects on analgesia duration.46 Lastly, this study only included patients classified as ASA I–III, and is not applicable to high-risk populations (such as, patients with chronic pain or multiple comorbidities).

Although this study demonstrates the clinical value of a single-shot LB-ACB, further refinement of postoperative pain management remains an important objective. With ongoing advances in digital healthcare, artificial intelligence offers a promising approach, as highlighted by D’Errico et al47 AI algorithms can continuously analyze patients’ VAS scores, forecast pain trajectories, and identify individuals likely to require rescue analgesia at an early stage, thereby enabling personalized and dynamically adjusted analgesic strategies. In addition, AI-enabled remote monitoring platforms can extend clinical oversight beyond hospital discharge by tracking pain scores, activity levels, and adverse events in real time via mobile applications. Such systems can detect abnormal trends and promptly initiate alerts or interventions, thereby improving postoperative care efficiency and reducing nursing workload, key challenges in current clinical practice.

Herein, we demonstrated that a single administration of LB-ACB delivers both safe and prolonged analgesia lasting up to 24 hours for TKA patients, facilitating early functional recovery, however, there is no marked gain beyond this time period. This finding supports the use of a single-injection LB-ACB as an important adjunct in multimodal analgesia for TKA. ACB is an effective component of multimodal analgesia for postoperative TKA patients, however, local anesthetics with varying durations of analgesia may influence its efficacy assessment.
