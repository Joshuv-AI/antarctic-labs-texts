Event-free survival as a surrogate for overall survival in locally advanced esophageal cancer: a correlation analysis of trials assessing definitive chemoradiation therapies
Authors: Weiguang Xue, Adriana Valderrama, Karthik Ramakrishnan, Jenny Zhou, Yan Meng, Sonal Bordia, Manish A Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTreatments for esophageal cancer are evolving rapidly, highlighting the need for surrogate endpoints to expedite patient access to therapy. Overall survival (OS) remains the gold standard endpoint in oncology clinical trials, but its assessment often requires extended follow-up, delaying treatment evaluations. Event-free survival (EFS) has emerged as a promising surrogate for OS in resectable locally advanced esophageal cancer. However, its validity in the context of definitive chemoradiotherapy (dCRT), the standard treatment for unresectable disease, has not been established. We assessed the trial-level correlation between EFS and OS in this setting.MethodsA systematic literature review identified randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating dCRT for unresectable locally advanced esophageal cancer. Studies reporting hazard ratios (HRs) for both EFS (or similarly defined early endpoints) and OS were included. Weighted linear regression (WLR) was used to assess the correlation between log(HR) for EFS and log(HR) for OS. The strength of the correlation was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2), with thresholds of ≥ 0.65 or ≥ 0.75 indicating good surrogacy. The robustness of the WLR model and correlation analyses was confirmed by leave-one-out cross-validations and sensitivity analyses, respectively.ResultsFourteen between-treatment comparisons from 11 RCTs (N = 2812) in unresectable locally advanced esophageal cancer were included. Eight studies were conducted in China and three in Europe, with sample size and follow-up ranging from 86 to 434 patients and 24 to 60 months, respectively. Reported median EFS was 17.5 months, whereas median OS was 25.1 months. Most patients had advanced-stage squamous cell carcinoma.The correlation analysis identified a strong EFS–OS correlation (estimated slope coefficient: 0.91; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.62–1.20, P<0.001; R2=0.80; 95% CI, 0.38–0.96), indicating that EFS was a significant predictor of OS. The leave-one-out cross-validation confirmed this association, with 71.4% of observed OS HRs falling within the predicted 95% CIs. Sensitivity analyses further supported the robustness of the correlation.ConclusionsWe identified a strong, statistically significant, correlation between EFS and OS, suggesting that EFS may serve as a valid surrogate endpoint for OS in patients with unresectable locally advanced esophageal cancer receiving dCRT, with potential to streamline clinical trials and regulatory assessments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15914-6.Keywords: Esophageal neoplasms, Locally advanced cancer, Definitive chemoradiotherapy, Unresectable tumors, Correlation analysis, Surrogate endpoints, Event-free survival, Overall survival

Background

Esophageal cancer is the eighth most common cancer globally and poses a substantial disease burden owing to its high morbidity and mortality [1–3]. Approximately 600,000 people were diagnosed with, and 544,000 died from, esophageal cancer in 2020 [2]. Esophageal cancer comprises two distinct subtypes. The most common is esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, which is predominant in the Asia, East Africa and South America, and has well-known risk factors, such as tobacco use, alcohol consumption, and a nutritionally poor diet [4]. By contrast, esophageal adenocarcinoma is primarily linked to obesity, gastroesophageal reflux disease, Barrett’s esophagus, and decreasing Helicobacter pylori seropositivity and is more prevalent in the West [4]. Gastroesophageal junction cancers (GEJ), typically classified as gastric cancers, share clinical and pathological features with esophageal adenocarcinoma [5].

Management is challenging due to tumor location and aggressive course, requiring multimodality and multidisciplinary approaches [1, 6, 7]. Treatment with curative intent generally involves neoadjuvant chemotherapy, chemoradiotherapy, surgery, or definitive chemoradiation (dCRT). Surgical resection remains the standard for early-stage disease, whereas dCRT is preferred for inoperable cases [1, 7].

Despite multimodal curative intent therapies, approximately 40% of patients experience recurrence and have poor prognosis, with estimated 3- and 5-year overall survival (OS) rates of 39.5% and 26.5%, respectively [8].

OS is the primary outcome for evaluating treatment efficacy in cancer clinical trials but is challenging to evaluate in early-stage disease. Large studies with long duration of follow-up are often needed for OS data maturity, and confounding factors (subsequent therapies, patient heterogeneity, varying follow-up times) hinder the interpretation of OS results across clinical studies. Early clinical endpoints such as disease-free survival (DFS), event-free survival (EFS), and progression-free survival (PFS) occur more frequently, requiring smaller sample sizes and less time to mature, potentially accelerating access to promising treatments [9–11]. These are increasingly used as surrogate measures for OS in oncology clinical trials. Both EFS and PFS are listed in the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Table of Surrogate Endpoints [12], and several oncology treatments have received FDA approval based on improvements in an early endpoint [13]. The European Medicines Agency and the FDA are also increasingly relying on surrogate measures for accelerated approval [9].

For early endpoints to be adopted as surrogates for OS, a strong and reliable association between the early surrogate endpoint and OS must be demonstrated [13]. Trial-level correlation analyses provide robust evidence of surrogacy by evaluating whether early endpoints accurately predict treatment effect on OS [14]. The strength of this correlation is measured by the coefficient of determination (R²); R2 values ≥ 0.7 are considered reliable surrogacy [9].

Few studies have evaluated the robustness of the correlation between early endpoints and OS in esophageal cancer; those that did focused on patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy [15–17], and most reported strong DFS–OS correlations [15, 16]. However, the EFS-OS correlation has not been demonstrated in patients with locally advanced esophageal cancer receiving dCRT, highlighting a crucial unmet need. To address this gap, we evaluated the trial-level correlation between EFS and OS in unresectable locally advanced esophageal cancer treated with dCRT. Although dCRT regimens and follow-up practices vary across institutions and trials, trial-level surrogacy analyses are specifically intended to evaluate whether treatment effects on earlier endpoints consistently translate into treatment effects on OS across therapeutic contexts. Demonstrating such consistency is clinically meaningful for establishing an early endpoint that is robust across real-world variability in dCRT delivery.

Methods

Systematic literature review

Clinical trials evaluating dCRT efficacy in unresectable locally advanced esophageal cancer were identified through a systematic literature review (SLR) conducted according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [18].

Studies published from inception to April 27, 2023, were retrieved from the Embase, Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE), MEDLINE In-process, Cochrane Controlled Register of Trials (CENTRAL), and Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (CDSR) databases. We considered randomized controlled trials (RCTs), non-RCTs, observational studies, and single-arm trials that met predefined eligibility criteria. Studies of adults (≥ 18 years) with newly diagnosed treatment-naïve, unresectable locally advanced (stages II-IVa) esophageal cancer who received dCRT were included. Studies conducted on populations other than those of interest and non-English-language publications were excluded.

Data from included studies were extracted into a predefined grid, and all RCTs were critically appraised for potential bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (version 2.0) [19].

Feasibility assessment

The feasibility of a correlation analysis across identified studies was determined by applying additional selection criteria: randomized controlled design, ≥ 40 patients in each randomized treatment arm, reported hazard ratio (HR) of early outcomes (EFS, PFS, DFS, relapse-free survival, recurrence-free survival, or distant-recurrence-free survival), and reported HR of OS.

Treatment effect on early outcomes and OS was evaluated from HRs; when these were missing, they were estimated using the method outlined in Guyot et al. from published Kaplan-Meier curves; [20] HRs and associated confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated from pseudo-individual patient data using Cox proportional hazards models.

Study endpoints

The final outcome was OS, defined as time from randomization to death from any causes. The early outcome was EFS, defined as time from randomization to the earliest of disease progression, distant metastases development, or death.

In this study, EFS represents a unifying term for early time-to-event endpoints occurring prior to death, recognizing that included trials adopted this concept using related but not identical definitions (e.g., PFS or DFS) depending on clinical context. A summary of early endpoint terminology, starting time, and event components across included trials is provided in Table 1. Although individual trials reported PFS, DFS, or related early endpoints with minor variation in terminology and starting time, only RCTs reporting early endpoints that included disease progression or recurrence and/or death as event components were included in the main analysis. The impact of definitional differences on study conclusions was evaluated through sensitivity analyses.

Statistical analysis

This trial-level correlation analysis evaluates whether relative treatment effects on early endpoints predict relative treatment effects on OS across trials employing diverse dCRT regimens, follow-up schedules, and endpoint assessment practices. We used weighted linear regression (WLR) to evaluate the correlation between EFS log(HR), the independent variable, and OS log(HR), the dependent variable. The unit observation in the regression was one comparison between two randomized arms. The weight of the performed analysis was based on the number of patients in each comparison, enabling greater influence from more precise treatment-effect estimates. Sample size was used as it is the most frequently applied approach in prior surrogacy analyses and has been consistently implemented in the literature [15, 17], including in a previous systematic review by Xie et al. [34]. This method appropriately assigns greater influence to trials contributing more robust information to the estimated association. If a study included three randomized arms, it contributed two sets of pairwise comparisons. An intercept term was used to allow for the possibility of non-zero OS effects when the EFS effect is null, which is consistent with prior surrogacy analyses and reflects potential downstream influences on OS not fully captured by EFS [17].

The robustness of the trial-level correlation between the treatment effects on EFS and OS was assessed in the main analysis by calculating R2 and the corresponding 95% CI. The percentile method with 10,000 bootstrap iterations was used to estimate the 95% CI for R2. Values of R2 ≥0.65 [9] and ≥ 0.75 [35] were considered indicative of good surrogacy.

The accuracy and consistency of the WLR model in predicting the HR for OS in the main analysis was evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validations. The regression model was run multiple times, each time leaving out one observation, and the OS HR was estimated based on the EFS HR in the observation that was left out. We assessed whether plots showed consistency in the direction of observed and predicted OS HRs (> 1.0 or < 1.0) and if the observed OS HRs fell within the 95% prediction interval.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted by adding or removing studies from the pool of RCTs included in the main analysis, based on characteristics such as study location and the definition of early clinical endpoints.

Results

Study characteristics

Of 36 RCTs identified through the SLR, 11 studies [21–31] comprising 14 between-treatment comparisons and 2812 patients met feasibility assessment criteria and were included in the main analysis (Fig. 1). Studies that did not report EFS, OS, HRs, or survival curves were excluded.

PRISMA flow diagram showing records identified, screened, excluded, and included in the final analysis. NMA, network meta-analysis; nRCT, non-randomized controlled trials

The main analysis included RCTs reporting similar definitions of EFS or PFS; their characteristics are presented in Table 1 and Supplemental Table S1. PFS was the early endpoint in all studies, and was evaluated from randomization (n = 8) [21–24, 28–31], enrolment (n = 2) [26, 27], or treatment initiation (n = 1) [25]. Eight studies were conducted in China [24–31] and three in Europe [21–23]. The median follow-up was 24–48 months in seven studies [22–24, 27–29, 31], > 48 months in three studies [25, 26, 30], and not reported in one study [21]. Median EFS and OS were 17.5 months and 25.1 months, respectively. Most patients across studies had high-risk disease, as indicated by the predominance of squamous cell carcinoma and advanced tumor stage, but manageable physical performance (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group score: 0–1). All studies were conducted before April 2019, predating the approval of immunotherapy as a first-line treatment for esophageal cancer. Risk of bias arising from randomization was low in four studies [22, 25, 28, 29] and moderate in seven studies; [21, 23, 24, 26, 27, 30, 31] there were no studies with high risk of bias (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Correlation analysis

Correlation between the log(HR) of EFS and OS

The main WLR analysis of 14 between-treatment comparisons from 11 RCTs found a strong statistical correlation. The estimated slope coefficient was 0.91 (95% CI, 0.62–1.20; P < 0.001) and R2 was 0.80 (95% CI, 0.38–0.96), indicating that log(HR) of EFS was a significant predictor of log(HR) of OS (Fig. 2).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the findings of the main findings. Inclusion of all RCTs regardless of whether an EFS definition was reported yielded 17 between-treatment comparisons from 13 RCTs; the estimated slope coefficient was 0.84 (95% CI, 0.56–1.13; P < 0.001) and R2 was 0.73 (95% CI, 0.35–0.94), consistent with a strong EFS-OS correlation. Restricting the analysis to 11 between-treatment comparisons from eight RCTs conducted in China produced an estimated slope coefficient of 0.92 (95% CI, 0.52–1.33; P = 0.001) and an R2 of 0.75 (95% CI, 0.21–0.95). Leave-one-out cross-validation (Supplementary Fig. S2) showed that observed OS HRs were within the 95% CIs of predicted OS HRs in 47.1% (8/17) and 63.6% (7/11) of comparisons in the first and second sensitivity analyses, respectively.

Discussion

Our analysis of RCTs evaluating dCRT in unresectable locally advanced esophageal cancer suggests a strong trial-level correlation between EFS and OS (R² =0.80; 95% CI, 0.38–0.96), exceeding established surrogacy thresholds [9]. Leave-one-out cross-validation supported the association, with 71.4% of observed OS HRs falling within predicted CIs.

One trial (YP2010138; observation d) [24] emerged as an outlier, with high PFS HR and low OS HR for cisplatin plus 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) versus docetaxel plus 5-FU. This study is limited by small sample size (N = 86) and reliance on HRs derived from published Kaplan-Meier curves may have contributed to variability in estimated treatment effects. The reported PFS of 55 months in one arm is also unusually long and was not observed in any other trials.

Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the main findings. Including the two RCTs that lacked early endpoint definitions yielded an R2 of 0.73, while limiting the analysis to studies from China yielded an R2 of 0.75. Both values are consistent with published thresholds for good surrogacy (Ciani et al., Lassere et al.)

These findings suggest that, across heterogeneous dCRT regimens and study designs, improvements in EFS are tightly associated with corresponding benefits in OS at the trial level. This supports consideration of EFS as a pragmatic early endpoint in comparative dCRT trials, enabling more timely therapeutic benefit assessment without requiring identical treatment platforms or uniform surveillance strategies.

While use of an earlier endpoint does not remove uncertainty in estimating OS benefit, validating EFS as a trial-level surrogate helps quantify how well treatment effects on EFS predict treatment effects on OS, supporting earlier interpretation of comparative efficacy without waiting for long-term OS follow-up. This may enable more timely decision-making in drug development and regulatory evaluation, particularly when longer-term outcomes are not yet mature.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate EFS as a surrogate for OS in patients with unresectable locally advanced esophageal cancer treated with dCRT. Prior studies have primarily examined the correlation between early endpoints and OS in neoadjuvant or adjuvant settings. The strong EFS–OS correlation observed in our study is consistent with findings by Ajani et al. [15], who reported marked DFS/PFS–OS correlations in neoadjuvant, perioperative, and adjuvant esophageal cancer/GEJC. These findings were recently confirmed using patient-level real-world data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results-Medicare database [36], which showed an individual-level DFS–OS correlation of 0.76 (95% CI, 0.70–0.80), a between-treatment effects correlation of 0.96 (95% CI, 0.80–0.99), and high predictive accuracy (91% internal; 89% external) [36]. In contrast, Kataoka et al. [17] reported low PFS–OS correlations in the neoadjuvant setting, with R2 values of 0.28 (95% CI, 0.00–0.90) and 0.084 (95% CI, 0.00–0.70). However, this analysis included studies dating back to 1990, when esophagectomy-related mortality was high (11%), and was further limited by the use of landmark methods and missing PFS definitions in several studies [17].

Our study has limitations inherent to trial-level correlation analysis, including lack of standardized thresholds for meaningful EFS improvement and the potential for overfitting in leave-one-out analyses, which may limit generalizability. We used EFS as the early endpoint, although included RCTs used other early endpoints, such as PFS and DFS, with modest variations in definitions. Heterogeneity in early endpoint terminology and starting times across trials is a limitation; however, sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the observed EFS-OS association was not driven by any specific definition. Variations in dCRT regimens, radiation doses, and follow-up schedules were present, but these are inherent to the dCRT literature and reflect real-world practice; trial-level surrogacy methods are designed to assess whether early endpoints remain predictive of OS despite these differences, as supported by the robustness of the observed EFS-OS relationship despite such heterogeneity. In two trials (YP2010138 [24] and ESO-Shanghai 2 [25]), HRs were derived from published Kaplan-Meier curves, potentially affecting accuracy; however, these were reconstructed using established methodology and internal validation, and the trials were retained to preserve informative randomized comparisons. Our study was based on aggregated data, hindering our ability to perform patient-level stratified analyses, for example by smoking status, tumor stage, or histology. Aggregate trial-level data also precluded explicit modeling of within-trial correlation in multi-arm studies contributing more than one comparison, although only a small number of trials contributed multiple comparisons. Sensitivity to alternative model specifications was not formally assessed. The lack of global trials and studies reporting EFS also represents a limitation. Finally, post-treatment factors including subsequent therapies and late treatment-related complications were not accounted for, which may influence OS. Recent clinical trials conducted after approval of immunotherapies for advanced esophageal cancer may show improved OS in the locally advanced setting.

Notwithstanding these limitations, our study provides a robust analysis based on a comprehensive SLR and a large, pooled sample. OS, which captures death from any cause including treatment-related mortality, remains the definitive endpoint. The present analysis did not seek to replace OS but to evaluate whether EFS can serve as a trial-level surrogate to enable early comparative assessment while OS data mature. The strong EFS-OS correlation (R2 = 0.80), confirmed through multiple sensitivity analyses and assessed using established surrogacy frameworks, supports the reliability of our findings.

This study has important implications. It contributes to the limited research conducted in this field, which is especially relevant given that the FDA lists EFS as a trial-level surrogate for OS without direct evidence of its clinical relevance. With a reported median EFS of 17.5 months and a median OS of 25.1 months across included trials, EFS may allow estimation of treatment effects on OS around 8 months earlier, enabling more timely efficacy assessments and potentially expediting regulatory decision-making. These findings support the inclusion of EFS as an endpoint in future dCRT trials, which could streamline study design and facilitate the evaluation of treatments where long-term OS data are difficult to obtain. By validating EFS as a surrogate for OS, this study provides a valuable tool to guide treatment decisions and underscores the need for standardized early clinical endpoint definitions to improve result comparability across trials. Further global research with comprehensive patient data and standardized endpoint definitions is needed to validate EFS as a surrogate for OS in the dCRT for esophageal cancer setting.

Conclusions

This is the first study to evaluate EFS as a surrogate for OS in unresectable locally advanced esophageal cancer. Based on 11 clinical trials and over 2,800 patients, we identified a trial-level EFS–OS correlation, exceeding established surrogacy thresholds. These findings suggest that EFS may serve as a valid surrogate endpoint for OS in dCRT trials in unresectable locally advanced esophageal cancer.
