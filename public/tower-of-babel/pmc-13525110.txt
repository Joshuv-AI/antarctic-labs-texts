AO Spine Clinical Practice Recommendations: Proximal Junctional Kyphosis and Failure Prevention

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy DesignLiterature Review with expert consensus–based clinical practice recommendations.ObjectivesTo synthesize current evidence on risk factors and preventive strategies for proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK) and proximal junctional failure (PJF) following adult spinal deformity (ASD) surgery, and to provide clinically actionable recommendations.MethodsA focused literature review was conducted by the AO Spine Knowledge Forum Deformity, identifying 30 relevant studies on PJK/PJF. Four studies were selected based on relevance to prevention strategies. Each study was critically appraised using a standardized framework. Final recommendations were established through multidisciplinary consensus using predefined criteria.ResultsPJK/PJF are multifactorial complications with reported incidence ranging from 20–40%, often occurring within the first two years postoperatively. Evidence suggests that radiographic alignment targets alone have limited predictive value for mechanical complications. Moderate-quality evidence supports the use of combined prophylactic implant strategies and avoidance of sagittal overcorrection to reduce PJF risk. Bone mineral density (BMD) consistently demonstrates a strong association with junctional complications, with lower DEXA T-scores and CT Hounsfield units at the upper instrumented vertebra correlating with increased risk. Low-quality evidence supports ligament augmentation as a junctional protection strategy, demonstrating reductions in proximal junctional angle progression and PJF incidence.ConclusionsPrevention of PJK/PJF requires a multimodal approach integrating patient biology, surgical technique, construct design, and individualized alignment goals. Based on current evidence, we recommend routine assessment and of bone quality, avoidance of sagittal overcorrection, and consideration of prophylactic junctional protection in high-risk patients. These recommendations are primarily conditional, reflecting the predominance of observational data.Keywords: adult spinal deformity, spinal instrumentation, spine surgery, proximal junctional failure, proximal junctional kyphosis, clinical practice recommendations

Introduction

Proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK) and proximal junctional failure (PJF) remain among the most challenging and persistent complications following adult spinal deformity (ASD) surgery. Their clinical manifestations span a broad spectrum, ranging from incidental radiographic changes to structural collapse with deformity progression, severe pain, neurologic deficit, and, in extreme cases, catastrophic spinal cord compression.

As ASD surgery is increasingly offered to older, frail, and medically complex patients with more severe deformities, complications have correspondingly increased.1,2 Radiographic complications account for nearly 20% of all major complications in ASD surgery, with PJK and PJF comprising approximately two-thirds of these events. 3 Reported incidence varies widely (6–60%) due to differences in definitions, follow-up, and patient populations, though most series report rates between 20% and 40%.4,5

Both conditions most frequently develop early, within the first two years after surgery; though approximately 20% of PJF cases may occur later. 4 While PJK is often considered a radiographic finding with limited clinical impact, progression to PJF carries a much higher likelihood of requiring revision surgery, with reported reoperation rates between 10% and 47%.6,7 These revision procedures also represent a substantial financial burden, with associated costs commonly estimated between $55,000 and $195,000 per case.6,8,9

Defining clinically meaningful PJK remains challenging. The widely used Glattes criteria (proximal junctional angle ≥10° and ≥10° increase from baseline) provide a practical radiographic threshold but insufficiently capture clinical relevance. 5 Subsequent classification systems, including those by Hart and Boachie-Adjei, attempted to incorporate mechanisms of failure beyond angular measurements; however, these frameworks have not achieved universal adoption due to limited guidance on natural history and treatment decision-making.7,10

Ultimately, although a clinically meaningful radiographic definition of PJK remains debated, surgeons tend to be less concerned with PJK itself than with clinical deterioration to PJF, which ultimately drives the need for reoperation. Consequently, the focus has shifted from radiographic PJK alone toward identifying patients at risk of progression to clinically significant PJF and current research increasingly emphasizes integrating patient-, tissue-, and surgery-related risk factors to improve prediction and prevention.

This review integrates findings from four key studies addressing preventive strategies for PJK/PJF in adult patients undergoing long posterior instrumented thoracolumbar fusions terminating at the pelvis, encompassing both upper thoracic to pelvis and lower thoracic to pelvis constructs. The reviewed studies address patient-specific biological risk factors, surgical interventions, and alignment principles. We contextualize the strengths and limitations of these approaches and, drawing on both evidence and expert consensus, we aim to offer conditional clinical recommendations that balance existing tools with individualized clinical judgment.

Methods

Members of the AO Spine Knowledge Forum Deformity identified 30 clinically relevant articles addressing PJK/PJF, covering risk factors, prevention strategies, classification, and epidemiology. Following discussion and consensus review, four studies (Table 1) were selected for detailed analysis based on their relevance to prevention and clinical understanding of PJK/PJF in ASD. Each study was critically appraised by a designated author using a standardized framework evaluating: (1) clinical rationale, (2) study summary, (3) methodology, (4) quality of evidence (high, moderate, low), and (5) strength of recommendation (strong or conditional). Final recommendations were established during a multidisciplinary consensus meeting where quality of evidence and strength of recommendations were determined according to the predefined criteria. 11

Article Summaries With Recommendation

The recommendations apply specifically to adult patients undergoing long posterior instrumented thoracolumbar fusions terminating at the pelvis, encompassing upper thoracic to pelvis and lower thoracic to pelvis constructs. Cervical to pelvis fusions and long segment constructs not terminating at the pelvis represent distinct clinical entities and fall outside the scope of these recommendations.

Article 1

Dial BL, et al. The impact of lumbar alignment targets on mechanical complications after adult lumbar scoliosis surgery. Eur Spine J. Jun 2022;31(6):1573–1582. doi:10.1007/s00586-022-07200-3 12

Clinical Rationale

The study aimed to evaluate whether commonly used lumbar sagittal alignment targets, specifically age-adjusted alignment offsets and the Global Alignment and Proportion (GAP) score, can predict mechanical complications after surgery for adult symptomatic lumbar scoliosis. Mechanical complications were summarized into PJK and pseudarthrosis.

Study Summary

The study was a post-hoc analysis of 165 surgically treated patients from the multicenter ASLS cohort with a minimum 2-year follow-up. Mechanical complications included symptomatic PJK and pseudarthrosis/rod fracture. The predictive performance of GAP parameters and age-adjusted offsets was assessed using logistic regression and ROC analysis. The authors reported that 25% of patients developed mechanical complications (PJK: 12.7%; pseudarthrosis/rod fracture: 17%). The total GAP score showed poor predictive ability (AUC 0.57), and none of its individual parameters reliably discriminated risk of mechanical complications. Hyperlordosis >15° beyond GAP-defined ideal lumbar lordosis was associated with increased odds of mechanical complications and pseudarthrosis. A higher lumbar distribution index (∼90%) was associated with fewer mechanical complications compared with lower values. Age-adjusted alignment offsets (PI-LL, pelvic tilt, T1 pelvic angle) demonstrated no meaningful ability to predict mechanical complications such as PJK, and overcorrection did not increase the risk of PJK.

Methodological Review

The strengths of this study include its multicenter cohort, which enhances the generalizability of the findings, as well as long-term follow-up with objective assessment of mechanical complications. However, the retrospective observational design limits causal inference and together with the absence of an a priori sample size calculation and inadequate assessment of important contributing factors such as bone mineral density, represents important sources of bias and confounding, ultimately supporting a low overall quality of evidence rating.

Quality of evidence: Low.

Recommendations for Integrating Into Clinical Practice

Radiographic lumbar alignment targets, whether GAP-based or age-adjusted, do not reliably predict mechanical complications when used in isolation. Mechanical failure after adult lumbar scoliosis surgery is multifactorial, and patient factors (bone health, construct design, surgical technique, biology) likely play a dominant role.; however, we would make a conditional recommendation to avoid lumbar hyperlordosis and optimize lordosis distribution rather than strictly achieving GAP or age-adjusted “ideal” values.

Article 2

Line BG, et al. Effective Prevention of Proximal Junctional Failure in Adult Spinal Deformity Surgery Requires a Combination of Surgical Implant Prophylaxis and Avoidance of Sagittal Alignment Overcorrection. Spine (Phila Pa 1976). Feb 15 2020;45(4):258–267. doi:10.1097/brs.0000000000003249 13

Clinical Rationale

A variety of implant-based strategies, including transverse process hooks, transition rods, and polyethylene tethers, have been used to mitigate the risk of PJK and PJF. Overcorrection of regional and global sagittal alignment parameters, however, has been shown to adversely affect postoperative global sagittal alignment. Accordingly, this study evaluated whether the use of prophylactic implants for PJK/PJF prevention alone (e.g. hooks, cement, or tethers at the proximal junction), compared with strategies aimed at avoiding sagittal overcorrection through adherence to age-adjusted spinopelvic alignment targets, is associated with a reduced incidence of PJF.

Study Summary

The authors performed a propensity score–matched analysis of surgically treated ASD patients from a multicenter database. Included were adults (>18 years) undergoing posterior fusion of at least five levels with a minimum of one year of follow-up. PJF was defined using region-specific angular and anterolisthesis thresholds derived from prior sensitivity analyses. Patients were categorized based on use of prophylactic implants for PJF prevention (IMPLANT vs NONE), including cement augmentation, transverse process hooks, or tethers. Postoperative sagittal alignment was also assessed, with patients classified as overcorrected (OVER) or aligned (ALIGN) based on age-adjusted targets.

Of 834 eligible patients, 625 met follow-up criteria (mean follow-up 2.6 years), with an overall PJF incidence of 13.9% and a surgical revision rate for PJF of 7.4%. PJF incidence was higher in NONE compared with IMPLANT (20.3% vs 10.6%), although revision rates were similar. Among implant strategies, PJF incidence did not differ significantly across cement, hook, and tether groups, although hooks were associated with a lower PJF incidence compared with NONE. Overcorrection without implant prophylaxis (OVER-NONE) demonstrated the highest PJF incidence (24.2%) and revision rates (11.7%), as well as earlier failure on Kaplan–Meier analysis. In contrast, among patients receiving implant prophylaxis, PJF incidence and revision rates were similar between OVER and ALIGN groups.

Methodological Review

The database is prospective and observational, without randomization or protocol-driven control of treatment assignment. While the multicenter design limits granularity in surgical indications and technical standardization, it improves external validity and generalizability. The large sample size and use of propensity score matching strengthen the overall quality of evidence and partially mitigate treatment selection bias, supporting an overall rating of moderate-quality evidence despite the observational design. The manuscript does not include a power analysis. While the overall sample size is reasonable for comparing implant prophylaxis versus no prophylaxis, the individual implant subgroups are relatively small and uneven. Based on the reported event rates, the study appears adequately powered for the overall comparison, and likely for hook constructs versus no prophylaxis, but not for most of the implant-specific subgroup analyses. As such, the lack of significant differences between implant types should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect limited statistical power rather than true equivalence. As a retrospective observational study, it is inherently limited by potential selection bias and residual confounding, particularly given the non-standardized use of different prophylactic strategies. While the relatively large cohort and clinically meaningful reduction in PJF with implant prophylaxis support a moderate level of confidence for the primary comparison (implant vs none), the study is underpowered for implant-specific subgroup analyses.

Quality of evidence: Moderate.

Recommendations for Integrating Into Clinical Practice

Adult spinal deformity patients who did not receive prophylactic surgical implants and who experienced sagittal overcorrection demonstrated the highest rates of PJF. Accordingly, we suggest a conditional recommendation of a combined strategy of prophylactic implant use and avoidance of sagittal overcorrection, guided by alignment parameters, to individualize spinal alignment and reduce the risk of potentially catastrophic PJF.

Article 3

Chen JW, et al. The association between bone mineral density and proximal junctional kyphosis in adult spinal deformity: a systematic review and meta-analysis. J Neurosurg Spine. Jul 1 2023;39(1):82–91. doi:10.3171/2023.2.Spine221101 . 14

Clinical Rationale

Low bone mineral density (BMD) is increasingly cited as a significant contributor to the development of PJK and PJF. Previous studies have used multiple methods of assessment for, and different definitions of low BMD. This study purports to unify previous publications to clarify the risk carried by low BMD on development of this common complication and thus provide recommendations for clinical practice.

Study Summary

Among 2,185 patients undergoing correction of adult spinal deformity, 537 developed PJK. BMD was assessed using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) in 11 of 18 studies, with 8 studies reporting comparable T-scores. The mean T-score was −1.43 in patients without PJK compared with −2.14 in those who developed PJK. In addition, six studies evaluated preoperative vertebral bone quality using computed tomography–derived Hounsfield units (HU), with four measuring HU at the upper-most instrumented vertebra (UIV). Patients who developed PJK demonstrated significantly lower bone density at the UIV, with mean HU values 32.35 units lower than those without PJK (169.62 vs 137.40, respectively).

Methodological Review

The authors conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis following PRISMA guidelines, searching PubMed and Web of Science for studies published between 2002 and 2022 using the terms PJK or PJF in combination with BMD, HU, or DEXA. Ninety-one studies were initially identified and screened by two independent reviewers, with methodological quality assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa scale. Eighteen studies ultimately met inclusion criteria. Reported odds or hazard ratios, ROC-derived cutoff values, and effect sizes were analyzed using a random-effects model, with heterogeneity and publication bias assessed through standard meta-analytic methods.

This methodology follows established standards for systematic reviews and meta-analyses, and the large-pooled sample size enhances the precision and generalizability of the findings. Though it suggests a higher level of evidence, its strength is constrained by the quality of the underlying studies. All included studies are retrospective and non-randomized, introducing potential selection bias and confounding, so the findings reflect associations rather than causation. The authors report statistically significant differences in BMD measures between patients with and without PJK, but there is moderate heterogeneity, variability in measurement methods (DEXA vs Hounsfield units), and some inconsistency in outcome definition. Despite these limitations, the study is methodologically rigorous and includes a large-pooled sample, furthermore the signal is consistent and clinically meaningful, supporting the relevance of bone quality. Taken together, the evidence is best viewed as moderate in quality.

Quality of evidence: Moderate.

Recommendations for Integrating Into Clinical Practice

We strongly recommend careful assessment of BMD in patients considered for correction of ASD, using DEXA and/or CT HU at the UIV. Lower T scores measured by DEXA and lower HU at the UIV were consistently and significantly associated with higher rates of PJK/F. While the effect of treatment of low BMD was not considered in this study, given the strength of the association between BMD and PJK/F, we conditionally recommend considering perioperative optimization of BMD as indicated with anabolic agents in this patient population.

Article 4

Safaee MM, et al,. Ligament augmentation for prevention of proximal junctional kyphosis and proximal junctional failure in adult spinal deformity. J Neurosurg Spine. 2018 May;28(5):512-519. doi: 10.3171/2017.9.SPINE1710. Epub 2018 Feb 23. PMID: 29473789 . 15

Clinical Rationale

PJK and PJF are thought to result from biomechanical stress at the transition zone between the rigid instrumentation and the untreated mobile segments. While multiple strategies have been proposed to mitigate this risk, there remains no consensus on the most effective approach. Ligament augmentation has been introduced as a technique designed to reinforce the posterior soft-tissue tension band at the upper instrumented vertebra (UIV), UIV+1, and UIV-1. This study evaluates whether ligament augmentation reduces the risk of PJK and PJF.

Study Summary

This study compared 100 consecutive patients treated with ligament augmentation to a consecutive cohort of 100 patients treated immediately before the introduction of the technique. In the augmentation group, sublaminar cables were placed through the spinous processes of the UIV, UIV+1, and UIV-1 to provide supplemental posterior support. The mean age was 66 years, two-thirds of the cohort were women, and approximately half were revision procedures.

The average increase in the Proximal Junctional Angle (PJA) was significantly lower in the augmentation group (6o) compared with the historical cohort (14o; p < 0.001). PJF occurred in 4 augmented patients versus 18 controls (p = 0.002). Rates of compression fractures at the UIV were similar despite higher use of vertebroplasty in the augmentation cohort. Multivariate analysis demonstrated that ligament augmentation was the only factor independently associated with smaller changes in PJA. Spinopelvic alignment parameters were comparable between groups and did not account for the observed differences.

Methodological Review

This study provides useful early evidence supporting ligament augmentation as a junctional protection strategy. However, its retrospective design with historical controls introduces important limitations, as surgical practices and adjunctive measures, such as UIV hooks and vertebroplasty, evolved over time, leaving potential for residual confounding despite multivariate adjustment. Although the mean follow-up of 12 months captures the early postoperative period when most PJK/PJF events occur, it does not allow assessment of long-term durability. In addition, the single-center experience and heterogeneous surgical indications limit generalizability. Nonetheless, the observed reduction in PJA progression and PJF is notable and consistent across analyses. Overall, the quality of evidence was rated as low due to the retrospective observational design and absence of randomized or controlled comparisons, despite conditional support for its use in high-risk patients.

Quality of evidence: Low.

Recommendation for Integrating Into Clinical Practice

Ligament augmentations is technically straightforward, adds minimal operative time, and carries low additional risk. Despite the retrospective nature of the data, the reduction in both radiographic PJK, and clinically significant PJF was noteworthy, therefore we conditionally recommend the use of ligament augmentation in ASD patients at higher risk, such as those undergoing long constructs, revision surgery, or 3-column osteotomies.

Discussion

Historical Evolution and Definitions: From Radiographic Observation to Clinical Failure

PJK/PJF have evolved from poorly characterized postoperative observations to a recognized and dreaded complication of ASD surgery. Despite the Glattes criteria’s widespread acceptance due to its simplicity and strong inter- and intraobserver reliability, subsequent experience revealed that radiographic PJK frequently fails to correlate with clinical outcomes, and clinical series have demonstrated comparable SRS scores in patients with and without PJK; suggesting that a low radiographic threshold captures many junctional changes that may be clinically inconsequential.5,7,16

In response, investigators sought to refine the definition by increasing the angular threshold to better identify clinically meaningful pathology. Helgeson et al 17 proposed a 15° cutoff in 2010, followed by Bridwell and colleagues, who increased the threshold to 20° in 2013. 18 Nevertheless, these higher thresholds similarly failed to consistently correlate with clinical deterioration or revision surgery.17,18 Most recently, Lovecchio et al applied decision curve analysis to determine PJA values predictive of revision surgery and reported that an absolute PJA of 28° and a change of 22° were the optimal cutoffs. 19 Although this represented a more data-driven approach, predictive performance remained limited and still failed to identify the majority of patients ultimately requiring revision, limiting clinical applicability.

In contrast to PJK, junctional failure is more consistently defined by features extending beyond angular measurements. PJF typically manifests as vertebral fractures at the UIV, UIV+1 or UIV+2, listhesis, ligamentous disruption, instrumentation failure, stenosis or progressive deformity associated with pain, neurologic compromise, or the need for revision surgery.4,20 Long-term follow-up studies demonstrate that nearly two-thirds of patients with initially asymptomatic PJK progress to PJF within five years, suggesting that PJK and PJF may represent points along a disease continuum rather than distinct entities, thus reinforcing the importance of prevention strategies. 4 Interestingly, the mechanism of failure appears to vary depending on UIV location. Compression fracture predominates in lower thoracic constructs, likely reflecting greater compressive loading vectors distally, whereas ligamentous disruption and angular progression are more common failure modes in upper thoracic constructs, where higher shear forces may play a greater role. 20 This biomechanical rationale could suggest that prevention strategies could reasonably be tailored to UIV location, favoring cement augmentation in lower thoracic and hook or tether constructs in upper thoracic configurations; however, direct comparative evidence supporting this distinction is currently lacking and prospective studies are needed before construct-specific recommendations can be made.

Physiologic adaptation rather than pathologic failure may explain some postoperative junctional changes. Lordosing of the upper lumbar and thoracic spine commonly compensates for sagittal malalignment, and restoration of lumbar lordosis may relax these compensatory mechanisms, resulting in a postoperative increase in PJA that should not be misinterpreted as pathologic PJK. Although normative segmental parameters could theoretically help define acceptable alignment changes, the wide variability in spinal morphology and compensation patterns makes reliable patient-specific thresholds impractical and difficult to establish.21,22 Ultimately, the goal is not simply to refine radiographic definitions of PJK, but to recognize patients at risk for progression and implement strategies that prevent junctional overload from the outset.

Evaluation and Optimization of BMD

A key message emerging from contemporary literature is that junctional failure is multifactorial, arising from the interaction between patient biology, surgical strategy, construct biomechanics, and postoperative alignment adaptation. No single variable adequately predicts failure and patient-related factors consistently influence risk. Advanced age, frailty, sarcopenia, obesity and especially reduced bone mineral density (BMD) have demonstrated strong associations with PJK and PJF.23,24

Preoperative BMD assessment is critical in ASD surgery, as low bone quality is strongly associated with proximal junctional complications and mechanical failure. Although DEXA has historically been used, lumbar measurements are frequently overestimated due to degenerative changes and prior instrumentation, prompting increasing use of CT-based HU measurements at the UIV and adjacent levels to better characterize local bone quality. Recent studies suggest that patients with HU values below roughly 160 at the UIV/UIV+1 have markedly higher rates of PJK and PJF, making this range a practical reference when assessing surgical risk.25-27 The systematic review and meta-analysis by Chen et al 14 included in our review, consolidated evidence showing that patients who develop junctional complications consistently demonstrate lower DEXA T-scores and reduced HU measurements at the UIV.

Poor bone quality predisposes patients to vertebral compression fractures and implant–bone interface failure, key mechanisms underlying PJF. Accordingly, careful preoperative bone quality assessment using both DEXA and CT-based HU measurements at or above the UIV is recommended, along with consideration of perioperative bone health optimization to reduce junctional complications after ASD surgery.

Common pharmacologic options include antiresorptive agents (bisphosphonates and denosumab), anabolic therapy (teriparatide) and most recently romosozumab, which combines anabolic and antiresorptive effects. No consensus exists regarding the optimal agent or duration of treatment. Among these therapies, teriparatide has been the most extensively studied and has been associated with reductions in vertebral fractures and implant-related complications. 28 Evidence also suggests that initiation even after surgery may be beneficial. 29 More recently, romosozumab has shown rapid improvements in bone quality, with short-term therapy increasing HU values at UIV+1 and reducing the incidence of PJK, supporting its potential role in reducing early instrumentation-related complications when surgical delay is not feasible.30,31

Optimization of Individualized Alignment Goals

Among the many factors implicated in proximal junctional complications, postoperative sagittal alignment consistently emerges as one of the strongest determinants of PJK/PJF risk. Multiple alignment classification systems or frameworks have been described in the literature. Most of them aiming to describe alignment targets to optimize outcomes, reduce mechanical complications and guide deformity correction. Earlier paradigms, including age-adjusted targets and the widely adopted SRS–Schwab classification, provided important foundations but lack the precision required for contemporary alignment planning.32,33 More recently, the T4–L1–hip axis framework has expanded upon concepts initially described in the Roussouly classification while incorporating continuous rather than categorical measurements.22,34

Restoring sagittal alignment has been shown to improve pain, function, and quality of life, but correction that exceeds a patient’s physiologic tolerance may shift excessive stress to the proximal junction, thus increasing the likelihood of mechanical complications. Findings from Dial et al 12 demonstrated that hyperlordosis beyond physiologic targets increased the risk of mechanical complications; however, strict adherence to alignment scores alone does not guarantee durability, underscoring that alignment must be contextualized within patient biology and construct design. Similarly, the paper by Line et al 13 showed that sagittal overcorrection, particularly in the absence of implant prophylaxis, significantly increased PJF incidence. Together, these findings emphasize that alignment should be individualized rather than simply maximized.

Accumulating evidence further indicates that both undercorrection and overcorrection relative to a patient’s “ideal” alignment increase the risk of PJK and PJF, underscoring the importance of proportional, patient-specific correction rather than pursuing absolute targets. While defining truly individualized alignment goals remains challenging, careful optimization of modifiable surgical and biomechanical factors remains essential to reducing mechanical complications in current practice. Alignment goals must therefore be interpreted within the broader clinical context, taking into account age, comorbidities, bone quality, and functional reserve. Ultimately, PJF often results from the cumulative biomechanical burden exceeding the patient’s biological capacity to tolerate correction.

Prevention Strategies

Surgical factors also play a critical role in modulating risk. Long constructs ending in the thoracic spine create abrupt transitions between rigid instrumentation and mobile segments, increasing mechanical stress at the proximal junction. Selection of the UIV, degree of soft-tissue disruption, construct stiffness, extensive corrections all amplify mechanical demands and influence proximal loading.23,35,36 Prevention strategies can be broadly categorized into optimization of alignment, protection of the junctional segment, and modification of biological risk factors. The complex and multifactorial mechanisms underlying PJK/PJF make it improbable that any single preventive measure can fully mitigate this complication.

Multiple surgical techniques have been proposed to mitigate the risk of PJK/PJF, largely by reducing stiffness mismatch at the junctional transition. These include posterior ligamentous augmentation using tethers or sublaminar bands, vertebral cement augmentation at junctional levels, placement of transverse process or laminar hooks above the UIV, and “soft-landing” strategies such as rod contouring, tapered rods, or transitional constructs designed to distribute forces more gradually across the proximal junction. 36 Biomechanical investigations consistently demonstrate that a gradual transition of motion theoretically reduces junctional overload. Translating these mechanical advantages into uniform clinical benefit has been challenging and clinical outcomes remain heterogeneous, with no universally accepted preventive strategy. Line et al demonstrated that prophylactic implants reduce PJF incidence, particularly when combined with avoidance of sagittal overcorrection. 13 Safaee et al 15 further demonstrated reduced PJA progression and PJF rates with ligament augmentation, suggesting that reinforcement of the posterior tension band mitigates junctional stress. Consistent findings across studies suggests that junctional protection strategies offer meaningful benefit, especially in high-risk patients undergoing long constructs.

Among the available options, ligamentous augmentation using tethers currently has the strongest clinical support. A recent comprehensive review identified 15 clinical studies evaluating proximal junctional tethering, of which 14 reported reductions in PJK and/or PJF compared with standard constructs. Despite variations in materials (polyethylene tape, sublaminar cables, or allograft constructs), fixation methods, weave patterns, and the number of instrumented adjacent levels (typically extending to UIV+1, UIV+2, or UIV+3), most studies demonstrate consistent reductions in junctional failure rates. 37 Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis reported that tether augmentation decreases the odds of PJK by 56% (OR 0.44) and PJF by 64% (OR 0.36), supporting its role as an effective preventive strategy. 38 Importantly, although tether placement increases index surgical cost by approximately $1,100 per case (In the U.S), it remains highly cost-effective by reducing the need for revision surgery. In a large clinical series, ligamentous augmentation lowered PJF rates by approximately 10.5%, corresponding to a number-needed-to-treat of 9.5 patients to prevent one revision procedure, yielding projected savings exceeding $1.1 million per 100 treated patients despite the modest increase in initial surgical cost. 39

Despite the absence of a universally accepted prophylactic technique, the cumulative evidence supports the use of junctional protection strategies in patients at risk for proximal junctional complications. While ongoing work is required to determine optimal configuration, tensioning, and patient selection, current data suggest that prophylactic junctional reinforcement, particularly with tether techniques, should be considered as part of a comprehensive PJK/PJF prevention strategy.

Integrating Prevention Into Clinical Practice

The four studies examined collectively (Table 1) highlight that PJK/PJF prevention requires a multimodal approach rather than reliance on any single intervention. Radiographic alignment, surgical technique, implant strategy, and biological factors must be simultaneously addressed. Clinical decision-making therefore requires balancing competing priorities: achieving adequate deformity correction while respecting patient tolerance, maximizing construct durability while minimizing surgical morbidity, and optimizing outcomes while acknowledging biological constraints. Surgeons must interpret alignment targets through the lens of age, frailty, bone quality, and expected functional demands.

The reviewed evidence supports several practical principles:

• Avoid excessive sagittal overcorrection, particularly hyperlordosis beyond physiologic requirements.

Despite advances, predictive accuracy for PJK/PJF remains limited. Future progress will likely emerge from integration of radiographic, biological, and functional data into predictive models capable of guiding surgical planning. Emerging research increasingly focuses on dynamic spinal behavior, muscle quality, and machine learning–based risk stratification models. Rather than categorizing patients into risk groups, future systems may provide individualized probability estimates of mechanical failure, allowing surgeons to tailor interventions accordingly.
