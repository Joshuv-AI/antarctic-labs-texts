ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn patients with acute respiratory failure (ARF) undergoing noninvasive ventilation (NIV), pressure-related skin injury is among the most frequent and clinically relevant complications. The reported prevalence of device-related pressure injuries is estimated at around 25%, while reported incidences of NIV-related facial pressure ulcers range from approximately 10–31%. These lesions, mainly localized over the nasal bridge, arise because the skin in this area is thin and supported by minimal subcutaneous tissue, making it highly vulnerable to ischemic and frictional forces exerted by the mask interface. In severe cases, skin breakdown may lead to pain, infection, or discontinuation of NIV, thereby compromising ventilatory support. The most commonly adopted preventive strategy involves the application of shaped hydrocolloid dressings; however, evidence regarding their actual effectiveness and the potential benefits of alternative protective devices remains limited.ObjectivesThe primary aim of this study is to compare skin injury-free survival in ARF patients receiving NIV treated with three different CE-marked protective systems. Secondary objectives include assessing device stability and protective performance, patient comfort, pain intensity, mask tolerance, timing and staging of skin lesions, cost-effectiveness, predictive factors for skin injury, and overall NIV success.MethodsThis is a multicenter, randomized, open-label, controlled trial involving 14 Italian pulmonology intensive care units and 1 Spanish Intensive Care Unit. Eligible patients with hypoxemic or mixed hypoxemic–hypercapnic ARF requiring NIV for more than 24 h will be randomly assigned to one of four study arms (three intervention groups and one control group). Each group will receive a different protective device—Tegaderm®, Duoderm®, or Allevyn Adhesive®—or standard care. Lesion grading, pain assessment, and device performance will be evaluated using standardized scoring systems.Planned OutcomesThe trial will provide comparative evidence on the efficacy of different protective dressings in preventing NIV-related facial skin injury, thereby supporting the development of evidence-based recommendations for clinical practice.Trial RegistrationNCT01351155.

Introduction

Noninvasive positive-pressure ventilation (NIV) is an increasingly adopted form of ventilatory support and an essential component in the management of patients with acute respiratory failure (ARF). According to a European survey, oronasal mask represents the preferred noninvasive interface for the treatment of ARF [1], followed by nasal masks, total face masks and helmets. However, prolonged use of facial masks often leads to a range of pressure-related skin injuries, including ulceration and necrosis [2, 3]. Reported prevalence of device-related pressure injuries is estimated at around 25%, while reported incidences of NIV-related facial pressure ulcers range from approximately 10–31% [4]. These injuries are associated with adverse outcomes, such as pain and patient discomfort, impaired quality of life [5], and increased healthcare costs [6]. They primarily affect the nasal bridge and surrounding facial areas, where the skin is thin and lies directly over the bone, making it particularly susceptible to ischemic and frictional forces generated by the mask’s pressure and movement. Prolonged or excessive pressure can exceed capillary perfusion pressure, leading to local tissue ischemia, hypoxia, and eventual skin breakdown [7].

Several risk factors have been linked to the development of NIV-related skin injuries in adult patients, including the total duration of NIV [8], the use of oro-nasal masks, and, potentially, the presence of humidification [9]. With regards to prevention, recent evidence highlights several interventions to reduce skin injury, including regular skin assessment, correct mask positioning, and the application of protective materials such as hydrocolloid or foam dressings beneath the mask [10].

To prevent skin ulceration, both the Minnesota Hospital Association and, more recently, the National Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel (NPUAP) recommend the use of wound dressings to reduce friction and shear forces [11–13]. Although several devices and protective interfaces have been proposed as skin-protective systems, comparative evidence remains limited.

In clinical practice, hydrocolloid dressings are the most commonly used protective interfaces during NIV [14], although most studies include small sample sizes and only a few have investigated alternative protective devices [15, 16]. This preference is supported by the physicomechanical properties of hydrocolloids, which form cohesive, hydrophilic, and conformable layers capable of distributing mechanical stress, reducing friction and shear forces, and maintaining adequate skin hydration at the skin–device interface. [17]. A meta-analysis [18] including 22 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) across pediatric, adult, and geriatric populations reported that hydrocolloid dressings effectively reduce the incidence of facial pressure ulcers in patients undergoing NIV. However, because these conclusions are derived from RCTs of generally lower methodological quality, further validation is required [18]. Therefore, given the limited evidence on both the efficacy and the optimal selection of protective dressings, a multicenter controlled trial comparing different devices is warranted to validate current practice and avoid unnecessary healthcare costs. With this study, we aim not only to cover an important gap in the existing literature but also to contribute to the development of evidence-based recommendations for skin protection during NIV in clinical practice.

Methods

Study Design

This is a multicenter, randomized, open-label, controlled trial conducted across ten Italian Pulmonary Intensive Care Unit (PICU) within the AIPO (Associazione Italiana Pneumologi Ospedalieri) network and 1 Spanish Intensive Care Unit (ICU) over a 12-month period. The participating centers include Arezzo, Trento, Florence, Reggio Emilia, Bologna, Lumezzane, Perugia, Palermo, Pisa, and Imperia, as well as Murcia (Spain).

The study compares three different protective systems (PSs) used in patients with ARF. The three protective systems selected for the study, Tegaderm®, Duoderm®, and Allevyn Adhesive®, were chosen because they represent three different categories of devices commonly used in clinical practice for the prevention of NIV-associated skin damage: transparent adhesive film, thin hydrocolloid and adhesive polyurethane foam. Their selection was therefore guided both by clinical relevance, mutated from clinical practice in pulmonary acute patients and from wound care experiences, and by the desire to compare materials with different physico-mechanical properties, in order to assess whether such differences may translate into a different protective effect. Each participating center applies the same standardized protocol and data-collection procedures using a shared case report form (CRF). Patients meeting inclusion criteria are consecutively enrolled and randomized to one of four study arms—three intervention groups and one control group—using a sealed-envelope system with center-specific randomization sequences. The inclusion of a control group was reviewed and approved by the ethics committees of all participating centers. Measurements

All participating centers adhered to the standardized protocol. NIV is delivered using a bi-tube ventilator, allowing continuous monitoring of expiratory tidal volume and flow/pressure curves. Ventilation is provided through a facial interface (oronasal and full-face masks) using pressure-targeted ventilation. The size is selected to minimize air leakage and maximize patient comfort. The type, model, and size of the NIV interface are recorded in the case report form and may be considered in the analysis, as in real clinical practice. Ventilatory settings are standardized: inspiratory pressure support is initially set at 10 cmH2O and increased in 2 cmH2O until the highest tolerated level, targeting a respiratory rate below 25 breaths/min without accessory muscle use and an expiratory tidal volume of 6–8 ml/kg, consistent with contemporary ERS/ATS guidance [19]. Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) is initially set at 5 cmH2O and adapted according to patient response and traces on flow/pressure curves, up to a maximum of 10 cmH2O⋅FiO2 is titrated to maintain SpO2 between 88% and 92% in hypercapnic ARF and between 92% and 95% in hypoxemic ARF. The inspiratory trigger is set at the lowest level to prevent auto-triggering. Expiratory cycling is based on either flow (pressure support mode) or time (pressure-controlled mode), according to patient comfort and ventilator–patient synchronization.

During spontaneous breathing sessions, oxygen therapy is administered to achieve the same SpO2 targets described for ventilatory support. All patients receive standard pharmacological treatment, including parenteral and inhaled therapies as clinically indicated. NIV can be discontinued after at least 48 consecutive hours of controlled oxygen therapy, if pH exceeds 7.35, PaCO2 is below 50 mmHg, PaO2/FiO2 is above 250, and the respiratory rate remains under 25 breaths per minute without the use of accessory muscles.

These devices were selected because they represent commonly used CE-marked protective systems with different physicochemical properties (film, hydrocolloid, and foam), allowing comparison across distinct categories of skin protection strategies.

In all intervention groups, the protective skin system (PSS) is applied at the start of NIV. The dressing is shaped into a “T” configuration, with the horizontal section placed over the nasal bridge and the vertical section extending approximately 2 cm below it. Replacement occurs if the PSS becomes displaced, shows signs of wear, is not tolerated by the patient, or after a maximum of 7 days of use.

Skin integrity is assessed by a designated nurse or head nurse at baseline, 4 h, 12 h, 24 h, and daily thereafter until NIV discontinuation. In the event of lesion development, follow-up continues until complete healing or hospital discharge. For non-transparent systems (Duoderm®), skin is assessed at baseline and at day 7, unless earlier replacement is required (e.g., due to displacement or material degradation).

Skin monitoring includes both the assessment of skin vulnerability and the evaluation of specific practical aspects related to the protective systems. The Braden scale [20, 21] is administered before NIV initiation and after 7 days to assess baseline risk and changes over time. In addition, a three-level semi-quantitative score (low, medium, and high efficacy) is used to assess the following practical aspects of the protective systems:ease of application;ease of removal;system stability (tendency to displacement);mask stability during NIV;air leakage at the mask–face interface;degree of material wear;skin moisture or sweating beneath the dressing;overall cost.

Planned Outcomes

Primary Outcome

The primary aim of this study is to compare facial skin injury-free survival during mask ventilation among patients with ARF receiving NIV with three CE-marked protective systems versus the control group.

Failure is defined as the development of pressure-related skin lesions of at least stage 2, requiring the implementation of a rescue strategy with an alternative device.

Data Analysis

Descriptive Statistics

Variables collected in the CRFs will be reported as means, standard deviations (SD), and corresponding minimum and maximum values when expressed as continuous measures. Categorical or nominal variables will be summarized in contingency tables with absolute frequencies and percentages.

Inferential Statistics

Baseline Group Comparability

Group comparability at baseline will be assessed using one-way ANOVA for normally distributed continuous variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normal continuous variables, and the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. When multiple comparisons are required, appropriate adjustments for type I error inflation will be applied (Dunn–Bonferroni adjustments).

Primary Endpoint Analysis

The primary endpoint is the occurrence of the first pressure injury of > stage 1 within 7 days.

This outcome will be analyzed as a time-dependent event using Kaplan–Meier survival curves, between-group comparisons with the log-rank test, and Cox proportional hazards regression to estimate the effect of the protective systems (HR and 95% CI).

Secondary Outcomes Analysis

Lesion severity will be evaluated using linear trend tests or the Kruskal–Wallis test.

The time to onset of PI > 1 will be assessed through Kaplan–Meier estimates, log-rank tests, and univariable Cox models. Pain scores will be compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Discomfort will be analyzed as an ordinal variable using the χ2 test. The Braden scale will be considered both as a continuous variable and dichotomized (≤ 16 vs > 16), with analyses including Cox models, stratified Kaplan–Meier curves, and descriptions of changes from the start to the end of NIV.

A multivariable stepwise logistic regression will be used to identify independent risk factors associated with the development of pressure injuries of stage > 1. Ordinal scores (stability, displacement, leakage, wear, sweating) will be compared using trend tests or the Kruskal–Wallis test. NIV duration and hospital length of stay will be analyzed with the Kruskal–Wallis test.

Sample Size Calculation

Sample size was calculated based on the variable “failure of NIV-related skin-damage prevention,” defined as the development of pressure lesions > stage 1. Based on available data [23–26] the estimated failure rate is 80% in the control group, with an expected reduction to < 60% in the treatment groups and ≤ 50% in at least one intervention group. With α = 0.05 and β = 0.10, a total of 252 patients (63 per group) is required to ensure 90% power to detect statistically significant differences using the χ2 test (references [21, 22, 27, 28]).

Strengths and Limitations

In this multicenter randomized controlled trial, several strengths can be identified; nevertheless, some potential limitations should also be acknowledged.

First, as a multicenter study, it benefits from a large and diverse patient population and the involvement of experienced clinical teams, which enhances both the applicability and the clinical relevance of the findings.

Thanks to the methodological strength of the study design, the trial allows a direct comparison of three CE-marked protective dressings under standardized clinical conditions. Noninvasive ventilation settings, monitoring schedules, and lesion assessment procedures are harmonized across centers through shared case report forms and protocols. The primary endpoint is objective, clinically meaningful, and immediately applicable to real-world practice.

Concerning the potential weakness of the study, blinding of staff and patients is not feasible given the visible differences between the protective systems, which could introduce observer bias.

Secondly, the study was designed in 2010. Since then, both NIV interface technology and certain methodological aspects of multicenter trials have further evolved. Although patient recruitment and data collection were completed on schedule, the study experienced a temporary pause. We have recently resumed working with the dataset, and the statistical analysis is now underway. These contextual differences should be considered when interpreting the findings, although the underlying clinical issue of mask-related skin injury and its mechanisms remain unchanged.

As this is a multicenter study reflecting real-world clinical practice, complete standardization of the brand and model of NIV interfaces across all centers was not feasible. However, the use of interfaces followed shared clinical criteria, and the ventilatory approach was standardized. Furthermore, the model, make, and size of the interfaces were systematically recorded and analyzed so any potential biases correlated with these factors should be prevented or at least kept at minimum ground.

Finally, strictly related to the dated protocol of the study, the potential protective role of integrating the application of the high flow nasal cannula device to wean the patients from NIV during an episode of ARF has not been contemplated.

Ethics

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the central Ethics Committee of the coordinating center and by the local ethics committees of all participating institutions prior to patient enrolment. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before inclusion in the study. In cases of impaired consciousness, consent was obtained from a legally authorized representative or next of kin. Participants retained the right to withdraw consent at any time without any impact on their standard medical care.

All procedures performed in this study were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

All patient data were handled in compliance with applicable data protection regulations. Data were collected using anonymized case report forms and securely stored at each participating site. Access to the study database was restricted to authorized investigators only.

All participants provided informed consent for participation in the study and for the publication of anonymized data.

Dissemination

The results of this study will be disseminated through presentations at national and international scientific meetings and submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals, regardless of the findings. All investigators will have access to the final study data and will participate in the interpretation of results and preparation of manuscripts. Any protocol amendments or deviations will be communicated to the ethics committees of all participating centers.

The results of this study are expected to have important implications for clinical practice, including the translation of findings into practical recommendations, the development of standardized care protocols, and improved prevention of NIV-related skin injury.
