The impact of depression and death anxiety on the quality of life of the older people
Authors: Seyyed Mansour Kashfi, Ali Haydoura, Hasan Bahja, Sadaf Rezaei, Maryam Yazdankhah, Amirhossein Kamyab, Ali Khani Jeihooni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAging involves physiological and psychological changes that can profoundly affect the quality of life of older adults. Among these, depression and death anxiety are common psychological concerns that influence well-being in distinct ways. This study examined the effect of depression and death anxiety on the quality of life of older adults in Iran, interpreting the findings within the broader context of varying research outcomes.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted from September to December 2019 on 700 older people in Shiraz, Iran. Data collection tools included demographic information, the Beck Depression Inventory, the Death Anxiety Inventory, and the Quality-of-Life Questionnaire. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 24, applying descriptive statistics (percentage, frequency, central indices, and dispersion), Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests, the Pearson correlation coefficient, and linear regression by the simultaneous method. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe mean scores for quality of life, depression, and death anxiety were 48.56 ± 9.38, 29.16 ± 8.23, and 20.10 ± 6.43, respectively. The analysis showed a significant negative effect of depression on the quality of life (β = − 0.714, p < 0.001), indicating that as depression increases, quality of life decreases. In contrast, there was no significant effect of death anxiety on the quality of life (β = 0.039, p = 0.315). Additional findings revealed that demographic factors such as marital status, educational level, and living conditions significantly influenced the quality of life.ConclusionDepression has a substantial negative effect on the quality of life of older adults, while death anxiety shows no significant impact. These psychological issues, particularly depression, are widespread in the older adult population, and with an aging population, addressing mental health issues is becoming increasingly crucial to ensuring the quality of life for older adults. Future studies should further explore the complex dynamics between these factors to guide effective interventions.Keywords: Aged, Depression, Death anxiety, Quality of life, Mental health

Introduction

The global population is aging at an unprecedented rate. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), by 2050, the number of people aged 65 and older will exceed 1.5 billion—more than double the 703 million recorded in 2019. At that time, older adults will comprise about 16% of the world’s population [1]. This demographic shift has brought increasing attention to the physical, social, and mental health needs of older adults [2].

Iran is experiencing a similarly rapid aging trend. Currently, about 10% of Iran’s population is aged 65 years and above [3]. The number of people aged 65 and older in Iran is projected to increase from approximately 5.8 million in 2023 to over 17 million by 2049, effectively tripling within 26 years—an aging rate significantly faster than that observed in countries such as France (157 years), England (100 years), and the United States (89 years) to achieve similar demographic shifts [4]. By 2030, Iran is expected to rank third among countries with the fastest-growing older populations, following Bahrain and the United Arab Emirates [5].

As the population ages, mental health challenges have become increasingly common among older adults [6]. Depression is one of the most prevalent disorders in this group, often resulting from factors such as loneliness, decreased functional capacity, lack of social support, and physical disability [7]. The World Health Organization defines depression as persistent sadness and a loss of interest or pleasure in previously rewarding activities, with its prevalence among older adults estimated to be as high as 27% [7].

Alongside depression, death anxiety is another psychological concern that may affect the well-being of older individuals. As people grow older and become more aware of their mortality, they may experience intense fears related to death, including feelings of helplessness, isolation, and existential uncertainty [8–12]. Death anxiety is characterized by an individual’s emotional and cognitive responses to the awareness of death, often influenced by cultural and personal beliefs [9–11]. These psychological responses can have a meaningful impact on the quality of life (QOL) of older adults.

Quality of life has emerged as a key outcome in health research involving aging populations. The WHO defines it as “the perception of one’s position in life in the context of the culture and value systems in which they live, and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards, and concerns” [13]. For older people, QOL encompasses aspects such as independence, social functioning, physical well-being, and emotional resilience [14, 15]. A wide range of demographic, social, environmental, and psychological factors—including depression and death anxiety—have been found to influence QOL in older adults [16].

Despite the growing body of research in this area, the combined influence of depression and death anxiety on the quality of life in older adults remains inconsistent across studies. While many have reported a strong inverse relationship between depression and QOL, the role of death anxiety is less clear and appears to vary based on sociocultural context.

Given these inconsistencies, and considering the unique cultural, familial, and religious structures in Iran, this study aimed to investigate the effects of depression and death anxiety on the quality of life among older adults. Specifically, we sought to answer the following questions: (1) What is the effect of depression on the quality of life of older adults? (2) What is the effect of death anxiety on the quality of life of older adults? (3) How do demographic factors such as marital status, educational level, and living conditions influence the relationship between these psychological factors and quality of life? Addressing these questions may provide insights for designing culturally appropriate interventions to support mental health and well-being in aging populations.

Methods

Study design and sampling procedure

This cross-sectional study was conducted from September to December 2019 among older adults (≥ 65 years) in Shiraz, a large and demographically diverse urban center in southern Iran. Shiraz was selected due to its established primary healthcare infrastructure and access to comprehensive electronic health records, enabling efficient sampling and data collection across various socio-economic and cultural groups.

A stratified cluster sampling method was used. The city was geographically divided into health service catchment areas, from which two major urban health centers—Martyrs of the Revolution and Valfajr—were purposively selected based on population density and geographic representation. Each center oversees multiple neighborhood health bases; from each center, four were randomly selected, resulting in eight total clusters.

Within each selected health base, a list of eligible individuals aged 65 and older with active health records was compiled. A proportional random sampling approach was applied to ensure that each base contributed participants in proportion to its elderly population size.

Based on Khaki et al. (2017), with an expected correlation coefficient of 0.1, a confidence level of 90% (Z = 1.645), and a power of 80% (Z = 0.84), the required sample size was calculated as 614 using the standard formula for correlational studies [17]:

To account for a potential 10% non-response rate (≈ 61 participants), the sample size was increased to 675 and then rounded up to 700 to ensure balanced proportional allocation across the selected clusters. The final sample of 700 older adults (≥ 65 years) was successfully recruited from eight selected health bases in Shiraz.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were aged 65 years or older, resided in Shiraz, were able to communicate effectively, expressed willingness to participate, and had no documented history of psychiatric hospitalization. Eligibility was confirmed through a two-step screening process: (1) reviewing health records to identify prior diagnoses of severe psychiatric disorders or hospitalizations due to mental illness, and (2) conducting brief cognitive and emotional assessments during initial contact. Individuals were excluded if they had a history of severe psychiatric disorders (including hospitalization for depression), significant physical or mental disabilities that could interfere with completing the questionnaires, marked psychological distress, inability to comprehend the study process, or unwillingness to participate.

Data collection

Data collection was carried out through face-to-face interviews conducted either at participants’ homes or at local health centers, depending on each participant’s preference and accessibility. Trained public health researchers conducted the interviews to ensure clear communication and accurate responses, particularly considering the advanced age and potential sensory or cognitive limitations among participants. Prior to the interviews, researchers introduced themselves, explained the study’s objectives and procedures in simple and understandable language, and obtained written informed consent. For participants who were illiterate, the consent form was read aloud in the presence of a witness, and verbal consent was obtained and documented with a fingerprint in place of a signature, in accordance with accepted ethical practices in similar settings [18].

During the interviews, four structured instruments were used to collect data. First, a demographic questionnaire gathered detailed background information, including age, sex, marital status, education level, employment status, monthly income, insurance coverage, and living arrangements. This was followed by three validated scales: the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) for assessing depressive symptoms, the Templer Death Anxiety Scale for evaluating anxiety related to death, and the LEIPAD Elderly Quality of Life Questionnaire for measuring multiple dimensions of quality of life in older adults. All questions were read aloud, and additional explanations were provided when necessary to ensure comprehension. This approach enhanced data accuracy and helped build trust and cooperation between the participants and the research team.

Beck depression inventory

The Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) was used to assess depression status in older adults. This questionnaire consists of 21 items, each with four statements scored from 0 to 3, reflecting increasing severity of depressive symptoms for that item [19]. The scores from all items are summed to yield a total score ranging from 0 to 63, which is used to determine depression severity. In this study, total scores of 1–18 indicated no depression (normal), 19–28 indicated mild depression, 29–35 indicated moderate depression, and 36–63 indicated severe depression. The validity and reliability of the BDI have been confirmed in multiple studies [20–22]. The Persian version of the BDI has been validated in several studies conducted in Iran, confirming its reliability. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the BDI was 0.89, indicating excellent internal consistency for assessing depression among older adults. Previous research, such as the study by Dadfar and Kalibatseva (2016), has also supported the psychometric robustness of the BDI in Iranian clinical samples [23].

Templer death anxiety questionnaire

The Templer Death Anxiety Scale is a tool for measuring death-related anxiety that has the most application at its level [24]. The death anxiety questionnaire has 15 items. While some adaptations use a 3-point Likert format (e.g., high = 2 points, rarely = 1 point, never = 0), in this study we used the original yes-or-no response format. Each “yes” response was scored as 1 point, and each “no” response as 0 points. Therefore, the total score ranged from a minimum of 0 to a maximum of 15, with higher scores indicating more death anxiety.

The validity of the Death Anxiety Scale has been shown to have acceptable validity [24, 25]. Scoring and analysis of this scale were done using Tavakoli’s survey, which is based on the binary scoring method [26]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the Templer Death Anxiety Questionnaire was 0.83, demonstrating good internal consistency. This aligns with previous validations of the scale within Iranian samples, where Soleimani’s survey is often used to confirm its reliability and validity [27].

Lipad elderly quality of life questionnaire

The LEIPAD Elderly Quality of Life Questionnaire was developed by De Leo et al. in 1998 as an internationally applicable tool to assess quality of life among older adults across diverse cultural settings [28]. It has been adapted and standardized in Iran, with confirmed validity and reliability in Persian-speaking elderly populations. The questionnaire evaluates quality of life across seven dimensions: physical function (5 items), self-care (6 items), depression and anxiety (4 items), mental performance (5 items), social functioning (3 items), sexual function (2 items), and life satisfaction (6 items). Each item is scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (worst condition) to 3 (best condition), resulting in a total possible score between 0 and 93, with higher scores indicating better quality of life [28].

In the Iranian context, interpretative guidelines have been established to classify quality of life based on LEIPAD scores. A score below 71.5 is generally considered indicative of poor quality of life, while scores above this threshold reflect good quality of life. This cutoff has been supported by validation studies showing a sensitivity of 82.3% and specificity of 61.8% for detecting poor quality of life [29]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the LEIPAD Questionnaire was 0.91, demonstrating excellent internal consistency. This aligns with previous findings on similar elderly quality of life scales validated in Persian, where Cronbach’s alpha values ranged from 0.66 to 0.95.

Ethical considerations

After approval of the plan and obtaining the ethical code through the approval of the ethics committee of the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR. SUMS. REC.1398.650), older people covered by eight health centers with health records were randomly selected and used for the study. We explained the purpose of the study and how to perform the work after introducing ourselves to each of the study subjects. Additionally, we obtained the written consent of the study subjects to participate in the study.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 24. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were used to summarize demographic and psychological variables. Inferential analyses were conducted to assess associations and predictors. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine bivariate relationships between quality of life, depression, and death anxiety. To assess the independent effects of depression, death anxiety, and demographic variables on quality of life, multiple linear regression analysis was performed. Additionally, comparisons of quality of life, depression, and death anxiety across demographic groups were conducted using independent-samples t-tests for binary variables and one-way ANOVA for variables with more than two categories. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study included 700 older adults with a mean age of 71.22 ± 8.84 years, with a minimum age of 65 and a maximum age of 92 years. Among them, 38.00% were male and 62.00% were female. Regarding marital status, 89.29% were married, 5.71% were widowed, 3.00% were divorced, and 2.00% were single. In terms of education, 41.14% had completed high school, 30.29% had middle school education, 14.57% had elementary education, 8.86% held university degrees, and 5.14% were illiterate.

With respect to employment status, 81.71% were unemployed or retired, and 18.29% were employed. A majority of participants (84.57%) had health insurance, while 15.43% did not. In terms of living arrangements, 47.71% lived with their spouse and children, 38.57% lived only with their spouse, 8.86% lived alone, and 4.86% lived with other relatives. For monthly income, 43.14% earned less than 40 million Iranian Rials, 44.86% earned between 40 and 80 million Rials, and 12.00% earned more than 80 million Rials (Table 1).

The young-old group (65–74) exhibited the lowest depression score (27.34 ± 7.89), followed by the middle-old group (75–84) with a moderate depression score (29.87 ± 8.46). A statistically significant increase in depression was observed as participants moved into older age categories (p < 0.001). Similarly, quality of life scores showed a downward trend with advancing age. The young-old group had the highest quality of life score (50.16 ± 8.87), followed by the middle-old group (48.02 ± 9.19). This pattern highlights the negative impact of aging on overall well-being, with the oldest-old group exhibiting the lowest quality of life scores (p < 0.001). In contrast to depression, no significant differences were found in death anxiety scores among the three age groups (p = 0.315). The death anxiety scores remained relatively stable, with mean scores of 19.82 ± 6.35, 20.45 ± 6.76, and 20.07 ± 6.21 for the young-old, middle-old, and oldest-old groups, respectively.

The overall mean scores for quality of life, depression, and death anxiety were 48.56 ± 9.38, 29.16 ± 8.23, and 20.10 ± 6.43, respectively. Pearson correlation analysis showed a strong negative correlation between depression and quality of life (r = − 0.62, p < 0.001), indicating that higher levels of depression were associated with lower quality of life. Death anxiety was not significantly correlated with quality of life (r = − 0.05, p = 0.18). However, a weak but statistically significant positive correlation was observed between depression and death anxiety (r = 0.22, p < 0.001), suggesting a modest association between these two psychological variables. Among the demographic variables examined, marital status showed a notable pattern, with married participants reporting higher mean QOL scores (50.47 ± 9.18) compared to divorced (39.22 ± 12.62) and widowed individuals (37.84 ± 9.90), although these differences did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.084). After adjusting for all variables in the model, none of the demographic factors—including age, gender, marital status, educational level, employment status, insurance coverage, living conditions, and monthly income—were statistically significant predictors of QOL (all p > 0.05). Discussion

Depression and death anxiety are two important psychological factors that can influence the quality of life (QOL) in older adults. The findings showed that depression had a strong and statistically significant negative effect on QOL, whereas death anxiety did not demonstrate a significant association. This aligns with prior research indicating that depression in later life is a key predictor of reduced life satisfaction, increased frailty, and impaired daily functioning [30, 31]. Depression in older individuals may present as persistent sadness, hopelessness, fatigue, appetite and sleep disturbances, anxiety, and somatic complaints without a clear medical cause [31, 32]. In this study, better quality of life was observed among participants who were not divorced and lived with their spouses and children, suggesting the protective effect of supportive social relationships. These results underscore the critical importance of identifying and addressing depression in older populations to improve both psychological and functional well-being. Comparative evidence supports these conclusions: in a Portuguese cohort of community-dwelling seniors, both anxiety and depressive symptoms were significantly associated with reduced QOL [33], and a European systematic review found that improvements in depression often correspond to enhanced QOL in older populations [34].

These findings also align with the broader goals of the World Health Organization’s initiative for the United Nations Decade of Healthy Aging (2021–2030), which emphasizes the importance of maintaining and improving the functional ability and well-being of older people. Addressing mental health concerns such as depression is a central component of achieving healthy aging, as it supports older adults in remaining active, connected, and engaged with their communities [35]. Therefore, the integration of mental health services into geriatric care is not only vital for individual well-being but also essential to fulfilling international public health commitments toward aging populations.

In the present study, older adults who were married, literate, employed, and living with their spouses and children reported higher QOL scores and lower levels of depression. Conversely, those who were divorced, illiterate, unemployed, living alone, or earning less than 40 million Iranian Rials per month experienced lower QOL. These findings are consistent with existing literature that links social and economic stability to better mental health outcomes in aging populations [3, 36, 37]. Education plays a particularly important role, as it enhances access to resources, promotes self-efficacy, and supports healthier lifestyles—all of which are associated with reduced depressive symptoms [38]. Similar patterns have been reported in international data from the SHARE survey, where marital status, education, and social engagement were linked to higher QOL among older adults across Spain and Northern Europe [39].

Interestingly, while death anxiety is often considered a significant concern in older adults due to their increased proximity to mortality, the findings did not support a significant association between death anxiety and quality of life. This result is consistent with the study by Khaki et al. [17], which also found no meaningful relationship between death anxiety and QOL in older people. In the Iranian context, several interrelated cultural, spiritual, and religious factors may buffer the psychological impact of death anxiety. Iran’s predominantly Islamic culture emphasizes the belief in an afterlife, divine will, and the concept of death as a natural transition rather than an end, which may foster acceptance and reduce existential distress. Strong adherence to religious practices, such as daily prayers, Qur’anic recitation, and participation in mourning rituals, has been shown to provide psychological comfort, promote meaning-making, and facilitate coping with mortality [36, 40–42].

Moreover, the structure of extended family systems in Iran, where older adults often live with children or close relatives, offers sustained emotional support, social engagement, and a sense of belonging, which can further mitigate the adverse psychological effects of contemplating death. These cultural and familial dynamics may explain why, in the results, death anxiety did not emerge as a significant predictor of QOL, underscoring the need for future research to explore the protective roles of spiritual beliefs and social structures in Iranian aging populations. In contrast, research from other countries has shown different patterns. For example, among community-dwelling older adults in China, higher death anxiety was associated with lower life satisfaction [43], and a Turkish study found that elderly oncology patients with elevated death anxiety experienced poorer QOL and greater loneliness [44]. These differences suggest that sociocultural and spiritual contexts may moderate the relationship between death anxiety and QOL.

The findings regarding the protective roles of education and marital status also reflect broader evidence showing that being married and educated contributes to better emotional health and reduced psychological distress [38, 45]. Social support derived from family relationships can foster a sense of belonging and safety, while education often leads to improved problem-solving skills, health literacy, and access to services. Evidence from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing also confirms that social connectedness and education are associated with higher well-being in later life [46].

Taken together, this study reinforces that while both depression and death anxiety are prevalent concerns in older populations, depression appears to exert a more immediate and measurable impact on quality of life. Given the increasing proportion of older adults in Iran’s population [4, 5], identifying and addressing depression in this group should be a public health priority. Interventions targeting at-risk individuals, particularly those who are socially or economically vulnerable, can contribute meaningfully to enhancing the quality of life and overall mental health in aging communities. Comparative research from multiple regions underscores the need for culturally sensitive strategies that address both universal and context-specific determinants of QOL in older adults.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several methodological strengths, including the use of validated instruments such as the Beck Depression Inventory, the Death Anxiety Scale, and the LEIPAD Quality of Life Questionnaire, which allowed for a multidimensional assessment of older adults’ psychological well-being and quality of life. Data were analyzed using robust statistical methods, including correlation and regression analyses, which strengthened the interpretation of observed relationships. However, the study also has several limitations. Although 700 participants were included, the sample may not be large or diverse enough to generalize findings to the entire older adult population in Iran. The stratified cluster sampling method, while structured, focused on specific health centers in one city, limiting external validity. The reliance on self-reported data introduces potential bias due to recall error or social desirability. Additionally, the cross-sectional design prevents any conclusions about causality between depression, death anxiety, and quality of life. Other potentially influential factors—such as physical health status, level of social support, and cultural beliefs—were not assessed, and unmeasured confounding variables may have influenced the results. Future longitudinal and multi-site studies are recommended to validate and expand upon these findings.

Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrate that depression has a significant negative impact on the quality of life of older adults. The results showed a strong negative correlation between depression and quality of life, with increased levels of depression being associated with a decrease in overall well-being. Conversely, death anxiety did not show a statistically significant relationship with the quality of life, suggesting that, in this sample of older adults, death anxiety may not be a major determinant of life quality. Demographic factors such as marital status, educational level, employment, and living conditions also significantly influenced quality of life, with married individuals, those with higher education, and those living with spouses and children reporting better outcomes. These results highlight the critical need for mental health interventions, especially targeting depression, to enhance the quality of life for older adults.

Suggestions

Given the significant impact of depression on the quality of life of older adults, it is crucial to prioritize effective treatment interventions. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) has been identified as a highly effective treatment for depression in older adults. CBT equips individuals with coping strategies to manage negative thoughts and behaviors, thereby improving mental health outcomes. In addition to CBT, other therapeutic approaches such as medication, regular exercise, and enhanced social support systems can significantly contribute to alleviating depression symptoms in this population.

Moreover, addressing death anxiety, although not found to significantly affect quality of life in this study, remains important for the well-being of older adults. Open and honest conversations about death and dying, facilitated through counseling or group discussions, can help individuals confront and manage their fears related to death. Incorporating these therapeutic strategies into mental health care programs for older adults may further improve their quality of life and overall psychological well-being.
