An unusual presentation of membranous nephropathy with primary and secondary features: a case report
Authors: Aanya Shah, Stephen Baker, Dar B Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMembranous nephropathy (MN) is a common cause of nephrotic syndrome in adults, broadly classified as primary or secondary based on etiology and pathologic findings. We present a single unusual case report of a 75-year-old woman with an 11-year course of MN featuring concurrent characteristics of both primary and secondary disease. On two separate kidney biopsies for the same patient, she demonstrated histopathologic features consistent with Class V lupus nephropathy alongside positive PLA2R staining — a combination that creates significant diagnostic uncertainty. Despite the overlapping features, the patient achieved remission on two separate occasions using different immunosuppressive regimens: cyclosporine initially, followed by Mycophenolate mofetil and prednisone upon relapse over a decade later. This case contributes to the emerging literature on PLA2R positivity in lupus membranous nephropathy and highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges clinicians face when primary and secondary MN features coexist. The long-term follow-up and dual treatment response on a single patient reported here add to a limited body of published cases and carry implications for management of this increasingly recognized overlap syndrome.Keywords: Membranous nephropathy, Proteinuria, Lupus, Nephrotic syndrome, Case report

Introduction

Membranous nephropathy (MN) is a subset of glomerular nephropathy that clinically manifests as nephrotic range proteinuria, edema, and low albumin levels. Treatment options for membranous nephropathy have advanced significantly in the last decades [1]. Membranous nephropathy is a group of diseases which share a common histopathological pattern including the presence of immunoglobulin and complement-containing immune deposits in a subepithelial pattern [2]. In 2009, antibodies against the M-type PLA2R were identified in an adult with idiopathic MN. This finding was a turning point in our understanding of MN. The classification of MN is becoming more precise due to an increasing number of target antigens being identified [1]. MN can be either primary or secondary. Primary MN describes the disease when there is a humoral autoimmune response to a podocyte antigen and there is an absence of secondary features. MN arising in the setting of concomitant processes such as infections, or drug exposure, is denoted secondary MN [2].

Primary MN and secondary MN are clinically distinct. Primary MN, which accounts for 75 to 80% of total cases of MN, stems from antibodies against the phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R) antigen in 70–80% of primary MN cases. This is associated with Major Histocompatibility Complex, Class II, DQ Alpha 1 (HLA-DQA1). Indeed, higher levels of PLA2R at diagnosis are associated with higher proteinuria, lower serum albumin, and lower rates of remission at one year [3]. Of the remaining cases, 15% to 20% of the antibodies are against the neural epidermal growth factor-like 1 and 1% to 5% are against thrombospondin. Causes of secondary MN include infections (hepatitis B), neoplasms (adenocarcinoma), drugs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs), heavy metal poisoning (gold or metal), autoimmune diseases (systemic lupus erythematosus) and finally miscellaneous (hematopoietic stem cell transplant) [1]. Our case is a rare presentation over an overlapping picture of MN that exhibits both primary and secondary features on pathology, which adds to a growing body of literature that our understanding of primary and secondary MN as being two distinct disease processes may be simplistic.

Case presentation

Episode #1

In 2013, a 75-year-old female presented to our clinic with lower extremity swelling. Her past medical history was significant for hypothyroidism and cholecystectomy. She was initially referred to a cardiologist and the subsequent cardiac workup was unremarkable and failed to reveal a specific etiology. The patient reported worsening symptoms despite starting a high dose of furosemide. She also reported significant hematuria and proteinuria. At interview, the patient stated she had no history of kidney disease. She also denied any nausea, fever, or diarrhea. She did report that her blood pressure had not been under adequate control recently, but was previously adequately managed. The patient denied having any history of alcohol or drug abuse. The patient stated that she had stopped smoking a month prior to her visit. Before she stopped, she was smoking one cigarette a day for 20 years. She was married and had children in good health. The patient’s family history was significant for depression and thyroid issues in her mother. The patient’s father’s history was not known. There was no reported family history of kidney disease. On physical examination, the patient had a regular rhythm and lungs were clear to auscultation. She did have 2–3 + pitting edema up to the mid thigh. The rest of the exam was unremarkable.

In summary, we had a patient with proteinuria and hematuria in the setting of lower extremity edema. The patient did not have evidence of pulmonary symptoms, rash, or joint pains. The lack of these symptoms made an active vasculitis unlikely. ANCA serologies were negative. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen was negative. CBC, CMP, and ESR/CRP were normal. Serologic studies, including anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) and double-stranded deoxyribonucleic acid (dsDNA) were also performed and were unremarkable. Complement studies C3C, C4C, and CH50 were also normal. Given these negative findings, it was decided to pursue a CT guided kidney biopsy in order to establish a definitive diagnosis.

Despite showing evidence for diffuse immunoglobulin staining and secondary MN, the patient was diagnosed with primary MN. Staining for PLA2R was not done at this time secondary to the fact that PLA2R was identified as a primary autoantigen in primary MN around 2009 and routine staining for the antigen on biopsy was not implemented until the late 2010s. However, PLA2R staining was done on the second biopsy. Based on treatment recommendations and the potential for spontaneous remission, the patient was observed conservatively on angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI) therapy for a period of six months. Of note, the kidney biopsy was concluded to reflect primary MN.

The patient’s proteinuria worsened over the next several months. Per evidence based recommendations, the patient was started on immunosuppression with a combination of prednisone and cyclosporine. The patient responded well to treatment and had remission of disease. She was continued on low dose cyclosporine.

Episode # 2

In 2024, the same patient returned to the office complaining of recurrent lower extremity swelling. On lab evaluation, the patient was found to have a dramatic increase in proteinuria. In addition, repeat serologic studies showed her to have positive ANA titers and dsDNA levels at 35 IU/mL. Complement levels were normal as follows: C3C (144 mg/dL), C4C (28 mg/dL), and CH50 (> 60 U/mL). Of note, there was no reported joint pain, rashes, or ulcers. The findings of recurrent proteinuria along with positive lupus serologies prompted a repeat of a kidney biopsy to distinguish between primary MN and secondary MN due to lupus. Serologic testing for PLA2R was not done.

Despite having biopsy features once again of secondary, more specifically Class V lupus nephropathy, the patient was again diagnosed with primary MN due to the positive anti PLA2R staining. She was started on treatment with Mycophenolate mofetil and Prednisone due to prior Cyclosporine failure. She responded favorably to the treatment and achieved remission of proteinuria, which persists. Her repeat labs over the past two years show consistent absence of proteinuria and normal kidney function. She is tolerating a reduced dose of Mycophenolate mofetil along with Prednisone well and adhering to the medication.

Discussion

This case is an example of an unusual presentation of MN with features of both primary and secondary MN. While staining for anti PLA2R has been described in lupus Class V MN, it is rare. In a recent study, 10 out of 190 patients with systemic lupus erythematosus had circulating PLA2R. The antibodies were detected in 7 patients with membranous lupus nephritis (MLN). PLA2R staining was demonstrated in 5 out of the 7 biopsies. Anti-PLA2R levels were associated with active disease but not proteinuria. The presence of anti -PLA2R antibodies were associated with higher remission rates and increased time to remission compared to those patients who were serologically negative [4].

Recently, anti-PLA2R antibodies were found to correlate with clinical disease activity. In serial investigations, Beck et al., [5] showed that anti-PLA2R levels could decrease and disappear after treatment with induced or spontaneous remission seen in patients. In a study from a Chinese cohort, it was discovered that patients with lupus can have anti-PLA2R antibodies with antibodies being found in 1 of 20 patients with membranous lupus nephritis. These antibodies can also be found in a small number of patients with cancer or Hepatitis B. These findings suggest that PLA2R may be present in cases with secondary forms of MN or there may be differences between ethnic groups [6].

In our patient, the response to Cyclosporine during the first course of treatment was excellent with resultant remission that lasted many years. When she relapsed several years later, she was treated like a patient with Class V lupus MN rather than primary nephropathy despite PLA2R levels being positive. She once again had an excellent response to treatment, except this time the drug of choice was Mycophenolate mofetil, a drug traditionally used to treat lupus. This case is a unique example of a kidney biopsy with features that suggest lupus MN, but which stains positively for PLA2R antibodies. Indeed, this combination is rather rare based on few studies discussing the prevalence of PLA2R positivity in Class V lupus MN. One small study indicated the prevalence of the aforementioned overlap at 22.2% [7].While the patient responded to Mycophenolate mofetil and prednisone, this alone cannot be used to distinguish primary MN from Class V lupus MN, as both conditions can respond to the aforementioned agents. Our case highlights a rare presentation of MN with lupus-like features treated successfully on repeated occasions using different drug regimens. Indeed, the rarity is evidenced by a case report recently published outlining a similar clinical scenario and noting that only 12 cases of an overlap between MN and Class V lupus nephropathy have been included in PubMed [8]. It is also noted that PLA2R positivity is increasingly being identified in Class V lupus MN and thus this case may represent an overlap of two pathologies rather than two distinct disease processes making the case a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge. Thus, it may be necessary to use other markers, such as staining for various IgG subclasses, in order to form a more detailed picture regarding the predominant presenting pathology given that primary and secondary MN may have differing staining patterns [9]. Given that new antigens are being identified for MN, our classification and treatment will likely become more targeted in the future [10].

In conclusion, this case highlights the complex nature of reconciling between PLA2R positivity on kidney biopsy suggesting primary MN with serologic and other pathologic features indicating Class V lupus MN. It could be therefore speculated that primary and secondary MN, especially Class V lupus MN, may be part of a disease spectrum rather than two separate, distinct pathologies. Cases like ours further illustrate how we many need to change our thinking and approach to MN. This overlap is increasingly being detected pointing towards a more complex and challenging therapeutic picture.
