Selective temporary protective loop ileostomy in complex rectal resection for deep infiltrating endometriosis: a prospective matched cohort study on bowel function, quality of life, and postoperative complications
Authors: Dawan Jamal Hawezy, Waleed Baqer, Araz Omar Fatah, Marwa Fadhil Alsaffar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the impact of temporary protective loop ileostomy (PLI) on postoperative complications, bowel function, and quality of life (QoL) in women undergoing rectal surgery for deep infiltrating endometriosis (DIE) requiring concomitant vaginal and rectal repair.MethodsIn this prospective observational cohort study conducted at Baghdad Teaching Hospital, Medical City, and Kamal Al-Samarrai Hospital (April 2023–April 2024), 230 women underwent colorectal endometriosis surgery. From this population, 42 women with technically feasible colorectal anastomoses were selected; 21 who received a temporary protective loop ileostomy (PLI) were matched 1:1 by age and key risk factors to 21 women without PLI (WPLI). Postoperative outcomes, including the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center Bowel Function Instrument (MSKCC-BFI score) and QoL (EHP-5 score), were assessed using validated Arabic versions of psychometric instruments at baseline and one year postoperatively. Surgical complications were classified per Clavien-Dindo criteria.ResultsBoth groups demonstrated significant improvement in pain symptoms, bowel function, and QoL at 12-month follow-up (p < 0.05). Observed differences in postoperative bowel function (MSKCC-BFI: 86.4 ± 4.3 vs. 88.2 ± 4.4; p > 0.05) and EHP-5 scores (28.1 ± 5.3 vs. 31.3 ± 4.3; p > 0.05) should be interpreted as exploratory trends given limited power. Postoperative complication rates (9.5% vs. 14.3%; p = 0.211) suggest a possible pattern.ConclusionsIn women with DIE and technically feasible colorectal anastomoses, selective use of temporary protective loop ileostomy was not associated with significant detriment to bowel function or QoL in this small matched cohort and may be linked to lower rates of severe anastomotic complications. Given the limited sample size and observational design, these findings should be considered preliminary. Larger, adequately powered randomized trials with extended follow-up are warranted to confirm these observations.Keywords: Deep infiltrating endometriosis, Protective loop ileostomy, Surgical treatment, Quality of life

Introduction

Deep infiltrating endometriosis (DIE) is a severe form of endometriosis defined by endometrial-like tissue penetrating more than 5 mm beneath the peritoneum [1]. Affecting 10–15% of women of reproductive age, DIE poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [2]. It commonly involves multiple pelvic organs, with the rectosigmoid junction accounting for 70–88% of intestinal lesions [3, 4]. Bowel involvement occurs in 8–12% of cases, reaching up to 37% in selected populations [5]. Surgical management of DIE with bowel involvement poses considerable challenges, particularly when both rectal and vaginal suturing are required [6]. These cases often feature multifocal nodules infiltrating the posterior vaginal fornix and anterior rectal wall, resulting in significant anatomical distortion [7, 8]. Surgical strategies range from conservative rectal shaving to more extensive procedures such as discoid or segmental bowel resection [9]. Selection of the surgical approach depends on factors including lesion size, infiltration depth, circumferential involvement, and proximity to the anal verge [9, 10].

Complete excision of bowel-infiltrating endometriosis is associated with substantial morbidity. Anastomotic leakage occurs in 1–6% of cases and represents a major complication linked to prolonged hospitalization, reoperation, and rare mortality [11]. Other reported complications include rectovaginal fistulas, pelvic abscesses, and urinary dysfunction [12]. Although temporary protective loop ileostomy (PLI) is intended to divert fecal flow, routine PLI use is not consistently supported by evidence [13], as recent studies demonstrate that prophylactic stomas do not significantly reduce fistula rates compared with selective use [14]. Instead, anastomotic height is a stronger predictor of risk, with anastomoses located less than 8 cm from the anal verge associated with a threefold increase in fistula rates [8]. Furthermore, stomas carry their own morbidity, including dehydration, peristomal skin complications, and parastomal hernias, which may substantially impair quality of life [15].

Assessment of functional outcomes following stoma creation and reversal has gained increasing attention. Validated tools such as the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center Bowel Function Instrument (MSKCC-BFI) provide standardized evaluation of bowel function after sphincter-preserving surgery [16], while the Endometriosis Health Profile-5 (EHP-5) assesses disease-specific quality of life across five domains: pain, control and powerlessness, emotional well-being, social support, and self-image [17]. Despite frequent use of PLI in complex endometriosis surgery, robust evidence regarding its long-term functional and quality-of-life impact in this population remains limited. Available data largely derive from oncologic cohorts, whose demographic and clinical characteristics differ markedly from young, otherwise healthy women with endometriosis [18, 19]. Emerging evidence suggests that persistent bowel dysfunction, including fecal incontinence and symptoms resembling low anterior resection syndrome, may occur even after stoma reversal [20]. Given ongoing controversy and limited functional data, comparative studies evaluating the impact of PLI on postoperative complications, bowel function, gastrointestinal symptoms, and health-related quality of life in patients with deep infiltrating endometriosis are critically needed. This prospective matched cohort study addresses this gap by comparing outcomes in DIE patients undergoing rectal resection with or without PLI, using culturally adapted, validated functional and quality-of-life instruments in a population for which evidence remains scarce.

Patients and methods

Patients

Between April 2023 and April 2024, 230 women who underwent surgery for colorectal endometriosis at Baghdad Teaching Hospital, Medical City, and Kamal Al-Samarrai Hospital (Baghdad, Iraq) were prospectively enrolled in this observational cohort study. Of these, 100 patients underwent rectal shaving or conservative procedures without colorectal anastomosis, 88 required colorectal resection with permanent diversion because a safe anastomosis was not feasible, and 42 women with a technically feasible colorectal anastomosis formed the analytic cohort for the present comparison. Among these, 21 women received a temporary protective loop ileostomy (PLI) during surgery. For comparative analysis, 21 women who underwent similar surgery without protective ileostomy (WPLI) were selected using a 1:1 matching protocol based on age and key rectal complication risk factors (e.g., extent of disease, anastomotic level, comorbidities) to minimize confounding. Patients in whom intraoperative assessment contraindicated a safe colorectal anastomosis, leading to definitive diversion, were not eligible for inclusion in the matched PLI and WPLI cohorts. Intraoperative decision-making followed a three-tier algorithm (low-risk anastomosis without diversion, intermediate-risk anastomosis with selective temporary PLI, and high-risk cases with contraindication to anastomosis and permanent diversion), and only intermediate-risk cases with technically sound anastomoses were included in the matched analysis (Fig. 1). The study was approved by the General Medical College of Koya University Ethics Committee (Approval No. 25/1011) on March 10, 2023. Prior to inclusion, written informed consent will be obtained from all patients. Inclusion criteria comprised all patients with histologically confirmed colorectal endometriosis who required either segmental resection or full-thickness discoid excision of the rectal wall, followed by rectal suturing or colorectal anastomosis, with or without simultaneous vaginal suturing and with or without the use of a protective stoma. Exclusion criteria included rectal shaving without rectal lumen entry, bowel procedures other than segmental resection with colorectal anastomosis, pathologically unconfirmed endometriosis, bowel surgery performed for non-endometriosis indications (e.g., neoplastic lesions), intraoperative complications requiring bowel intervention, surgical contraindications or incomplete procedures, spontaneous or medically induced menopause, and uncontrolled major psychiatric disorders.

Patient flow diagram and three-tier intraoperative risk-stratification algorithm

The surgical approach was determined preoperatively. All patients were fully informed of the surgical objectives, anticipated risks, and benefits. Before surgery, each patient consulted with a colorectal surgeon regarding the potential need for a diverting stoma. Using a 1:1 matching ratio, an equal number of women with a protective loop ileostomy (PLI) and without PLI (WPLI) were selected for comparative analysis. No standardized surgical protocol was imposed on the operating teams; decisions regarding stoma formation and type (ileostomy vs. colostomy) were based on intraoperative findings and local practice, at the discretion of the lead surgeon. Among patients in whom a colorectal anastomosis was technically feasible, surgical decision-making followed a three-tier risk stratification approach: (1) low-risk anastomoses that did not require diversion; (2) intermediate-risk anastomoses for which a temporary protective diversion was constructed; and (3) high-risk cases in which anastomosis was contraindicated and permanent diversion was required (these cases were excluded from the comparative analysis). The indications for PLI among patients with a technically feasible colorectal anastomosis were as follows. A protective loop ileostomy was performed when intraoperative assessment identified any of the following conditions: (1) an anastomotic height of less than 6 cm from the anal verge; (2) multifocal rectal resection with circumferential involvement exceeding 50%; (3) compromised tissue perfusion, as assessed by the methylene blue dye test; (4) excessive tension at the anastomotic site; or (5) intraoperative leakage of bowel contents. These criteria operationalized a risk stratification framework derived from the colorectal cancer literature, in which anastomotic height, tissue perfusion, and anastomotic tension are the strongest predictors of anastomotic leakage. A protective loop ileostomy was constructed only when intraoperative assessment confirmed a technically sound anastomosis in the presence of intermediate-risk features, whereas cases with persistent, noncorrectable tension or severely impaired perfusion underwent permanent diversion and were excluded from matched analyses. The criteria were defined a priori based on evidence demonstrating reduced leakage rates with diversion under comparable risk conditions. Intraoperative perfusion was assessed by direct visual inspection, evaluation of pulsatile bleeding, and selective methylene blue dye testing, while anastomotic tension was evaluated by confirming complete bowel mobilization and the ability to construct a tension-free anastomosis without force at the rectal level.

Data source and collection

An audit request was submitted to the Department of Audit and Research at Baghdad Teaching Hospital, Medical City, and Kamal Al-Samarrai Hospital, and formal approval to conduct the study was obtained. Data for the matched cohort were extracted from the primary enrolled population. Matching variables were carefully chosen based on clinical relevance and established risk factors for postoperative rectal complications, ensuring comparability between PLI and WPLI groups. To collect one-year postoperative data, patient records were reviewed for documented outcomes. Follow-up outcome questionnaires were distributed to patients via registered delivery. Long-term follow-up beyond 12 months was not available at the time of analysis due to the prospective enrollment period (April 2023–April 2024). Extended follow-up data are being collected, and future analyses will report outcomes at 24 and 36 months postoperatively. Bowel function was assessed using the psychometric Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center Bowel Function Instrument (MSKCC-BFI) [21, 22]. The MSKCC-BFI is an 18-item validated instrument assessing bowel function specific to patients undergoing rectal surgery, covering aspects of diet, frequency, and urgency over the preceding four weeks, with scores ranging from 18 to 90, higher scores representing better bowel function [21, 22]. The Arabic versions of the MSKCC-BFI and the EHP-5 were developed through a forward-backward translation process by independent bilingual translators. The translated versions were reviewed and reconciled by an expert panel and then pilot-tested in 20 patients to assess clarity and cultural appropriateness. The process included backward translation by independent translators blinded to the original, followed by consensus review. The final versions were tested for reliability and validity. Stoma-specific complications (dehydration, parastomal hernia, prolapse, stenosis, necrosis), reversal timing, and associated complications were systematically collected from patient records and postoperative visits.Reversal complications were classified per Clavien–Dindo criteria and recorded until six months after closure. Statistical analyses were performed by an investigator blinded to group identities, with groups coded as A and B until final interpretation.

Statistical analysis

A formal sample-size calculation was conducted prior to data collection using an anticipated MSKCC-BFI difference of 5 points (SD ± 6), α = 0.05, power = 0.80, yielding a required sample of 34 patients per group. Due to resource constraints, a post hoc power of 60% was achieved with 21 patients per group, indicating an increased risk of type II error for medium effect sizes. Statistical analyses were performed by SPSS software (IBM SPSS version 24). The Shapiro–Wilk test and non-parametric tests were applied for testing the normal distribution of quantitative variables. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation for quantitative variables and as frequency (percentage) for qualitative variables. Comparative analysis of PLI or WPLI groups was done by the chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. To compare continuous parametric and non-parametric variables, the unpaired Student’s t-test and Mann–Whitney test were used, respectively. Paired Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to compare continuous variables within the same group. Anastomotic height (distance from the anal verge, in centimeters) was measured intraoperatively and recorded for all patients. Correlation between anastomotic height and postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ III) was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ). Additionally, logistic regression analysis was performed to estimate odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for complications in relation to anastomotic height (< 8 cm vs. ≥ 8 cm). A P-value of less than 0.05 was set as significant. A post hoc power analysis was conducted based on the observed effect sizes for bowel function (MSKCC-BFI) and quality of life (EHP-5) outcomes. Given the sample size of 21 patients per group, the study achieved an estimated power of 0.42–0.58 to detect moderate intergroup differences (Cohen’s d = 0.5) at a two-sided α of 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the patients

During the study period, 230 women underwent surgery for colorectal endometriosis between April 2023 and April 2024. Among these, 42 women had a technically feasible colorectal anastomosis and form the basis of the present analysis: 21 received a PLI and 21 underwent similar surgery without PLI (WPLI), matched on age and key rectal complication risk factors. None of the patients were lost to follow-up or revoked consent. Laparoscopic surgery was performed in 95% of cases in both groups, with one patient per group requiring laparotomy due to extensive pelvic adhesions. Two experimental groups had similar demographic and clinical data. There were no significant differences between groups (P > 0.5) (Table 1). Patients frequently complained of dysmenorrhea and dyschezia with a comparable degree of severity between experimental groups (Table 1). Baseline intestinal function in the PLI and WPLI groups was measured by the MSKCC-BFI (73.4 ± 4.6 in the PLI group vs. 75.3 ± 8.1 in the WPLI, p = 0.26). The quality of life was 48.3 ± 4.4 for the PLI group vs. 53.1 ± 5.2 for the WPLI (p=0.31) based on EHP-5 questionnaires (Table 1).

The postoperative complication rate was 9.52% and 14.28% in the PLI and WPLI groups, respectively. According to the classification of Dindo and Clavien, complications did not have significant differences between the two groups (Table 2). Stoma-specific morbidity in the PLI group was low: no cases of dehydration requiring hospitalization, parastomal hernia, prolapse, or skin irritation requiring medical intervention were observed during follow-up. One PLI patient required a blood transfusion for severe anemia unrelated to stoma output. In the WPLI group, one patient developed a rectovaginal fistula managed conservatively, and one patient in each group experienced anastomotic dehiscence requiring reoperation. Additionally, one patient had a persistent fever (> 38.3 °C) and needed prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy to complete the resolution of symptoms. In this analyzed cohort, no patient received a primary anastomosis under conditions of persistent tension or suspected hypoperfusion; when such high-risk features persisted despite standard maneuvers, a permanent diversion was performed and these cases were not included in the matched groups. Within the study cohort, protective loop ileostomy was only considered when the anastomotic site fulfilled all criteria for a technically safe anastomosis and one or more predefined risk criteria were present. The mean hospital stay was significantly shorter in the PLI group compared to WPLI (1.1 ± 0.1 vs. 6.3 ± 2.2 days; p < 0.05) (Table 2), potentially reflecting differences in postoperative recovery protocols imposed by stoma care. Discoid excisions predominated in PLI (71% vs. 52%, p = 0.20), reflecting lower anastomotic heights (7.8 ± 1.5 vs. 10.1 ± 2.2 cm, p < 0.01). 71% PLI vs. 29% WPLI anastomoses were < 8 cm (p < 0.01), per selective PLI criteria prioritizing distal lesions.

Postoperative follow-up

All 21 protective loop ileostomies were reversed at a median of 4.2 months postoperatively (range 3–6 months). Stoma reversal procedures had a mean hospital stay of 1.5 ± 0.5 days. No surgical or medical complications occurred during stoma reversal, and no anastomotic strictures were observed at six months following closure. No reoperations or permanent stomas were required.Both groups demonstrated significant improvements from baseline in endometriosis-associated pain symptoms (dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, dyschezia, dysuria, chronic pelvic pain), digestive function (MSKCC-BFI score), and quality of life (EHP-5 score) (all p < 0.05) (Table 3). No significant differences were observed in postoperative symptoms, digestive function, or quality of life scores between PLI and WPLI groups(p > 0.05) (Table 4). The absence of statistically significant differences for some endpoints likely reflects the limited statistical power of this cohort, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation of null results in rare complications.

Correlation of anastomotic height and complications

The mean anastomotic height was 9.2 ± 1.8 cm (range 6–12 cm). Spearman’s analysis demonstrated a significant inverse correlation between anastomotic height and the incidence of major complications (Clavien–Dindo ≥ III; ρ = − 0.46, p = 0.008). On logistic regression, anastomoses < 8 cm from the anal verge were associated with a higher risk of major complications (OR 3.2, 95% CI 1.2–8.4; p = 0.021). The empirically identified 8 cm threshold is consistent with, and complementary to, the a priori 6 cm cut-off used in the intraoperative PLI decision-making algorithm, both indicating increasing risk as anastomotic height decreases.

Discussion

This prospective observational study assessed the impact of temporary protective loop ileostomy (PLI) on postoperative symptoms, bowel function, and quality of life (QoL) in women with deep infiltrating endometriosis (DIE) undergoing concomitant vaginal and rectal repair, in whom a colorectal anastomosis was technically feasible and diversion was selectively performed according to predefined intermediate‑risk criteria, rather than in the entire population of patients undergoing colorectal surgery for DIE. At one year postoperatively, digestive function and QoL were not impaired in patients with PLI compared with matched patients without protective diversion. Both groups demonstrated significant and comparable improvements in endometriosis-related symptoms, including dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, dyschezia, dysuria, and chronic pelvic pain. Although protective ileostomy is an established surgical practice, the originality of this study lies in its rigorous prospective matched-cohort design focusing on young, reproductive-age women with complex DIE. By using culturally adapted, validated functional and QoL instruments and systematically evaluating selective diversion criteria, this study addresses an important evidence gap relevant to individualized surgical care in this population. Unlike routine prophylactic ileostomy in oncologic resections [23], this study addresses selective protective diversion in intermediate-risk patients. Recent prospective studies in endometriosis cohorts demonstrate that selective ileostomy, restricted to cases with specific intraoperative risk factors, does not compromise functional outcomes compared with non-diverted patients, supporting risk-stratified rather than routine diversion protocols.

Our findings are consistent with emerging evidence indicating that selective use of protective ileostomy in colorectal surgery for DIE is safe and effective, without compromising medium-term functional outcomes or QoL [18, 24]. Protective loop ileostomy is routine in oncologic colorectal surgery [25], but remains less clearly defined in endometriosis surgery, where selective, risk-adapted criteria are increasingly supported by evidence. Patients with DIE differ fundamentally from cancer patients: younger, healthy females without neoadjuvant radiotherapy, with intact pelvic vascularization, and without mesorectal excision. These factors reduce ischemic anastomotic risk, diminishing the need for routine diversion. The pragmatic choice of ≤ 6 cm as the PLI threshold reflects the observed risk gradient; our empirical finding that anastomoses ≤ 8 cm carried 3.2-fold increased complication risk (OR 3.2, 95% CI 1.2–8.4, p = 0.021) supports anastomotic height, rather than stoma presence alone, as the dominant anatomic predictor of fistula risk, consistent with international colorectal guidelines. This distinction is particularly relevant because patients with DIE represent a younger, predominantly healthy female population without exposure to neoadjuvant therapies and with distinct pelvic anatomy and superior vascularization compared with colorectal cancer patients [26, 27]. Furthermore, the absence of mesorectal excision and pelvic irradiation in DIE surgery likely reduces ischemic risk, thereby enhancing anastomotic healing and potentially diminishing the need for routine diversion. Although the postoperative complication rate was numerically lower in the PLI group (9.5%) than in the non-PLI group (14.3%), this difference did not reach statistical significance, likely owing to the limited sample size. Importantly, protective loop ileostomy was selectively performed only in cases of technically sound anastomoses. When significant, uncorrectable tension or inadequate perfusion persisted despite standard mobilization, anastomosis was avoided altogether. Surgical management strictly adhered to international colorectal surgery guidelines, ensuring that diversion was not used as a substitute for unsafe anastomotic practice.

An anastomotic leakage rate of 4.8% was observed, which is higher than most reports in DIE (1.7–6%) and in young colorectal cancer patients without neoadjuvant therapy (1–2%) [14, 28, 29]. Consistent with the findings of De Las Casas et al. (2022), lower anastomotic height was associated with an increased risk of leakage [30]. This elevated rate may reflect DIE-specific surgical complexity, including multifocal bowel involvement, anatomical distortion, and residual confounding. Additionally, despite matching, unmeasured factors influencing tissue perfusion or anastomotic tension may not have been fully accounted for. These findings underscore the importance of meticulous intraoperative assessment and judicious use of protective loop ileostomy, particularly in patients with low anastomoses or multiple risk factors [28, 29]. Of clinical relevance, rectovaginal fistula, a severe complication associated with substantial morbidity and requiring complex reparative surgery, occurred exclusively in the PLI group. This observation suggests that PLI may mitigate the severity of anastomotic complications leading to fistula formation, in line with colorectal literature demonstrating the protective effect of diversion against clinically significant leaks and pelvic sepsis [31]. Nevertheless, current evidence does not support the routine use of PLI in all DIE patients undergoing rectal and vaginal suturing. Instead, risk stratification incorporating anastomotic height, tissue quality, intraoperative findings, and patient comorbidities should guide selective diversion decisions [8]. Assessment of QoL using the validated Endometriosis Health Profile-5 (EHP-5) questionnaire revealed substantial improvement in both groups, with no significant between-group differences. These findings indicate that temporary diversion does not adversely affect patient-centered outcomes in the intermediate postoperative period. Patient education and multidisciplinary care likely contributed to this acceptance, mitigating the psychological burden commonly associated with stoma formation [32]. Stoma-related morbidity was minimal. No cases of dehydration requiring hospitalization, parastomal hernia, prolapse, or skin complications necessitating intervention were observed. The absence of dehydration-related readmissions is lower than rates typically reported after diverting ileostomy in general colorectal surgery (approximately 10–20%) [33, 34]. likely reflecting the younger age, low comorbidity burden, structured education, and close follow-up in this cohort. Nonetheless, mild dehydration managed in outpatient settings may have been underreported, and results should be interpreted with caution. All protective loop ileostomies were successfully reversed within 3 to 6 months postoperatively, without surgical or medical complications at reversal and without permanent stoma conversion [35]. These findings reinforce the safety of stoma reversal in this population and indicate minimal additional burden associated with PLI.The strengths of this study include its prospective design, use of validated and culturally adapted functional and QoL assessment tools, and rigorous matching to reduce confounding. In young, otherwise healthy women, the decision to perform temporary protective diversion must balance the prevention of catastrophic pelvic sepsis against the physical, psychosocial, and fertility-related implications of a stoma. Recent literature supports a selective, individualized approach rather than routine diversion, emphasizing anastomotic level, intraoperative findings, and patient health status [36, 37]. QoL considerations, including body image and reproductive concerns, should be carefully weighed against the protective benefits of diversion. Although our matched-cohort design aimed to minimize bias, larger prospective studies focusing on patient-reported outcomes remain essential to fully elucidate these trade-offs [36]. An alternative strategy, the so-called “ghost” or “virtual” stoma, in which a bowel segment is exteriorized without maturation and can be rapidly converted if complications arise, has been proposed to reduce the immediate psychosocial burden while maintaining a safety margin. Although evidence remains limited, preliminary reports suggest potential benefits in carefully selected patients, balancing risk reduction with improved QoL [38, 39]. Future studies should further evaluate this approach as part of a patient-centered surgical strategy in DIE.

This study has several limitations. The modest sample size (n = 21 per group) achieved post hoc power of 0.42–0.58 to detect moderate between-group differences, increasing Type II error risk [40]. The observational, non-randomized design and potential selection bias necessitate cautious interpretation. While observed complication rates (9.5% vs. 14.3%) favor selective diversion, this difference lacked statistical significance and must be interpreted as preliminary, hypothesis-generating evidence. Follow-up was limited to 12 months, precluding assessment of long-term functional outcomes, fertility, and disease recurrence, key endpoints in this young population. Finally, the single-center, single-country design may limit generalizability, underscoring the need for multicenter international studies [41]. Nonetheless, given the paucity of prospective functional outcome data specifically addressing protective loop ileostomy in young women with complex DIE, this study provides valuable evidence to inform surgical decision-making in this population. These results are hypothesis-generating and should not be considered definitive clinical recommendations. Adequately powered multicenter randomized controlled trials with extended follow-up (24–36 months) and validated patient-reported outcomes are necessary to establish evidence-based diversion criteria in this population.

Conclusion

In this prospective matched cohort of women with DIE and technically feasible anastomoses, temporary protective ileostomy did not adversely affect bowel function or quality of life at one year and showed numerically lower complication rates. However, the small sample size (n = 21 per group), non-randomized design, and limited follow-up restrict conclusions. These preliminary findings support selective risk-stratification for protective diversion but should not guide practice without larger, adequately powered randomized trials. Clinical decisions regarding ileostomy must remain individualized, incorporating intraoperative risk factors, patient factors, and informed preference. Multicenter randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up are necessary to provide evidence-based guidance.
