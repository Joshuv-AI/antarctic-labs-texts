Nursing students’ learning preferences and satisfaction levels: pathways to effective education
Authors: Noha Mohamed Rashed, Mervat Hussein Ahmed Aly, Sudharani B Banappagoudar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding nursing students learning preferences may help educators design more engaging educational experiences. However, evidence regarding the relationship between learning preferences, student satisfaction, and academic performance remains inconsistent.AimTo examine the associations between nursing students’ preferences, satisfaction levels, and grade point average (GPA).DesignThis was A Descriptive cross-sectional study.SettingCollege of Nursing, Misr University for Science and Technology, Egypt.SampleA convenient sample of 306 undergraduate nursing students from all academic levels was used.ToolsData were collected using a socio-demographic questionnaire, the Student Satisfaction Scale, and Kolb’s Learning Preferences Questionnaire. Introduction

Learning preferences, which are defined by cognitive, emotional, and physiological characteristics, influence how students absorb and process information. These styles vary based on individual abilities and preferences, affecting both teaching and learning [1]. Understanding learning preferences enables educators to tailor their pedagogical approaches, thereby enhancing student performance [2]. Research has confirmed the link between learning preferences and academic success, with various frameworks applied to health sciences education. One such model, Felder and Solomon’s Index of Learning Preferences, identifies students’ preferences for receiving and processing information and has been used in studies involving dental students [3, 4].

Learning preference refers to an individual’s preferred way of processing information, as identified by the Learning Preference Inventory [5]. Kolb’s Learning Preference Theory, a widely recognized model, describes learning as a four-stage cyclical process: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation [6]. Learning preferences are shaped by cognitive, emotional, physical, and environmental factors. Kolb categorizes learners into four styles: Convergent (abstract conceptualization + active experimentation), Divergent (concrete experience + reflective observation), Assimilative (abstract conceptualization + reflective observation), and Accommodative (active experimentation + concrete experience) [6].

Learning preferences play a crucial role in shaping students’ educational experiences and satisfaction [7]. When teaching methods align with students’ preferred learning styles, they tend to engage more actively, retain information better, and achieve higher academic success [8]. Research shows association between students who receive tailored instruction—whether visual, auditory, kinesthetic, or a mix—report higher satisfaction levels in their education [9].

Research highlights the importance of learning preferences in education. Studies have shown that auditory, assimilative, visual, group, and kinesthetic learning preferences are commonly preferred among students in health sciences and are often linked to stronger academic performance [10–12].

Student satisfaction plays a vital role in ensuring success for both institutions and individuals, particularly in the competitive and technology-focused field of higher education [13]. This concept is multidimensional and shaped by various elements, including communication, online engagement, adaptability, workload, technological support, instructor expertise, and feedback [14]. The foundation of satisfaction in online learning lies in social cognitive theory, the interaction equivalency theorem, and social integration theory, all of which highlight the importance of interaction in constructing knowledge [9]. Involving students in academic and extracurricular activities further boosts their satisfaction [15].

Student satisfaction serves as a vital performance metric for higher education institutions, influencing resource allocation across nations [16]. For instance, Australia’s funding model Ties University funding to the quality of student educational experiences, emphasizing the importance of satisfaction as a key factor in institutional achievement [17].

Educational satisfaction is influenced by factors such as instructional design, interaction with instructors and peers, and the availability of learning resources [18]. Students who struggle to adapt to a mismatched teaching style may feel disengaged or frustrated, which negatively impacts their motivation and academic performance [19]. Conversely, personalized and flexible teaching strategies that accommodate diverse learning preferences are associated with students’ confidence, participation, and overall satisfaction [20].

Moreover, the integration of technology in education has expanded opportunities for personalized learning, allowing students to access materials in formats that best suit their learning preferences [21]. By recognizing and incorporating different learning preferences, educators can create a more inclusive and supportive learning environment, ultimately leading to improved student satisfaction and academic outcomes [22].

Significance of the study

It is important to understand the learning preferences and satisfaction levels of nursing students associated with nursing education [23]. Modifying teaching strategies to align with preferred learning styles has been associated with improvements in academic performance, knowledge retention, and the development of clinical skills [24, 25]. Models such as Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory and the VARK model highlight the need for varied instructional strategies to address the diverse ways students’ process and absorb information [26, 27].

Aligning instructional techniques with learners’ preferences could improve satisfaction and motivation, but also foster greater engagement, which in turn reduces burnout and increases student retention rates [28, 29]. Moreover, such alignment promotes the development of metacognitive skills, empowering students to become self-directed learners who can monitor, manage, and regulate their learning, which is a vital attribute for lifelong professional competence [24, 27].

Curriculum designs that incorporate learning preferences provide more effective and inclusive educational experiences [30]. As learning preferences are shaped by various demographic, cultural, and academic factors, it is essential to implement adaptive teaching strategies that accommodate this diversity [3, 31]. Overall, the present study supports a student-centered approach that may be associated with variations in educational outcomes and better prepares nursing graduates for the evolving demands of clinical practice [32].

Research design

A descriptive cross-sectional research design was used to achieve the study aim.

Study setting

The study was conducted at the College of Nursing, Misr University of Science and Technology, 6th October City, Egypt. The university is a private institution affiliated with the Ministry of Higher Education and has been established for over 25 years. It includes 14 colleges and received QS Stars recognition in 2020.

Study subjects

A convenient sample of all academic levels (first to fourth year) enrolled in the 2024–2025 academic year at the Nursing College, Misr University for Science and Technology, was selected. The sample size was calculated as follows:

Tools of data collection

Data were collected using a structured online questionnaire developed via Google Forms, which included the following three instruments:

Tool I: Socio-demographic characteristics questionnaire

This instrument was designed by the researchers for this study based on a review of the recent related literature [1, 3], and [23]. It was designed to collect sociodemographic and academic data, including age, gender, current academic year, current semester credits, and the last semester Grade Point Average (GPA).

Tool II: Student satisfaction scale

This self-reporting scale was originally developed by Fieger [34] and used with permission. It was designed to measure students’satisfaction with learning. It has 19 items across three subscales: teaching, assessment, and generic skills, and learning experiences.

Scoring system

Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). Higher numbers indicate greater satisfaction of the participants.

Validity and reliability

The scale demonstrated good internal consistency in the present study, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.77 for teaching, 0.80 for assessment, and 0.82 for generic skills and learning experiences. Additionally, the instrument has established concurrent validity (r = 0.78), which supports its suitability for assessing student satisfaction.

Tool III: Learning preferences questionnaire

This self-reporting questionnaire was used to measure nursing students’ learning preferences based on Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory [28]. The tool classifies learners into four Preferences: Diverging (feeling/watching), assimilating (watching/thinking), converging (doing/thinking), and accommodating (doing/feeling), corresponding to the four stages of the learning cycle.

Scoring system

The questionnaire employs a forced-choice format, where respondents choose between statements representing different learning modes. Scores are calculated for each learning preference, and the dominant learning preferences are identified as the highest scoring category, while multiple preferred learning preferences may be identified based on scoring thresholds.

Validity and reliability

The instrument demonstrated acceptable content validity, as it adequately represented the four learning modes of Kolb’s theory. In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients indicated acceptable internal consistency, confirming the reliability of the tool for assessing learning preferences in the Saudi context.

Pilot study

A pilot study was conducted with 10% of the total sample (31 students) to test the clarity of the questions and statements, as well as the feasibility, objectivity, and consistency of the tools used. This study also aimed to detect any ambiguity within the tools. Necessary modifications were made based on the results, and pilot data were excluded from the main study.

Field work

A total of 1200 undergraduate nursing students enrolled in the 2024/2025 academic year were invited to participate. Of these, 306 completed the questionnaire, yielding a response rate of 25.5%. No. Additional inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied beyond enrollment in the nursing program. The questionnaire link was distributed via an official academic WhatsApp group. Two reminders were sent over a period of two weeks.

Statistical design

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27. Descriptive statistics and chi-square tests were used to examine the associations between variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Preferred learning preferences were defined as any Learning Preference category in which students scored above the established threshold; therefore, multiple preferences could be identified. In contrast, dominant learning preferences were defined as the highest scoring category for each student. Effect sizes (Cramér’s V) were calculated to assess the strength of the associations.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical Committee of Port-Said University (National Committee affordable) (Approval ID: 48, issued on April 6, 2025). Informed consent, confidentiality, and voluntary participation were obtained. An official request for study approval was submitted to the Dean and Vice Dean of Nursing College. Following approval, the researcher met with the department heads to explain the study objectives and procedures. Their consent and cooperation were obtained to facilitate the data collection. Ensuring voluntary participation and maintaining confidentiality, as well as data security. Personal identifiers were removed to preserve anonymity, and all data were stored securely and were accessible only to the research team members. A link to an online Google Form survey was provided prior to the completion of the survey. Participants provided informed consent electronically before accessing the questionnaire by selecting an agreement option on the first page. No identifying information was collected to ensure the participants’ anonymity.

Discussion

This study examined the associations between nursing students’ learning preferences, satisfaction levels, and academic performance using Kolb Experiential Learning Theory [28, 35]. This model was selected because of its strong relevance to experiential and clinical education, which are central components of nursing training [25, 27, 33].

This study aimed to examine the relationship between nursing students’ learning preferences and their satisfaction levels. This study measures four types of learning preferences according to Kolb’s model: Diverging, Assimilating, Converging, and Accommodating [28, 36].

The current findings revealed that participants from different academic levels in college were included in the study. Most participants were first-year students, with a mean age of 19.6 ± 1.7 years. Approximately one-third of the students were academically at risk (GPA < 2.50). Similar variations in academic performance among nursing students have been reported in previous studies [27], emphasizing the need to explore educational factors influencing achievement. Consistent with the study by University of Illinois Chicago [37, 38], reported a 10% increase in nursing student enrollment, marking the largest class in its history, with significant growth in first-year admissions. Conversely, another study found that 73.2% of nursing students achieved high cumulative grades, with only 4% obtaining low grades, suggesting a lower proportion of students at academic risk [33, 39].

The present study found that the majority of students expressed satisfaction with their educational experience. Student satisfaction is recognized as a multidimensional construct influenced by instructional quality, interaction, feedback, and institutional support, in addition to learning preferences [13, 15, 40]. A similar study found that nursing students exhibited high levels of satisfaction with their clinical rotation experiences, attributing this to effective mentorship and a conducive clinical learning environment [2, 41]. In addition, nursing students at Al-Baha University in Saudi Arabia were satisfied with their online learning experiences during the COVID-19 pandemic, indicating adaptability and contentment with the educational methods employed [3].

In contrast, 23.3% of university students in Australia expressed dissatisfaction with the quality of their degrees in the academic year 2023 in Australia, with factors such as stress, mental health issues, and financial difficulties contributing to this sentiment [9].

This finding also shows that Diverging and Assimilating were the most preferred learning styles among students, suggesting a tendency toward observation, reflection, and structured conceptual understanding. However, Accommodating was the most frequently identified dominant Learning Preference. Similar distributions have been reported in nursing education studies, where Diverging and Assimilating styles were predominant [31, 42], while other research has identified Converging and Accommodating as dominant styles [25, 27, 30].

Significant associations were found between Assimilating, Converging, and Diverging styles and higher satisfaction levels. This finding shows an association between these learning preferences and a higher satisfaction level. These findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating positive associations between Learning Preference alignment and student satisfaction in health science education [8, 43]. However, other studies have reported no significant relationship between learning preferences and satisfaction [44, 45], suggesting that additional contextual factors may influence these outcomes.

Significant associations were observed between Assimilating, Converging, and Divergent styles and higher GPA. Students with analytical and problem-solving learning preferences were observed to have higher GPA levels with these learning preferences. Similar associations between learning preferences and academic achievement have been documented in health science education [10, 11, 46]. Nevertheless, some studies have reported no significant relationship between dominant Learning Preference and academic performance [44, 47], indicating that achievement is influenced by multiple academic and personal factors.

Although significant associations were observed, the cross-sectional design of the study does not allow for causal interpretation. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that matching instructional strategies to learning preferences improves outcomes. Rather, learning preferences are more appropriately understood as flexible and influenced by context and experience [44, 47].

While learning preferences are widely applied in educational research, some scholars have questioned the empirical strength of fixed Learning Preference classifications [44, 47]. Therefore, the learning preferences in this study should be interpreted as learning preferences rather than stable or deterministic traits.

However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The low response rate (25.5%) and overrepresentation of first-year students may introduce selection bias, limiting the generalizability of the results. Additionally, the single-institution setting restricts the applicability of the findings to broader nursing student populations in the future.

Limitations

This study was conducted in a single private nursing college, which limits its generalizability. The cross-sectional design of this study prevented causal inference. Second, the data were self-reported, which may have introduced response bias. The response rate was 25.5%, and participation was voluntary. This may introduce non-response bias, as students who chose to participate may differ systematically from non-respondents. In addition, the sample was heavily weighted toward first-year students, which may limit the generalizability of the findings for senior cohorts.

Conclusion

The study identified significant associations between nursing students learning preferences, satisfaction levels, and academic performance. However, because of the cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be established. These findings may inform future research and guide the consideration of diverse instructional approaches in nursing education.
