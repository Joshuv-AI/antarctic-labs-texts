The physicochemical design, structure and permeability of the JCVI-syn3A minimal cell membrane
Authors: Caterina Presutti, Mert Bozoflu, Joanna Juhaniewicz-Dębińska, Lauren A Lowe, Naomi Woltinge, Arathi Das, Marc C A Stuart, Siewert J Marrink, Bert Poolman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMinimal cells offer a platform to uncover the fundamental physicochemical principles of cellular life. While genomes, proteomes, and metabolic networks of cells have been elucidated, the lipidome, which determines the physicochemical identity of the membrane, remains only partially characterized. Yet, the membrane is a central determinant of cellular viability, regulating solute permeability, mechanical stability, and environmental cues. Here, we investigate membranes that recapitulate the lipid composition of the minimal cell JCVI-syn3A, which is distinguished by an unusually high cholesterol content and simple composition. Combining cryo-electron microscopy, Langmuir monolayer experiments, permeability assays, and coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations, we demonstrate that cholesterol and sphingomyelin act as dominant condensing agents that stabilize the membrane while preserving an ordered yet fluid state. These lipids enhance lipid packing and acyl-chain order, whereas cardiolipin, POPC, and DOPG counterbalance condensation by promoting fluidity and compressibility. Cholesterol–sphingomyelin interactions emerge as a key thermodynamic driver that fine-tunes membrane order, fluidity, and permeability. Remarkably, membranes containing up to 60 mol% of cholesterol remain permeable to water and physiologically relevant osmolytes at rates compatible with growth of JCVI-syn3A. Together, our results define the physicochemical principles underlying minimal cell membranes and reveal how lipid composition enables passive permeation while maintaining membrane integrity.Subject terms: Membrane lipids, Membrane biophysics, Synthetic biology, Cryoelectron microscopy

Introduction

Understanding the molecular mechanisms that govern reactions, translocation, signaling, and homeostasis within cells is essential for comprehending the principles that regulate cellular life. Simplifying a model to its basics is an effective strategy to deduce general rules, which can later be applied and implemented in more complex systems. Recent advances in synthetic biology have demonstrated that it is possible to create minimal cells with reduced genomes and to transplant cellular chassis with synthetic genomes. When minimizing cells, non-essential genes are removed while preserving the essential functions of a living cell1,2. However, a complicating factor can be that many genes are only essential in specific genetic contexts and are therefore considered quasi-essential3,4.

The creation of JCVI-syn3A from Mycoplasma mycoides capri serves as an effective benchmark for understanding the fundamental principles that govern cellular life at the molecular level. The genome of JCVI-syn3A, approximately 0.5 Mbp in size3, is contained in a cell with a volume of around 0.03 μm³ (femtoliter), which is comparable to that of large-unilamellar vesicles with a diameter of 400 nm. Such vesicles are used in the bottom-up approach of synthetic biology, where cell-like systems are constructed from molecular components5. While JCVI-syn3A metabolism and genome synthesis have gained considerable attention, comparatively little is known about the minimal lipid requirements of cells. Identifying a minimal lipidome is important because lipid membranes are the fundamental barrier that defines the cell, they ensure selective permeability, enable energy conservation and signal transduction, and support a variety of other essential cellular processes.

Mycoplasmas lack a cell wall and possess only a single membrane, making them structurally simple and easily accessible for membrane studies. Owing to their parasitic lifestyle, they lack the genes for most biosynthetic pathways, including those needed to produce the building blocks for lipid and protein synthesis. They thus depend on the exogenous supply of molecules from the host or the culture medium6. Most of these molecules need to be taken up by transporters, although some can enter or leave the cell by passive diffusion, which depends on the intrinsic permeability of the lipid bilayer. The exogenous supply of lipids allows manipulation of the lipid composition. Hence, the lipidome of JCVI-syn3A is not genetically encoded as the proteome is, but is instead shaped by the availability of molecules in the environment and biophysical constraints. This can be viewed as an evolutionary bottleneck: the lipid composition is (largely) shaped through the selection of membrane properties rather than the incorporation of specific lipid molecules.

Recent studies illustrate the implications of selection by environmental availability. Justice et al.7 showed that JCVI-syn3A is viable on a diet composed almost exclusively of diether-phosphocholine (PC) and cholesterol, with 99 mol% of its membrane composed of these two lipids. The specific growth rate was very low (doubling time of about 1 day) and was improved when diether-PC was replaced by the ester type lipid 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) (doubling time of ~14 h). However, in the POPC-containing medium, JCVI-syn3A still synthesised some diether-PG and cardiolipin through the activity of an uncharacterised gene product7. Since PG is absent from the growth medium and JCVI-syn3A lacks a complete de novo pathway for this lipid, the supplied phospholipids (the ester type POPC) may be hydrolysed and yield intermediates such as diacylglycerol and phosphatidic acid that are subsequently remodelled into PG8–10. Thus, cholesterol and POPC may form the basic lipidome of JCVI-syn3A but additional lipids can be formed.

Thornburg et al.11 have shown that a more complex membrane composition with cholesterol, sphingomyelin (SM), cardiolipin (CL), POPC, and DOPG present at 60:18:16:4:2 mol%, supports growth with a doubling time of 105 min. This lipid composition may reflect the need to balance membrane condensing and fluidizing effects. This study also shows that a minimal cell can not only thrive with a highly reduced genome (less than 500 genes), but also with a carefully tuned minimal lipidome. The unusually high cholesterol content of JCVI-syn3A can be understood in light of its evolutionary ancestor, Mycoplasma2,12,13. The membrane composition of Mycoplasmas is quite unusual, largely because, unlike phytoplasmas and acholeplasmas, most Mycoplasmas cannot synthesise fatty acids6. They lack fatty acid desaturases, the enzymes that convert saturated fatty acids into unsaturated ones, which are essential for maintaining membrane fluidity at lower temperatures6. To compensate for this effect, they incorporate large amounts of sterols into membranes that are otherwise rich in saturated lipids such as sphingomyelin6. Under these conditions, cholesterol prevents the formation of a solid-gel state. It stabilises a Lo phase (liquid-ordered phase), thereby maintaining membrane integrity and sufficient fluidity in the absence of a cell wall and desaturation enzymes11,14.

Rottem et al.14 have shown that Mycoplasma cells, adapted to low cholesterol levels, undergo pronounced lipid phase transitions, leading to impaired membrane transport and altered enzyme activity. By contrast, cells enriched in cholesterol did not exhibit such transitions, suggesting that the high sterol content maintains membranes in an intermediate ordered but still fluid state that secures both structural stability and the proper function of membrane proteins14. Compared to its parental strain, M. mycoides capri, JCVI-syn3A synthesizes similar levels of anionic lipids such as PG and CL. PC and SM are taken up from the environment, reaching ~10 mol% SM in M. mycoides and ~20 mol% in Syn3A, while PC accounts for ~20 mol% in M. mycoides and only ~4 mol% in Syn3A and 3B2,11,13,15. The higher cholesterol content (~45–50 mol% in M. mycoides versus ~ 60 mol% in JCVI-syn3A), together with the differences in PC and SM abundance, may reflect differences in the environment in which they were growing. We propose that the lipid composition of the first forms of life was determined by the availability of amphiphiles (fatty acids, lipids) and that physicochemical properties such as fluidity and permeability were the guiding principles for the evolution of the organisms.

Just as cholesterol plays a key role in maintaining optimal membrane properties in Mycoplasma, by condensing the bilayer and suppressing gel-phase formation and crystallization11,14, the earliest forms of life likely depended on chemically distinct but functionally analogous molecules. Branched-chain isoprenoid lipids, particularly terpenoids, are strong candidates for this role in primitive protocells16. Pure fatty acid-based membranes, while prebiotically plausible, are inherently unstable under fluctuating pH and temperature; terpenoids could have helped overcome these limitations, as methyl branching along the isoprenoid chain disrupts close inter-chain packing, prevents crystallization, maintains membrane fluidity across wide temperature ranges, and reduces membrane permeability through increased interpenetration of neighboring branched chains16. This parallel suggests that cholesterol-dependent membranes, such as those of JCVI-syn3A studied here, and branched-chain isoprenoid membranes characteristic of Archaea, and potentially early protocells, represent two alternative physicochemical solutions to the same fundamental requirement: a membrane that is simultaneously fluid, mechanically stable, and sufficiently impermeable to sustain cellular homeostasis. The physicochemical parameters characterized in this study, structure, lipid packing, phase-state, and permeability, are therefore relevant not only to the biology of minimal cells but also to the broader question of which membrane properties were necessary and sufficient for the emergence of the first living cells.

In this paper, we use a range of biophysical experimental techniques and molecular dynamics simulations to investigate the physicochemical properties of membranes mimicking those of JCVI-syn3A. In addition to the native-like composition (60% cholesterol, 18% SM, 16% CL, 4% POPC, 2% DOPG), we examine mixtures in which cholesterol and POPC were reciprocally varied and the other lipids were kept constant. This design was motivated by the recent studies of Justice et al., demonstrating that the lipidome of M. mycoides and JCVI-syn3A can be tuned by the exogenous lipid environment, and that cholesterol together with a single phosphatidylcholine species constitutes a minimal two-component membrane sufficient to support life7. In this context, cholesterol and POPC define a functionally minimal core, making their reciprocal variation a biologically meaningful compositional axis to explore.

Results

Cryo-EM reveals formation of stable vesicles from JCVI-Syn3A native-like lipid composition

Given that the experimentally determined membrane composition of JCVI-syn3A is rather unusual11, we first examined whether vesicles could be formed at a high cholesterol percentage of 60 mol%. To this end, we prepared vesicles by film hydration using three lipid compositions: Mixture 1 (60:18:16:4:2 mol% cholesterol: SM: CL: POPC: DOPG), mimicking the membrane composition of JCVI-syn3A11, Mixture 2 (40:18:16:24:2 mol%), and Mixture 3 (20:18:16:44:2 mol%). Thus, the three mixtures only differ in the ratio of cholesterol to POPC.

Following extrusion through 200 nm pore-size membranes, morphology was examined by cryo-EM and vesicle size distribution was assessed by dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis. Cryo-EM images confirmed that vesicles were formed across all conditions (Fig. 1a–c), revealing a heterogeneous population of both unilamellar and multilamellar vesicles without visible morphological defects (Supplementary Fig. 3). The vesicles appeared predominantly spherical and enclosed by a continuous lipid bilayer, indicating that even at 60 mol% cholesterol, the lipid mixtures retained their ability to self-assemble into closed bilayer structures. From DLS analysis, the intensity-weighted distributions revealed a main population centered around 90–150 nm radius (corresponding to 180–300 nm diameter) (Supplementary Fig. 4), consistent with the expected size of 200 nm pore-extruded vesicles. Mixtures 1 and 2 (60% and 40% cholesterol) displayed broader size distributions extending to ~600 nm, reflecting the reduced membrane deformability at high cholesterol content, while Mixture 3 (20% cholesterol) yielded a narrower, more monodisperse population consistent with a more fluid and mechanically deformable membrane.

Langmuir monolayers reveal fluid-like behavior of native JCVI-syn3A lipids

To gain insight into the molecular packing and phase state of the components of the lipid mixtures, we studied the properties of the individual lipids and the lipid mixtures at the air-buffer interface in Langmuir monolayers. From each surface pressure–area per molecule (π/A) isotherm, we calculated the lift-off area A0 (Å2), area per molecule (Å2), collapse area (Å2), collapse pressure (mN/m), and the compression modulus (mN/m). The lift-off area represents the initial molecular area at which intermolecular interactions begin and surface pressure becomes detectable (π ≈ 0.5 mN/m)17. A large lift-off area, A0, indicates that molecules remain non-interacting over relatively wide surface areas, consistent with a disordered and fluid arrangement characteristic of a liquid-expanded (LE) phase. In contrast, a small A0 implies that intermolecular contacts arise at much smaller molecular areas, reflecting tighter packing and stronger interactions within the LE region, which can facilitate the subsequent transition toward more condensed phases at higher surface pressures17. The π–A isotherms, compression modulus, and summary table of the key parameters of the individual lipid components are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1 and the accompanying text.

The π–A isotherms of the mixtures show a systematic shift toward larger molecular areas when the cholesterol content decreases from 60 mol% (Mixture 1) to 20 mol% (Mixture 3), indicating progressively looser molecular packing (Fig. 2a). Consistently, the lift-off area increased from 92.5 Å² to 104.6 Å² and 129.1 Å² for Mixtures 1, 2, and 3, respectively (Fig. 2a), which reflects a transition from a more condensed to a more expanded monolayer regime when the cholesterol concentration decreases. To further characterize monolayer packing and mechanical properties, we evaluated the compression modulus (Cs−1; Eq. 1, Methods). The maximum values of Cs−1 correspond to the most compressed state of the monolayer, which is manifested as the highest peak point of the Cs−1 vs π graphic18,19. In addition to reflecting molecular packing, the compression modulus provides insight into the physical state of the monolayer. As proposed by Davies and Rideal in the 1960s18, Cs−1 values of 0–12.5 mN/m correspond to a gaseous (G) state, 12.5–50 mN/m to a LE state, and 100–250 mN/m to a (liquid-condensed) LC state18.

The compression modulus values also decreased with lower cholesterol content (146, 137, and 125 mN/m for Mix 1, 2, and 3) (Fig. 2b), revealing a significant effect of lipid composition on the compression modulus (p < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 2b) and demonstrating that membranes become more compressible and less ordered when the cholesterol fraction is reduced. Despite these quantitative differences, all three mixtures remained within the LC regime, consistent with the stabilising influence of cholesterol and sphingomyelin. The key data are summarized in the table of Fig. 2e.

The interactions between molecules in mixed monolayers can be quantitatively assessed through the excess area of mixing (Aexc) and the excess Gibbs free energy (ΔGexc), which is directly derived from π–A isotherms (See “Methods”, Eqs. 2 and 3).

The excess area of mixing quantifies deviations from ideal mixing behavior in a monolayer. A value of Aexc = 0 corresponds to either ideal miscibility or complete immiscibility of the components, whereas any deviation from zero reflects partial miscibility20. When Aexc is positive, repulsive interactions between molecules result in looser packing and molecules occupy a larger area than predicted for an ideal mixture. This is indicative of poor mixing of lipids and the potential for phase separation. When Aexc is negative, the components mix well and pack more tightly than expected in an ideal system, reflecting favorable interactions between the components, such as strong attractive forces or good molecular arrangement. Two main effects govern this interaction: hydrophobic attraction between the acyl chains, and attractive electrostatic forces between charged headgroups21.

The magnitude of these interactions can be obtained from the π/A isotherms, which estimate the excess of Gibbs free energy21, ΔGexc(See Methods, Eq. 3).

For Mixture 1 and 2, 40–60 mol% of cholesterol, Aexc values are consistently negative across the compression range, leading to negative ΔGexc values (Fig. 2c, d). This indicates strong attractive interactions between the lipid components, resulting in compact monolayers. In contrast, Mixture 3 (20 mol% cholesterol) displayed slightly positive Aexc values and ΔGexc values close to zero (Fig. 2c, d), reflecting weaker lipid–lipid interactions, looser molecular packing, and a tendency toward reduced miscibility. This finding is surprising because cholesterol typically produces negative Aexc and ΔGexc values22,23. To further investigate the lipid mixing behavior of the membrane, we performed Brewster Angle Microscopy (BAM) (Supplementary Fig. 2c). For Mixture 3, BAM reveals persistent lateral heterogeneity across the entire surface pressure range, with contrast variations and dispersed bright features remaining visible upon compression, indicating that the monolayer remains laterally inhomogeneous across the entire surface pressure. This behavior, together with the close-to-zero or slightly positive excess area and ΔGexc values, is consistent with non-ideal mixing and lateral phase coexistence rather than a uniform phase.

Stopped-flow fluorescence assays reveal permeability of the native membrane to small osmolytes

Passive permeation of small molecules was likely crucial in early forms of life, before complex transport proteins had evolved, and it remains essential in modern forms of life. We performed permeability measurements, using a stopped-flow fluorescence assay, to quantify the diffusion of water (solvent), glycerol (simple carbon and energy source), and D-ribose (building block of nucleic acids) across membranes of lipid vesicles with varying cholesterol contents. We find that the permeability coefficient (P, cm/s) decreases systematically with cholesterol content for all three permeants (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 5). Water is the most permeable solute (P = 3.12 ± 1 × 10–3 cm/s for Mixture 1; 6.27 ± 1.7 × 10–3 cm/s for Mixture 2; 1.44 ± 0.1 × 10-2 cm/s for Mixture 3), consistent with its small size and high permeability, even though many cells do have aquaporins24,25. The permeability coefficients for glycerol and D-ribose are much lower, and, consistent with their size and structure, the permeation of D-ribose is 2-orders of magnitude slower than that of glycerol (Supplementary Fig. 5d–f). Yet, these permeability coefficients are in a range that enables substantial fluxes across the membrane in the absence of transporters or porins. Averaged kinetic traces are presented in Fig. 3 (glycerol) and Supplementary Fig. 5 (water and D-Ribose), while representative individual fits and residuals are shown in Supplementary Fig. 6.

Overall, these results are consistent with Frallicciardi et al.24, who demonstrated that membrane permeability is largely determined by the phase state, decreasing drastically from liquid-disordered (Ld) to liquid-ordered (Lo) and gel phases. Here, the insights are primarily based on simplified binary or ternary lipid mixtures. In contrast, the JCVI-syn3A membrane is composed of a more complex and distinctive combination of lipids, including cholesterol, cardiolipin, sphingomyelin, POPC, and DOPG, present in specific proportions. Such compositional complexity introduces non-trivial physicochemical interactions that cannot be directly extrapolated from simpler model systems. In agreement with Frallicciardi et al.24, we observe that increasing cholesterol content in JCVI-syn3A-mimicking membranes progressively reduces the permeability to water, glycerol, and ribose. Despite the unusually high fraction of order-promoting lipids in the JCVI-syn3A membrane (60 mol% cholesterol plus 18 mol% sphingomyelin), the measured water permeability (~3.0 × 10⁻³ cm/s) is remarkably similar to that reported for POPC membranes containing 45 mol% cholesterol (~2.38 × 10⁻³ cm/s)24. According to that study, the transition from the Ld to the Lo phase occurs between 15 and 30 mol% cholesterol in POPC, with further sterol incorporation shifting the bilayer into a cholesterol-saturated Lo regime. Our results suggest that JCVI-syn3A membranes also operate within this Lo plateau.

Molecular dynamics simulations reveal membrane packing details

To provide a molecular-level understanding of how cholesterol shapes the organization and properties of the JCVI-syn3A membrane, we performed coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations with the Martini force field. By coarse-graining atomistic details into effective beads, the Martini model allows for efficient sampling of molecular configurations, all the way to the whole-cell scale26–29.

To mimic the experimentally derived native-like JCVI-syn3A membrane composition (Mixture 1), a spherical vesicle (~200 nm in diameter) containing 60 mol% cholesterol was constructed and simulated for 1 μs (Fig. 4). The vesicle remained stable throughout the simulation, consistent with the experimental observations, and maintained a tightly packed bilayer organization with cholesterol molecules intercalated between neighboring lipid tails across the bilayer (Fig. 4).

Beyond the native cholesterol regime, we additionally simulated a highly cholesterol-enriched vesicle containing 80 mol% cholesterol for 1 μs to further examine membrane organization under extreme cholesterol loading. In contrast to the 60 mol% cholesterol, the vesicles with 80 mol% cholesterol developed local cholesterol-rich interdigitated, monolayer-like regions after approximately 200 ns, which progressively expanded throughout the simulations (Supplementary Fig. 7). This behavior indicates that excessive cholesterol loading can alter canonical bilayer organization, whereas the native-like membrane with 60 mol% cholesterol remains stable over the same timescale.

We next performed a systematic set of coarse-grained simulations on smaller lamellar bilayers to quantitatively characterize the effect of cholesterol on membrane structure, dynamics, and mechanics. The simulated lipid compositions corresponded to the experimental mixtures (Mixtures 1–3, Fig. 1), while additional systems spanning a broader cholesterol range were included to probe membrane behavior under extreme conditions (Supplementary Fig. 8, Table S1). These included cholesterol-free membrane (0 mol%), highly cholesterol-enriched membranes (65–70%), and a pure POPC bilayer as a control. Simulations were performed at both 293 K, matching the experimental conditions, and 310 K, corresponding to the optimal growth temperature of JCVI-syn3A, similar to Mycoplasma mycoides.

Density profiles revealed the canonical organization expected for stable bilayers across the entire cholesterol concentration and temperature range, as illustrated for Mixture 1 at 310 K (Fig. 5a). A small population of cholesterol molecules was also detected in the bilayer midplane, in line with previous simulations of cholesterol-containing membranes, and this fraction increased with overall cholesterol content as reported in earlier simulation studies30 (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10).

At 293 K, bilayer thickness remained within a narrow range across the mixtures containing 20–60 mol% cholesterol (Mixtures 1–3) (Fig. 5b, Table S2). At 310 K, however, a clearer cholesterol-dependent trend emerges: MD simulations spanning a broader cholesterol range (0–70 mol%) reveal a progressive increase in membrane thickness with increasing sterol content up to 60 mol%, beyond which the thickness approaches a plateau (Fig. 5b, Table S2).

Consistent with the experimental observations, increasing cholesterol concentration reduced the average area occupied by each lipid (Fig. 5c, Table S3) and increased lipid tail ordering (Fig. 5d, Tables S4 and S5), reflecting the condensing effect of cholesterol on the bilayer.

For the cholesterol-free system (POPC:SM:CL:DOPG, 63:18:17:2 mol%), the membrane exhibited gel-fluid phase coexistence at 293 K (Supplementary Fig. 11), driven by the presence of sphingomyelin. This behavior was reflected in the higher membrane thickness and increased lipid ordering observed for the cholesterol-free membrane at 293 K. It is also consistent with the expected phase behavior of cholesterol-free Mycoplasma/JCVI-like membranes at low temperature, where cholesterol suppresses gel formation and phase separation14. At cholesterol concentrations above 60%, membrane thickness and lipid ordering approached a plateau, while cholesterol increasingly populated the bilayer midplane, suggesting altered cholesterol accommodation and a saturation of cholesterol-induced membrane ordering under these conditions31. Notably, the emergence of cholesterol-rich interdigitated regions in the 80 mol% vesicle provides a structural correlate for this saturation behavior.

Linked to the increase in lipid ordering, molecular dynamics simulations showed that the water permeability decreased approximately six-fold when the cholesterol concentration was increased from 20% to 60% (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Fig. 12, Table S6). While the simulations reproduced the experimentally observed decrease in permeability (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 5a), the magnitude of the cholesterol-induced barrier effect was larger in the simulations. Lateral lipid diffusion was also reduced with cholesterol enrichment (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Fig. 13, Table S7 and S8), consistent with the emergence of a more ordered and less dynamic bilayer. Note that absolute values of dynamic quantities obtained from coarse-grained models need to be considered with care and are usually overestimated due to smoothening of the underlying energy landscape32.

At the mechanical level, membranes became progressively stiffer with increasing cholesterol content, as reflected by the increase in bending rigidity (Fig. 5g, Table S9). This trend closely mirrors the experimental increase in the compression modulus measured from Langmuir monolayers (Fig. 2b, e), indicating that both approaches capture the cholesterol-dependent stiffening of the membrane. Bending rigidity reached its highest values between approximately 40 and 60 mol% cholesterol, before decreasing at higher cholesterol contents.

Rupture pressure, defined as the critical lateral pressure at which a preformed pore underwent irreversible expansion, increased with cholesterol content (Fig. 5h, Supplementary Fig. 14, Table S10), indicating that cholesterol enrichment enhances the mechanical robustness of the JCVI-syn3A envelope, which is important for cells without a cell wall. Rupture pressure increased upon cholesterol addition and remained elevated at high cholesterol concentrations.

Consistent with the observed increase in membrane stiffness, the bilayer area compressibility modulus also increased strongly with cholesterol content (Table 1). Although direct quantitative comparison is difficult because the experiments probe monolayer rather than bilayer compression, both approaches indicate that cholesterol increases membrane resistance to compression. Similar cholesterol-dependent increases in compressibility have also been reported for phosphatidylcholine–cholesterol bilayers33.

To assess the robustness of these conclusions to variations in lipid composition, we additionally examined the effect of cardiolipin acyl-chain composition. Because cardiolipin contains four acyl chains, variation in its acyl-chain composition can generate a broader range of molecular structures than in two-tailed phospholipids. Together with lipidomic evidence that cardiolipin composition in mycoplasmas depends on the available fatty acid pool7, this made cardiolipin a relevant candidate for testing whether our conclusions depend on the exact lipid species used. We therefore performed additional simulations in which tetraoleoyl cardiolipin (TOCL; 18:1) was replaced by the more unsaturated tetralinoleoyl cardiolipin (TLCL; 18:2) in Mixtures 1–3 (Supplementary Fig. 15a and 15b). Despite the increased degree of unsaturation, the cholesterol-dependent trends in membrane thickness and area per lipid were preserved, indicating that the condensing effect of cholesterol is robust to this variation in cardiolipin acyl-chain composition. TLCL-containing membranes also showed enhanced local cholesterol–sphingomyelin co-enrichment, but this remained dynamic and did not develop into stable macroscopic domains over the 5 μs simulations (Supplementary Fig. 15c).

Together, these simulations demonstrate how cholesterol shapes the structural, dynamic, and mechanical properties of the JCVI-syn3A membrane, in close agreement with the experimental observations. By reducing permeability, enhancing lipid ordering, and increasing mechanical robustness, cholesterol emerges as a key determinant of membrane stability in the JCVI-syn3A minimal cell.

Discussion

We combined cryo-TEM, Langmuir monolayer measurements, permeability assays, and molecular dynamics simulations to define the physicochemical principles governing permeability and membrane integrity in minimal cell membranes. Our results show how lipid packing, miscibility of components, and the combined variation of cholesterol and POPC content shape the physical state and functional properties of minimal cell membranes composed of the five main lipids of JCVI-syn3A cells.

Although cholesterol is known to exert a thickening effect in bilayers composed of POPC34 and DPPC/cholesterol mixtures35, we do not observe a significant increase in thickness when cholesterol content is increased from 20% to 60% in our membrane lipid mixtures (Fig. 5b). The absence of major changes in bilayer thickness may be explained either by a compensatory effect of the increased POPC concentration relative to cholesterol content or by a saturation of the cholesterol-induced ordering. Indeed, the MD simulations show that the thickness follows a saturation behavior (Fig. 5b). The bilayer thickness changes only marginally between 20% and 60%, suggesting that the saturation point may be reached at a concentration below 60% cholesterol. This “early” plateau is consistent with the presence of sphingomyelin. Because cholesterol and sphingomyelin form relatively stable complexes36–40, the membrane might reach its effective sterol saturation at lower cholesterol percentages. Thus, two complementary effects: saturation of SM/cholesterol–driven ordering and disordering by increased concentrations of POPC may largely average out the cholesterol-induced thickening.

The condensing behavior of cholesterol is supported by the Langmuir monolayer analysis, which provides direct insight into the packing properties of the individual lipids and their mixtures. Cholesterol and sphingomyelin display strong condensing effects, forming compact and rigid monolayers, whereas DOPG and cardiolipin show loose packing, which relates to the electrostatic repulsions and to the large lift-off areas (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Compared to other DO lipids such as DOPE and DOPC, DOPG displays a much larger lift-off area, because, like cardiolipin, it has a negatively charged headgroup25. The associated electrostatic repulsions increase the effective molecular area and disfavor the tight packing of the molecules25. The tendency of DOPG and cardiolipin monolayers to form loosely packed, highly fluid films reflects not only their bulky anionic headgroups but also the unsaturated acyl chains. Moreover, cardiolipin destabilises compact packing at the air–water interface due to its relatively small, negatively charged headgroup, which is connected to four acyl chains and together results in a large effective molecular area41. As a result, cardiolipin-containing monolayers typically display a high lift-off area and a low compression modulus, consistent with loose molecular packing and enhanced elasticity41,42 (Supporting Fig. 1a and b). POPC shows intermediate behavior, confirming its more expanded and disordered character compared to cholesterol and sphingomyelin43. Together, these results underline the distinct packing properties of each lipid and provide the basis for understanding the behavior of complex mixtures, here represented by sphingomyelin, cholesterol, POPC, DOPG plus cardiolipin.

Cholesterol increases membrane packing and order, and these effects are reinforced by saturated lipids like sphingomyelin (Figs. 2e, 5d, g). Nevertheless, all mixtures tested remain within the LC state. Since our mixtures are complex and involve multiple (five) lipid species, their behavior can also be interpreted by comparison with published data on binary and ternary systems. Cholesterol is well documented to exert a strong condensing effect34 at low to intermediate concentrations (<30 mol%) in DPPC monolayers, whereas at higher levels (>30–50 mol%) it tends to segregate, thereby losing its ability to compact the film35. Moreover, cholesterol condenses monolayers of monounsaturated and unsaturated phospholipids (POPC, DOPC) efficiently, while the effect is smaller or nearly ideal in saturated systems with DPPC or DPPG, but in all cases the insertion of cholesterol stabilises the films thermodynamically (ΔGexc <0)23. However, while the condensing effect of cholesterol appears greater in fluid monolayers such as POPC or DOPC, its solubility in unsaturated acyl chains is substantially lower than in saturated or monounsaturated lipids44.

Molecular dynamics simulations45,46 have shown that cholesterol shows a higher affinity for saturated acyl chains (e.g., DPPC, SM) than unsaturated ones, because it aligns better with the membrane axis of the bilayer, promoting order and stable packing. In membranes with unsaturated chains (e.g., DOPC), cis double bonds introduce “kinks” in the acyl chains, which hinder alignment with cholesterol and create disorder. The ‘entropic cost’ of placing cholesterol next to unsaturated chains causes the latter to assume less favorable conformations, making the membrane less stable. Instead, in membranes enriched in saturated chains, cholesterol inserts without an entropic penalty and stabilizes the packing. In agreement with these simulations, experimental studies37 have demonstrated that among SM/DOPC, SM/DPPC, and SM/cholesterol mixtures, the attractive interactions between SM and cholesterol are the strongest, with the optimal cholesterol/SM molar ratio being 1:3. In line with these findings, cholesterol drives lateral segregation and the formation of Lo domains in mixed membranes47. Consistently, the excess free enthalpy of mixing (ΔGexc) is most favorable for SM/cholesterol mixtures compared to DPPC/SM and DOPC/SM systems, indicating weaker or even repulsive interactions in the latter cases, thereby favoring partial phase separation and domain formation37.

Cholesterol and sphingomyelin are also well known as key constituents of lipid rafts, where their lateral interactions drive the formation of ordered microdomains in LD membranes40,47. They combine high acyl chain order with lateral fluidity, allowing them to act as dynamic platforms for protein recruitment and membrane organisation48. Cholesterol- and sphingomyelin-rich rafts are compositionally stable, yet laterally dynamic and capable of shape fluctuations and coalescence while retaining liquid-ordered properties48. In contrast, cardiolipin, POPC, and DOPG, represent the more fluid constituents. Cardiolipin expands the LE phase when added to DPPC membranes, thereby increasing elasticity and reducing packing density41. POPC, with one unsaturated oleoyl chain, introduces disorder and fluidity. Similarly, DOPG also leads to looser packing and more fluidity. Together, these lipids counterbalance the condensing effects of cholesterol and sphingomyelin by promoting expanded and more compressible bilayers.

Overall, our data indicate that the interplay between condensing lipids (cholesterol, SM) and fluidising components (CL, POPC, DOPG) defines the packing and mechanical properties of the membranes, with Mixture 1 representing the most ordered and Mixture 3 the most fluid state. Even though the relative amount of POPC is high in Mixture 3 (44 mol%), the compression modulus remains within the range characteristic of the liquid-condensed (LC) state (122 mN/m). This indicates that the disordering effect of POPC is effectively counterbalanced by the strong condensing effect of cholesterol and sphingomyelin. In addition, although the lipid composition varies across the mixtures, the π–A isotherms retain a similar overall shape and exhibit collapse at comparable surface pressures. This suggests that the fundamental monolayer organization and collapse mechanism are preserved, with compositional changes primarily shifting the molecular packing density rather than altering the mode of collapse.

Our results are consistent with the findings of Safronova et al.15, who reported that the lipid order of M. mycoides and JCVI-syn3B membranes, quantified by Pro12A generalised polarisation (GP), lies in the range of ∼0.35-0.45. These values indicate relatively ordered, low-fluidity membranes comparable to those of the plasma membrane of mammalian cells, with M. mycoides exhibiting a GP value of ~0.35 and JCVI-syn3B a value of ~0.45 at 30 °C, consistent with a higher cholesterol and sphingomyelin content15. Safronova et al.15 investigated lipid order in the temperature range of 30-37 °C to determine whether M. mycoides and JCVI-syn3A maintain membrane viscosity within physiological limits (homeoviscous adaptation)49. In the absence of homeoviscosity, lipid order would increase upon cooling, but this turned out not to be the case15. M. mycoides appeared slightly more effective in preserving its lipid order than JCVI-syn3A.

To explore how thermodynamic stabilisation and lateral organisation are coupled in the complex lipid mixtures, we assessed their miscibility through excess area and excess free energy of mixing derived from the π–A isotherms. The negative ΔGexc values (Fig. 2d) indicate that the mixtures are thermodynamically stabilized by strong cholesterol–sphingomyelin interactions, even though this stabilization does not require complete lateral homogeneity. In bilayers and vesicles, such stabilization is expected to translate into the coexistence of cholesterol/SM-enriched Lo regions embedded in a fluid matrix45,46, providing a balance between membrane order, elasticity, and heterogeneity. Consistent with this view, solid-state ²H NMR analysis of POPC/SM/cholesterol bilayers revealed the coexistence of nanoscopic cholesterol/SM-enriched regions in the Ld phase at temperatures well above the main phase transition, supporting thermodynamic stabilization despite persistent nanoscale lateral heterogeneity50.

Overall, considering both literature and our experimental data, the three mixtures display distinct but related membrane behaviors. Mixture 1 (60 mol% cholesterol) can be described as a cholesterol-saturated system dominated by cholesterol–sphingomyelin interactions, resulting in a highly ordered, LC membrane. The low fraction of unsaturated lipids (4 mol% POPC and 2 mol% DOPG) is insufficient to generate macroscopic Ld domains. Although nanoscale heterogeneities cannot be excluded, the negative excess area of mixing and ΔGexc values reflect dominant attractive interactions, primarily driven by cholesterol–cholesterol and cholesterol–SM condensation effects.

In Mixture 2 (40 mol% cholesterol), cholesterol–SM interactions remain predominant, as reflected by the still negative thermodynamic parameters. The membrane retains a condensed character, but with increased fluidity compared to Mixture 1. This behavior is consistent with previous studies on DOPC:Chol:SM (1:2:1) systems51, which remain in a condensed state but do not form stable, well-defined raft domains. Brewster Angle Microscopy (BAM) observations in such systems typically show initial coexistence of gas and liquid phases at low surface pressure, followed by the formation of a more homogeneous layer upon compression, suggesting limited lateral segregation.

Mixture 3 (20 mol% cholesterol) exhibits the most complex and physically interesting behavior. Despite remaining within the LC regime based on compression modulus values, the membrane is significantly more fluid due to the high POPC content (44 mol%). Notably, the combined presence of sphingomyelin and cholesterol is still sufficient to maintain an overall condensed character, indicating that the disordering effect of POPC is counterbalanced rather than dominant. In contrast to the other mixtures, the thermodynamic signature of Mixture 3 changes markedly, with excess area and ΔGexc values becoming close to zero or slightly positive. This behavior is consistent with non-ideal mixing and suggests weaker net interactions at the macroscopic level. However, in multicomponent systems containing saturated lipids, unsaturated lipids, and cholesterol, such thermodynamic signatures are often associated with lateral phase separation into coexisting Lo and Ld phases over a broad compositional range, including cholesterol concentrations around 20–30 mol%52,53. In these systems, cholesterol-rich Lo domains are typically enriched in saturated lipids such as sphingomyelin, while unsaturated lipids like POPC preferentially partition into the Ld phase. Importantly, when phase coexistence occurs, the mean molecular areas derived from π–A isotherms represent an average over coexisting domains rather than a single homogeneous phase. This averaging effect can lead to apparent excess area values that are close to zero or positive, even in the presence of locally condensed domains. This interpretation is supported by Brewster Angle Microscopy (BAM) observations (Supplementary Fig. 2c) for Mixture 3, which reveal lateral heterogeneity across the entire surface pressure range, typical of the LC state. Furthermore, lipid domains in such systems are not necessarily detectable at the optical microscopy scale. Depending on composition and temperature, domain sizes may fall within the nanoscale regime, remaining invisible in GUV imaging despite the presence of thermodynamically distinct phases54. This is particularly relevant for mixtures with a high fraction of POPC, where reduced domain size is commonly observed.

The combination of moderate cholesterol content and a high proportion of unsaturated lipids in Mixture 3 creates conditions favorable for compositional heterogeneity and phase separation. These effects can significantly influence the apparent thermodynamic parameters derived from Langmuir monolayers. While previous studies on DOPC:Chol:SM (2:1:1) systems51, reported homogeneous behavior with negative ΔGexc, it is important to note that in our system, the fluidizing lipid is POPC rather than DOPC. The different acyl chain composition, together with the presence of 16 mol% cardiolipin, may modulate lipid packing and influence lateral organization of the membrane. Under conditions where fluidizing and condensing components are balanced, this may lead to the emergence of membrane heterogeneity, potentially affecting the distribution and stability of ordered domains.

In addition to its structural effects, cardiolipin, together with other anionic lipids such as phosphatidylglycerol, is well known to play a key role in membrane protein function and stability. This suggests that its presence in minimal membranes may be primarily driven by the functional requirements of embedded proteins. For instance, the mitochondrial ADP/ATP carrier (AAC) exhibits maximal activity in membranes containing ~10 mol% cardiolipin, indicating specific lipid–protein interactions are required for optimal transporter function55. Similarly, the bacterial ABC transporter OpuA from Lactococcus lactis, which mediates glycine betaine uptake under osmotic stress, strongly depends on membrane composition and requires anionic phospholipids such as PG and CL for activity56.

Consistent with the Langmuir monolayer analysis, permeability measurements show that POPC, cardiolipin, and DOPG counteract the strong condensing effect of cholesterol and sphingomyelin. Our measurements show that the permeability coefficient decreases systematically with cholesterol content, confirming the general principle that sterols modulate permeability through changes in lipid order24. Similarly, Safronova et al.15 reported that the higher lipid order of JCVI-syn3B membranes correlates with reduced permeability: fluorescein diacetate (FDA) permeability was approximately 3-fold higher in M. mycoides than in JCVI-syn3B. Our permeability experiments show that water, glycerol and ribose permeate with permeability coefficients of 10-3 - 10-2 cm/s for water, 10-6 to 10-7 cm/s for glycerol and 10-8 to 10-9 cm/s for ribose. These values indicate that channel proteins (aquaporins, glyceroporins) are not required for water and glycerol transport to support growth of Mycoplasmas. We note that multilamellar vesicles introduce additional membrane barriers, which can lead to a systematic underestimation of the absolute permeability values, and that this occurs in a manner that is difficult to correct. However, since this effect applies consistently across all cholesterol concentrations tested, the relative trends in permeability as a function of lipid composition remain valid and do not affect the conclusions from the measurements.

To assess the physiological relevance of these passive fluxes, one can apply Fick’s first law, whereby the total molecular flux across the membrane is the product of the permeability coefficient, the transmembrane concentration gradient, and the cell surface area. Assuming a 10 mM external concentration and low intracellular levels, a JCVI-syn3A-sized cell (radius ~0.2 µm) would accumulate ~10,000 glycerol and ~150 ribose molecules per second. These passive fluxes correspond to theoretical doubling times of ~2.5 h for glycerol and ~4 days for ribose if these molecules would be the sole carbon sources. While glycerol uptake occurs at rates compatible with cellular growth, approaching the doubling time of JCVI-syn3A (~2 h)57, even the slower ribose flux remains non-negligible and be sufficient for e.g. the synthesis of nucleic acids. Our data indicate that passive permeation can contribute meaningfully to carbon acquisition in small cells with high surface-to-volume ratios.

The observations for glycerol are particularly relevant because glycerol metabolism is a key factor in the virulence of Mycoplasma pneumoniae. Glycerol metabolism is mediated by glycerol-3-phosphate oxidase, which oxidises glycerol-3-phosphate, using oxygen as the electron acceptor, generating hydrogen peroxide and contributing to oxidative stress in host epithelial cells6. Thus, the ability of glycerol to diffuse across the membrane, even at high cholesterol levels, is critical for the pathogenic potential of Mycoplasmas. In the context of JCVI-syn3a, glycerol permeability is particularly relevant because it is required for the synthesis of essential anionic phospholipids. As shown by lipidomic analyses from the Saenz lab11, the uptake of exogenous phosphatidylcholine, sphingomyelin, and cholesterol from the medium represents the primary mechanism for rapidly altering cellular lipid biomass11. At the same time, the passive diffusion of small metabolites such as glycerol, together with fatty acids like palmitate and oleate, enables the endogenous synthesis of PG and CL, thereby completing the minimal lipidome required for membrane stability and function11.

Taken together, our Langmuir and permeability data are in line with studies of Rottem et al.14, who demonstrated that the unusually high cholesterol content of mycoplasma membranes prevents lipid crystallisation. Fluorescence polarisation and differential scanning calorimetry revealed no detectable phase transition in native M. mycoides var. capri membranes with 20–25 mol% of cholesterol, whereas adapted strains with much lower sterol levels (~3 mol% of the total lipid) did undergo phase transitions.

The molecular dynamics simulations extend the experimental observations by revealing how cholesterol is accommodated within the JCVI-syn3A membrane over a broad compositional range. While increasing cholesterol content promotes tighter packing, reduced permeability, and enhanced mechanical stability, several membrane properties displayed a common saturation-like behavior. Membrane thickness, lipid ordering, bending rigidity, and rupture pressure increased up to approximately 40–60 mol% cholesterol, after which further cholesterol enrichment produced only modest additional changes. This saturation coincided with an increasing population of cholesterol molecules in the bilayer midplane, suggesting that excess cholesterol is progressively accommodated through alternative packing modes rather than continued enhancement of membrane order. Consistent with this interpretation, vesicles containing 80 mol% cholesterol developed cholesterol-rich interdigitated regions that were absent in the native-like membrane. Together, these findings indicate that the cholesterol-rich JCVI-syn3A membrane operates close to a regime in which additional cholesterol incorporation yields diminishing structural and mechanical returns, highlighting both the stabilizing role and the packing limits of cholesterol in minimal-cell membranes. JCVI-syn3A membranes occupy a finely constrained physicochemical regime in which order and fluidity coexist to ensure permeability and function. This conserved balance enables survival of Mycoplasma species despite extreme lipid compositions and suggests that membrane properties themselves impose evolutionary constraints. We propose that the physicochemical identity of the membrane acts as a blueprint for life, restricting viable evolutionary trajectories to a narrow window of stability, permeability, and adaptability. By defining this window in a minimal cell, our work reveals fundamental principles that may have governed the emergence of the first protocells and continue to shape all cellular life.

Materials and methods

Materials

Lipids were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AL). The following lipids were used: Cholesterol (Chol), Egg sphingomyelin (DPSM, CAS: 383907-87-7), 1’,3’-bis[1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho]-glycerol (sodium salt) (Cardiolipin, CL. CAS: 115404-77-8), 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC, CAS: 26853-31-6), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-(1′-rac-glycerol) sodium salt (DOPG, CAS: 67254-28-8). The hypertonic solutions used for the permeability measurements were prepared from potassium chloride (pro analysis; BOOM, Meppel, Netherlands), glycerol (BOOM, Meppel, Netherlands), and D-(-)-Ribose (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany).

Preparation of vesicles

Liposomes were prepared using the following synthetic lipid mixtures: cholesterol: sphingomyelin: cardiolipin: POPC: DOPG (60:18:16:4:2 mol%, Mixture 1); cholesterol: sphingomyelin: cardiolipin: POPC: DOPG (40:18:16:24:2 mol%, Mixture 2); and cholesterol: sphingomyelin: cardiolipin: POPC: DOPG (20:18:16:44:2 mol%, Mixture 3). Lipids (stocks of 25 mg/ml for each component) were dissolved in chloroform and mixed at the desired molar ratio to a final concentration of 10 mg/ml. After mixing, the organic solvent was removed by evaporation with a rotary evaporator (Rotovapor r-3; BUCHI, Flawil, Switzerland), and the lipid film at the bottom of the flask was subsequently rehydrated in 100 mM KPi pH 7.0 to a concentration of 10 mg of total lipid/ml at 50 °C, with gentle rotation for 5 min. The suspension was briefly sonicated to ensure complete dispersion of the lipid film, and, subsequently, the sample was extruded through a 200 nm polycarbonate membrane to increase vesicle homogeneity and increase the fraction of unilamellar vesicles.

Cryo-TEM

Liposomes 2.5 μL (5 mg/ml) were placed on a glow-discharged holey carbon-coated grid (Quantifoil 3.5/1, QUANTIFOIL Micro Tools GmbH). After blotting, the corresponding grid was rapidly frozen in liquid ethane (Vitrobot, FEI) and kept in liquid nitrogen until measurement. The grids were observed with a Gatan model 626 cryostage in a Tecnai T20 Field Electron and Ion Company (FEI) cryo-electron microscope operating at 200 keV. Cryo-TEM images were recorded under low-dose conditions on a slow-scan Charge-Coupled Device (CCD) camera.

Langmuir monolayer measurements

The surface pressure–area per molecule (π–A) isotherms were measured in 100 mM NaPi, pH 7.0, using the Wilhelmy paper plate with an accuracy of 0.1 mN/m. Lipid solutions were spread using a micro syringe (Hamilton–Bonaduz, Switzerland) on the buffer subphase, and the solvent was allowed to evaporate over 5 min before starting the compression of the film with the trough barriers moving at a rate of 1.5 cm2/min. Each isotherm was repeated at least three times to confirm its reproducibility.

BAM experiments

Brewster angle microscopy (BAM) was employed to monitor changes in the morphology and physical properties of Langmuir monolayers. Measurements were performed using a Langmuir trough with a surface area of 841 cm². Monolayers were prepared following the same protocol as for the π–A isotherm experiments. BAM images covered an area of approximately 800 µm × 430 µm and were acquired at a lateral resolution of 2 µm.

Liposomes for calcein experiments

A stock of calcein (from Sigma-Aldrich) was prepared at a concentration of 100 mM in 50 mM KPi, and the pH of the calcein stock was adjusted to 7.0 using 5 M NaOH. Individual lipid components (stock solutions of 25 mg/ml in chloroform) were combined at the desired molar ratio to a final lipid concentration of 3 mg/ml. The organic solvent was removed by rotary evaporation (Rotovapor R−3; BÜCHI, Flawil, Switzerland) to yield a dry lipid film, which was subsequently rehydrated in 1 ml solution of 10 mM calcein (self-quenching concentration) and in 90 mM KPi (pH 7.0) at 50 °C for 5 min with gentle agitation. The osmolality of the vesicle lumen was ∼190 mosmol/kg, which equals that of the assay buffer (100 mM KPi pH 7.0). After extrusion through a 200 nm polycarbonate filter, the vesicles were separated from free calcein dye on a 22-cm-long Sephadex-G75 (Sigma-Aldrich) column pre-equilibrated with 100 mM KPi pH 7.0. The collected 1 mL fractions containing the calcein-filled liposomes were identified by eye, using an ultraviolet lamp and diluted in a total volume of 12 mL of 100 mM KPi pH 7.0.

Stopped-flow experiments

Permeability measurements were conducted using the method of Frallicciardi et al.58. Briefly, a stopped-flow apparatus (SX20; Applied Photophysics, Leatherhead, Surrey, UK) operated in single-mixing mode was used to measure the changes in the fluorescence intensity kinetics upon application of an osmotic upshift to the vesicles filled with calcein. The hypertonic solutions (100 mM KCl in 100 mM KPi pH 7.0; 240 mM glycerol in 100 mM KPi pH 7.0; and 100 mM ribose in 100 mM KPi, pH 7.0) and the vesicle solutions were loaded each in one syringe, and forced first through the mixer (1:1 mixing ratio and 2 ms dead time) and then injected into the optical chamber (20 μL volume and 2 mm path length). Since the hypertonic solution and vesicles are mixed in the chamber at a mixing ratio of 1:1, the final concentration of KCl, glycerol and ribose is 50, 120, and 50 mM, respectively; the basal medium was 100 mM KPi pH 7.0. Calcein was excited at 495 nm and the emitted light, collected at 90°, was filtered by a Schott long-pass filter and detected by a photomultiplier tube with 10 μs time resolution. The voltage of the photomultiplier was automatically selected and kept constant during each set of experiments. Three acquisitions fi(t) were performed for each experimental condition. By osmotically shocking the vesicles, the water efflux causes vesicle shrinkage and increases the calcein concentration thus decreasing the fluorescence signal. When the hypertonic solution contains permeable osmolytes, these molecules will diffuse into the vesicles and (partially) restore the volume, which is seen as an increase in fluorescence. The decrease in fluorescence upon water influx provides information on the permeability coefficient for water, whereas the increase in fluorescence at later times yields the permeability coefficient for the (permeable) osmolyte. A detailed description of the model and analysis of the data are presented in refs. 58,59.

Processing of the data to obtain permeability coefficients

A detailed protocol for the fitting of the stopped-flow kinetic data is given in ref. 58. Briefly, the raw data are preprocessed in MATLAB (R2023a; The MathWorks, Natick, MA). First, the N curves, where N represents the number of acquisitions (N = 3), which we call fi(t), acquired in a given experimental condition are averaged (F(t) = N − 1∑fi(t)) and normalized to 1 at time zero (F(t)/F(0)). Second, a fit of the kinetics data is performed to calculate the time evolution of the calcein fluorescence (F(t)/F(0)). The averaged <F(t)/F(0)> curves, along with the vesicle size distribution profile, determined by Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS, see below), are fed to the fitting routine script ‘Ft_ODEfit.m’, which computes the relaxation curves of the calcein concentration by numerical solution of the system of differential equations describing the dynamics of a spherical vesicles upon osmotic upshift. Using the Stern–Volmer equation with dynamic quenching constant KSV, the numerical solution is used to calculate F(t)/F(0). After feeding the fitting script with the relaxation curves and the vesicle distribution profile, the pKa of the acid and the osmolyte concentration must be set to start the fitting routine. For impermeable osmolytes, like KCl, two fitting parameters are used: Pw (water permeability coefficient, cm/s) and the quenching constant KSV (M-1). For permeable osmolytes such as glycerol, pKa 14.4 (PubChem database, compound ID: 753), D-Ribose, pKa 11.8 (Sudeshna Sen et al., 2014) (PubChem database, compound ID: 5779), the water permeability coefficient Pw is fixed to the value obtained with a membrane impermeable osmolyte, whereas KSV and PO (osmolyte permeability coefficient, cm/s) are obtained from fitting of the data. We repeat the fitting routine at least ten times, using each of the ten size distributions acquired by DLS.

Size distribution of vesicles

The size distribution of the vesicles was measured by DLS using the DynaPro NanoStar Detector (Wyatt Technology, Santa Barbara, CA). For the DLS measurements, the vesicles were diluted with 100 mM KPi, pH 7 to a concentration in the range of 2 μg/mL–2 mg/mL. Measurements were performed with a scattering angle of 90°. For each measurement, at least 10 acquisitions of 20 s each were performed at a temperature of 20 °C. For each acquisition, at least 1.5 million counts were recorded. The correlation curves and the intensity-weighted distributions were obtained with the built-in analysis software.

Molecular dynamics simulations

All molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS 2024.160 with the Martini 3 coarse-grained force field61, including the most recent models for lipids62 and cholesterol63. Spherical vesicles were generated using TS2CG64, whereas planar bilayers were generated using insane65. Simulated membrane compositions are summarized in Table S1. Vesicular systems were used to investigate membrane organization at the cell scale and included a native-like JCVI-syn3A membrane together with a highly cholesterol-enriched (80 mol%) variant. Planar bilayers were used for systematic characterization of cholesterol-dependent structural, dynamic, and mechanical properties and spanned cholesterol concentrations from 0 to 70 mol%. The vesicular systems were composed of approximately 410,000 lipids in total, whereas the planar systems contained approximately 673 lipids per leaflet. To study water permeation, symmetric double-bilayer systems were constructed by replicating the planar bilayers along the z-axis (Supplementary Fig. 16). All systems were solvated with Martini water and 0.15 M NaCl.

Simulations were carried out at both 293 K and 310 K, using the velocity-rescale thermostat66 with lipids and solvent coupled separately. The two temperatures were chosen to represent the experimental conditions and the physiological growth temperature of JCVI-syn3A, respectively. Pressure coupling was semi-isotropic for planar bilayers and isotropic for the vesicles. During equilibration, the Berendsen barostat was used67, whereas production simulations employed the c-rescale barostat for bilayers68 and the Parrinello–Rahman barostat for the vesicles69. Electrostatics were treated using the reaction-field scheme with a relative dielectric constant of 15, and van der Waals interactions used a 1.1 nm cutoff with potential-shift modifiers; the neighbor list cutoff was 1.35 nm70. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all directions. Planar bilayers were simulated for 5 μs, vesicles for 1 μs, and the double-bilayer systems for 2 μs, following energy minimization and short equilibration stages (Supplementary Table S11 and S12).

Structural properties were calculated for the planar systems, to quantify how cholesterol modulates membrane organization. Z-density profiles were computed using “gmx density” to resolve the spatial arrangement of lipid headgroups, tails, cholesterol–ROH groups and water across the membrane normal. Area per lipid was obtained from the instantaneous box area divided by the number of lipids per leaflet. Membrane thickness was measured as the average phosphate–phosphate interleaflet distance using MDAnalysis71,72and Lipyphilic73. Lipid tail order parameters were determined using an in-house script implementing the standard Martini tail-orientation analysis.

Dynamic properties were evaluated to characterize membrane mobility and transport. Lateral diffusion coefficients were extracted from the two-dimensional mean square displacement of lipid headgroups using ‘gmx msd’ and the Einstein relation. Water permeability was computed using the transition-based counting method74, in which permeation events correspond to full translocations of water molecules across a symmetric double-bilayer. Permeation events were identified using MDVoxelSegmentation, a voxel-based neighbor segmentation method enabling consistent spatial and temporal assignment of water molecules to compartments75. Crossing rates were calculated from the final 500 ns of each trajectory, block-averaged and normalized by the bilayer area and bulk water density to obtain permeability coefficients.

Mechanical properties were assessed to determine how cholesterol alters membrane rigidity and rupture behavior. Bending rigidity was calculated from Fourier-transformed height-fluctuation spectra using the Helfrich–Canham elastic formalism76,77, with low-q fits performed following Fowler et al.78. Rupture pressure was evaluated using planar bilayers containing a preformed pore (~1.5 nm radius). This approach bypasses the stochastic pore-nucleation step and enables direct comparison of the membrane’s ability to resist pore expansion. The critical rupture pressure was determined by applying negative lateral pressures in 2 bar increments and identifying the lowest pressure at which the pore expanded irreversibly. The area compressibility modulus was obtained from equilibrium fluctuations of the bilayer area following the method of Doktorova et al.33.

Additional analysis and visualization were performed using Python with Matplotlib79 for plotting, VMD80 for generating molecular renderings and simulation snapshots, and MartiniGlass81 for enhancing VMD visualization of coarse-grained Martini simulations through automated topology processing and continuous-chain rendering. Unless stated otherwise, reported uncertainties correspond to the standard error of the mean (SEM) obtained from block averaging. Rupture-pressure uncertainties were estimated from three replicates using a pressure resolution of 2 bar.

Data Availability Statement

All simulation input files, representative coordinate files, analysis scripts, and processed datasets supporting the findings of this study are publicly available at Zenodo [https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20613506]. The repository contains the molecular dynamics simulation systems, analysis workflows, and processed data used to generate the figures and tables presented in the manuscript and Supplementary Information. Source data underlying the figures and tables are provided within the Source Data File. Source data are provided with this paper.

Custom analysis scripts used in this study are available as part of the Zenodo repository [https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20613506].
