Convergence of Momentum-Based Optimization Algorithms with Time-Varying Parameters
Mathukumalli Vidyasagar

Abstract
In this paper, we present a unified algorithm for stochastic optimization that makes use of a “momentum” term; in other words, the stochastic gradient depends not only on the current true gradient of the objective function, but also on the true gradient at the previous iteration. Our formulation includes the Stochastic Heavy Ball (SHB) and the Stochastic Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (SNAG) algorithms as special cases. In addition, in our formulation, the momentum term is allowed to vary as a function of time (i.e., the iteration counter). The assumptions on the stochastic gradient are the most general in the literature, in that it can be biased, and have a conditional variance that grows in an unbounded fashion as a function of time. This last feature is crucial in order to make the theory applicable to “zero-order” methods, where the gradient is estimated using just two function evaluations. We present a set of sufficient conditions for the convergence of the unified algorithm. These conditions are natural generalizations of the familiar Robbins-Monro and Kiefer-Wolfowitz-Blum conditions for standard stochastic gradient descent. We also analyze another method from the literature for the SHB algorithm with a time-varying momentum parameter, and show that it is impracticable. This paper is dedicated to Alain Bensoussan on the occasion of his 85th birthday.

Introduction

1.1
Scope of the Paper

In this paper, we study “momentum-based” algorithms for optimization,
where the update of the current guess is based on not only the
current guess and a random search direction (stochastic gradient), but also
the previous guess.
Familiar examples of momentum-based algorithms are the Stochastic
Heavy Ball (SHB) and the Stochastic Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (SNAG)
algorithms.
Until now, by and large these algorithms have been treated separately
in the literature.
In this paper, we introduce a general model for optimization algorithms
that incorporate a momentum term,
which is versatile enough to include both the SHB and SNAG algorithms
as special cases.
Since Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) is a special case of SHB, our
analysis is applicable to SGD as well, though SGD is
not

momentum-based.
A proper literature review is given in Section
3
.
But for now we note that
we are aware of only
[
19
]
and its predecessors

[
31
,
35
]
that present a “Stochastic
Unified Momentum (SUM)” algorithm.
Our formulation is more general than the ones in these references,
in that the momentum
parameter is allowed to vary with time (iteration counter).
More important, we permit all the parameters in the algorithm,
including the step size and the momentum parameter, to be random.
This makes the present analysis applicable to “block” updating,
unlike previous work in

[
19
,
31
,
35
]
.

Our analysis is not restricted to convex objectve functions.
Assuming only that the gradient
\nabla J(\cdot)
of the objective
function
J:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is globally Lipschitz-continuous,
we show that

\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}=0,

(1)

where
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\}
are the iterations of the argument.
This is a stronger conclusion than

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\min_{0\leq\tau\leq t}\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\tau})\|_{2}^{2}=0.

(2)

To see this, suppose
\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T})={\bf 0}
for some
T
;
then (
2
) holds, but not necessarily (
1
).
If the convexity of
J(\cdot)
is replaced by
the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KL) property,
then we deduce the almost-sure convergence of the iterations to the
set of global minimizers.
If the objective function satisfies the stronger Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL)
property, then we not only deduce the almost-sure convergence of the algorithm,
but also find bounds on the
rate
of convergence.
When the stochastic gradient is unbiased and has bounded variance,
these bounds match the best-achievable upper bounds for arbitrary

convex
objective functions, as shown in
[
2
]
.
However, a novel feature of our analysis is that the assumptions on the
stochastic gradient are the most general (i.e., least restrictive) to date.

We use the same assumptions as in
[
14
,
15
]
,
reproduced here in Section
4.1
.
Specifically, it permitted for the stochastic gradient to be “biased”
in the sense that its conditional expectation need not equal the true gradient.
Further, the conditional variance of the stochastic gradient is permitted to
grow with time (i.e., the iteration counter), and also as function
of the distance to the set of optima, in an unbounded manner.
This generality allows us to analyze so-called zeroth-order methods,
which approximate the gradient using only function evaluations.
Existing papers cannot directly handle this approach.
We derive conditions under which the algorithm converges almost surely,
and not just in probability or in the mean.
Our conditions are natural generalizations of the well-known Robbins-Monro
(RM) or or Kiefer-Wolfowitz-Blum (KWB) conditions, and are thus less
restrictive than those in the literature.
All of these points are brought more clearly in Section
3
,
in which
we carry out a literature survey, and compare our results to previous results.

2
Formulation of the General Algorithm

In this section, we set up a general class of algorithms studied
in the paper, and then show that both the
Stochastic Heavy Ball (SHB) and Stochastic Nesterov Accelerated Gradient
(SNAG) algorithms become special cases of the general algorithm, with
suitable choices of the parameters.

The problem is to minimize a
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
objective function
J:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
.
The assumptions on
J(\cdot)
are stated in Section
4.1
; here
we state only the general form of the algorithm and its special cases.

Throughout the paper the symbol
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
denotes the
“argument” of the function
J(\cdot)
, which we hope will converge to
the set of minimizers of
J(\cdot)
.
However, the iterative algorithm is not based on updating
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}

directly.
Rather, it is defined in terms of two auxiliary vectors, denoted here
by
{\bf w}_{t}
and
{\bf v}_{t}
.
The relationship between
{\bf w}_{t}
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}
is given by

{\bf w}_{t}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\epsilon_{t}{\bf v}_{t}.

(3)

The general algorithm consists of updating formulas for
{\bf w}_{t}
and
{\bf v}_{t}
,
as follows:

{\bf w}_{t+1}={\bf w}_{t}+a_{t}{\bf v}_{t}-b_{t}\alpha_{t}{\bf h}_{t+1},

(4)

{\bf v}_{t+1}=\mu_{t}{\bf v}_{t}-\alpha_{t}{\bf h}_{t+1}.

(5)

In this formulation,
\alpha_{t}
is known as the
step size
,
also known as the “learning rate” in the machine learning literature,
while
\mu_{t}
is known as the
momentum parameter
.
In addition,
\{a_{t}\}
,
\{b_{t}\}
,
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
are sequences of real
constants that can be adjusted to make (
4
)–(
5
)
mimic various standard algorithms.
Usually they are viewed differently from the sequences
\{\mu_{t}\}

and
\{\alpha_{t}\}
.
More assumptions on these three constants are given in Section
4.1
.
Further,
{\bf h}_{t+1}
is a random vector that is an approximation to

\nabla J({\bf w}_{t})
(note, not
\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
), known as the
stochastic gradient
.
All of our analysis pertains to the behavior of
{\bf w}_{t}
and
{\bf v}_{t}
.
However, the conclusions can be translated back to the behavior of
the original argument variable
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}
, using (
3
).

Now it is shown that both SHB and SNAG are special cases of (
4
)
and (
5
) for suitable choices of the various constants.
Since Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) is a special case of SHB,
it too is a special case of the above algorithm.
However, SGD is not a momentum-based algorithm.

The Heavy Ball method was first introduced in
[
25
]
,
where both
\alpha_{t}
and
\mu_{t}
are fixed constants.
In the present context, the objective is to solve the equation

\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})={\bf 0}
using noisy measurements of the gradient.
In this more general setting, the formulation of SHB is

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\mu_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1})-\alpha_{t}{\bf h}_{t+1},

(6)

where
\alpha_{t}
is the step size,
\mu_{t}
is the momentum parameter,
and
{\bf h}_{t+1}
is a random approximation to
\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
.
To put (
6
) in the form (
4
)–(
5
), define

{\bf v}_{t}:={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1},{\bf w}_{t}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}.

(7)

With these definitions, it is easy to show that the update equations
for
{\bf w}_{t}
and
{\bf v}_{t}
are

{\bf w}_{t+1}={\bf w}_{t}+\mu_{t}{\bf v}_{t}-\alpha_{t}{\bf h}_{t+1},

(8)

{\bf v}_{t+1}=\mu_{t}{\bf v}_{t}-\alpha_{t}{\bf h}_{t+1}.

(9)

See for example
[
30
]
.
These equations are of the form (
4
)–(
5
) if we define

\epsilon_{t}=0,a_{t}=\mu_{t},b_{t}=1.

The Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NSG) algorithm was introduced in

[
21
]
.
In the current notation, with the possibility of the gradient being
stochastic, and the momentum coefficient being allowed to vary with
t
,
it can be stated as follows (following
[
33
, Eqs. (3)–(4)]
):

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\mu_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1})-\alpha_{t}{\bf h}_{t+1},

(10)

where
{\bf h}_{t+1}
is a random approximation of

\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\mu_{t}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1}))
, and not
\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
.
We analyze (
10
) using the reformulation in

[
5
, Eqs. (6)–(7)]
.
To accommodate the shift in the argument of
\nabla J(\cdot)
, we proceed
as follows:
Define

{\bf v}_{t}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1},{\bf w}_{t}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\mu_{t}{\bf v}_{t}.

(11)

Then the updating formulas are given by

[
5
, Eqs. (6)–(7)]
as

{\bf v}_{t+1}=\mu_{t}-\alpha_{t}{\bf h}_{t+1},

(12)

which is the same as (
9
), and

{\bf w}_{t+1}={\bf w}_{t}+\mu_{t+1}\mu_{t}{\bf v}_{t}-(1+\mu_{t+1})\alpha_{t}{\bf h}_{t+1}.

(13)

These equations are of the form (
4
) and (
5
) with

\epsilon_{t}=1\;\forall t,a_{t}=\mu_{t+1}\mu_{t},b_{t}=1+\mu_{t+1}.

(14)

Finally, since SGD is a special case of SHB with
\mu_{t}\equiv 0
for all
t
,
it too is a special case of the general algorithm
(
4
)–(
5
).

3
Literature Review

In this section, we present a very brief review of the relevant
results from the literature on SHB and SNAG, and compare the results
in this paper against those.
Because the area of optimization is vast, our comparison is restricted
only to those papers that study the convergence of the SHB or SNAG algorithms.
A more detailed review of momentum-based algorithms is given in

[
26
, Section 1.1]
.
We begin with some general remarks on various approaches, and then come to
the papers that are the most relevant to our paper.

3.1
General References

The Heavy Ball (HB) algorithm is introduced in
[
25
]

for the minimization of a strictly convex objective function
J(\cdot)

mapping
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
into
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Suppose the condition number of the Hessian
\nabla^{2}J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

(that is, the ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalues)
is bounded by
R
as a function of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
Moreover, let
J^{*}
denote the global minimum of
J(\cdot)
.
Then the HB method can be shown to converge at the rate of

\exp(-2\sqrt{(R-1)/(R+1)}t)
.
This is faster than the rate of
\exp(-2(R-1)/(R+1)t)
for
steepest descent (GD).
The Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NAG) method is introduced in

[
21
]
.
Subsequent research has shown that (i) for a convex function
J(\cdot)

with
\nabla J(\cdot)
being Lipschitz-continuous, the iterations converge
to the optimal value at a rate of
O(t^{-2})
, compared to
O(t^{-1})

for GD.
(ii) Moreover, no method can achieve a faster rate.

After the publication of these two seminal papers, a great deal
of analysis was carried out on these algorithms.
We mention only a small part of the literature that is directly relevant
to the present paper.
A fruitful approach to analyzing the behavior of momentum-based
algorithms such as HB and NAG is to study their
associated ODEs on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Whereas the ODE associated with GD is of first-order, the ODEs
associated with momentum-based methods are of second-order, due
to the presence of the “delay” terms.
The ODE associated with NAG is analyzed in
[
32
]
, when
the step size
\alpha
is held constant, while the momentum coefficient

\mu_{t}
varies with time.
It is shown that the “optimal” schedule for
\mu_{t}
is

\mu_{t}=(t+2)/(t+5)
.
In
[
3
]
, the rate of convergence of this ODE
is analyzed further by imposing additional structure on
J(\cdot)
,
such as the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz property
1
1

1

This property
and the stronger
Polyak-Łojasiewicz property are defined in Section
4.1
.

It is shown that, in certain situations, it is possible for classical
steepest descent method to outperform NAG.
The second-order ODE associated with HB
is analyzed in
[
1

4
]
,
when

satisfies the Polyak-Łojasiewicz property.
In all of the above formulations, it is assumed that the “stochastic
gradient”
{\bf h}_{t+1}

equals the true gradient
\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
; thus these models do not allow
for measurement errors.
Hence the analysis applies only to HB or NAG, not SHB or SNAG.

Now we come to more recent research on HB.
In much of the literature, attention is focused in the
convergence
in expectation
, or
convrgence in probability
of various algorithms.
In the review paper
[
9
]
,
the emphasis is almost exclusively on convergence in expectation.
SHB and SNAG are discussed in

Other research on the convergence of HB

In

The function

is assumed to be convex, and to have a globally

The authors

Rather, they show that

In
[

, the authors study the SHB for

The iterations are shown to converge to a minimum,

Now we give our interpretation of the results in
[
30
]
.
There are two restrictive features of these results.
First, the conditions (
19
) are more stringent than the standard
Robbins-Monro conditions, namely

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\eta_{t}^{2}<\infty,\quad\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\eta_{t}=\infty,

(20)

Compared to (
20
),
there are two extra assumptions in (

the synthetic step size
\eta_{t}
is decreasing, and
(ii) the summation of
\eta_{t}/\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\eta_{\tau}

is divergent.
Since
S_{t}
is an increasing sequence, the divergence of this summation
is a more restrictive assumption than the second Robbins-Monro condition
in (
20
)

The second challenge in this approach is that, given the
original

step size and momentum sequences, there is no easy way to verify whether
(
19
) is satisfied.
This is why, in
[
30

, the authors

begin with
the sequence
\{\eta_{t}\}
, which appears to us to
be somewhat unnatural.
If
\mu_{t}=\mu

,

\lambda_{t}=\lambda_{0}=\frac{\mu}{1-\mu},\;\forall t,\eta_{t}=\frac{1}{1-\mu}\alpha_{t},\;\forall t.

Since
\eta_{t}

\alpha_{t}

\{\alpha_{t}\}

\{\eta_{t}\}

However, if
\mu_{t}

Consequently, a Robbins-Monro like assumpion such as

In the other direction, if

This formulation allows us to handle the so-called zeroth-order methods,

4.1.1
Assumptions on the Objective Function

We begin with two “standing” assumptions on the objective function

Note that these assumptions are made in almost every paper in this area.

Lemma 1

Suppose (S1) and (S2) hold.

Then

Next we introduce three properties (or assumptions), known respectively as

As a prelude, we define the class of functions of Class

Again, all of this material can be found in

Definition 1

A function

(PL)

There exists a constant

(KL’)

There exists a function

\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}\geq\psi(J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})),\;\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(29)

(NSC)

This property consists of the following assumptions, taken together.

(a)

The function

has compact level sets.
For every constant

(b)

There exists a number

\rho({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\leq\eta(J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})),\;\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in L_{J}(r).

PL stands for the Polyak-Łojasiewicz condition.
In

]
, Polyak introduced (

and showed that it is sufficient to ensure that iterations converge at

is convex.
The property (KL’) is weaker than (PL).
A good review of these properties can be found in
[

]

(NSC) stands for “nearly strongly convex.”
Every strongly convex function satisfies this property, but so do others.
It is obvious that, if (NSC) is satisfied, then

4.1.2
Assumptions on the Stochastic Gradient

Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
denote the

For an

4

With these notational conventions in place we state the assumptions

{\bf h}_{t+1}

We begin by defining

If

Most papers in the literature assume that

The last equation in (

such that

E_{t}(\|{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2})\leq M_{t}^{2}[1+J({\bf w}_{t})],\;\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\;\forall t.

Equation (

As we will see in subsequent sections, while

has to be “asymptotically unbiased.”
In contrast, (

This feature is essential to permit the analysis of so-called zeroth-order

some but not necessarily all
components of
{\bf w}_{t}

and
{\bf v}_{t}
are updated.
Let
S_{t}\subseteq[d]
denote the components of
{\bf w}_{t}
and of
{\bf v}_{t}

that are updated at each
t
.
Then both the cardinality and the elements of
S_{t}

can be random, and can vary with
t
.

In
[
26
, Section 4]
, three different methods of block
updating are studied, which are now reprised for the convenience of the reader.
Note that Option 1 is the full-coordinate update.

Option 2: Single Coordinate Update:

At time
t
, choose an index
\kappa_{t}\in[d]
at random with a
uniform probability, and independently of previous choices.
Let
{\bf e}_{\kappa_{t}}
denote the elementary unit vector with a
1

as the
\kappa_{t}
-th component and zeros elsewhere.
Then define

{\bf h}^{(2)}_{t+1}=d{\bf e}_{\kappa_{t}}\circ{\bf h}_{t+1},

(49)

where
\circ
denotes the Hadamard, or component-wise, product of two
vectors of equal dimension.

Option 3: Multiple Coordinate Update:

This option is just coordinate update along multiple
coordinates chosen independently at random.
At time
t
, choose
N
different indices
\kappa_{t}^{n}
from
[d]

with replacement
, with each choice being independent of the rest,
and also of past choices.
Moreover, each
\kappa_{t}^{n}
is chosen from
[d]
with uniform probability.
Then define

{\bf h}^{(3)}_{t+1}:=\frac{d}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\bf e}_{\kappa_{t}^{n}}\circ{\bf h}_{t+1}.

(50)

Option 4: Bernoulli Update:

At time
t
, let
\{B_{t,i},i\in[d]\}
be independent Bernoulli
processes with success rate
\rho_{t}
.
Thus

\Pr\{B_{t,i}=1\}=\rho_{t},\;\forall i\in[d].

(51)

It is permissible for the success probability
\rho_{t}
to vary with time.
However, at any one time, all components must have the same success
probability.
Then define

{\bf v}_{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\bf e}_{i}I_{\{B_{t,i}=1\}}\in\{0,1\}^{d}.

(52)

Thus
{\bf v}_{t}
is a random vector, and

v_{t,i}
equals
1
if
B_{t,i}=1
, and equals
0
otherwise.
Now define

{\bf h}^{(4)}_{t+1}=\frac{1}{\rho_{t}}{\bf v}_{t}\circ{\bf h}_{t+1}.

(53)

It is necessary to clear up one important aspect of block updating.
It is
not the case
that the full stochastic gradient
{\bf h}_{t+1}

is computed first , and the set
S_{t}
of components to be updated is
determined afterwards.
In that approach, there would not be any savings in computational effort.
Rather, the set
S_{t}
is determined
first
, and afterwards
only the components
h_{i,t+1},i\in S_{t}
are computed.
If a method such as back-propagation is used, then computing

only some
components of a gradient is not noticeably cheaper
than computing the full gradient.
However, if the SPSA approach is used, then (
45
) can be applied,
by choosing the Rademacher variable
\Delta_{i,t}=0
for
i\not\in S_{t}
.
If
|S_{t}|\ll d
, this approach results in considerable savings in
computation.

With all these preliminaries, we now state the theorem on block updating.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose the stochastic gradient
{\bf h}_{t+1}
satisfies the bounds
(
34
) and (
35
).
Suppose that in (
5
)–(
3
), the quantity
{\bf h}_{t+1}
is replaced by

{\bf h}_{t+1}^{(k)}
for
k=2,3,4
.
Further, suppose that when Option
4
is used, then

\inf_{t}\rho_{t}=:\bar{\rho}>0.

Then the conclusions of Theorem
1
or Theorem
2

continue to hold under the same assumptions on
J(\cdot)
and the
same assumptions on
\{\alpha_{t}\}
.

Because the theorem is a straight-forward application of

[
26
, Theorem 8]
once Theorem
2
is proved,
the proof of this theorem is omitted.

5
Proofs of the Main Results

5.1
Transformation of Variables

The convergence analysis of (
4
)–(
5
) is based on
carrying out a linear transformation of the variables such that
the resulting equations are “nearly” decoupled, and are
exactly

decoupled if all terms
a_{t},b_{t},\epsilon_{t},\mu_{t}
are constant.
In contrast, in
[
30
]
, the authors propose a
linear transformation
that achieves exact decoupling even when
\mu_{t}
varies with
t
.
As shown in the Appendix, this approach is
untenable
when

\mu_{t}
is monotonic, either decreasing or increasing.
In contrast, our approach does not suffer from such limitations.
Moreover, as shown in the results stated in Section
4
,
our conditions
for the convergence of the algorithm in (
4
)–(
5
)
are natural generalizations of the familiar Robbins-Monro

[
28
]
or the Kiefer-Wolfowitz-Blum

[
18
,
8
]
conditions, unlike in

[
30
]
.

Let us rewrite (
4
)–(
5
) as

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf w}_{t+1}\\
{\bf v}_{t+1}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I&a_{t}I\\
0&\mu_{t}I\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf w}_{t}\\
{\bf v}_{t}\end{array}\right]-\left[\begin{array}[]{c}b_{t}I\\
I\end{array}\right]\alpha_{t}{\bf h}_{t+1},

(54)

where each
I
denotes
I_{d\times d}
.
Define

A_{t}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I&a_{t}I\\
0&\mu_{t}I\end{array}\right],\Lambda_{t}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I&0\\
0&\mu_{t}I\end{array}\right].

(55)

Then
A_{t}
is the coefficient matrix in (
54
) and
\Lambda_{t}

is the matrix of the eigenvalues of
A_{t}
.
In order to diagonalize
A_{t}
into
\Lambda_{t}
, we compute the matrix of
eigenvectors of
A_{t}
, as follows:

Z_{t}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I&-\frac{a_{t}}{1-\mu_{t}}I\\
0&\mu_{t}I\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I&-k_{t}I\\
0&\mu_{t}I\end{array}\right],Z_{t}^{-1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I&k_{t}I\\
0&\mu_{t}I\end{array}\right],

(56)

where as already defined in (
37
), we have that

k_{t}=\frac{a_{t}}{1-\mu_{t}}.

(57)

Then
Z_{t}^{-1}A_{t}Z_{t}=\Lambda_{t}
.
Next, define

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf u}_{t}\\
{\bf v}_{t}\end{array}\right]=Z_{t}^{-1}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf w}_{t}\\
{\bf v}_{t}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf w}_{t}+k_{t}{\bf v}_{t}\\
{\bf v}_{t}\end{array}\right].

(58)

Here we take advantage of the fact that the bottom block of
Z_{t}^{-1}
is

[0\;\;I]
.
Hence, in effect,
{\bf w}_{t}
is replaced by
{\bf u}_{t}
, but
{\bf v}_{t}

is left unaltered.
Hence the update equation for
{\bf v}_{t}
also remains as (
5
).

Next we compute the update equation for
{\bf u}_{t}
.

{\bf u}_{t+1}={\bf w}_{t+1}+k_{t+1}{\bf v}_{t+1}={\bf w}_{t+1}+k_{t}{\bf v}_{t+1}+\delta_{t}{\bf v}_{t+1},

(59)

where

\delta_{t}=k_{t+1}-k_{t}=\frac{a_{t+1}}{1-\mu_{t+1}}-\frac{a_{t}}{1-\mu_{t}}.

(60)

Now observe that

{\bf w}_{t+1}+k_{t}{\bf v}_{t+1}={\bf w}_{t}+a_{t}{\bf v}_{t}-b_{t}\alpha_{t}{\bf h}_{t+1}+k_{t}\mu_{t}{\bf v}_{t}-k_{t}\alpha_{t}{\bf h}_{t+1}.

However

k_{t}\mu_{t}+a_{t}=a_{t}\left(\frac{\mu_{t}}{1-\mu_{t}}+1\right)=\frac{a_{t}}{1-\mu_{t}}=k_{t}.

Hence we can write

{\bf w}_{t+1}+k_{t}{\bf v}_{t+1}={\bf w}_{t}+k_{t}{\bf v}_{t}-\alpha_{t}(b_{t}+k_{t}){\bf h}_{t+1}={\bf u}_{t}-\alpha_{t}(b_{t}+k_{t}){\bf h}_{t+1}.

(61)

The last term in (
59
) becomes

\delta_{t}{\bf v}_{t+1}=\delta_{t}\mu_{t}{\bf v}_{t}-\delta_{t}\alpha_{t}{\bf h}_{t+1}.

(62)

Substituting from (
61
) and (
62
) into (
59
)
gives the final form of the update equation for
{\bf u}_{t}
.

\begin{split}{\bf u}_{t+1}&={\bf u}_{t}+\delta_{t}\mu_{t}{\bf v}_{t}-(b_{t}+k_{t}+\delta_{t})\alpha_{t}{\bf h}_{t+1}\\
&={\bf u}_{t}+\delta_{t}\mu_{t}{\bf v}_{t}-(b_{t}+k_{t+1})\alpha_{t}{\bf h}_{t+1},\end{split}

(63)

while the updating equation for
{\bf v}_{t}
remains as before, namely

{\bf v}_{t+1}=\mu_{t}{\bf v}_{t}-\alpha_{t}{\bf h}_{t+1}.

(64)

These are the two equations whose behavior is analyzed in the remainder
of the paper.
Based on the analysis, we make inferences about the behavior
{\bf w}_{t}
,
and eventually,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}
.
Note that these two equations are
not
decoupled in general, due to the
presence of the term
\delta_{t}\mu_{t}{\bf v}_{t}
in (
63
).
However, in the special case where both
a_{t}
and
\mu_{t}
are constant,
then
\delta_{t}=0
for all
t
, and the equations are indeed decoupled.
This is the approach used in
[
26
]
to study the SHB
algorithm when
\mu_{t}
is constant.
More generally, if both
a_{t}
and
\mu_{t}
converge to some some constants
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, then
\delta_{t}\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, and the equations become
“asymptotically decoupled.”
We can draw some useful conclusions when
\delta_{t}\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

5.2
The Robbins-Siegmund Theorem and Some Extensions

The proof of Theorem
1
makes use of
a fundamental result in the convergence of stochastic processes,
known as the “almost supermartingale” theorem due to
Robbins and Siegmund
[
27
, Theorem 1]
.
It is also found in
[
6
]
and in
[
11
]
.
The theorem states the following:

Lemma 3
.

Suppose
\{V_{t}\},\{f_{t}\},\{g_{t}\},\{h_{t}\}
are
stochastic processes taking values in
[0,\infty)
, adapted to some
filtration
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}
, satisfying

E_{t}(V_{t+1})\leq(1+f_{t})V_{t}+g_{t}-h_{t}\mbox{ a.s.},\;\forall t,

(65)

where, as before,
E_{t}(V_{t+1})
is a shorthand for
E(V_{t+1}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t})
.
Then, on the set

\Omega_{0}:=\{\omega\in\Omega:\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}f_{t}(\omega)<\infty\}\cap\{\omega:\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}g_{t}(\omega)<\infty\},

we have that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}V_{t}
exists, and in addition,

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}h_{t}(\omega)<\infty
.
In particular, if
P(\Omega_{0})=1
, then
\{V_{t}\}
is bounded
almost surely, and
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}h_{t}(\omega)<\infty
almost surely.

The following theorem is a straight-forward, but useful extension
of Lemma
3
.
It is Theorem 5.1 of
[
14
,
15
]
.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
\{V_{t}\},\{f_{t}\},\{g_{t}\},\{h_{t}\},\{\alpha_{t}\}
are

[0,\infty)
-valued stochastic processes
defined on some probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,P)
, and
adapted to some filtration
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}
.
Suppose further that

E_{t}(V_{t+1})\leq(1+f_{t})V_{t}+g_{t}-\alpha_{t}h_{t}\mbox{ a.s.},\;\forall t.

(66)

Define

\Omega_{0}:=\{\omega\in\Omega:\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}f_{t}(\omega)<\infty\mbox{ and }\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}g_{t}(\omega)<\infty\},

(67)

\Omega_{1}:=\{\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}(\omega)=\infty\}.

(68)

Then

1.

Suppose that
P(\Omega_{0})=1
.
Then the sequence
\{V_{t}\}
is bounded almost surely, and
there exists a random variable
W
defined on
(\Omega,\Sigma,P)
such that

V_{t}(\omega)\rightarrow W(\omega)
almost surely.

2.

Suppose that, in addition to
P(\Omega_{0})=1
, it is also true that

P(\Omega_{1})=1
.
Then

\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}h_{t}(\omega)=0\;\forall\omega\in\Omega_{0}\cap\Omega_{1}.

(69)

3.

Further, suppose there exists a function
\eta(\cdot)
of Class
{\mathcal{B}}

such that
h_{t}(\omega)\geq\eta(V_{t}(\omega))
for all
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
.
Then
V_{t}(\omega)\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
for all
\omega\in\Omega_{0}\cap\Omega_{1}
.

Theorem
1
allows us to infer convergence, but does not
provide any information about the
rate
of convergence.
Now we define the concept of a rate of convergence of stochastic
processes, following a similar definition in
[
20
]
.

Definition 2
.

Suppose
\{Y_{t}\}
is a stochastic process, and
\{f_{t}\}

is a sequence of positive numbers.
We say that

1.

Y_{t}=O(f_{t})
if
\{Y_{t}/f_{t}\}
is bounded almost surely.

2.

Y_{t}=\Omega(f_{t})
if
Y_{t}
is positive almost surely, and

\{f_{t}/Y_{t}\}
is bounded almost surely.

3.

Y_{t}=\Theta(f_{t})
if
Y_{t}
is both
O(f_{t})
and
\Omega(f_{t})
.

4.

Y_{t}=o(f_{t})
if
Y_{t}/f_{t}\rightarrow 0
almost surely as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

With this definition, the following theorem holds; it is Theorem 5.2 of

[
14
,
15
]
.
Similar results can be found in
[
20
]
.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose
\{V_{t}\},\{f_{t}\},\{g_{t}\},\{\alpha_{t}\}
are
stochastic processes defined on some probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,P)
,
taking values in
[0,\infty)
, adapted to some
filtration
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}
.
Suppose further that

E_{t}(V_{t+1})\leq(1+f_{t})V_{t}+g_{t}-\alpha_{t}z_{t}\;\forall t,

(70)

and in addition, almost surely

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}f_{t}(\omega)<\infty,\quad\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}g_{t}(\omega)<\infty,\quad\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}(\omega)=\infty.

Then
V_{t}=o(t^{-\lambda})
for every
\lambda\in(0,1]
such that
there exists a finite
T>0
such that

\alpha_{t}(\omega)-\lambda t^{-1}\geq 0\;\forall t\geq T,

(71)

and in addition

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(t+1)^{\lambda}g_{t}(\omega)<\infty,\quad\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}[\alpha_{t}(\omega)-\lambda t^{-1}]=\infty.

(72)

5.3
Proof of Theorem
1

The proof of Theorem
1
is based on applying the Robbins-Siegmund
theorem stated here as Lemma
3
to the “Lyapunov function”

V_{t}:=J({\bf u}_{t})+\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}.

(73)

We will find an upper bound for
E_{t}(V_{t})
in the form

E_{t}(V_{t})\leq V_{t}+f_{t}V_{t}+g_{t}-\frac{1-\bar{\mu}^{2}}{2}-\frac{\alpha_{t}\bar{b}}{2}\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-F_{t},

(74)

where the sequences
\{f_{t}\},\{g_{t}\}
are nonnegative and summable,
and
F_{t}
is a quadratic form in
\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})
and
\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}

which is positive definite
for sufficiently large

t
,
say for all
t\geq T
.
(In case these entities are random, these conditions hold almost surely),
Since we can always start our analysis at time
T
, we can neglect the term

-F_{t}
for all
t\geq T
, and apply Lemma
3
.

Going forward, we will avoid a lot of cumbersome notation if we agree to
refer to a nonnegative sequence
\{z_{t}\}
as a Well-Behaved Function (WBF)
if there exist
nonnegative summable
sequences
\{f_{t}\},\{g_{t}\}

such that

z_{t}\leq g_{t}+f_{t}V_{t},\;\forall t\geq 0.

(75)

In case the various entities are random, the assumptions (inequality and
summability) hold almost surely.
Clearly the sum of two WBF is again a WBF, and a WBF multiplied by
a bounded sequence is again a WBF.
Therefore any WBF can be absorbed into the terms
g_{t}+f_{t}V_{t}
,
and it is not necessary to keep careful track of them.

Bounding
E_{t}(V_{t+1})
involves several intricate computations.
For this purpose, it is now shown that the first two conditions in
(
40
) imply that

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}^{2}B_{t}<\infty,\quad\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}^{2}B_{t}^{2}<\infty.

(76)

The proof of this claim is as follows:
The first bound in (
40
) implies in particular that
\alpha_{t}\rightarrow 0

as
t\rightarrow\infty
, and hence
\alpha_{t}
is bounded, say by
\bar{\alpha}
.
Therefore

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}^{2}B_{t}\leq\bar{\alpha}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}B_{t}<\infty.

This is the first bound in (
76
).
As for the second bound, recall that
every (absolutely) summable sequence is also square summable.
Therefore we can append the two bounds in (
76
)
to the three bounds in (
40
).

The first step in proceeding further is to reformulate the bounds
(
34
) and (
35
), which are stated in terms of

\nabla J({\bf w}_{t})
, in terms of
\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})
.
Accordingly, we modify (
32
) by defining

\bar{{\bf x}}_{t}={\bf z}_{t}-\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})=E_{t}({\bf h}_{t+1})-\nabla J({\bf u}_{t}).

(77)

The objectives are to find bounds for
\|\bar{{\bf x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
and

E_{t}(\|{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2})
in terms of
V_{t}
.
Throughout we use the bound (
26
), namely

\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2LJ({\bf u}_{t})
.
We also make repeated use of the obvious inequalities

x\leq(1+x^{2})/2,xy\leq(x^{2}+y^{2})/2,\;\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(78)

We begin with a bound for
\|\bar{{\bf x}}_{t}\|_{2}
.
Observe that

\bar{{\bf x}}_{t}={\bf z}_{t}-\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})={\bf x}_{t}+\nabla J({\bf w}_{t})-\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})

Hence it follows from (
58
) and (
34
) that

\begin{split}\|\bar{{\bf x}}_{t}\|_{2}&\leq\|{\bf x}_{t}\|_{2}+L\|{\bf w}_{t}-{\bf u}_{t}\|_{2}\\
&\leq B_{t}(1+\|\nabla J({\bf w}_{t})\|_{2})+Lk_{t}\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}\\
&\leq B_{t}(1+\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}+Lk_{t}\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2})+Lk_{t}\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}\\
&\leq B_{t}(1+\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}+L\bar{k}\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2})+L\bar{k}\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}.\end{split}

(79)

Next, we can find a bound for
\|\bar{{\bf x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
starting
from (
79
), and arrive at

\|\bar{{\bf x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\leq B_{t}^{2}(1+\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}+L\bar{k}\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2})^{2}+2B_{t}L\bar{k}(1+\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}+L\bar{k}\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2})\cdot\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}\\
+(L\bar{k})^{2}\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}.

(80)

Note that terms of the form
\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}
,
\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}
,
\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}
,

\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}\cdot\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}
and
\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

can be bounded by terms of the form
C_{1}+C_{2}V_{t}
for suitable constants

C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.
Clearly
\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\leq V_{t}
.
The rest can be bounded repeatedly using (
78
).
Specifically

\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}(1+\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}^{2})\leq\frac{1}{2}+LJ({\bf u}_{t})\leq\frac{1}{2}+LV_{t},

\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}(1+\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}^{2})\leq\frac{1}{2}(1+V_{t}),

\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2LJ({\bf u}_{t})\leq 2LV_{t},

\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}\cdot\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}(\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}^{2})\leq LJ({\bf u}_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}^{2})\leq\max\{L,1/2\}V_{t}.

Applying all these bounds to (
79
) shows that

\|\bar{{\bf x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\leq B_{t}^{2}(D_{11}+D_{12}V_{t})+B_{t}(D_{21}+D_{22}V_{t})+(L\bar{k})^{2}\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}^{2},

(81)

for suitable constants
D_{11}
through
D_{22}
.

For future use, we also bound
\|{\bf z}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
.
Since
{\bf z}_{t}=\bar{{\bf x}}_{t}+\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})
, we can write

\begin{split}\|{\bf z}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}&\leq\|\bar{{\bf x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+2\|\bar{{\bf x}}_{t}\|_{2}\cdot\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}+\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\\
&\leq\|\bar{{\bf x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+[\|\bar{{\bf x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]+\|\nabla J({\bf u}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\\
&=2\|\bar{{\bf x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+4LJ({\bf u}_{t}).\end{split}

(82)

Since
J({\bf u}_{t})\leq V_{t}
, we can substitute from (
81
)
into (
82
) to obtain the bound

\begin{split}\|{\bf z}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}&\leq B_{t}^{2}(D_{11}+D_{12}V_{t})+B_{t}(D_{21}+D_{22}V_{t})+4LJ({\bf u}_{t})+(L\bar{k})^{2}\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\\
&\leq B_{t}^{2}(D_{11}+D_{12}V_{t})+B_{t}(D_{21}+D_{22}V_{t})+\max\{4L,(L\bar{k})^{2}\}V_{t}.\end{split}

(83)

With these bounds in place, we now proceed to prove (
73
).
Clearly

E_{t}(V_{t+1})=E_{t}(J({\bf u}_{t+1}))+E_{t}(\|{\bf v}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}).

(84)

So we bound each of these two terms individually.
First, it follows from (
64
) that

\|{\bf v}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mu_{t}{\bf v}_{t}-\alpha_{t}{\bf h}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}\\
=\mu_{t}^{2}\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}-2\alpha_{t}\mu_{t}\langle{\bf v}_{t},{\bf h}_{t+1}\rangle+\alpha_{t}^{2}\|{\bf h}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}.

Therefore, from (
33
), we get

E_{t}(\|{\bf v}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2})=\mu_{t}^{2}\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}-2\alpha_{t}\mu_{t}\langle{\bf v}_{t},{\bf z}_{t}\rangle+\alpha_{t}^{2}[\|{\bf z}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+E_{t}(\|{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2})].

(85)

We can estimate the last two terms separately.

First,

-2\alpha_{t}\mu_{t}\langle{\bf v}_{t},{\bf z}_{t}\rangle\leq 2\alpha_{t}\mu_{t}\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}\cdot\|{\bf z}_{t}\|_{2}\leq 2\alpha_{t}\bar{\mu}\|{\bf v}_{t}\|_{2}\cdot\|{\bf z}_{t}\|_{2}.

6
Conclusions and Future Research

In this paper, we have presented a “unified” momentum-based algorithm
for convex and nonconvex optimization, which includes both the Stochastic
Heavy Ball (SHB) and Stochastic Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (SNAG)
algorithms as special cases.
One novel feature of our algorithm is that the momentum parameter
is permitted to vary with time (iteration counter).
We build on the “transformation of variables” approach in

[
26
]
to convert the updating equations into two

asymptotically decoupled
equations; note that when the momentum
parameter is constant, the transformed equations would be
exactly

decoupled, as in
[
26
]
.
However, the scope of the present paper is broader, because only the SHB
algorithm is studied in
[
26
]
.

We have also analyzed the behavior of the decoupling approach proposed in

[
30
]
for the SHB algorithm, and showed that it is
not feasible when the momentum parameter varies with time.
The details can be found in the Appendix.

There are several interesting problems that are worthy of further
investigation, of which we mention only one.
In this paper, even though the momentum parameter is allowed to vary with time,
it is assumed that it is bounded away from one.
However, in the standard version of SNAG, the momentum parameter approaches
one; hence the theory in this paper does not apply.
It is quite possible that the method of proof employed here, based on the
Robbins-Siegmund theorem on the convergence of “almost super-martingales,”
cannot be made to work for the standard version of SNAG.
If so, it would be worthwhile to identify alternate approaches.

Appendix

In this appendix, we analyze the behavior of the solutions of
(
16
) introduced in
[
30
]
,
reproduced here for the convenience of the reader.

\lambda_{t+1}=\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\mu_{t}}-1,\eta_{t}=(1+\lambda_{t+1})\alpha_{t}

Recall from (
19
) that the convergence conditions in

[
30
]
involve the “synthetic” step size sequence

\{\eta_{t}\}
.
The objective of the Appendix is to show that this approach is not feasible.
Specifically, if
\{\mu_{t}\}
is a decreasing sequence, then
\lambda_{t}\rightarrow\infty
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.
Thus, even if the original step size sequence
\{\alpha_{t}\}
is
square-summable, the synthetic sequence of step sizes
\{\eta_{t}\}

need not be.
Thus the assumptions in (
19
) are strictly stronger than
the standard Robbins-Monro conditions, which is what is assumed here.
In the other direction, if
\{\mu_{t}\}
is an increasing sequence
bounded away from
1
, eventually
1+\lambda_{t+1}<0
, thus leading to
a negative step size
\eta_{t}
, which is absurd.
Thus the point is that, while the approach in

[
30
]
is quite elegant, it is not practical.

We begin by presenting a “closed-form” formula for
\lambda_{t+1}
as a function
of the
\mu_{t}
sequence.
Write the first equation in (
16
) as

\begin{split}\lambda_{t+1}&=\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\mu_{t}}-1=\frac{\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{t-1}}{\mu_{t}}+\frac{\lambda_{t-1}}{\mu_{t}}-1\\
&=\frac{1}{\mu_{t}}(\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{t-1})+\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{t}}-\frac{1}{\mu_{t-1}}\right)\lambda_{t-1}+\frac{\lambda_{t-1}}{\mu_{t-1}}-1\\
&=\lambda_{t}+\frac{1}{\mu_{t}}(\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{t-1})+\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{t}}-\frac{1}{\mu_{t-1}}\right)\lambda_{t-1}.\end{split}

(112)

Therefore

\lambda_{t+1}-\lambda_{t}=\frac{1}{\mu_{t}}(\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{t-1})+\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{t}}-\frac{1}{\mu_{t-1}}\right)\lambda_{t-1}.

(113)

It is easy to show by induction
that a “closed-form” solution to (
113
) is

\lambda_{t+1}=\lambda_{t}+\left[\prod_{\tau=1}^{t}\frac{1}{\mu_{\tau}}\right](\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0})+\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\left[\prod_{s=\tau}^{t-1}\frac{1}{\mu_{s}}\right]\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{\tau}}-\frac{1}{\mu_{\tau-1}}\right)\lambda_{\tau-1},

(114)

where empty products are taken as
1
and empty sums are taken as
0
.
Note that
\lambda_{0}
is unspecified.
So if we take
\lambda_{0}=\mu_{0}/(1-\mu_{0})
, then

\lambda_{1}=\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\mu_{0}}-1=\frac{1}{1-\mu_{0}}-1=\frac{\mu_{0}}{1-\mu_{0}}=\lambda_{0}.

With this choice, the first term in (
114
) drops out;
but this is not much of a simplification.
Note also that if
\mu_{t}=\mu
for all
t
, then
\lambda_{t}=\lambda_{0}
for all
t
.

Now let us analyze the behavior of
\lambda_{t}
in two specific situations:
(i)
\{\mu_{t}\}
is a strictly decreasing, i.e.,
\mu_{t}<\mu_{t-1}

for all
t
, and (ii)
\{\mu_{t}\}
is strictly increasing, but
bounded above by some
\bar{\mu}<1
.
In principle, the closed form solution (
114
) can be used to
analyze arbitrary sequences
\{\mu_{t}\}
.
However, the two situations studied here are perhaps the most natural.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose
\lambda_{0}
is chosen as
\mu_{0}/(1-\mu_{0})
, so that
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{0}
.
Suppose further that
\mu_{t}<\mu_{t-1}
for all
t\geq 1
.
Then
\lambda_{t}\rightarrow\infty
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

The first step is to show that
\lambda_{t+1}>\lambda_{t}
for all
t\geq 1
.
The proof is by induction.
First, for
t=1
, we have that

\lambda_{2}=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\mu_{1}}-1>\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\mu_{0}}-1=\frac{1}{1-\mu_{0}}-1=\lambda_{1}.

Next suppose
\lambda_{t}>\lambda_{t-1}
.
Then

\lambda_{t+1}=\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\mu_{t}}-1>\frac{\lambda_{t-1}}{\mu_{t-1}}-1=\lambda_{t}.

This completes the proof by induction.

Next, we invoke the recursion (
113
).

\lambda_{t+1}-\lambda_{t}=\frac{1}{\mu_{t}}(\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{t-1})+\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{t}}-\frac{1}{\mu_{t-1}}\right)\lambda_{t-1}.

The fact that
\mu_{t}<\mu_{t-1}
implies that

\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{t}}-\frac{1}{\mu_{t-1}}\right)\lambda_{t-1}>0,\;\forall t\geq 2.

Hence

\lambda_{t+1}-\lambda_{t}>\frac{1}{\mu_{t}}(\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{t-1})>\frac{1}{\mu_{2}}(\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{t-1}),\;\forall t\geq 2.

As a consequence
we get

\lambda_{t+1}-\lambda_{t}>\left[\prod_{s=2}^{t}\frac{1}{\mu_{s}}\right](\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})>\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{2}}\right)^{t-1}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}),\;\forall t\geq 2.

We can add the above bound for all
t
.
Because it is a telescoping sum, we get

\lambda_{t+1}=\lambda_{2}+\sum_{k=2}^{t}(\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda_{k})\geq(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})\sum_{k=2}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{2}}\right)^{k-1}\rightarrow\infty\mbox{ as }t\rightarrow\infty.

∎

Lemma 5
.

Suppose
\mu_{t-1}<\mu_{t}<1
for all
t
, and that

\prod_{\tau=2}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{\tau}}\right)\rightarrow\infty\mbox{ as }t\rightarrow\infty.

(115)

Then there exists a finite
t_{0}
such that

1+\lambda_{t}<0,\;\forall t\geq t_{0}.

(116)

In particular, if
\mu_{t}\leq\bar{\mu}<1
for all
t
, then we can take

t_{0}=3+\log_{(1/\bar{\mu})}\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}.

(117)

Proof.

Observe that

\lambda_{2}=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\mu_{1}}-1<\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\mu_{0}}-1=\lambda_{1}.

Now suppose that
\lambda_{t}<\lambda_{t-1}
.
Then

\lambda_{t+1}=\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\mu_{t}}-1<\frac{\lambda_{t-1}}{\mu_{t-1}}-1=\lambda_{t}.

After observing that

\frac{1}{\mu_{t}}-\frac{1}{\mu_{t-1}}<0,

we can rewrite(
113
) as

\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{t+1}=\frac{1}{\mu_{t}}(\lambda_{t-1}-\lambda_{t})+\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{t-1}}-\frac{1}{\mu_{t}}\right)\lambda_{t-1}.

(118)

Now suppose
\lambda_{\tau}>0
for
\tau=1,\cdots,t
.
Then (
118
) implies that

\lambda_{\tau}-\lambda_{\tau+1}>\frac{1}{\mu_{\tau}}(\lambda_{\tau-1}-\lambda_{\tau})>\left(\prod_{k=2}^{\tau}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}\right)(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}).

(119)

\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{t+1}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}(\lambda_{\tau}-\lambda_{\tau-1})>\left[\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\left(\prod_{k=2}^{t}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}\right)\right](\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}).

(120)

Consequently

\begin{split}\lambda_{t+1}&<\lambda_{1}-\left[\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\left(\prod_{k=2}^{\tau}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}\right)\right](\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\\
&<\lambda_{1}-\left(\prod_{k=2}^{t}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}\right)(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}).\end{split}

(121)

Now choose
T
such that

\left(\prod_{k=2}^{T}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}\right)>\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}.

(122)

This is possible in view of (
115
).
Then there are two possibilities:
(i)
\lambda_{\tau}>0
for
\tau=2,\cdots,T
.
Then
\lambda_{T+1}<0
by virtue of (
122
).
In this case we have that

\lambda_{T+2}=\frac{\lambda_{T+1}}{\mu_{T+1}}-1<-1.

Therefore
\lambda_{T+2}+1<0
.
The argument can be repeated, to show that
\lambda_{t}+1<0
for
all
t\geq T+2
.
Hence we can take
t_{0}=T+2
in (
116
).
(ii) There exists a
\tau
between
2
and
T
such that
\lambda_{\tau}\leq 0
.
By the above reasoning, it follows that

\lambda_{\tau+1}=\frac{\lambda_{\tau}}{\mu_{\tau}}-1\leq-1<0.

Therefore

\lambda_{\tau+2}=\frac{\lambda_{\tau+1}}{\mu_{\tau+1}}-1<-1,\mbox{ or }\lambda_{\tau+2}+1<0.

As above, this leads to the conclusion that

\lambda_{t}+1<0
for all
t\geq\tau+2
.
Since
\tau\leq T
, we can conclude as before that
\lambda_{t}+1<0
for
all
t\geq T+2
.
Hence we can again take
t_{0}=T+2
in (
116
).

To prove the last claim, suppose that
\mu_{t}\leq\bar{\mu}<1
for all
t
.
Then we can replace (
122
) by

\left(\frac{1}{\bar{\mu}}\right)^{T-1}\geq\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}.

Solving for
T
and choosing
t_{0}=T+2
gives (
117
).
∎
