Adaptive estimation for nonparametric circular regression with errors in variables
Tien Dat Nguyen , Thanh Mai Pham Ngoc

Abstract
This paper investigates the nonparametric estimation of a circular regression function in an errors-in-variables framework. Two settings are studied, depending on whether the covariates are circular or linear. Adaptive estimators are constructed and their theoretical performance is assessed through convergence rates over Sobolev and Hölder smoothness classes. Numerical experiments on simulated and real datasets illustrate the practical relevance of the methodology.



Keywords
: Circular data, Errors-in-variables model, Adaptive estimation, Deconvolution.

. Primary 62G08, secondary 62H11.

1
Introduction

We consider the problem of circular regression with errors in variables. We observe the i.i.d. dataset
(Z_{1},\Theta_{1}),\dots,(Z_{n},\Theta_{n})
where

\Theta_{i}=m(X_{i})+\zeta_{i}\quad\quad\textrm{(mod $2\pi$)}.

and

Z_{i}=X_{i}+\varepsilon_{i},

with the responses
\Theta_{i}
and the regression errors
\zeta_{i}
lying in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the unit circle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

We consider two scenarios: the first where the covariates
X_{i}
belong to
\mathbb{S}^{1}
(circular predictor case), and the second where the
X_{i}
lie in
[0,1]
(linear predictor case). The predictors
X_{i}
are latent, and the covariate errors
\varepsilon_{i}
are i.i.d. unobservable random variables with density
f_{\varepsilon}
, whose characteristic function is assumed to be known. The
\varepsilon_{i}
are independent of
X_{i}
. Our objective is to estimate the circular regression function
m
nonparametrically from the i.i.d. dataset
(Z_{1},\Theta_{1}),\dots,(Z_{n},\Theta_{n})
. The term circular data is used to distinguish such data from those supported on the real line (or its subsets), henceforth referred to as linear data.

Circular or angular data are encountered in various scientific fields, such as biology (e.g., directions of animal migration), bioinformatics (e.g., protein conformational angles), geology (e.g., rock fracture orientations), medicine (e.g., circadian rhythms), forensics (e.g., crime timing), and the social sciences (e.g., time-of-day or calendar effects). Comprehensive surveys of statistical methods for circular data can be found in Mardia and Jupp
[
31
]
, Jammalamadaka and SenGupta
[
21
]
, Ley and Verdebout
[
29
]
, and recent advances are compiled in Pewsey and García-Portugués
[
35
]
.

The circular response in our model introduces substantial statistical challenges compared to classical regression with linear responses. Circular regression necessitates methodologies that respect the geometry of the circle. In the parametric setting, various ad hoc approaches have been proposed (see
[
20
]
,
[
25
]
,
[
26
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
27
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
16
]
). To the best of our knowledge, even without measurement errors, nonparametric circular regression has been explored in only a few works, such as
[
11
]
,
[
34
]
and
[
22
]
. Only one recent study
[
9
]
addresses the case involving errors in variables. Errors-in-variables models arise in many experimental contexts where data are corrupted by measurement errors. These models have been extensively studied on the real line (see
[
33
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
1
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
23
]
,
[
24
]
), but much less so in the circular response setting. Such models are particularly demanding as they involve a deconvolution step in the estimation process.

As previously mentioned, the only work addressing nonparametric circular regression with measurement errors is
[
9
]
, hence this problem is largely unexplored in statistic. In that study, the authors propose a deconvolution kernel estimator for both the circular and linear covariate cases. Their approach depends critically on the choice of key parameters: a kernel bandwidth (related to the regression estimation) and a spectral cutoff (related to deconvolution). However, the selection of these parameters is not theoretically addressed, and no convergence rates or adaptive procedures are provided. By adaptation, we mean that the estimator does not require prior knowledge of the regularity of the regression function and can automatically adjust to it–a feature essential in practice. In their numerical implementation, the authors of
[
9
]
rely on cross-validation to select the kernel bandwidth and choose arbitrary spectral cutoff values. In contrast, we propose a novel data-driven estimation procedure for the circular regression function in both settings: circular and linear predictors. We introduce adaptive rules for bandwidth selection inspired from the Goldenshluger and Lepski method (see
[
19
]
) and deconvolution density problems (see
[
5
]
) and establish convergence rates for the pointwise risk over Sobolev and Hölder classes. Our methodology is based on concentration inequalities. Finally, we assess the performance of our estimators through both simulation studies and real data applications. We show that our statistical procedure achieves satisfactory performance.

.

. Observe that
\mbox{atan2}(0,0)
is not defined.

2
Regression with a circular response

Circular data are fundamentally different from linear data due to their periodicity, and thus require specific techniques. To measure the closeness between two angles
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
, we do not consider the natural distance

d(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}):=\min\left\{\big|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}+2k\pi\big|:k\in\mathbb{Z}\right\},\quad\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in[-\pi,\pi),

but focus on
d_{c}
with

d_{c}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}):=1-\cos(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}),\quad\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in[-\pi,\pi),

which is extensively used in the literature of directional statistics (see for instance Section 2 in the seminal monograph by Mardia and Jupp
[
31
]
, Section 3.2.1 of
[
29
]
,
[
11
]
or
[
32
]
). Note that the divergence
d_{c}
corresponds to the usual squared Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Indeed, the angles
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
determine the corresponding points
(\cos\theta_{1},\sin\theta_{1})
and
(\cos\theta_{2},\sin\theta_{2})
respectively on the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Then, the usual squared Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
reads

\displaystyle\big(\cos\theta_{1}-\cos\theta_{2}\big)^{2}+\big(\sin\theta_{1}-\sin\theta_{2}\big)^{2}=2.\big[1-\cos(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})\big]=2.d_{c}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}).

Hence,
\sqrt{d_{c}}
is a distance on
[-\pi,\pi)
and we naturally look for a measurable function
m
such that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[d_{c}(\Theta,m(X))\big]=\underset{f:\;\Omega\rightarrow[-\pi,\pi)}{\textrm{min}}\;\mathbb{E}\big[d_{c}(\Theta,f(X))\big],

(1)

where
\Omega
denotes either
[0,1]
or
\mathbb{S}^{1}
depending on the nature of the predictors
X_{i}
and the minimum is taken over
[-\pi,\pi)
-valued functions
f
that are measurable with respect to the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
X
. It is interesting to notice that the minimization problem (
1
) is directly linked to the definition of the Frechet mean on the circle (see
[
2
]
).
Furthermore, in directional statistics, the problem of finding such a regression function
m(X)
as defined in (
1
) has been already considered (see
[
34
]
and
[
32
]
).

Now let us work conditionally to
X
. For
x\in\Omega
, let

m_{1}(x):=\mathbb{E}\big(\sin(\Theta)|X=x\big)\quad and\quad m_{2}(x):=\mathbb{E}\big(\cos(\Theta)|X=x\big).

(2)

Moreover, write for an arbitrary function
f:\Omega\rightarrow[-\pi,\pi)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\cos(\Theta-f(X))|X\big]

\displaystyle=\cos(f(X))m_{2}(X)+\sin(f(X))m_{1}(X)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(m_{2}(X))^{2}+(m_{1}(X))^{2}}\cos(f(X)-\gamma(X)),

where
\gamma:\;\Omega\rightarrow[-\pi,\pi)
is defined for
x\in\Omega

\cos(\gamma(x)):=\dfrac{m_{2}(x)}{\sqrt{(m_{2}(x))^{2}+(m_{1}(x))^{2}}}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\sin(\gamma(x)):=\dfrac{m_{1}(x)}{\sqrt{(m_{2}(x))^{2}+(m_{1}(x))^{2}}}.

Observe that

\gamma(x)=\mbox{atan2}(m_{1}(x),m_{2}(x)).

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\underset{f:\;\Omega\rightarrow[-\pi,\pi)}{\textrm{min}}\;\mathbb{E}\big[d_{c}(\Theta,f(X))\big]

\displaystyle=1-\underset{f:\;\Omega\rightarrow[-\pi,\pi)}{\textrm{max}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\big[\cos(\Theta-f(X))|X\big]\Big]

\displaystyle=1-\underset{f:\;\Omega\rightarrow[-\pi,\pi)}{\textrm{max}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sqrt{m_{1}^{2}(X)+m_{2}^{2}(X)}\cos(f(X)-\gamma(X))\Big].

Finally the minimizer of the minimization problem (
1
) is achieved for

f(x)=\gamma(x)=\mbox{atan2}\big(m_{1}(x),m_{2}(x)\big).

In conclusion, the circular nature of the response is taken into account by the arctangent of the ratio of the conditional expectation of sine and cosine components of
\Theta
given
X
.
Accordingly, we tackle the regression problem by estimating the function

m(x)=\mbox{atan2}(m_{1}(x),m_{2}(x)),\quad x\in\Omega,

(3)

with
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
defined in (
2
).

Remark 2.1
.

Observe that if
m_{1}(x)=m_{2}(x)=0
, then
m(x)
is not defined. This occurs if and only if

\phi_{1}(f(\cdot|x)):=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}f(\theta|x)d\theta=0,

where
f(\cdot|x)
denotes the conditional density of
\Theta|X=x
. Note that
\phi_{1}(f(\cdot|x))
plays a specific role in the literature of directional statistics. See for instance Section 3.4.2 of
[
31
]
.

In the sequel, we estimate the circular regression function
m
as defined in (
3
) under the condition

\phi_{1}(f(\cdot|x))\not=0.

(4)

Since the function
\mbox{atan2}(w_{1},w_{2})
is undefined when
w_{1}=w_{2}=0
, it is reasonable to consider the following assumption:

Assumption 2.2
.

m_{1}(x)\not=0\quad\textrm{ or }\quad m_{2}(x)\not=0,\quad\textrm{ for }x\in\Omega,

and let
\delta>0
be defined as

\delta=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\min\big(|m_{1}(x)|,|m_{2}(x)|\big)&\mbox{ if }&m_{1}(x)\not=0\mbox{ and }m_{2}(x)\not=0,\\[5.69046pt]
|m_{1}(x)|&\mbox{ if }&m_{2}(x)=0,\\[5.69046pt]
|m_{2}(x)|&\mbox{ if }&m_{1}(x)=0.\end{array}\right.

(5)

Assumption 2.3
.

Assume that
there exists
\delta_{X}>0
such that

\displaystyle f_{X}(x)\geq\delta_{X},\hskip 5.69046pt\textrm{ for all }\hskip 2.84544ptx\in\Omega.

(6)

Now let us expose the overall estimation strategy. In regression with random design, the classical idea to estimate the regression functions
m_{i}
with
i\in\{1,2\}
is to write
m_{i}
as a ratio
\frac{m_{i}f_{X}}{f_{X}}
, with
f_{X}
the density of
X
. Hence one usually considers estimators of intermediate functions
p_{i}
defined as:

p_{i}:=m_{i}f_{X},\quad i\in\{1,2\},

along with an estimator of
f_{X}
obtained by a deconvolution step due to the errors-in-variables setting. In constrat, here in our circular response setting, the estimator of
f_{X}
does not have any impact because of the ratio involved in the
atan2
function (see Definition
1.1
) which entails that
\mbox{atan2}(m_{1},m_{2})=\mbox{atan2}(p_{1},p_{2})
. This is actually a specificity of the circular response regression compared with the linear one. Accordingly, our strategy will consist in, first estimating
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
and then
m=\mbox{atan2}(p_{1},p_{2})
.

3
Circular predictor

3.1
Model

We begin by considering the case where the predictor is circular, which represents the most challenging and novel setting. More specifically, we study the following model:

\Theta=m(X)+\zeta\quad\textrm{(mod $2\pi$)},

with
\Theta,X
and
\zeta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. The
X
are latent and we only have access to:

Z=X+\varepsilon\quad\textrm{(mod $2\pi$)}.

We assume that
X
and
\varepsilon
are independent and the characteristic function of the density
f_{\varepsilon}
is known. From an i.i.d dataset
(Z_{1},\Theta_{1}),\dots,(Z_{n},\Theta_{n})
, we aim to estimate
m:\mathbb{S}^{1}\mapsto\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

3.2
Assumptions

We shall present our results in terms of Sobolev classes. Due to circular covariates, we deal with Fourier series instead of Fourier transforms. For any function
f(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}f^{\star}(l)e^{-\mathrm{i}lx}
in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
let

\|f\|^{2}_{{W}_{\beta}}:=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}|f^{\star}(l)|^{2}(1+l^{2})^{\beta}.

We define the class
\mathcal{W}(\beta,S)
to be

\mathcal{W}(\beta,S)=\{f\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1}):\;\|f\|^{2}_{W_{\beta}}\leq S^{2}\}.

(7)

Following
[
8
]
, we make the following assumptions about the noise in the variables.

Assumption 3.1
.

The noise
\varepsilon
is called ordinary smooth (OS) if there exist
\nu,a_{0},a_{1}>0
, such that

a_{0}(1+|l|)^{-\nu}\leq|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|\leq a_{1}(1+|l|)^{-\nu},\quad l\in\mathbb{Z}.

(8)

The noise
\varepsilon
is called supersmooth (SS) if there exist
a_{0},a_{1},b,a>0
and
c\in\mathbb{R}

a_{0}(|l|+1)^{c}\exp(-b|l|^{a})\leq|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|\leq a_{1}(|l|+1)^{c}\exp(-b|l|^{a}),\quad l\in\mathbb{Z}.

(9)

Let us recall that some well-known circular densities belong to these two types of noise. Examples of supersmooth densities include the densities of wrapped Normal and
wrapped Cauchy distributions; conversely, the wrapped Laplace and the wrapped Gamma densities are examples of ordinary smooth ones.

3.3
Preliminary estimators

As explained at the end of Section
2
, the target function
m
is given by
m=\mbox{atan2}(m_{1},m_{2})=\mbox{atan2}(p_{1},p_{2})
with

p_{1}(x)=\mathbb{E}(\sin\Theta_{1}|X=x)f_{X}(x)
and
p_{2}(x)=\mathbb{E}(\cos\Theta_{1}|X=x)f_{X}(x)
. We first aim to find estimates of
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
.
To this end, we adopt an orthogonal projection method onto the Fourier basis of
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
.

In the nonparametric framework, the usual idea is to find an estimator of the
L
-th projection of
p_{i},i\in\{1,2\}
namely an estimator of

p_{i,L_{i}}(x):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{|l|\leq L_{i}}p_{i}^{\star}(l)e^{-\mathrm{i}lx},\;x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}.

We propose the following estimates for
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{1,L_{1}}(x)=\sum_{|l|\leq L_{1}}\frac{1}{2\pi n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sin(\Theta_{j})\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}lZ_{j}}}{f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)}e^{-\mathrm{i}lx},\;x\in\mathbb{S}^{1},

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{2,L_{2}}(x)=\sum_{|l|\leq L_{2}}\frac{1}{2\pi n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\cos(\Theta_{j})\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}lZ_{j}}}{f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)}e^{-\mathrm{i}lx},\;x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}.

The expression of
\hat{p}_{1,L_{1}}(x)
(same line of reasoning for
\hat{p}_{2,L_{2}}(x)
) is justified by the fact that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{p}_{1,L_{1}}(x)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{|l|\leq L_{1}}\frac{e^{-\mathrm{i}lx}}{f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)}\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{E}(\sin(\Theta_{1})e^{\mathrm{i}l(X_{1}+\varepsilon_{1})}|X_{1})))=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{|l|\leq L_{1}}e^{-\mathrm{i}lx}\mathbb{E}(e^{\mathrm{i}lX_{1}}m_{1}(X_{1}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{|l|\leq L_{1}}p_{1}^{\star}(l)e^{-\mathrm{i}lx}=p_{1,L_{1}}(x),

which entails that
\widehat{p}_{1,L_{1}}(x)
is a unbiased estimator of
p_{1,L_{1}}(x)
.

3.3.1
Bias and Variance

We have the following bias-variance decomposition of the pointwise risk of
\hat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x)
:

\mathbb{E}|\widehat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x)-p_{i}(x)|^{2}=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}|\widehat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x)-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x)]|^{2}}_{=:Var(\hat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x))}+{|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x)]-p_{i}(x)|^{2}}.

Proposition 3.2
.

For
i\in\{1,2\}
, if
p_{i}\in\mathcal{W}(\beta_{i},S_{i})
with
\beta_{i}>\frac{1}{2}
, the bias is controlled as follows

|\mathbb{E}[\widehat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x)]-p_{i}(x)|=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left|\sum_{|l|\geq L_{i}}p_{i}^{\star}(l)\right|\leq C(S_{i},\beta_{i})L_{i}^{-\beta_{i}},

with
C(S_{i},\beta_{i})
a constant depending on
S_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
. The variance is controlled by

Var(\widehat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x))\leq V_{0}(n,L_{i})

with

V_{0}(n,L_{i}):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}n}\min\left\{\left(\sum_{|l|\leq L_{i}}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|}\right)^{2},\|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\ell_{1}}\sum_{|l|\leq 2L_{i}}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|^{2}}\right\}.

Furthermore, we have that

1.

If the noise
\varepsilon
is OS (satisfying (
8
)) with
\nu>1
and provided that
L_{i}\geq 1
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
, we have

Var(\widehat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x))\leq\frac{\max\{1,\|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\ell_{1}}\}}{(2\pi)^{2}n}L_{i}^{2\nu+1}.

2.

If the noise
\varepsilon
is SS (satisfying (
9
)), we have

Var(\widehat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x))\leq\frac{\max\{1,\|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\ell_{1}}\}}{(2\pi)^{2}n}L_{i}^{-2c+1}e^{2bL_{i}^{a}}.

The proof of Proposition
3.2
is given in Section
6.1.1
.

3.3.2
Rates of convergence

When the noise
\varepsilon
is ordinary smooth and the functions
p_{i}
belong to a Sobolev class
\mathcal{W}(\beta_{i},S_{i})
,
i\in\{1,2\}
, we deduce from Proposition
3.2
, via the usual bias-variance tradeoff, that the optimal choice of the projection level is given by:

L_{i,{opt,OS}}=\arg\min_{L\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\left\{L^{-2\beta_{i}}+\frac{1}{n}L^{2\nu+1}\right\}\Rightarrow L_{i,{opt,OS}}\varpropto{n}^{\frac{1}{2\beta_{i}+2\nu+1}},

and the rate of convergence for the pointwise risk to estimate
p_{i}
is
n^{-\frac{2\beta_{i}}{2\beta_{i}+2\nu+1}}
. This corresponds to
the usual minimax rate of convergence for estimating a univariate function of regularity
\beta_{i}
in an ordinary ill-posed inverse problem of severity
\nu
(see Chapter 3 in
[
33
]
). One remarks that the optimal level
L_{i,{opt,OS}}
depends on the regularity
\beta_{i}
which is unknown. This problem will be tackle in Section
3.4
where we propose a data-driven procedure to select
L_{i}
.

When the noise
\varepsilon
is supersmooth, and the functions
p_{i}
belong to a Sobolev class
\mathcal{W}(\beta_{i},S_{i})
, again from Proposition
3.2
, we deduce that the optimal levels are given by:

L_{i,{opt,SS}}=\arg\min_{L\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\left\{L^{-2\beta_{i}}+\frac{1}{n}L^{-2c+1}e^{2bL^{a}}\right\}\Rightarrow L_{i,{opt,SS}}\varpropto L_{{opt,SS}}:=\left(\frac{\log n}{2b}\right)^{\frac{1}{a}}.

(10)

The subsequent rates of convergence for the pointwise risk to estimate
p_{i}
are
\left({\log n}\right)^{\frac{-2\beta_{i}}{a}}
.

In the case of a supersmooth noise, unlike in the OS noise setting, the optimal level
L_{opt,SS}
does not depend on the regularity of the functions
p_{i}
, but solely on the noise parameters, which are assumed to be known. As a consequence, no level selection procedure is required in this case.

3.4
Adaptive estimation

As shown in the previous section, when the noise is ordinary smooth, it is necessary to design a data-driven procedure for selecting the levels
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
. To this end, we employ a Goldenshluger-Lepski selection rule adapted to projection methods and indirect observations (see
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
). Consider the following collection of levels:

\mathcal{L}=\left\{L\in\{1,\dots,n\},\frac{\sum_{|l|\leq 2L}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|^{2}}}{\left(\sum_{|l|\leq L}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|}\right)^{2}}\geq\frac{\log n}{n}\right\}.

(11)

Note that condition expressed in
(\ref{set-L})
amounts to have
L\precsim\frac{n}{\log n}
.

For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let us set

A(L_{i},x)=\sup_{L_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}}\left\{|\widehat{p}_{i,L_{i}^{\prime}}(x)-\widehat{p}_{i,L_{i}\wedge L_{i}^{\prime}}(x)|-\sqrt{V(n,L_{i}^{\prime})}\right\}_{+},

(12)

with

V(n,L_{i})=c_{0,i}\log(n)V_{0}(n,L_{i}),

and
c_{0,i}>0
is a tuning constant which will be specified later while
V_{0}(n,L_{i})
has been defined in Proposition
3.2
.

Finally, we define our selected levels as

\hat{L}_{i}:=\arg\min_{L_{i}\in\mathcal{L}}\left\{A(L_{i},x)+\sqrt{V(n,L_{i})}\right\}.

(13)

We now obtain the following theorem which states an oracle inequality.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose that the noise
\varepsilon
is OS (satisfying (
8
)). Let
q\geq 1
and

\min\{c_{0,1},c_{0,2}\}\geq\frac{64(2+q)^{2}}{\min(1,\|f_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\|_{\ell_{1}})}.

Then with probability larger than
1-4n^{-q}
we have

|\widehat{p}_{i,\hat{L}_{i}}(x)-p_{i}(x)|\leq\inf_{L_{i}\in\mathcal{L}}\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{|l|\geq L_{i}}|p_{i}^{\star}(l)|+\frac{5}{2}\sqrt{V(n,L_{i})}\right\},\quad i\in\{1,2\}.

(14)

The proof of Theorem
3.3
is given in Section
6.1.2
.

Theorem
3.3
highlights the bias-variance decomposition of the pointwise risk. The first term of the r.h.s is a bias term (see Proposition
3.2
) while the second term is a variance term.

3.4.1
Rates of convergence for
m

In this section, we derive rates of convergence for estimating the target regression function
m
.

In the case of an ordinary smooth noise, we set the final estimator of
m
to be:

\widehat{m}_{\hat{L}}(x):=\mbox{atan2}(\widehat{p}_{1,\hat{L}_{1}}(x),\widehat{p}_{2,\hat{L}_{2}}(x)).

(15)

In the case of a supersmooth noise, remind that no selection level is required (see Section
3.3.2
) and we have adaptation for free. Then, the estimator of
m
is defined as:

\widehat{m}_{L_{opt,SS}}(x):=\mbox{atan2}(\widehat{p}_{1,L_{opt,SS}}(x),\widehat{p}_{2,L_{{opt,SS}}}(x)),

with
L_{{opt,SS}}
defined in (
10
).

The oracle inequality stated in Theorem
3.3
serves as a key tool for deriving convergence rates for the estimation of
m
in the presence of ordinary smooth noise. In contrast, obtaining convergence rates in the supersmooth noise setting is a straightforward consequence of the proofs established for the ordinary smooth case. The next theorem presents the convergence rates.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
p_{1}
belongs to
\mathcal{W}(\beta_{1},S_{1})
and
p_{2}
belongs to
\mathcal{W}(\beta_{2},S_{2})
.

1.

Assume that the noise
\varepsilon
is OS (satisfying (
8
)) with
\nu>1
. Let
q\geq 1
and suppose that
\min\left\{c_{0,1};c_{0,2}\right\}\geq\frac{64(2+q)^{2}}{\min(1,\|f_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\|_{\ell_{1}})}.
Then, for
n
sufficiently large,

\mathbb{E}\Big[d_{c}(\widehat{m}_{\hat{L}}(x),m(x))\Big]\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\delta^{2}}\,\max\left\{\psi_{n}^{2}(\beta_{1},\nu),\psi_{n}^{2}(\beta_{2},\nu)\right\},

where
the convergence rates are
\psi_{n}(\beta_{i},\nu)=\big(\log n/n\big)^{\frac{\beta_{j}}{2\beta_{i}+2\nu+1}}
, the constant
\delta
is defined in (
5
) and
C_{1}
is a constant depending on
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},S_{1},S_{2},c_{0,1},c_{0,2}
and
f_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
.

2.

Assume that the noise
\varepsilon
is SS (satisfying (
9
)). For
n
sufficiently large,

\mathbb{E}\Big[d_{c}(\widehat{m}_{L_{opt,SS}}(x),m(x))\Big]\leq\frac{C_{2}}{\delta^{2}}\,\max\left\{\check{\psi}_{n}^{2}(\beta_{1},a),\check{\psi}_{n}^{2}(\beta_{2},a)\right\},

with
\check{\psi}_{n}(\beta_{i},a)=\left({\log n}\right)^{\frac{-\beta_{i}}{a}}
, the constant
\delta
defined in (
5
) and
C_{2}
is a constant depending on
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},S_{1},S_{2}
and
f_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
.

The proof of Theorem
3.4
is given in Section
6.1.3
.

Remark 3.5
.

Note that when
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=\beta
, the resulting rates are
\left(\log n/n\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+2\nu+1}}
and
\left(\log n\right)^{-2\beta/a}
, which correspond to the optimal rates for adaptive estimation of a univariate regression function and for pointwise risk in an errors-in-variables framework (see Section 3.3.2 in
[
33
]
). The presence of a logarithmic factor in the rate under ordinary smooth noise is expected, since we deal with pointwise adaptive estimation.

4
Linear predictor

In this section, we consider the case where the predictor
X\in[0,1]
.

4.1
Model

We study the following model:

\Theta=m(X)+\zeta\quad\textrm{(mod $2\pi$)}

with
\Theta,\zeta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
and
X\in[0,1]
. The variable
X
is unobserved and we only have access to

Z=X+\varepsilon.

We assume that
X
and
\varepsilon
are independent and the characteristic function of density the
f_{\varepsilon}
is known.
From an i.i.d dataset
(Z_{1},\Theta_{1}),\dots,(Z_{n},\Theta_{n})
, we want to estimate
m:[0,1]\mapsto\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

4.1.1
Assumptions

We present our results in terms of Hölder classes that are adapted to local estimation.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\beta>0
and
\Lambda>0
. The Hölder class
\mathcal{H}(\beta,\Lambda)
is the set of functions
f:(0,1)\longmapsto\mathbb{R}
, such that
f
admits derivatives up to the order
\lfloor\beta\rfloor
, and for any
(y,\widetilde{y})\in(0,1)^{2}
,

\displaystyle\left|\dfrac{d^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}f}{(dy)^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}(\widetilde{y})-\dfrac{d^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}f}{(dy)^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}(y)\right|\leq\Lambda\hskip 2.84544pt\big|\widetilde{y}-y\big|^{\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}.

We make the following assumptions on the covariates’ noise.

Assumption 4.2
.

The noise
\varepsilon
is called ordinary smooth (OS) if there exist
r,c_{\varepsilon},C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\displaystyle c_{\varepsilon}\big(1+|t|\big)^{-r}\leq\big|f_{\varepsilon}^{\star}(t)\big|\leq C_{\varepsilon}\big(1+|t|\big)^{-r},\hskip 5.69046pt\quad t\in\mathbb{R}\,.

(16)

The noise
\varepsilon
is called supersmooth (SS) if
there exist constants
{c}_{\varepsilon}>0
,
{C}_{\varepsilon}>0
,
\gamma>0
,
\rho>0
and
\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle{c}_{\varepsilon}\big(1+|t|^{2}\big)^{-\rho_{0}/2}\exp\big(-\gamma|t|^{\rho}\big)\leq\big|f_{\varepsilon}^{\star}(t)\big|\leq{C}_{\varepsilon}\big(1+|t|^{2}\big)^{-\rho_{1}/2}\exp\big(-\gamma|t|^{\rho}\big),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(17)

Due to the linear covariates, we shall use kernel estimation.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
K:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be an integrable function on
\mathbb{R}
. We say that
K
is a kernel if it satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(y)dy=1
. For
h>0
, we define
K_{h}(\cdot):=\frac{1}{h}K(\frac{\cdot}{h})
.
Also, define

\displaystyle M(K):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\left\|K\right\|_{1}&,\textrm{ if }\left\|K\right\|_{1}<\infty\\[5.69046pt]
\left\|K^{\star}\right\|_{\infty}&,\textrm{ otherwise }\end{array}\right..

We now consider the following assumptions on the kernel
K
, following
[
5
]
. A classical one to control the bias is:

Assumption 4.4
.

The kernel
K
is of order
\mathcal{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, i.e.

(i)

C_{K,\mathcal{\kappa}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+|y|)^{\mathcal{\kappa}}\,|K(y)|dy<\infty
;

(ii)

\forall k\in\left\{1,...,\lfloor\mathcal{\kappa}\rfloor\right\}
,
\int_{\mathbb{R}}y^{k}\,K(y)dy=0
.

Another one to ensure the estimators remain finite is:

Assumption 4.5
.

If
f_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
satisfies Assumption (
16
), we assume there exists
C_{(K^{\star})}>0
(for simplicity, we omit the dependence on
r
in the notation of
C_{(K^{\star})}
) such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|K^{\star}(t)\big|(1+|t|)^{r}dt<\sqrt{C_{(K^{\star})}}\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{ and }\hskip 14.22636pt\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|K^{\star}(t)\big|^{2}(1+|t|)^{2r}dt<C_{(K^{\star})}.

On the other hand, if
f_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
satisfies Assumption (
17
) we will consider the sinc kernel
K(y)=\frac{\sin(y)}{\pi y}
for
y\in\mathbb{R}\char 92\relax\{0\}
and
K(0)=\frac{1}{\pi}
, whose Fourier Transform has a compact support, more precisely,
K^{\star}(t)=\mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}(t)
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
. The sinc kernel has
M(K)=\left\|K^{\star}\right\|_{\infty}=1
and satisfies Assumption
4.5
.

4.2
Preliminary estimators

Now, with
j\in\left\{1;2\right\}
, for
x\in[0,1]
and a bandwidth
h_{j}>0
, we propose an estimator for
p_{j}
as

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{1,h_{1}}(x)

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle{\sum_{k=1}^{n}}\Big(\sin(\Theta_{k})\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\mathrm{i}tx}e^{\mathrm{i}tZ_{k}}\dfrac{K_{h_{j}}^{\star}(t)}{f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(t)}dt\Big),

(20)

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{2,h_{2}}(x)

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle{\sum_{k=1}^{n}}\Big(\cos(\Theta_{k})\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\mathrm{i}tx}e^{\mathrm{i}tZ_{k}}\dfrac{K_{h_{j}}^{\star}(t)}{f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(t)}dt\Big).

(21)

The form of our estimators for
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are justified by the fact that, for any
x\in[0,1]
and
h_{1},h_{2}>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\widehat{p}_{1,h_{1}}(x)\big)

\displaystyle=K_{h_{1}}\ast p_{1}(x)\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\mathbb{E}\big(\widehat{p}_{2,h_{2}}(x)\big)=K_{h_{2}}\ast p_{2}(x).

(22)

Indeed we have
for
x\in[0,1]
and
h_{1}>0
, using the independence between
X
and
\varepsilon
and Parseval’s equality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\widehat{p}_{1,h_{1}}(x)\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big(\sin(\Theta_{1})\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\mathrm{i}tx}e^{\mathrm{i}tZ_{1}}\dfrac{K_{h_{1}}^{\star}(t)}{f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(t)}dt\Big)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\mathrm{i}tx}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathbb{E}\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[\sin(\Theta_{1})\,|\,X_{1}\big]\hskip 2.84544pte^{\mathrm{i}tX_{1}}\Big)\mathbb{E}\Big(e^{\mathrm{i}t\varepsilon_{1}}\Big)\dfrac{K_{h_{1}}^{\star}(t)}{f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(t)}dt

\displaystyle=K_{h_{1}}\ast p_{1}(x),

and the same computations handle for
\widehat{p}_{2,h_{2}}
.

4.3
Bias and variance

Let us derive the bias and variance of our estimates.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
j\in\{1,2\}
. Suppose that
p_{j}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}(\beta_{j},\Lambda_{j})
, with
\Lambda_{j},\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{*}_{+}
. Suppose that the kernel
K
satisfies Assumption
4.5
and Assumption
4.4
with an index
\mathcal{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\mathcal{\kappa}\geq\beta_{j}
. Then

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}\big(\widehat{p}_{j,h_{j}}(x)\big)-p_{j}(x)\Big|\leq C_{K,\mathcal{\kappa}}\,\Lambda_{j}\,h_{j}^{\beta_{j}},\,

\displaystyle\textrm{ and }\,\mbox{Var}\big(\widehat{p}_{j,h_{j}}(x)\big)\leq\widetilde{V}_{0}(n,h_{j}),

with the constant
C_{K,\mathcal{\kappa}}
defined in Assumption
4.4
and

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{0}(n,h_{j})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}n}\min\left\{\left\|\dfrac{\hskip 0.28436ptK_{h_{j}}^{\star}\hskip 0.28436pt}{f_{\varepsilon}^{\star}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|f_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\right\|_{1}\,;\,\left\|\dfrac{\hskip 0.28436ptK_{h_{j}}^{\star}\hskip 0.28436pt}{f_{\varepsilon}^{\star}}\right\|_{1}^{2}\right\}\hskip 0.28436pt.

Furthermore, we have that

1.

If the noise
{\varepsilon}
is OS (satisfying (
16
)) with
r>1
, then if
h_{j}\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{0}(n,h_{j})\leq\dfrac{C}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,n^{-1}h_{j}^{-1-2{r}}.

2.

If the noise
{\varepsilon}
is SS (satisfying (
17
)) then

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{0}(n,h_{j})\leq\dfrac{C}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,n^{-1}h_{j}^{(\rho-1)_{+}}h_{j}^{-2\rho_{0}-1+\rho}\exp\big(2\gamma\,h_{j}^{-\rho}\big)\,.

The proof of Proposition
4.6
is done in Section
6.2.1
.

From Proposition
4.6
, we deduce by the bias variance tradeoff the following rates of convergence to estimate the function
p_{i}
, which are similar to the circular covariates case:

1.

If the noise
{\varepsilon}
is OS, the optimal bandwidth is of order
{n}^{-\frac{1}{2\beta_{j}+2r+1}}
which yields a convergence rate of order
n^{\frac{-2\beta_{j}}{2\beta_{j}+2r+1}}
. Consequently, one needs a data-driven bandwidth selection since the optimal bandwidth depends on
\beta_{j}
which is unknown. This will be tackled in the next section.

2.

If the noise
{\varepsilon}
is SS, then the optimal bandwidth does not depend on the regularity
\beta_{j}
and is of order

h_{opt,SS}:=\left(\frac{\log n}{2\gamma}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\rho}}

(23)

and there is no need to select a bandwidth. The subsequent rates of convergence are of order
(\log n)^{\frac{-2\beta_{j}}{\rho}}
.

4.4
Adaptive estimation

We now focus on the case of ordinary smooth noise. Let us describe how to select in a data-driven way the bandwidths
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
for the estimators
\widehat{p}_{1,h_{1}}(x)
and
\widehat{p}_{2,h_{2}}(x)
, respectively. This selection will be done among a convenient grid
\mathcal{H}
, defined as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\left\{h=k^{-1},k\in\mathbb{N}^{*},k\leq n:\hskip 2.84544pt\left\|\dfrac{K^{\star}_{h}}{f_{\varepsilon}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}_{2}\Bigg(\left\|\dfrac{K^{\star}_{h}}{f_{\varepsilon}^{\star}}\right\|_{1}\Bigg)^{-2}\geq\dfrac{\log n}{n}\right\}.

(24)

Again, we propose a Goldenshluger-Lepski selection procedure to select
{h}_{1}
and
{h}_{2}
. More precisely, for
x\in[0,1]
, we define:

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}(h_{j},x)=\sup_{h_{j}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\big|\widehat{p}_{j,h_{j},h_{j}^{\prime}}(x)-\widehat{p}_{j,h_{j}^{\prime}}(x)\big|-\sqrt{\widetilde{V}_{j}(n,h_{j})}\right\}_{+},

where
\widetilde{V}_{j}(n,h_{j}):=\tilde{c}_{0,j}\log(n)\widetilde{V}_{0}(n,h_{j})
,
\tilde{c}_{0,j}>0
are tuning parameters and

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{j,h_{j},h_{j}^{\prime}}(x):=\big(K_{h_{j}^{\prime}}\ast\widehat{p}_{j,h_{j}}\big)(x)

so that
\widehat{p}_{j,h_{j},h_{j}^{\prime}}(x)=\widehat{p}_{j,h_{j}^{\prime},h_{j}}(x)
for any
h_{j},h_{j}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
.
Thus, an adaptive bandwidth is selected as

\displaystyle\widehat{h}_{j}:=\underset{h_{j}\in\mathcal{H}}{\textrm{argmin}}\left\{\widetilde{A}(h_{j},x)+\sqrt{\widetilde{V}(n,h_{j})}\right\}.

(25)

The following theorem establishes an oracle-type inequality for the estimators
\widehat{p}_{j,\widehat{h}_{j}}
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Consider the collection of bandwidths
\mathcal{H}
defined in (
24
). Let
j\in\{1,2\}
,
q\geq 1
and assume that
\tilde{c}_{0,j}\geq 16\big(2+q\big)^{2}\big(1+M(K)\big)^{2}\dfrac{1}{\min\left\{\left\|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1},1\right\}}
with
M(K)<\infty
. Then, with probability larger than
1-4n^{-q}
,

\displaystyle\big|{\widehat{p}_{j,\widehat{h}_{j}}(x)}-p_{j}(x)\big|\leq\inf_{h_{j}\in\mathcal{H}}\Big\{\Big(1+2M(K)\Big)\hskip 2.84544pt\left\|p_{j}-K_{h_{j}}*p_{j}\right\|_{\infty}+3\hskip 2.84544pt\sqrt{\widetilde{V}_{j}(n,h_{j})}\Big\}\hskip 5.69046pt.

The proof of Theorem
4.7
is given in Section
6.2.2
.

4.4.1
Rates of convergence for
m

In the case where the noise
{\varepsilon}
is OS, the final estimator of the target regression
m
is

\widehat{m}_{\widehat{h}}(x)=\mbox{atan2}\big(\widehat{p}_{1,\widehat{h}_{1}}(x),\widehat{p}_{2,\widehat{h}_{2}}(x)\big).

(26)

In the case of a SS noise, remind that no selection level is required, and we set the bandwidths to the value
h_{opt,SS}
defined in (
23
). We then define the estimator of
m
as

\widehat{m}_{h_{opt,SS}}(x):=\mbox{atan2}(\hat{p}_{1,h_{opt,SS}}(x),\hat{p}_{2,h_{opt,SS}}(x)).

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}>0
. Suppose that
p_{1}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}(\beta_{1},\Lambda_{1})
,
p_{2}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}(\beta_{2},\Lambda_{2})
and the kernel K satisfies Assumption
4.5
and Assumption
4.4
with an index
\kappa\geq\max\big\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\big\}
.

1.

Assume that the noise
{\varepsilon}
is OS with
r>1
. Let
q\geq 1
and suppose that
\min\left\{\tilde{c}_{0,1};\tilde{c}_{0,2}\right\}\geq 16\big(2+q\big)^{2}\big(1+M(K)\big)^{2}\dfrac{1}{\min\left\{\left\|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1};1\right\}}~
. Then, for
n
sufficiently large,

\mathbb{E}\Big[d_{c}(\widehat{m}_{\widehat{h}}(x),m(x))\Big]\leq\frac{\widetilde{C}_{1}}{\delta^{2}}\,\max\left\{\psi_{n}^{2}(\beta_{1},r);\psi_{n}^{2}(\beta_{2},r)\right\},

where
the convergence rates are
\psi_{n}(\beta_{j},r)=\big(\log n/n\big)^{\frac{\beta_{j}}{2\beta_{j}+2r+1}}
, and the constant
\delta
is defined in (
5
) and
\widetilde{C}_{1}
is a constant depending on
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},c_{0,1},c_{0,2},K
and
f_{\varepsilon}
.

2.

Assume that the noise
{\varepsilon}
is SS. Then for
n
sufficiently large we have

\mathbb{E}\Big[d_{c}(\widehat{m}_{h_{opt,SS}}(x),m(x))\Big]\leq\frac{\widetilde{C}_{2}}{\delta^{2}}\,\max\left\{\check{\psi}_{n}^{2}(\beta_{1},\rho);\check{\psi}_{n}^{2}(\beta_{2},\rho)\right\},

with
\check{\psi}_{n}(\beta_{j},\rho)=\big(\log n\big)^{-\frac{\beta_{j}}{\rho}}
and the constant
\delta
is defined in (
5
) and
\widetilde{C}_{2}
is a constant depending on
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},c_{0,1},c_{0,2},K
and
f_{\varepsilon}
.

The proof of Theorem
4.8
is done in Section
6.2.3
.

We obtain similar convergence rates as in the case of circular predictors. When
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=\beta
, the resulting convergence rates are
\left(\log n/n\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+2r+1}}
and
\left(\log n\right)^{-2\beta/\rho}
, which match the minimax optimal rates for adaptive estimation of a univariate regression function and for pointwise risk in an errors-in-variables context.

Numerical results

In this section, we illustrate numerically our procedure. Both simulations and a real data example are considered.

5.1
Simulations

5.1.1
Linear predictor case

We consider the following linear-circular regression model:

\displaystyle\textrm{ (LC)}.\quad\Theta

\displaystyle=\mbox{atan2}\big(20X-11,(10X-5)^{2}+2\big)+\zeta\quad\textrm{(mod $2\pi$)},

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=X+\varepsilon,

(27)

where the covariate

f_{vM(0,10)}

For the covariates’ noise

f_{\varepsilon}^{\star}(t)=\dfrac{1}{1+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,t^{2}},\textrm{ with }t\in\mathbb{R},

which gives an example of ordinary smooth noises with degree of ill-posedness

(in Assumption (

)).

displays an illustration of our statistical setting.

.

and

, for which the bandwidths are selected among the following collection

In the selection procedure, we need to tune the two parameters
\tilde{c}_{0,1}

\mathcal{R}

6.1.1
Proof of Proposition
3.2

Proof.

First, let us upperbound the bias.
Assume that
p_{i}\in W(\beta_{i},S_{i})
with
\beta_{i}>\frac{1}{2}
. As
\mathbb{E}[\hat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x)]=p_{i,L_{i}}(x)
we have that
|\mathbb{E}[\hat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x)]-p_{i}(x)|=|p_{i,L_{i}}(x)-p_{i}(x)|
. And we get

\displaystyle 2\pi|p_{i}(x)-p_{i,L_{i}}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{|l|\geq L_{i}}|p_{i}^{\star}(l)|=\sum_{|l|\geq L_{i}}|p_{i}^{\star}(l)|(1+l^{2})^{\beta_{i}/2}(1+l^{2})^{-\beta_{i}/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\sum_{|l|\geq L_{i}}|p_{i}^{\star}(l)|^{2}(1+l^{2})^{\beta_{i}})^{1/2}(\sum_{|l|\geq L_{i}}(1+l^{2})^{-\beta_{i}})^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(S_{i},\beta_{i})L_{i}^{-\beta},

where
C(S_{i},\beta_{i})
is a constant depending on
S_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
.

Let us prove the variance bounds. For
i\in\{1,2\}
we have

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{2}nVar(\widehat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{|l|\leq L_{i}}e^{\mathrm{i}lZ_{1}}e^{-\mathrm{i}lx}\frac{1}{f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)}\right|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\sum_{|l|\leq L_{i}}e^{\mathrm{i}lZ_{1}}e^{-\mathrm{i}lx}\frac{1}{f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)}\sum_{|l^{\prime}|\leq L_{i}}e^{-\mathrm{i}l^{\prime}Z_{1}}e^{\mathrm{i}l^{\prime}x}\frac{1}{\overline{f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l^{\prime})}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{|l|,|l^{\prime}|\leq L_{i}}f_{Z}^{\star}(l-l^{\prime})e^{-\mathrm{i}(l-l^{\prime})x}\frac{1}{f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)}\frac{1}{\overline{f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l^{\prime})}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{|l|,|l^{\prime}|\leq L_{i}}|f_{Z}^{\star}(l-l^{\prime})|\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|}\frac{1}{|{f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l^{\prime})}|}=\sum_{|\tilde{l}|,|l|\leq L_{i}}|f_{Z}^{\star}(\tilde{l})|\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|}\frac{1}{|{f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l-\tilde{l})|}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{|\tilde{l}|\leq L_{i}}|f_{Z}^{\star}(\tilde{l})|\left(\sum_{|l|\leq L_{i}}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{|l|\leq L_{i}}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l-\tilde{l})|^{2}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{|\tilde{l}|\leq L_{i}}|f_{Z}^{\star}(\tilde{l})|\left(\sum_{|l|\leq L_{i}}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{|l|\leq 2L_{i}}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|^{2}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{|\tilde{l}|\leq L_{i}}|f_{X}^{\star}(\tilde{l})||f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{l})|\sum_{|l|\leq 2L_{i}}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|^{2}}\leq\sum_{|l|\leq 2L_{i}}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|^{2}}\sum_{|l|\leq L_{i}}|\int e^{\mathrm{i}lx}f_{X}(x)dx||f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{|l|\leq 2L_{i}}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|^{2}}\sum_{|l|\leq L_{i}}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|}\leq\|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\ell_{1}}\sum_{|l|\leq 2L_{i}}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|^{2}}.

But we also have that

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{2}nVar(\widehat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{|l|\leq L_{i}}e^{ilZ_{1}e^{-ilx}}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|}\right|^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{|l|\leq L_{i}}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|}\right)^{2},

hence

Var(\widehat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x))\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}n}\min\left(\left(\sum_{|l|\leq L_{i}}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|}\right)^{2},\|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\ell_{1}}\sum_{|l|\leq 2L_{i}}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|^{2}}\right).

We now turn to the last point of the proposition which gives upper bounds for the variance when noise assumptions are made.

If the noise is ordinary smooth, we have provided
\nu>1
(to ensure that
\|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\ell_{1}}
is finite) and
L_{i}\geq 1

\displaystyle Var(\widehat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x))\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}n}\max(1,\|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\ell_{1}})\min(L_{i}^{2\nu+2},L_{i}^{2\nu+1})\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}n}\max(1,\|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\ell_{1}})L_{i}^{2\nu+1}.

If the noise is supersmooth, then we get if
L_{i}\geq 1

\displaystyle Var(\widehat{p}_{i,L_{i}}(x))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}n}\max(1,\|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\ell_{1}})\min(L_{i}^{-2c+1}e^{2bL_{i}^{a}},L_{i}^{-2c+2}e^{2bL_{i}^{a}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}n}\max(1,\|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\ell_{1}})L_{i}^{-2c+1}e^{2bL_{i}^{a}},

which completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Proposition 6.1
.

Suppose that the noise

is OS. For all

\displaystyle 2n^{-\alpha}

(33)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|\widehat{p}_{i,L_{i}\wedge L_{i}^{\prime}}(x)-p_{L_{i}\wedge L_{i}^{\prime}}(x)|\geq c_{1}(\alpha)\sqrt{V(n,L_{i}^{\prime})}]

\displaystyle 2n^{-\alpha},

(34)

as soon as

Proof.

To prove Proposition
6.1
we rely on the Bernstein inequality which is recalled in the next lemma.

Lemma 6.2

Let

be
n
i.i.d variables and
S_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(T_{k}-\mathbb{E}(T_{k}))

(35)

with

and
|T_{1}|\leq b
.

Now let us prove Proposition
6.1
. We will prove it for

is strictly identical.

\widehat{p}_{1,L_{1}}(x)=\sum_{|l|\leq L}\frac{1}{2\pi n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sin(\Theta_{j})\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}lZ_{j}}}{f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)}e^{-\mathrm{i}lx},x\in[-\pi,\pi).

Then setting

We have that

\displaystyle|T_{L_{1},j}(x)|\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{|l|\leq L_{1}}\frac{1}{|f^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(l)|}=:b(L_{1})

(36)

\displaystyle Var(T_{L_{1},j}(x))=nVar(\widehat{p}_{1,L_{1}}(x))\leq nV_{0}(n,L_{1}),

(37)

using Proposition
3.2
for the last inequality. Now applying Bernstein inequality to the variables

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|\widehat{p}_{1,L_{1}}(x)-\mathbb{E}(\widehat{p}_{1,L_{1}}(x))|\geq\eta(L_{1})]

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|\sum_{j=1}^{n}T_{L_{1},j}(x)-\mathbb{E}(T_{L_{1},j}(x))|\geq n\eta(L_{1})]

\displaystyle 2\max\left\{\exp\left(-\frac{n\eta^{2}(L_{1})}{4nV_{0}(n,L_{1})}\right),\exp\left(-\frac{n\eta(L_{1})}{4b(L_{1})}\right)\right\}.

\displaystyle\quad 2\max\Bigg\{\exp\Bigg(-\frac{nc_{1}^{2}(\alpha)V(n,L_{1})}{4nV_{0}(n,L_{1})}\Bigg),\exp\Bigg(-\frac{nc_{1}(\alpha)\sqrt{V(n,L_{1})}}{4b(L_{1})}\Bigg)\Bigg\}.
