Distances between operators acting on different Hilbert spaces
Olaf Post, Sebastian Zimmer

Abstract
. The aim of this article is to define and compare several distances (or metrics) between operators acting on different (separable) Hilbert spaces. We consider here three main cases of how to measure the distance between two bounded operators: first by taking the distance between their unitary orbits, second by isometric embeddings (this generalises a concept of Weidmann) and third by quasi-unitary equivalence (using a concept of the first author of the present article). Our main result is that the unitary and isometric distances are equal provided the operators are both self-adjoint and have 0 0 in their essential spectra. Moreover, the quasi-unitary distance is equivalent (up to a universal constant) with the isometric distance for any pair of bounded operators. The unitary distance gives an upper bound on the Hausdorff distance of their spectrum. If both operators have purely essential spectrum, then the unitary distance equals the Hausdorff distance of their spectra. Using a finer spectral distance respecting multiplicity of discrete eigenvalues, this spectral distance equals the unitary distance also for operators with essential and discrete spectrum. In particular, all operator distances mentioned above are equal to this spectral distance resp. controlled by it in the quasi-unitary case for self-adjoint operators with 0 0 in the essential spectrum. We also show that our results are sharp by presenting various (counter-)examples. Finally, we discuss related convergence concepts complementing results from our first article [ post-zimmer:22 ] .

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Distances for operators acting in different spaces

Two bounded operators acting in a common complex Hilbert space can be
compared by taking the operator norm of their difference. It is then
known that this norm is an upper bound on the Hausdorff distance of
their spectra (see for example (
1.7
) taken
from
[
herbst-nakamura:99
, App. A]
). In particular, norm
convergence of operators implies convergence of their spectra with the
same convergence speed or possibly faster.

In applications, it often happens that the underlying spaces also vary
with the convergence parameter, e.g. when considering

•

domain perturbations (see
e.g.
[
daners:08
,
arrieta-lamberti:17
,
anne-post:21
]
and
references therein);

•

limits of spaces where the dimension shrinks, such as operators
acting on thin neighbourhoods of embedded metric graphs, so-called

graph-like manifolds
or
thick graphs
, see
e.g.
[
post:12
,
post-simmer:20b
]
and references therein);

•

discrete Laplacians as approximations of Laplacians on metric
spaces such as the Laplacian on the Sierpiński gasket or other
post-critically finite fractals (see e.g.
[
post-simmer:18
]

or
[
post-simmer:19
, Sec. 2]
and references therein. In this
case, typically, one space is finite dimensional while the other is
not.

The aim of the present paper is to define different distance and
convergence notions for such problems. We use the notion “distance”
here, as
\operatorname{d}_{\text{que}}
defined below is not a metric in the proper sense (we
show that the triangle inequality for
\operatorname{d}_{\text{que}}
is fulfilled only up to
a constant, see Lemma
).

For
n=1,2
, let
\mathscr{H}_{n}
be a separable Hilbert space. The Banach
space of bounded linear operators from
\mathscr{H}_{1}
into
\mathscr{H}_{2}
is denoted
by
\mathsf{Lin}(\mathscr{H}_{1},\mathscr{H}_{2})
. Moreover, we set

\mathsf{Lin}(\mathscr{H}_{n}):=\mathsf{Lin}(\mathscr{H}_{n},\mathscr{H}_{n})
. In the sequel, we consider two
operators
R_{n}\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathscr{H}_{n})
.

Unitary distance

A natural approach to compare operators acting in varying Hilbert
spaces is to use
unitary equivalence
. For

R_{n}\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathscr{H}_{n})
(
n=1,2
) we define their
unitary
distance
as

(1.1)

\operatorname{d}_{\text{uni}}(R_{1},R_{2}):=\inf\bigl\{\,\|{UR_{1}U^{*}-R_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{Lin}(\mathscr{H}_{2})}}\,\bigl|\bigr.\,U\in\mathsf{Uni}(\mathscr{H}_{1},\mathscr{H}_{2})\,\bigr\},

where
\mathsf{Uni}(\mathscr{H}_{1},\mathscr{H}_{2})
is the set of all unitary operators

U\colon\mathscr{H}_{1}\longrightarrow\mathscr{H}_{2}
and

\|{R_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{Lin}(\mathscr{H}_{2})}}=\sup\{\,\|{R_{2}u_{2}}\|_{{\mathscr{H}_{2}}}\,|\,\|{u_{2}}\|_{{\mathscr{H}_{2}}}\leq 1\,\}

is the operator norm. In this approach one necessarily needs that

\mathscr{H}_{1}
and
\mathscr{H}_{2}
have the same dimension (i.e. the cardinality of
an orthonormal Hilbert space basis), otherwise we set

\operatorname{d}_{\text{uni}}(R_{1},R_{2})=\infty
. In particular, this distance is not
applicable to the case of finite-dimensional approximations of
self-adjoint operators on infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.

Crude multiplicity functions

A useful tool in understanding the unitary distance is the
crude
multiplicity function

\alpha_{R_{n}}
defined for a self-adjoint
operator
R_{n}
by

(1.2)

\alpha_{R_{n}}(\lambda):=\lim_{r\to 0}\rank\mathbbm{1}_{(\lambda-r,\lambda+r)}(R_{n})\in\bar{\mathbb{N}}_{0}:=\{0,1,2,\dots\}\cup\{\infty\}.

Here,
\mathbbm{1}_{B}(R_{n})
denotes the spectral projection of the self-adjoint
operator
R_{n}
onto the Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}
. Crude multiplicity
functions were first used implicitly by Gellar and
Page
[
gellar-page:74
]
. Later, Azoff and Davis
in
[
azoff-davis:84
]
named them “crude multiplicity functions”,
defined a Lévy-Prokhorov distance

\delta(\alpha_{R_{1}},\alpha_{R_{2}})
and showed that this distance
equals
\operatorname{d}_{\text{uni}}(R_{1},R_{2})
. In particular, the crude multiplicity
function
\alpha_{R_{n}}
is a complete invariant of the (operator norm)

closure
of the unitary orbit

\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{n})=\{\,U_{n}R_{n}U_{n}^{*}\,|\,U\in\mathsf{Uni}(\mathscr{H}_{n})\,\}
. We
explain the concept of crude multiplicity functions and related
distances in Subsection
2.3
.

Some known results on the unitary distance and its
relation to spectra

We compare now the unitary distance with some distances between the
spectra: For the essential spectrum

(see Section

(1.3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}_{\text{Hausd}}(\Sigma_{1,\text{ess}},\Sigma_{2,\text{ess}}):=

\displaystyle\inf\{\,\varepsilon>0\,|\,\Sigma_{1,\text{ess}}\subset B_{\varepsilon}(\Sigma_{2,\text{ess}})\text{ and }\Sigma_{2,\text{ess}}\subset B_{\varepsilon}(\Sigma_{1,\text{ess}})\,\}

where

is the (closed)
\varepsilon

of

, see (
2.6
) for more details on the Hausdorff

The Hausdorff distance is too simple for the discrete spectrum, as it
does not see multiplicities: operators with spectrum

and

(with multiplicity) will have Hausdorff distance

, as the
underlying set in both cases is

. Therefore, for

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}_{\text{disc}}(R_{1},R_{2}):=

\displaystyle\inf\bigl\{\,\varepsilon>0\,\bigl|\bigr.\,\forall F\in\mathcal{F}(\Specsymb_{\mathrm{disc}}(R_{1}))\colon\rank\mathbbm{1}_{F}(R_{1})\leq\rank\mathbbm{1}_{B_{\varepsilon}(F)}(R_{2})\text{ and }

(1.4)

\displaystyle\hskip 34.5021pt\forall F\in\mathcal{F}(\Specsymb_{\mathrm{disc}}(R_{2}))\colon\rank\mathbbm{1}_{F}(R_{2})\leq\rank\mathbbm{1}_{B_{\varepsilon}(F)}(R_{1})\,\bigr\},

taking multiplicities into account. Here,

denotes the
set of all finite subsets of

. For short, we set

(1.5)

\operatorname{d}_{\text{spec}}(R_{1},R_{2}):=\max\{\operatorname{d}_{\text{Hausd}}(\Specsymb_{\mathrm{ess}}(R_{1}),\Specsymb_{\mathrm{ess}}(R_{2})),\operatorname{d}_{\text{disc}}(R_{1},R_{2})\}.

We start with Theorem

Isometric distance

A less restrictive approach to define a distance for operators acting
in different Hilbert spaces is inspired by Weidmann’s concept of a
generalisation of norm resolvent convergence
in
[
weidmann:00
, Sec. 9.3]
. We embed both spaces
\mathscr{H}_{1}
and

\mathscr{H}_{2}
isometrically into a common Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
. The

isometric distance
of
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
is then defined by

(1.8a)

\operatorname{d}_{\text{iso}}(R_{1},R_{2})=\inf\bigl\{\,\|{D_{\mathscr{H},\iota_{1},\iota_{2}}(R_{1},R_{2})}\|_{{\mathsf{Lin}(\mathscr{H})}}\,\bigl|\bigr.\,\iota_{n}\in\IsoX(\mathscr{H}_{n},\mathscr{H}),n=1,2\,\bigr\},

where the infimum is taken over all Hilbert spaces
\mathscr{H}
and
isometries
\iota_{n}\colon\mathscr{H}_{n}\longrightarrow\mathscr{H}
. Here

(1.8b)

D_{\mathscr{H},\iota_{1},\iota_{2}}(R_{1},R_{2}):=\iota_{1}R_{1}\iota_{1}^{*}-\iota_{2}R_{2}\iota_{2}^{*}.

Note that in the isometric setting, different dimensions of
\mathscr{H}_{1}

and
\mathscr{H}_{2}
are allowed; in contrast to the unitary distance.

The definition of
\operatorname{d}_{\text{iso}}
is inspired by the Gromov-Hausdorff distance
between two arbitrary metric spaces, where the infimum of all possible
isometric embeddings into a third metric space is taken, see
Remark
.

Our first main result is now equality of both distances under a mild
assumption:

Theorem B
.

Assume that
R_{n}
are self-adjoint and bounded operators in a
separable Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}_{n}
for
n=1,2
. If

(1.9a)

0\in\Specsymb_{\mathrm{ess}}(R_{1})\cap\Specsymb_{\mathrm{ess}}(R_{2})

then we have

(1.9b)

\operatorname{d}_{\text{uni}}(R_{1},R_{2})=\operatorname{d}_{\text{iso}}(R_{1},R_{2}).

Note that the inequality
\geq
in (
1.9b
) is trivial
as
\iota_{1}=U_{12}
and
\iota_{2}=\id_{\mathscr{H}_{2}}
are isometries into

\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{2}
.

The condition (
1.9a
) implies that both Hilbert
spaces
\mathscr{H}_{1}
and
\mathscr{H}_{2}
are infinite-dimensional. Moreover, we
cannot drop this condition as counterexamples show (see
Examples
–
). As in most examples
one has unbounded operators such as various Laplacians

\Delta_{n}\geq 0
, we consider convergence of their resolvents

R_{n}=(\Delta_{n}+1)^{-1}
. Note that in this

1.9a
) is always fulfilled for the resolvents.

For the proof of the non-trivial inequality
\leq

in (

) we use crude multiplicity functions, namely
that
\alpha_{\iota_{n}R_{n}\iota_{n}^{*}}=\alpha_{R_{n}}

if (

) holds (see Lemma

Quasi-unitary distance

We introduce now a third distance for
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, based on

quasi-unitary equivalence
, a quantitative generalisation of
unitary equivalence introduced by the first author in
[
post:06
]
.
We define the
quasi-unitary distance
of
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
as

(1.10a)

\operatorname{d}_{\text{que}}(R_{1},R_{2})=\inf\bigl\{\,\delta_{J}(R_{1},R_{2})\,\bigl|\bigr.\,J\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathscr{H}_{1},\mathscr{H}_{2}),\|{J}\|\leq 1\,\bigr\},

where

(1.10b)

\delta_{J}(R_{1},R_{2}):=\max\bigl\{\|{R_{1}^{*}(\id-J^{*}J)R_{1}}\|^{1/2},\|{R_{2}^{*}(\id-JJ^{*})R_{2}}\|^{1/2},\\
\|{JR_{1}-R_{2}J}\|,\|{JR_{1}^{*}-R_{2}^{*}J}\|\bigr\}.

The operator

If

with dense range, as

quasi-unitary equivalence compared to
[

]

Assume that

1.2.
Convergence of operators acting in different spaces

We now address the question of convergence of a sequence of operators

R_{n}
acting in Hilbert spaces
\mathscr{H}_{n}
towards an operator
R_{\infty}

acting in
\mathscr{H}_{\infty}
. In our previous article
[

[

]

[

]

(1.12)

in

1.3.
Related works

Probably the starting point of relations between the spectra and norms
of operators is an inequality formulated in
[
weyl:12
, Satz I]
for
operators

in

with kernel function

\displaystyle\bigl\lvert{\lambda_{k}(R_{1})-\lambda_{k}(R_{2})}\bigr\rvert\leq\max_{k}\bigl\lvert{\lambda_{k}(R_{1}-R_{2})}\bigr\rvert=\|{R_{1}-R_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{Lin}(\mathsf{L}_{2}({[a,b]}))}},

where

ordered decreasingly according to the absolute value of

\lambda_{k}(R_{n})
and repeated according to their multiplicity. Taking
now unitarily equivalent operators do not change the left hand side
of (

operators gives

minimum, namely, when both operators are diagonalised and eigenvalues
are ordered properly. In this case, we have even equality of the left
hand side and

. A good overview of related results
for matrices or compact operators can be found in
Bhatia
[
bhatia:07
]
and references therein. One of the main goals
of
[

]
is to express distances between eigenvalues of two
operators (matrices)

and
R_{2}
acting in a common Hilbert space

\mathscr{H}
in terms of
\|{R_{1}-R_{2}}\|
. Some recent developments on
generalisations of Schatten classes can be found
in
[
gueven-bandtlow:21
]
. A focus on non-self-adjoint matrices
and operators is given in
[
gil:03
, Sec. 4]
. We will relate
eigenvalue matchings of compact (and Schatten class) operators acting
in different spaces and its relation with operator distances such as

\operatorname{d}_{\text{uni}}
and
\operatorname{d}_{\text{iso}}
in
[
post-zimmer:pre24b
]
(see
also
[
zimmer:24
]
). .

We already mentioned the work of Gellar and Page
[
gellar-page:74
]

who characterised the closure of the unitary orbit of a normal
operator in terms of, what is called here
crude multiplicity
function
. It was introduced under this name by Azoff and

[

]
, where they showed that the unitary

Spectra and crude multiplicity functions

In this section, we fix some notation and collect facts used later for

Denote by

the power set of
\mathbb{R}

2.1.
Operators in a Hilbert space

All Hilbert spaces considered here such as

and

the space of all bounded operators equipped with

\|{\cdot}\|_{{\mathsf{Lin}(\mathscr{H}_{1},\mathscr{H}_{2})}}

\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{1}=\mathscr{H}_{2}

Elements

\displaystyle\Specsymb_{\mathrm{disc}}(R):=\{\,\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,\text{$1\leq\dim\ker(R-\lambda)<\infty$, $\lambda$ is isolated in $\Specsymb_{\mathrm{}}(R)$}\,\},

i.e., it is the set of all eigenvalues of finite multiplicity, which

. The spectrum is a unitary invariant, i.e.,

(2.1)

\Specsymb_{\mathrm{}}(R_{1})=\Specsymb_{\mathrm{}}(UR_{1}U^{*})

for any unitary map

(note that

is bijective). The same
is true for the discrete and essential spectrum. For simplicity, we
mostly consider only self-adjoint operators here, some results extend
to normal or even non-self-adjoint operators.

Denote by
\mathsf{Proj}(\mathscr{H})
the set of orthogonal projections in
\mathscr{H}
. The

associated with a self-adjoint operator

\mathscr{H}
is the map
\mathbbm{1}_{(\cdot)}(R)\colon\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow\mathsf{Proj}(\mathscr{H})
, i.e.

denotes the spectral projection of

onto

B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
. Moreover,
\lambda\in\Specsymb_{\mathrm{}}(R)
if and only if

\lambda
is in the support of this projection-valued measure, i.e.,
if

(2.2)

\forall\varepsilon>0\colon\mathbbm{1}_{(\lambda-\varepsilon,\lambda+\varepsilon)}(R)\neq 0.

2.2.
The Hausdorff distance of arbitrary sets in metric spaces

The Hausdorff distance of two subsets
A_{1},A_{2}\subset M
in a given
metric space
(M,d)
is defined as

(2.3)

\operatorname{d}_{\text{Hausd}}(A_{1},A_{2}):=\max\bigl\{\acute{\operatorname{d}}_{\text{Hausd}}(A_{1},A_{2}),\acute{\operatorname{d}}_{\text{Hausd}}(A_{2},A_{1})\bigr\},

where

(2.4)

\acute{\operatorname{d}}_{\text{Hausd}}(A_{1},A_{2}):=\sup_{x_{1}\in A_{1}}d(x_{1},A_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{d}_{\text{Hausd}}(x,A):=\inf_{a\in A}d(x,a).

Equivalently, we have the representation

(2.5)

\operatorname{d}_{\text{Hausd}}(A_{1},A_{2})=\inf\{\,\varepsilon>0\,|\,A_{1}\subset B_{\varepsilon}(A_{2})\text{ and }A_{2}\subset B_{\varepsilon}(A_{1})\,\}

for the Hausdorff distance, where

(2.6)

B_{\varepsilon}(A):=\bigcup_{a\in A}B_{\varepsilon}(a)\qquad\text{and}\qquad B_{\varepsilon}(a):=\{\,x\in M\,|\,d(x,a)\leq\varepsilon\,\}.

2.3.

We introduce a concept first used implicitly by Gellar and

and then introduced under the name

[

The main and name-giving example comes from the spectral
characterisation of the discrete and essential spectrum of a
self-adjoint operator in terms of its crude multiplicity function, see

distance between two crude multiplicity functions defined in

is

necessarily unitarily equivalent, only the essential and discrete
spectra (including multiplicities) agree. We comment on this fact in

Here, we first define crude multiplicity functions abstractly without
referring to operators; using an equivalent characterisation
in
[

Let

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbb{N}}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bar{\mathbb{N}}_{0}:=\{0\}\cup\overline{\mathbb{N}}=\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}.

2.1 Definition

(crude multiplicity function)
.

A map
\alpha\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\bar{\mathbb{N}}_{0}

(a)

\alpha

(b)

\lambda\in\alpha^{-1}(\mathbb{N})
there is

\varepsilon_{\lambda}>0
such that

.

2.2 Remark
.

We can replace (
a
) by

(
a

\alpha^{-1}(\{\infty\})
is closed in
\mathbb{R}
.

It follows that the

of

is closed in
\mathbb{R}
.

We give an important characterisation for crude multiplicity functions
in terms of a bounded and self-adjoint operator in Proposition

2.3 Definition

(discrete and essential support)

Let

(a)

(b)

3.
Unitary distance and crude multiplicity functions

In this section, we review and adopt briefly the results of Azoff and
Davis
[
azoff-davis:84
]
on the equality of the unitary distance of
two self-adjoint operators with the Lévy-Prokhorov distance of their
crude multiplicity functions needed for our analysis. A proof for
normal operators (with some modifications if discrete spectrum appears
(see Subsection
1.3
) is given in
[
davidson:86
]
.

3.1.
Unitary distance and crude multiplicity functions for
operators in the same space

Let
R
,
R_{1}
,
R_{2}\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathscr{H})
be bounded and self-adjoint
operators acting in a separable Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
.

The group of unitaries
\mathsf{Uni}(\mathscr{H})
acts isometrically on the space of
bounded operators
\mathsf{Lin}(\mathscr{H})
via
U_{*}(R):=URU^{*}
The corresponding

unitary orbit
is denoted by

(3.1)

\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R):=\{\,U_{*}(R):=URU^{*}\,|\,U\in\mathsf{Uni}(\mathscr{H})\,\}.

Let
\alpha
,
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
be the crude multiplicity
functions of
R
,
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, respectively (see Subsection
2.3
).

In order to refer to the corresponding operators of the crude
multiplicity function, we write

(3.2)

\operatorname{d}_{\text{cmf}}(R_{1},R_{2}):=\delta(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})

for the distance of the crude multiplicity functions
\alpha_{n}

associated with
R_{n}
(
n=1,2
).

We denote the
distance
between the unitary orbits by

(3.3)

\dist\bigl(\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{1}),\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{2})\bigr):=\inf\bigl\{\,\|{U_{1}R_{1}U_{1}^{*}-U_{2}R_{2}U_{2}^{*}}\|_{{\mathsf{Lin}(\mathscr{H})}}\,\bigl|\bigr.\,U_{1},U_{2}\in\mathsf{Uni}(\mathscr{H})\,\bigr\}.

As the group
\mathsf{Uni}(\mathscr{H})
acts isometrically on
\mathsf{Lin}(\mathscr{H})
, we have

\displaystyle\dist(\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{1}),\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{2}))=\dist(R_{1},\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{2})),

and also

\displaystyle\acute{\operatorname{d}}_{\text{Hausd}}(\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{1}),\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{2}))=\sup_{U_{1}\in\mathsf{Uni}(\mathscr{H})}\dist(U_{1}R_{1}U_{1}^{*},\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{2}))=\dist(R_{1},\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{2})),

where
\acute{\operatorname{d}}_{\text{Hausd}}
has been defined in (
2.4
).
Interchanging
the roles of
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, one then sees

(3.4)

\dist\bigl(\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{1}),\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{2})\bigr)=\operatorname{d}_{\text{Hausd}}\bigl(\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{1}),\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{2})\bigr),

where the Hausdorff distance is defined in (
2.3
).
The main result of Azoff and Davis
[
azoff-davis:84
, Thm. 1.3]
is
now the following:

3.1 Proposition
.

Let
R_{n}\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathscr{H})
(
n=1,2
) be two self-adjoint and bounded
operators with crude multiplicity functions
\alpha_{n}
acting in the
same Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
. Then we have

(3.5)

\dist(\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{1}),\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{2}))=\delta(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}).

The proof of “
\geq
” (
[
azoff-davis:84
, Prp. 2.3]
) is rather
easy; while “
\leq
” (
[
azoff-davis:84
, Sec. 4]
) needs more
advanced techniques including a generalisation of the marriage theorem
to infinite sets.

3.2.
Unitary distance zero

It is an interesting fact of operator theory that the unitary orbit is
(in general)
not
closed in the operator topology, i.e., if

R_{2}
is in the closure of
\mathsf{Uni}_{*}(R_{1})
, or equivalently,

\operatorname{d}_{\text{uni}}(R,\widetilde{R})=0

\widetilde{R}
is (in general)
not
unitarily
equivalent with
R
(i.e., there is not always a
U\in\mathsf{Uni}(\mathscr{H})

such that
\widetilde{R}=URU^{*}
, but only a sequence of unitary operators

with

\|{U_{n}RU_{n}^{*}-\widetilde{R}}\|_{{\mathsf{Lin}(\mathscr{H})}}\to 0
. Nevertheless, from
Theorem
3.3
and Proposition
2.6
(

\widetilde{R}
and
R
have the same discrete and essential spectrum. In
particular,
R
and
\widetilde{R}
might have different (essential) spectral
type: let

is the Lebesgue measure
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
a measure singular

\mu_{1}
and without atoms (a singular continuous

be an enumeration of

abrahamse:78

The case when
