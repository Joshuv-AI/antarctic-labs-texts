Contrasting low- and high-resolution features for HER2 scoring using deep learning
Authors: Ekansh Chauhan, Anila Sharma, Amit Sharma, Vikas Nishadham, Karan Vrajlal Padariya, Asha Ghughtyal, Ankur Kumar, Gurudutt Gupta, Anurag Mehta, CV Jawahar, PK Vinod
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer, the most common malignancy among women, requires precise detection and classification for effective treatment. Among immunohistochemistry (IHC) biomarkers, HER2 plays a critical role in guiding therapy decisions. In particular, a recent clinical trial has shown that 3-way classification of HER2 (0, low, and high) using IHC is essential for identifying patients with HER2 low expression who may benefit from new targeted therapies. However, traditional IHC classification relies on the expertise of pathologists, making it labor-intensive and prone to significant inter-observer variability. To address these challenges, this study introduces the India Pathology Breast Cancer Dataset, comprising HER2 IHC slides from 500 patients, with a primary focus on automating 3-way HER2 classification. Evaluation of multiple deep learning models revealed that an end-to-end ConvNeXt network using low-resolution IHC images achieved an F1 score of 83.52%, representing an improvement of 5.35% over patch-based methods. Class-wise F1 scores were 75.6% for HER2-0, 82.4% for HER2-low, and 91.5% for HER2-high, indicating the challenge in distinguishing HER2-0 and HER2-low cases. This study highlights the potential of simple yet effective deep learning techniques to significantly improve accuracy and reproducibility in breast cancer classification, supporting their integration into clinical workflows for better patient outcomes.Keywords: HER2-low breast cancer, Immunohistochemistry, Deep learning, Digital pathology

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most prevalent cancer among women worldwide. Higher incidence rates in developed countries are due to better detection methods, whereas higher mortality in developing countries is due to limited access to early detection and treatment.1 Breast cancer subtyping relies on accurate quantification of receptor protein expression in tissue samples, which is essential for selecting appropriate therapies and improving prognosis. One of the significant biomarkers for subtyping is HER2 (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2), which promotes cell growth, proliferation, and survival through multiple signalling pathways.2 HER2-positive breast cancers are more aggressive than other subtypes. It is overexpressed in about 15–30% of breast cancers, with status determined by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and in situ hybridization (ISH).3 Quantification is done by visualizing staining intensity and membrane completeness of tumor cells.

Despite advances in molecular assays, IHC remains the most widely used and practical method for HER2 assessment in routine clinical practice due to its cost-effectiveness, ease of use, and compatibility with standard pathology workflows. Accurate determination of HER2 status helps in determining patients for HER2-targeted treatments that significantly improve survival. Traditionally, HER2 IHC results are interpreted using a binary classification system, distinguishing HER2-negative from HER2-positive cases based on amplification or overexpression (Table 1). A more detailed 4-tier scoring system (0, 1+, 2+, and 3+) is used in practice to refine this interpretation, classifying stained samples as negative (0/1+), equivocal (2+), or positive (3+). In this system, score 0 indicates no staining or faint/incomplete membrane staining in <10% of tumor cells; 1+ indicates faint/incomplete membrane staining in >10% of cells; 2+ (equivocal) corresponds to weak to moderate complete membrane staining in >10% of tumor cells; and 3+ (positive) reflects strong, complete membrane staining in >10% of tumor cells.4 Equivocal (2+) cases require further confirmation via ISH to determine HER2 amplification status. However, a new 3-way classification scheme, gaining prominence due to recent findings from the DESTINY-Breast04 trial, which categorizes HER2 status into three groups: 0, Low (IHC 1+ and 2+ without ISH amplification), and High (IHC 3+ or 2+ with ISH amplification).5, 6, 7 This scheme recognizes the therapeutic potential for patients with low HER2 expression, who may benefit from new treatments like trastuzumab-deruxtecan.

Despite the clinical importance of accurate HER2 scoring, IHC scoring by visual assessment is labor-intensive and is subject to considerable inter-observer variability and reproducibility issues. Differences in staining quality and the inherent heterogeneity of tumor tissues, with various levels of marker expression, further complicates accurate classification. Krishnamurthy et al. (2024) reported that a mean inter-observer variability of 72.4% among pathologists across all HER2 scores.8 These challenges underscore the critical need for automated methods that can augment current IHC practice.

Automated analysis using artificial intelligence (AI) offers a promising solution to improve consistency and reproducibility in HER2 classification. Advancements in whole-slide scanning systems are enabling the digitalization of tissue slides. By leveraging deep learning models trained on IHC images, AI-based systems can reduce inter-observer variability and support the adoption of the 3-way classification scheme in routine clinical workflows. However, a significant challenge in breast cancer research is the scarcity of well-annotated datasets that capture the genetic, environmental, and lifestyle diversity of non-Western populations. Whereas most existing datasets are derived from Western cohorts,9 which have contributed to the development of computational models and diagnostic tools, there is still a need for region-specific data, particularly from Asia, to ensure the broader applicability and effectiveness of these models in diverse populations.

Most existing machine learning studies rely on patch-level labels, membrane segmentation, or manual region of interest (ROI) selection. Saha et al. (2018) proposed a deep learning framework for semantic segmentation and classification of cell membranes and nuclei to assist in HER2 scoring.10 Cordeiro et al. (2018) introduced a method that bypasses segmentation and manual ROI selection, focusing on classifying HER2 scores into four categories.11 Kabakçı et al. (2021) proposed a method based on color deconvolution and hybrid multi-level thresholding for segmenting nuclei and boundaries, relying on expert-chosen regions of interest.12 Wu et al. (2024) showed that distinguishing HER2 IHC scores of 0 and 1+ is particularly challenging due to HER2 heterogeneity and observer variability, highlighting a significant disadvantage of manual scoring methods.13 Tewary et al. (2022) introduced AutoIHCNet for HER2 scoring on manually vetted ROIs, with limited patient data.14 A recent study employed DenseNet and ViT models, utilizing small datasets and patch-level annotations.15 These methods, while foundational, often involve extensive manual efforts and limited scalability due to data constraints, underscoring the need for more efficient approaches.

ROIs may not capture all relevant information from whole-slide images (WSIs) and manual annotation of ROIs by pathologists is time-consuming. Therefore, WSI-based analysis methods have been proposed to directly predict outcomes from entire WSIs. Multiple instance learning (MIL) is effective for slide-level predictions when pixel-wise annotations are unavailable. Prior studies have demonstrated excellent performance with specific combinations of feature extractors and aggregators using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) slides.16,17 MIL's ability to operate at the slide level helps mitigate challenges associated with patch-level approaches, such as high computational costs and annotation variability. There is a need to develop methods that enable HER2 classification with greater granularity, capturing subtle differences in HER2 expression levels.

Given the clinical relevance of IHC and the need to streamline HER2 assessment, we curated a breast cancer dataset and evaluated multiple computational approaches for classifying HER2 status from IHC slides. By leveraging slide-level labels, we developed end-to-end convolutional neural network (CNN)- and MIL-based classifiers and compared their performance with patch-level pipelines relying on patch-level labels. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to apply deep learning to low-resolution WSIs for 3-way HER2 classification. The end-to-end CNN-based method demonstrates superior performance compared to other approaches. These models have the potential to assist pathologists by reducing inter-observer variability and improving diagnostic accuracy in routine IHC workflows.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and description

The dataset utilized in this study was retrospectively collected from the Rajiv Gandhi Cancer Institute and Research Centre (RGCIRC), a premier tertiary cancer care center in India. The India Pathology Breast Cancer Dataset comprises 500 HER2 IHC-stained slides from breast cancer patients, each paired with a corresponding H&E slide. HER2 IHC was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded breast carcinoma specimens using the Ventana PATHWAY system with an anti-HER2/neu (4B5) rabbit monoclonal primary antibody and the Ventana UltraView DAB Detection Kit (Ventana, Tucson, AZ, USA). All slides were digitized using Hamamatsu's NanoZoomer S210 at 40× magnification, achieving a resolution of 0.23 μm/pixel, ensuring detailed visualization of cellular structures. Ground-truth HER2 scores were provided by expert pathologists based on membrane staining intensity and the percentage of positively stained cells, following ASCO/CAP (American Society of Clinical Oncology and the College of American Pathologists) guidelines. Each case was independently reviewed by two experienced pathologists. In cases of discrepancy, a consensus review was conducted to arrive at the final HER2 score, which was used as the ground truth. For cases classified as equivocal, ISH was performed to confirm HER2 amplification. Table 2 shows the distribution of IHC slides among the various HER2 categories. A subset of 382 matched IHC and H&E slides was annotated by pathologists using QuPath software (version 0.5.1).18 The H&E slides were included specifically to assist pathologists in identifying regions of invasive carcinoma during the annotation of HER2 IHC-stained slides. Annotations of regions corresponding to HER2 scores were drawn on the IHC slides with side-by-side reference to the corresponding H&E images. Each annotated slide underwent a secondary review to ensure annotation accuracy, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or by involving a third expert when needed. The study was approved by the RGCIRC institutional review board (Reference Number: RGCIRC/IRB-BHR/75/2023). All patient data were anonymized to protect confidentiality, adhering to ethical guidelines for handling clinical data.

Methodology

We employed three complementary approaches for HER2 classification from IHC images: (1) MIL, (2) end-to-end slide-level CNN classification, and (3) patch-level classification pipeline with aggregation. Each method addresses distinct challenges associated with HER2 scoring, leveraging various levels of annotation and resolution.

MIL-based classifier

We used MIL to handle high-resolution WSIs with only slide-level HER2 labels, without requiring pixel-level annotations. For feature extraction, we employed the UNI model,19 a general-purpose self-supervised vision transformer pretrained on over 100 million tissue patches using DINOv2 framework. UNI captures high-level visual features but faces limitations in IHC applications due to its H&E training background. We evaluated two feature aggregation methods, CLAM20 and DTFD,21 for HER2 scoring classification. CLAM evaluates and ranks patches within a whole slide by assigning attention scores based on their significance. It aggregates these scores to form a slide-level representation for classification. DTFD uses pseudo-bags by dividing instances into smaller bags to refine attention-based MIL models. This approach improves feature extraction and prediction accuracy. In this study, we used the aggregated feature selection (AFS) strategy from DTFD to enhance classification performance despite potential label noise. The MIL framework for HER2 classification faces several challenges in meeting ASCO/CAP guidelines. Mixed subtypes within a single slide can obscure minor subtypes, complicating accurate classification. Additionally, the guideline requiring at least 10% of tumor cells of the labeled category on each slide presents a challenge, as traditional MIL struggles with aggregating dispersed information across patches. Label ambiguity in heterogeneous tumor slides further complicates the process, as MIL assumes clear labels for each bag. Whereas attention mechanisms can help prioritize critical instances, they may not fully capture the spatial and proportional nuances required for accurate HER2 scoring. Therefore, a modified approach incorporating spatial relationships is needed for reliable HER2 classification.

End-to-end slide-level CNN classifier

To address these challenges, we trained CNNs directly on low-resolution images. For this, we utilized the ConvNeXt architecture,22 a modern CNN known for high performance in image classification tasks. ConvNeXt incorporates several advancements over traditional CNNs, including depthwise convolutions for reduced computational complexity, residual connections for stable training and inverted bottlenecks blocks, similar to the design of transformer models, which enhance feature extraction and overall model performance.

ConvNeXt employs Group Normalization instead of Batch Normalization, which is particularly effective with small batch sizes and improves training stability. The model uses Gaussian Error Linear Units for smoother activation functions compared to Rectified Linear Units. It also integrates larger kernel sizes, such as 7 × 7, to capture more global context, and incorporates design elements from Vision Transformers, including hierarchical structures and a patch-based approach to process high-resolution images efficiently. ConvNeXt's original implementation employs advanced training strategies such as the AdamW optimizer, learning rate scheduling and data augmentation, contributing to its state-of-the-art performance on image classification benchmarks. ConvNeXt was benchmarked against widely used CNN architectures, including ResNet-50, DenseNet-201, and ViT-Base. This approach provides a robust end-to-end framework for slide-level HER2 classification.

Patch-level classification pipeline

End-to-end CNNs are effective for generalization with limited data but face challenges for HER2 classification on low-resolution images, primarily due to the loss of crucial morphological and color details. Low-resolution images may fail to capture fine details necessary for precise classification, potentially degrading performance. Additionally, reliance on slide-level labels introduces inconsistencies due to inter-observer variability.

To overcome these limitations, we implemented a patch-level classification pipeline using the ConvNeXt model. Patches were extracted from slides with partial annotations and labeled based on the corresponding slide. Each patch was independently classified into one of the HER2 categories (0, 1+, 2+, or 3+). Slide-level HER2 status was predicted by aggregating patch-level predictions using majority voting or machine learning classifiers. In majority voting, each patch contributes a single vote based on its predicted class, and the slide-level label is determined by the class receiving the highest number of votes. For the machine learning approach, the distribution of patch-level predictions, represented as class counts, was used as input features to train Random Forest, support vector machine (SVM), and MLP to predict the slide-level label. This patch-based classification approach enables more localized analysis of the slide, allowing for a finer assessment of HER2 expression. By training the ConvNeXt model with patch-level labels, this method captures variability across different regions of the tissue. Such detailed analysis aligns with the clinical reality of intra-tumor heterogeneity and may contribute to more accurate and clinically relevant HER2 scoring.

Experiment setup

The primary task was 3-way HER2 classification, distinguishing between HER2-0, HER2-low, and HER2-positive categories. This was compared with binary and 4-way HER2 classification tasks (Table 1). For all models, data were partitioned patient-wise into training (80%), validation (10%), and hold-out test (10%) sets. To assess robustness, 10-fold stratified cross-validation was performed. In each fold, models were trained on 80% of the data, validated on 10%, and evaluated on the independent 10% hold-out test set. All reported metrics, including standard deviations, were computed on the hold-out test data across folds, reflecting the models' generalization performance. Models were trained for 25–100 epochs with early stopping based on validation loss, using the Adam optimizer and various data augmentations, including flipping, rotations, and affine transformations. Cross-entropy, class weights, and focal loss functions were employed for multi-class classification tasks.

For preprocessing, we performed foreground and background segmentation of WSIs using contrast enhancement and Gaussian blurring. Otsu's thresholding and watershed segmentation were then applied to isolate the background. For MIL classifiers, high-resolution patches of size 224 × 224 were extracted using the CLAM pipeline and processed with a pretrained UNI model. Various feature aggregation methods were evaluated. For the CNN-based classifiers, including ResNet-50, DenseNet-201, ViT-Base, and ConvNeXt-S (small), low-resolution downsampled WSIs (224 × 224 and 512 × 512) were used.

For patch-level classification, a patch-level dataset was created from slide-level data with partial annotations. Patches were extracted at multiple resolutions and checked for significant overlaps with annotated regions using a minimum intersection threshold of 5%. White and black patches were excluded based on pixel ratio thresholds (70% and 20%, respectively), and Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) was applied for quality control. Patches that met all criteria were saved into resolution-specific directories and labeled based on their intersecting annotation, ensuring accurate and meaningful labeling. Patch-level predictions from ConvNeXt were generated for all slides, and these outputs were subsequently used to derive slide-level classification. All machine learning classifiers (Random Forest, SVM, and MLP) were implemented using scikit-learn with default parameters.

All models were implemented using the PyTorch framework.23 Pre-existing models, including ConvNeXt, CLAM, and DTFD, were obtained from their official GitHub repositories.24, 25, 26 Training was performed on a GPU server equipped with four NVIDIA RTX 3080 GPUs (12 GB each), enabling efficient parallel training and experimentation.

Results

The performance of the various methods for HER2 classification using different deep learning models was evaluated, with a primary focus on the relevant 3-way classification task. To assess the robustness and generalizability of the models and enable comparative evaluation, we additionally explored binary and 4-way classification tasks. The results provide insights into the strengths and weaknesses of each approach and highlight their respective capabilities in handling the complexities associated with HER2 scoring. The MIL-based classifiers (Approach-1), specifically CLAM-sb, CLAM-mb, and DTFD, demonstrated competitive performance across all classification tasks (Table 3 and Table S1). Among these, CLAM-sb demonstrated superior performance in the 3-way classification task with an AUC of 92.05, F1 score of 77.67, and accuracy of 78.6.

We also evaluated the performance of CNN-based classifiers (Approach-2) including ResNet-50, DenseNet-201, ViT-B, and ConvNext-S. Among these, ConvNext-S emerged as the top performer, outperforming both the CNN baselines and MIL models. In the 3-way classification task, it achieved an AUC of 91.79, F1 score of 83.52, and accuracy of 85.56 (Table 3). ConvNeXt-S also demonstrated superior performance in binary and 4-way HER2 classification tasks, highlighting its enhanced capability in feature extraction and classification (Table S2). Its improved performance can be attributed to the preservation of broader contextual information, which is critical for accurate HER2 scoring. In the slide-level classification pipeline, ConvNeXt-S operated on downsampled WSIs, capturing global tissue context at the expense of fine-grained histological detail.

To assess the consistency of ConvNeXt-S performance across cross-validation folds in the 3-way classification task, we report metrics computed on the held-out test sets from each fold, including AUROC, F1 score, accuracy, precision, and recall (Table S3). Across the 10 folds, the model achieves relatively consistent F1 scores (0.788–0.868) and AUROC values (0.896–0.953), indicating stable generalization across different data partitions. We also examined confusion matrices corresponding to the best- and worst-performing folds of ConvNeXt-S to understand sources of misclassification (Fig. 1a and b). These matrices indicate that the performance variability arises from the confusion between HER2-0 and HER2-low classes. This confusion likely arises from subtle differences in staining intensity and membrane staining patterns between HER2-0 and 1+, which are inherently challenging to distinguish.

A patch-level classifier was developed using patches extracted from annotated regions corresponding to HER2 scores (0, 1+, 2+, and 3+). Patches were extracted at three resolutions: low (average resolution: 1648 × 1471), moderate (average resolution: 4549 × 4017), and high (average resolution: 23,218 × 20,529). Each patch was individually classified using ConvNeXt-S. As shown in Table 4, the moderate-resolution setting yielded the best performance, achieving an AUC of 94.75, F1 score of 78.25, and accuracy of 81.43, striking a balance between spatial detail and computational efficiency. Whereas high-resolution patches provided more detail, they did not result in performance gains and incurred significantly higher computational costs. In contrast, low-resolution patches underperformed due to insufficient detail. This approach improves diagnostic precision by focusing on smaller regions within slides, effectively handling tumor heterogeneity and reducing overfitting through a larger number of training samples. Despite increased computational demands and the need for precise annotations, the patch-level method demonstrated robust performance in improving HER2 classification accuracy. Fig. 3 shows examples of patch-level predictions, including both correctly and incorrectly classified patches. Notably, some patches, especially from HER2-0 slides, do not contain invasive carcinoma. Although these patches were extracted from annotated regions corresponding to HER2 scores, tumor heterogeneity, coarse annotation, and patch-level sampling may have introduced noise into the dataset.

To aggregate patch-level predictions into slide-level classification, we initially employed a majority voting strategy. However, this approach yielded poor performance across all three resolutions (low, moderate, and high) for slide-level classification into 0, 1+, 2+, and 3+, indicating that majority voting may be a simplistic and ineffective aggregation method for HER2 classification (Table S4). To address this, we implemented a distribution-based slide-level classifier (Approach-3) using Random Forest, SVM, and MLP classifiers across the same three resolutions. As shown in Table 5, the model trained on the low-resolution setting performed strongly on the 3-way classification task, likely due to its ability to capture broader contextual features. Similar trends were observed in the binary and 4-way classification tasks (Table S5). Among the classifiers, MLP achieved the highest AUC across all classification tasks, whereas SVM achieved the best F1 scores for the 3-way classification. Moderate resolution also performed competitively (Table S6), particularly with Random Forest and MLP. In contrast, high resolution did not yield performance improvements over lower resolutions and remained computationally expensive (Table S7). These results suggest that critical histological features for HER2 classification can be captured at lower resolutions, enabling efficient and accurate diagnosis. Moderate resolution provides a practical trade-off between detail and efficiency, whereas high resolution may introduce unnecessary complexity and noise.

Discussion

This study explored AI approaches for HER2 scoring in breast cancer using IHC-stained slides, with each method offering unique strengths and challenges. By comparing MIL, end-to-end CNN, and patch-level classification strategies, we demonstrate how different architectural choices influence model performance and clinical applicability. Multiple resolutions were explored to accommodate the end-to-end ConvNeXt framework, which requires downsampled WSIs as input for practical processing. Each approach captures complementary aspects of HER2 expression, spanning global contextual patterns observable at lower resolutions and fine-grained regional variations requiring higher-resolution details. A key observation from our experiments is that increasing resolution during training did not significantly improve either patch- or slide-level performance. This indicates that while patch resolution matters, it may not be the primary factor limiting performance in this task. Instead, aggregation of patch-level predictions during inference appears to be more critical. Effective aggregation can better capture the spatial context and tumor heterogeneity within WSIs, resulting in a more accurate representation of HER2 expression at the slide level.

Among the evaluated approaches, the ConvNeXt-based end-to-end architecture (Approach-2) emerged as the most effective. ConvNeXt's advanced convolutional design, including depthwise convolutions and residual connections, allowed it to model intricate spatial patterns and staining variations in histopathological images effectively. Its ability to process WSIs holistically helped to preserve the spatial context, which is essential for tasks like HER2 scoring, where cell distribution and stain intensity are key factors. By enabling slide-level predictions from downsampled WSIs, ConvNeXt provides a practical and scalable framework for automated HER2 assessment.

Although Approach-2 achieved superior performance, Approach-1 and Approach-3 provided enhanced clinical interpretability. Approach-1 uses attention mechanisms to highlight critical regions within WSIs, ensuring that the model focused on clinically significant areas. Approach-3 provided a more detailed picture by classifying individual patches, aligning with clinical workflows that often focus on specific ROIs. In contrast, whereas Approach-2's low-resolution design proved effective for holistic classification, it lacked fine-grained interpretability.

The binary and 3-way classification datasets were derived from an initially balanced 4-way classification dataset, which introduced class imbalance. To mitigate this issue, various techniques were employed, including focal loss, class weighting, and data augmentation. Focal loss was applied to place greater emphasis on misclassified and minority-class samples, whereas class weighting adjusted the loss function according to class distribution. Data augmentation was applied to improve the representation of minority classes by diversifying the dataset. Despite these efforts, none of these methods resulted in substantial improvements in classification performance.

Most HER2 scoring approaches relied on annotated training data and multiple computational steps, including identifying tumor cells, classifying staining patterns based on membrane intensity and completeness, and deriving slide-level scores from cell counts. In contrast, we present a simplified solution using slide-level annotations and one of the largest available HER2 scoring datasets. Despite its simplicity, the ConvNeXt model achieved high performance, demonstrating that clinically relevant predictions can be derived even from low-resolution inputs. Such an approach can serve as a decision-support tool for triaging cases, ensuring consistency in borderline HER2 scores (0 vs. low), and identifying candidates for emerging HER2-targeted therapies.

A recent study demonstrated that an AI-based solution achieved high accuracy in HER2 scoring, reporting 92.1% agreement on slides with high-confidence ground truth, despite being trained on a relatively smaller dataset.8 There is a need to evaluate the concordance of our model with high-confidence ground truth, as we cannot exclude the potential impact of inter-observer variability in our ground truth (Fig. 2). While the models demonstrate promising capabilities, further validation and refinement are necessary to ensure their reliability and integration into clinical workflows. To facilitate this process, an application based on this model will be deployed at the hospital for validation and iterative improvement. Variations in staining procedures and images generated by different scanners can impact performance. Future efforts will focus on addressing current limitations and improving models to ensure practical utility in real-world scenarios. With further validation and integration into digital pathology systems, this framework can streamline HER2 assessment and enhance treatment planning in breast cancer care.

A key limitation of the patch-based approach is in the annotation and patch extraction process. Although IHC slides were annotated with reference to H&E, the annotations were not pixel-precise and did not involve formal co-registration between slides. As a result, some extracted patches, especially in HER2-0 cases, may contain little or no invasive carcinoma, potentially introducing label noise during training and evaluation. This issue is evident in some of the patches shown in Fig. 3. Despite this, the model demonstrated robust overall performance, suggesting some resilience to such variability. Future work will incorporate automated tumor detection on H&E slides and co-registration with IHC to improve patch-level accuracy.

A related methodological consideration is the use of the UNI model in the MIL-based classifier. Although UNI was pretrained on H&E-stained images, it generalizes well across staining modalities, with our framework achieving over 75% accuracy and F1 score across folds. Whereas domain shift between H&E and IHC images may influence performance, UNI provided a strong baseline feature extractor for our MIL framework, and future work employing IHC-specific pretrained encoders may further enhance performance.

In parallel, AI-based prediction of HER2 status directly from H&E-stained slides has shown promise.27 However, such methods rely on indirect morphological cues rather than direct protein expression. Our IHC-based approach provides a direct assessment of HER2 expression, aligned with current clinical practice. As part of ongoing research, we plan to analyze paired H&E slides from our dataset to explore the feasibility of 3-way HER2 classification in the Indian patient population using routine H&E staining.
