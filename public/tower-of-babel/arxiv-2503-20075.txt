. 1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

We start by noticing that
the Cauchy-Riemann operator
\partial_{\bar{z}}:=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y})
and its complex conjugate

\partial_{z}:=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}-i\partial_{y})
are building blocks of the 2D free Dirac operator

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}:=-2i\begin{pmatrix}0&\partial_{z}\\
\partial_{\bar{z}}&0\end{pmatrix}=-i\sigma_{1}\partial_{x}-i\sigma_{2}\partial_{y},

where
\sigma_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
and
\sigma_{2}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}

are the Pauli matrices, see, e.g.
[
5
]
. Since equation (
CR
) can be rewritten as
\partial_{z}\overline{r}=-r\times\overline{r}-i\gamma{\mathscr{W}}
, by using the free Dirac operator (
1.1
), we see that equation (
CR
) can be written in the following equivalent form

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}\begin{pmatrix}\overline{r}\\
r\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2i\\
-2i\end{pmatrix}r\times\overline{r}+\begin{pmatrix}2\\
-2\end{pmatrix}(\gamma{\mathscr{W}}).

Equation (
1.2
) can be regarded as a white-noise perturbation of a stationary Dirac equation. Dirac operators have wide-ranging applications in quantum mechanics, relativistic field theory, differential geometry,
and Clifford algebras
[
13
,
19
]
. By rewriting equation (
CR
) in terms of Dirac operators, we establish a connection to these areas, suggesting potential applications of our equation to these fields. We also remark that stationary deterministic Dirac equations have been studied in the literature
[
8
,
12
]
.

The quadratic term
r\times\overline{r}
in equation (
1.2
) represents a nonlinear self-interaction, where the solution
r

couples with its own complex conjugate, introducing an interesting mathematical structure.
One possible interpretation of this cross-product term is that it describes a nonlinear coupling between spinor-like components in a quantum mechanical system.
Nonlinearities of quadratic type appear, for example, in Dirac-Klein-Gordon systems containing an interaction term that couples a Dirac field with a scalar field, as well as an interaction term representing a self-interaction of the Dirac field
[
18
]
. Quadratic nonlinearities appear also in quadratic Dirac equations, which describe self-interacting fermions
[
18
]
.
From a mathematical perspective, the cross-product term plays a crucial role in balancing singularities introduced by the white noise
{\mathscr{W}}
.
Specifically, this nonlinearity contains a self-correcting
mechanism that cancels the most singular terms,
ensuring the existence of solutions as
limits in a Hölder space of negative order.
This interplay between the structure of our nonlinearity
and the solvability of the equation highlights an interesting and novel aspect
of this work.

Another motivation for studying equation (
CR
) comes from the
following stochastic equation

(1.3)

\displaystyle\Delta B=2\partial_{x}B\times\partial_{y}B+4\gamma{\mathscr{W}},

where
B:{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. The homogeneous version of
equation (
1.3
) can be derived
from the homogeneous Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert (LLG) equation

u\times\Delta u=0\mbox{ with }|u|=1

via the Bäcklund transformation

\nabla B:=\big(u\times\partial_{y}u,-u\times\partial_{x}u\big)
,
see e.g.
[
16
, p. 50-52]
.
This transformation
provides a mathematical connection between the LLG equation and
equation (
1.3
), suggesting that (
1.3
) can be viewed as a stochastic generalization of the LLG equation.

It is straightforward to prove, on a heuristic level, that
r=i\partial_{z}B

is a solution to (
CR
) if and only if
B
is a solution to (
1.3
).
This provides an additional motivation for studying equation (
CR
),
as it establishes a link between our stochastic equation
and a well-known physical model.
In Section
6
, we deduce the existence of
a solution
B
to (
1.3
) from the existence of a solution
r
to (
CR
)
under an additional assumption on the white noise
{\mathscr{W}}
.

Thus, in this work we are motivated to study equation (
CR
).
To the best of our knowledge, the Cauchy-Riemann operators
in the context of singular SPDEs have not been studied before.
Moreover, the periodic setting introduces
additional challenges, as the solvability of (
CR
) depends
on the mean-zero condition of the right-hand side.
More specifically, as we demonstrate in Lemma
A.1
,
the equation
\partial_{\bar{z}}r=f
is solvable
if and only if
\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}f=0
.

We remark that, unlike most singular SPDEs considered in the literature,
see e.g.
[
3
,
4
,
9
,
14
,
15
]
,
ours is of the first-order,
where the kernel of the operator
\partial_{\bar{z}}

increases the regularity by
1
. In this sense,
and also since
\partial_{\bar{z}}\partial_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta
,
our work is
related to recent studies of singular SPDEs involving the fractional
Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}

[
1
,
7
,
10
,
11
]
,
which can also be regarded as a first-order differential operator
due to its kernel’s regularity-increasing properties. However, our focus on the Cauchy-Riemann operator introduces different challenges and requires new techniques, distinct from those used for fractional Laplacians. By developing these techniques,
we aim to extend the existing framework to a broader class of first-order singular SPDEs.

1.2.
The method

In this paper, we consider the mild formulation of equation (
CR
), see e.g. (
3.2
),
with the complex-valued Green function
G=2\partial_{z}K

of the operator
\partial_{\bar{z}}
on

{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}
, where
K
is the Green function for the Laplacian with zero mean on the torus
{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}
.
From this “mild” equation, we deduce that the expected regularity of a solution to equation (
CR
) is
0^{-}
. This implies that the quadratic term
r\times\overline{r}
is ill-defined.
To overcome this difficulty,
we apply the method used by Da Prato–Debussche in
[
9
]
which involves
decomposing the solution
r
into a singular part and a smoother remainder.
More specifically,
we rewrite the mild formulation of equation (
CR
) with respect to
a new variable
R=r+\gamma\xi
, where
\xi:=2i\,G\ast{\mathscr{W}}
is the singular term.
This decomposition allows us to isolate and control
the additive term in the equation for
R
, namely, the term

(1.4)

\displaystyle\zeta:=2G\ast(\xi\times\overline{\xi}).

Let us observe that the term
\vartheta:=\xi\times\overline{\xi}
is ill-defined, since
\xi
has negative regularity
0^{-}
.
We overcome this difficulty by employing a smooth approximation of
\xi
, redefining

\vartheta
as the corresponding limit, and
showing that its components, we take the first component for certainty, are of the following form:

(1.5)

\displaystyle\vartheta_{1}=2\partial_{1}\big[(K\ast{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{2}}){\rm Re\,}\xi_{3}\big]-2\partial_{2}\big[(K\ast{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{2}}){\rm Im\,}\xi_{3}\big].

Deducing the above expression involves a renormalization procedure
via cancellation of divergent terms.
We observe that (
1.5
) is well defined
since the expression inside the square brackets
can be understood in the Young product sense, see
[
2
]
.
Moreover, this expression suggests that the expected regularity
of
\vartheta
is
-1^{-}
. Had this been the case, the expected regularity of

\zeta
, in (
1.4
), would be
0^{-}
and therefore the expected regularity of
R

would also be
0^{-}
. Such a regularity is too low for the product
R\times\overline{\xi}
to be well-defined. Hence, a fixed-point argument cannot be realized in a Hölder space of a positive regularity. A natural
way to remedy this would be to consider a lift of our equation to
a space of modeled distributions, as in
[
2
,
3
,
14
,
15
]
.

However, we are able to avoid using this approach by proving, exploiting another reformulation of (
1.5
), that
\zeta
has
regularity
1^{-}
, see Proposition
4.7
and Corollary
4.9
.
This subtle regularity analysis
represents a significant technical innovation and
is the cornerstone of our existence proof.
It allows us
to carry out the Banach fixed-point argument in the Hölder
space of a positive regularity
{\rm C}^{1-\kappa}({{\mathbb{T}}^{2}})
,
\kappa\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, by understanding
the vector product
R\times\overline{\xi}
, and other similar,
in the Young sense.
The main ingredient of our regularity analysis is
the following representation of the component
\vartheta_{1}
, as well as
\vartheta_{2}
and
\vartheta_{3}
:

\displaystyle\vartheta_{1}=2\partial_{1}\big[\big(K\ast{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{2}}-(K\ast{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{2}})(x)\big){\rm Re\,}\xi_{3}\big]-2\partial_{2}\big[\big(K\ast{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{2}}-(K\ast{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{2}})(x)\big){\rm Im\,}\xi_{3}\big],

for an arbitrary
x\in{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}
. As in (
1.5
),
a renormalization occurs through a cancellation of certain divergent terms.
We remark that including
(K\ast{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{2}})(x)
in the expression (
1.5
) is critical in our analysis.

It remains to notice that
to analyze the ill-defined product
\xi\times\overline{\xi}
, we employ a smooth white noise approximation by the standard mollifier and show the following convergence
rate
\|\xi\times\overline{\xi}-\xi^{\varepsilon}\times\overline{\xi^{\varepsilon}}\|_{{\rm C}^{-\kappa}({{\mathbb{T}}^{2}})}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\frac{\kappa}{8}}\|{\mathscr{W}}\|_{{\rm C}^{-1-\kappa}({{\mathbb{T}}^{2}})}
.
In
[
9
]
, the authors addressed
ill-defined products on a 2D torus by proving a convergence of Galerkin approximations in a negative-order Sobolev space.

1.3.
Main result

Definition 1.1
.

A 3D white noise on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
is a Gaussian random field

{\mathscr{W}}=({\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{1}},{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{2}},{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{3}}):{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}

with the following Fourier series representation:

(1.6)

\displaystyle{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}}_{j}=\eta_{j}+{\rm Re\,}\Big[\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\eta_{kj}e^{i(k,x)}\Big],\qquad j=1,2,3,

where
\eta_{j}
,
j=1,2,3
, are standard i.i.d. real Gaussian random variables
and
\eta_{kj}
,
j=1,2,3
,
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
,
are standard i.i.d. complex Gaussian random variables.
Furthermore, the series (
1.6
) converge in
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
.

Remark 1.2
.

We say that the white noise
{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}}_{j}
has zero mean over
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}

if, in representation (
1.6
),
\eta_{j}=0
. We say that

{\mathscr{W}}
has zero mean over
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
if
{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{1}},{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{2}}
, and
{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{3}}

have zero means over
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
.

The following is the main result of our paper.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and
let
{\mathscr{W}}=({\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{1}},{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{2}},{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{3}})
be a 3D white noise on
{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}
such that
{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{3}}

has zero mean over
{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}
. Further let

({\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{1}}^{\varepsilon},{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{2}}^{\varepsilon},{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{3}}^{\varepsilon})
be a mollification of
{\mathscr{W}}

by the standard mollifier
\rho_{\varepsilon}
,
that is,
{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}}_{k}^{\varepsilon}=\rho_{\varepsilon}\ast{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}}_{k}
,
k=1,2,3
.
Assume that
\kappa\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
.
Then, for every
\delta>0
, there exists a number
\varsigma>0
and a set
\Omega_{\delta}\subset\Omega
, depending also on
\kappa
, with
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{\delta})>1-\delta
,
such that for every
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3})

with
|\gamma|<\varsigma
, and for every
\varepsilon>0
,
the system

(mCR)

\begin{cases}\partial_{\bar{z}}r^{\varepsilon}_{1}=r_{2}^{\varepsilon}\overline{r^{\varepsilon}_{3}}-\overline{r_{2}^{\varepsilon}}r^{\varepsilon}_{3}+\gamma_{1}i{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{1}}^{\varepsilon},\\
\partial_{\bar{z}}r^{\varepsilon}_{2}=\overline{r_{1}^{\varepsilon}}r_{3}^{\varepsilon}-r_{1}^{\varepsilon}\overline{r_{3}^{\varepsilon}}+\gamma_{2}i{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{2}}^{\varepsilon},\\
\partial_{\bar{z}}r_{3}^{\varepsilon}=r_{1}^{\varepsilon}\overline{r_{2}^{\varepsilon}}-\overline{r_{1}^{\varepsilon}}r_{2}^{\varepsilon}+\gamma_{3}i{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{3}}^{\varepsilon},\end{cases}

considered for
\omega\in\Omega_{\delta}
,
has a
solution
r^{\varepsilon}=(r_{1}^{\varepsilon},r_{2}^{\varepsilon},r_{3}^{\varepsilon})\in{\rm C}^{\infty}({{\mathbb{T}}^{2}},\mathbb{C}^{3})

such that there exists a limit

r:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}r^{\varepsilon}\mbox{ in }{\rm C}^{-\kappa}({{\mathbb{T}}^{2}})\quad\text{for every $\omega\in\Omega_{\delta}$.}

Moreover, the convergence holds with the following rate:

(1.7)

\displaystyle\|r^{\varepsilon}-r\|_{{\rm C}^{-\kappa}({{\mathbb{T}}^{2}})}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\frac{\kappa}{8}}\quad\text{uniformly in}\;\;\omega\in\Omega_{\delta}.

Definition 1.4
.

By a solution
r^{\varepsilon}=(r^{\varepsilon}_{1},r^{\varepsilon}_{2},r^{\varepsilon}_{3})

to system (
mCR
) we understand a classical solution.
By a solution to (
CR
) we understand a limit of
r^{\varepsilon}
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
in some sense.

In other words, Theorem
1.3
can be rephrased in the following way.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and
let
{\mathscr{W}}=({\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{1}},{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{2}},{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{3}})
be a 3D white noise on
{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}
such that
{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{3}}

has zero mean over
{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}
. Then, equation (
CR
) has a solution.

Remark 1.6
.

Naturally,
instead of assuming that that the third component
{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{3}}

has zero mean over
{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}
, we could alternatively assume that the first

{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{1}}
or the second component
{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}_{2}}
has zero mean over
{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}
.

2.
Definitions and notations

In what follows, we denote by
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}:=[-\pi,\pi]^{d}/\!\sim

(where
\sim
is an equivalence relation that identifies opposite faces of the hypercube
[-\pi,\pi]^{d}
),
the
d
-dimensional torus which we will treat as a compact Riemannian manifold and a topological group. By
0
we will denote the corresponding zero element of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
.

By
\mathscr{D}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
we denote the space of
C^{\infty}
-class functions on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and by
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
the space of distributions on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
, i.e. the topological dual of
\mathscr{D}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
. Note that the space
\mathscr{D}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
, also called the space of test functions, is a
Fréchet space endowed with the naturally defined countable family of seminorms.
If
U
is an open subset of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
, then by
\mathscr{D}(U)
we denote the class of those test functions whose support is contained in
U
. Similarly, by
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(U)
we
denote the topological dual of
\mathscr{D}(U)
.

For a test function
\varphi\in\mathscr{D}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
,
\lambda>0
and
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
,
we define the rescaled centered function
\varphi^{\lambda}_{x}
as follows:

\displaystyle\varphi^{\lambda}_{x}(y):=\frac{1}{\lambda^{d}}\,\varphi\Big(\frac{y-x}{\lambda}\Big).

Furthermore, we write
\varphi_{x}
instead of
\varphi^{1}_{x}
.

Let
r\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Define the classes
of test functions

(2.1)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}^{r}:=\{\varphi\in\mathscr{D}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}):\|\partial^{k}\varphi\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1,\;\text{for all}\;0\leqslant|k|\leqslant r\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}_{\alpha}:=\{\varphi\in\mathscr{D}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}):\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\varphi(z)z^{k}dz=0,\;\text{for all}\;0\leqslant|k|\leqslant\alpha\},\,\mbox{ if }\alpha\geqslant 0,

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}^{r}_{\alpha}:=\mathscr{B}^{r}\cap\mathscr{B}_{\alpha},\,\mbox{ if }\alpha\geqslant 0,

where
k=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})
is a multiindex and if
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{d})\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
,

z^{k}:=z_{1}^{k_{1}}\dots z_{d}^{k_{d}}
.

Remark 2.1
.

Note that our definitions of
\mathscr{B}^{r}
,
\mathscr{B}_{\alpha}
, and
\mathscr{B}^{r}_{\alpha}
differ from those in
[
2
, Definition A.9]
, as we have taken into account the periodicity of our problem.

Define the dyadic interval

\displaystyle[0,1]_{\mathbb{D}}:=\{2^{-n},\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}.

Definition 2.2

(Periodic Hölder-Zygmund spaces
\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
)
.

Assume that
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. If
\alpha<0
, we denote by
r=[-\alpha+1]
the smallest positive integer
r>-\alpha
.
We define a “pseudo-norm”
|f|_{\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}}
for
f\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
as follows.

If
\alpha\geqslant 0
, then we put

(2.2)

\displaystyle|f|_{\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}}:=\sup_{\psi\in\mathscr{B}^{0}}|f(\psi)|+\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\!,\,\lambda\in[0,1]_{\mathbb{D}},\\
\varphi\in\mathscr{B}^{0}_{\alpha}\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(\varphi^{\lambda}_{x})|}{\lambda^{\alpha}}.

If
\alpha<0
, then, with
r=[-\alpha+1]
, we put

(2.3)

\displaystyle|f|_{\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\,\lambda\in[0,1]_{\mathbb{D}},\\
\varphi\in\mathscr{B}^{r}\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(\varphi^{\lambda}_{x})|}{\lambda^{\alpha}}.

We define the periodic Hölder-Zygmund space

\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}:=\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
as the space of distributions
f\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})

for which
|f|_{\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}}<\infty
.

Remark 2.3
.

By Lemma
C.7
, Definition
2.2
agrees
with the classical definition of a Hölder-Zygmund
space in the periodic setting (see Remark
C.6
)
only if
\alpha\notin{\mathbb{N}}
.
For
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
defines a distinct space,
which is not utilized in our analysis.

Remark 2.4
.

It is known that classical Hölder-Zygmund spaces are
not separable and that the space
C^{\infty}
is not dense
in them, see
[
17
]
. For this reason, we define so called
little Hölder-Zygmund spaces
{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}
.

Definition 2.5

(Periodic little Hölder-Zygmund spaces
{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
)
.

We define a periodic little Hölder-Zygmund space
{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}:={\scriptstyle\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})

as the closure of
{\rm C}^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
in
\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
. We furthermore
denote by
|\cdot|_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the restriction
of the norm (
2.2
), for
\alpha\geqslant 0
, and (
2.3
),
for
\alpha<0
, to
{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
.

Remark 2.6
.

It is important to notice that by Lemma
B.3
,
a Gaussian white noise
{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}}
on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
,
as well as its convolutions
K\ast{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}}
and
G\ast{\scriptstyle{\mathscr{W}}}

(see Definition
2.9
),
are elements of little Hölder-Zygmund spaces. Furthermore,
by Lemma
E.1
,
Young products are well defined (specifically, uniquely defined)
when they are restricted to little Hölder-Zygmund spaces.
We also remark that Young products are not uniquely defined
in classical Hölder-Zygmund spaces,
see
[
6
, Remark 14.2]
.
For these reasons, little Hölder-Zygmund spaces is a natural choice
for our analysis.

Remark 2.7
.

For simplicity of notation,
we often write
{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}
,
\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}
,
{\rm C}^{\alpha}
, and
{\rm C}^{\infty}

instead of
{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
,
\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
,

{\rm C}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
, and
{\rm C}^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
, respectively.

If
f
is
{\mathbb{C}}
-valued, then we set

|f|_{\alpha}^{2}:=|{\rm Re\,}f|^{2}_{\alpha}+|{\rm Im\,}f|^{2}_{\alpha}
.
The above definitions are for
{\mathbb{C}}
-valued distributions. If
f
is a
Y
-valued distribution,
where
Y
is an
n
-dimensional Euclidean space over
{\mathbb{C}}
, i.e.,
if
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
, where
f_{k}
are
{\mathbb{C}}
-valued, then
we define the norm

(2.4)

\displaystyle\|f\|^{2}_{\alpha}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}|f_{k}|^{2}_{\alpha}.

Remark that for
Y
-valued distributions, we use the notation

\|\cdot\|_{\alpha}
, rather than
|\cdot|_{\alpha}
, for respective Hölder-Zygmund norms.

Remark 2.8
.

In
[
2
, Definition A.9]
, the Hölder-Zygmund norms
are defined with respect to sets of test functions supported
on unit balls centered in the origin.
In Lemma
C.7
, we show that
both definitions are equivalent for
\alpha\notin{\mathbb{N}}
, that is, we can equivalently define
the Hölder-Zygmund norms as in Definition
C.3
.

Definition 2.9

(Green function for
\partial_{\bar{z}}
)
.

Let
K
be the Green function for the Laplacian on
{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}
.
We define

(2.5)

\displaystyle G:=2\partial_{z}K=\partial_{1}K-i\partial_{2}K.

We call
G
the Green function for the operator
-2\partial_{\bar{z}}
. Therefore,

-2G
is the Green function for the Cauchy-Riemann operator
\partial_{\bar{z}}
.
We also put

G_{1}:=\partial_{1}K\mbox{ and }G_{2}:=\partial_{2}K.

3.
Solving problem (
mCR
)

3.1.
Mild formulation of problem (
mCR
)

Let the function
G
be given by (
2.5
).
We define

(3.1)

\displaystyle|f|_{\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}}\lesssim|f|_{\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}}.

Let us prove the inverse inequality. Let
f\in\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}
,

\lambda\in(0,1]
, and let
\lambda_{n+1}=2^{-(n+1)}
,
\lambda_{n}=2^{-n}
,

n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, be two consecutive points from
[0,1]_{\mathbb{D}}

with the property

\displaystyle\lambda_{n+1}<\lambda\leqslant\lambda_{n}.

Note that
\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\lambda}<\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\lambda_{n+1}}=2
.

Let
\alpha<0
,
r=[-\alpha+1]
, and
\varphi\in\tilde{\mathscr{B}}^{r}
. We have

\displaystyle\frac{|f(\varphi^{\lambda}_{x})|}{\lambda^{\alpha}}\leqslant\Big(\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\lambda}\Big)^{\!d}\,\frac{|f(\psi^{\lambda_{n}}_{x})|}{\lambda_{n}^{\alpha}}<2^{d}\,\frac{|f(\psi^{\lambda_{n}}_{x})|}{\lambda_{n}^{\alpha}},\quad\text{where}\;\;\psi(y)=\varphi\Big(\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\lambda}y\Big).

Note that since
1\leqslant\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\lambda}<2
,

\displaystyle{\rm supp\,}\psi\subset B_{1}(0)\subset[-\pi,\pi]^{d}\quad\text{and}\quad\|D^{k}\psi\|_{\infty}\leqslant 2^{|k|}\|D^{k}\varphi\|_{\infty}\leqslant 2^{r}.

Therefore,

(C.4)

\displaystyle|f|_{\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}}\lesssim|f|_{\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}}.

Now let
\alpha\geqslant 0
and
\varphi\in\tilde{\mathscr{B}}^{0}_{\alpha}
.
We have

\displaystyle\frac{|f(\varphi^{\lambda}_{x})|}{\lambda^{\alpha}}\leqslant\Big(\frac{\lambda_{n+1}}{\lambda}\Big)^{\!d}\,\frac{|f(\psi^{\lambda_{n+1}}_{x})|}{\lambda_{n+1}^{\alpha}}<\frac{|f(\psi^{\lambda_{n+1}}_{x})|}{\lambda_{n+1}^{\alpha}},\qquad\text{where}\;\;\psi(y)=\varphi\Big(\frac{\lambda_{n+1}}{\lambda}y\Big).

Note that since
\frac{1}{2}<\frac{\lambda_{n+1}}{\lambda}\leqslant 1
,

\displaystyle{\rm supp\,}\psi\subset B_{2}(0)\subset[-\pi,\pi]^{d}\quad\text{and}\quad\|\psi\|_{\infty}=\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1.

Furthermore,
\psi
annihilates monomials of order up to

[\alpha]
, as
\varphi
does.
The evaluation of the first term in (
C.1
)
(for
x=0
) is straightforward.
Thus, we obtained (
C.4
) for
\alpha\geqslant 0
.
∎

C.7
.

Corollary C.8
.

It holds that for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\mathscr{Z}^{n}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\subset\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{n}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\quad\text{and}\quad|\cdot|_{\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{n}}\lesssim|\cdot|_{\mathscr{Z}^{n}}.

Lemma C.9
.

Suppose
\eta\in\mathscr{Z}^{-\kappa}
,
\kappa>0
. Then,

\displaystyle|\eta^{\varepsilon}|_{\mathscr{Z}^{-\kappa}}\leqslant|\eta|_{\mathscr{Z}^{-\kappa}},\quad\text{where}\quad\eta^{\varepsilon}:=\rho_{\varepsilon}\ast\eta.

Proof.

For the function

(\eta^{\bar{\varepsilon}})^{\varepsilon}:=\rho_{\varepsilon}\ast\eta^{\bar{\varepsilon}}
, we have

(\eta^{\bar{\varepsilon}})^{\varepsilon}(\varphi^{\lambda}_{x})=\int\eta^{\bar{\varepsilon}}(z-y)\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)\varphi^{\lambda}_{x}(z)dydz=\int\eta^{\bar{\varepsilon}}(z)\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)\varphi^{\lambda}_{x}(z+y)dydz\\
=\int\eta^{\bar{\varepsilon}}(\varphi^{\lambda}_{x-y})\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)dy.

Passing to the limit as
\bar{\varepsilon}\to 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\eta^{\varepsilon}(\varphi^{\lambda}_{x})=\int\eta(\varphi^{\lambda}_{x-y})\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)dy.

We have

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\,\lambda\in[0,1]_{\mathbb{D}},\\
\varphi\in\mathscr{B}^{[\kappa+1]}\end{subarray}}|\eta^{\varepsilon}(\varphi^{\lambda}_{x})|\leqslant\int\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\,\lambda\in[0,1]_{\mathbb{D}},\\
\varphi\in\mathscr{B}^{[\kappa+1]}\end{subarray}}|\eta(\varphi^{\lambda}_{x-y})|\rho_{\varepsilon}(y)dy\leqslant\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\,\lambda\in[0,1]_{\mathbb{D}},\\
\varphi\in\mathscr{B}^{[\kappa+1]}\end{subarray}}|\eta(\varphi^{\lambda}_{x})|,

where the latter inequality is true by the periodicity of
\varphi^{\lambda}_{x}
.
∎

Lemma C.10
.

Let
\kappa>0
and
\eta\in\mathscr{Z}^{-\kappa}
. Then,

\displaystyle|\partial_{i}\eta|_{\mathscr{Z}^{-\kappa-1}}\leqslant|\eta|_{\mathscr{Z}^{-\kappa}}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle|\partial_{i}\eta|_{\mathscr{Z}^{-\kappa-1}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\,\lambda\in[0,1]_{\mathbb{D}},\\
\varphi\in\mathscr{B}^{r},\,r=[2+\kappa]\end{subarray}}\frac{|\partial_{i}\eta(\varphi^{\lambda}_{x})|}{\lambda^{-1-\kappa}}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\,\lambda\in[0,1]_{\mathbb{D}},\\
\varphi\in\mathscr{B}^{r},\,r=[2+\kappa]\end{subarray}}\frac{|\eta(\partial_{i}\varphi^{\lambda}_{x})|}{\lambda^{-\kappa}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\,\lambda\in[0,1]_{\mathbb{D}},\\

Proposition C.11
.

Let
\alpha<0
.
The Hölder-Zygmund spaces
\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})

can be equivalently defined with the use of any integer

r>[-\alpha+1]
, that is, as the space of distributions

f\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
with the finite norm

(C.5)

\displaystyle|f|_{\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}}^{(r)}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\,\lambda\in[0,1]_{\mathbb{D}},\\
\varphi\in\mathscr{B}^{r}\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(\varphi^{\lambda}_{x})|}{\lambda^{\alpha}},\quad\text{where}\;\;r>[-\alpha+1],\;r\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Furthermore, the norms
|\cdot|_{\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}}^{(r)}
and
|\cdot|_{\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}}

are equivalent; in particular,
the norms
|\cdot|_{\alpha}
and
|\cdot|^{(r)}_{\alpha}
are equivalent, where
the latter is the restriction of
|\cdot|_{\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}}^{(r)}

to
{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
.

Proof.

Let
r>[-\alpha+1],\;r\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Define

(C.6)

\displaystyle|f|_{\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}}^{(r)}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\,\lambda\in(0,1],\\
\varphi\in\tilde{\mathscr{B}}^{r}\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(\varphi^{\lambda}_{x})|}{\lambda^{\alpha}}.

The equivalence of the norms (
C.5
) and (
C.6
)
follows by exactly the same argument as in the proof
of Lemma
C.7
. Let us prove the equivalence of

|\cdot|_{\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}}^{(r)}
and
|\cdot|_{\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}}
.

Since
\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})=\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap\mathscr{D}^{\prime}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
,
see Remark
C.6
, by
[
14
, Lemma 14.13]
,

|\cdot|_{\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}}^{(r)}
and
|\cdot|_{\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}}

define the same Banach space.
Note that

\displaystyle|f|_{\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}}^{(r)}\leqslant|f|_{\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}},\quad f\in\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha},

since the supremum in the definition of
|f|_{\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}}
is over a larger set.
Since the norms define the same Banach space, their equivalence
is a consequence of the open mapping theorem applied to the bounded linear operator

\displaystyle J:\,(\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha},|\cdot|_{\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}})\to(\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha},|\cdot|_{\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}}^{(r)}),\quad f\mapsto f.

Since, by Lemma
C.7

|\cdot|_{\tilde{\mathscr{Z}}^{\alpha}}

and
|\cdot|_{\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}}

the equivalence of the norms
|\cdot|_{\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}}
and

|\cdot|_{\mathscr{Z}^{\alpha}}^{(r)}
.
∎

Lemma C.12

C.6

Let us show the equivalence of the norms in

and
{\rm C}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})

If

Lemma C.13
.

\displaystyle|f|_{\mathscr{Z}^{-\kappa}}=
