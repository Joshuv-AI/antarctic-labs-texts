Obstacles to effective communication between physical/occupational therapists and patients in the Saudi Arabian context: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Turki Aljuhani, Abrar M Al-Mutairi, Abdullah Alfraih, Mohammed Alharbi, Turki AlAmri, Ali Alahmari, Abdulaziz Alotaibi, Mohammed Senitan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective communication between healthcare providers and patients is essential for high-quality care and patient satisfaction. Barriers to communication can lead to misunderstandings and poorer health outcomes. This study aims to identify communication obstacles between physical/occupational therapists (PTs/OTs) and patients in the Saudi Arabian context.MethodsThis cross-sectional study, which was conducted from September 2022 to January 2023, involved 228 patients and 46 therapists from the rehabilitation services department. Data were collected via two self-administered questionnaires and analyzed via Pearson’s chi-square test of independence and Fisher’s exact test for categorical nominal data to assess associations between variables. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsMost patients (25.4%) were young adults aged 18–30 years, predominantly Saudi nationals (96.5%), with undergraduate degrees (63%). High satisfaction with therapists was reported by 69.3% of patients. The key barriers identified included limited consultation time (15.8%) and the use of medical records (4.8%). Therapists, primarily young professionals aged 25–30 years (54.3%) with 3–5 years of experience (43.5%), also reported time limitations (30.4%), physical setup issues (37.0%), and poor patient history-taking (56.5%) as significant barriers. Additional findings from our study indicated that monolingual Arabic-speaking therapists faced greater challenges in understanding language (p = 0.02), and younger therapists (aged 25–30) reported greater difficulties in managing multiple problems during sessions (p = 0.03), highlighting age and linguistic background as significant factors influencing communication efficacy.ConclusionsAddressing communication barriers through targeted interventions, such as enhanced training, improved clinical structures, and advanced electronic health records, can improve patient satisfaction and treatment outcomes. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies and specific communication training programs.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-12077-7.Keywords: Communication barriers, Physical therapists, Occupational therapists, Patient satisfaction, Rehabilitation, Saudi Arabia, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Effective communication between health care providers plays an important role in health care practice. Communication gaps is known to lead to numerous negative outcomes in health quality and outcomes. This lack of communication between health care providers is a major contributor to increasing the risk of adverse events in patients [1, 2], thus leading to extended hospital stays and increased hospital spending [3, 4]. In addition, this lack of communication is linked with low patient satisfaction in the care that is provided, thus increasing stress for both health care providers and patients [5–8]. In fact, effective communication between health care providers is key in patients’ pain detection/management and fall prevention [9, 10].

Numerous recent studies have highlighted the benefits of effective communication between patients and healthcare providers, emphasizing its crucial role in improving patient outcomes and overall healthcare quality. For example, effective communication enhances patient adherence to treatment plans, as patients who understand their diagnoses and treatment regimens are more likely to follow medical advice [11, 12]. Studies have demonstrated that clear and empathetic communication significantly reduces patient anxiety and stress, leading to better mental health outcomes [13]. Additionally, patients who experience good communication with their healthcare providers report higher levels of satisfaction and trust in their care [14, 15]. This trust is particularly important in chronic disease management, where ongoing patient–provider interactions are essential for successful disease control and patient compliance [16].

Effective communication has also been linked to improved pain management, as patients are more likely to accurately report their pain levels and symptoms when they feel understood and supported by their healthcare providers [17]. Similarly, in pediatric care, studies have shown that parents' understanding of and involvement in their child's treatment significantly improve clinical outcomes and parent satisfaction [18]. Overall, the evidence underscores that fostering effective communication is fundamental to achieving high-quality, patient-centered care.

A cross-sectional study was conducted in Dubai, UAE, to assess the communication barriers between family physicians and patients in walk-in centers. The time limitations were ranked highly from both the physicians' and patients' points of view. This might be attributed to the fact that the study was conducted in a walk-in center. From the physicians' perspective, poor compliance with treatment and poor medical history were the major barriers. From the patient side, the most significant issues were a lack of education on their condition and not verifying their understanding. However, preoccupation with medical records was not considered a significant barrier. Parallel findings were reported in a similar study [19]. Both doctors and patients considered language to be a minimal barrier. This might be due to the multicultural background of the family physicians in Dubai.

While the aforementioned research provides valuable insights into communication barriers in walk-in centers in Dubai, our study ventures into relatively uncharted territory. Specifically, we shift our focus to the communication dynamics between PTs and OTs and their patients in a rehabilitation setting. While many studies explore the communication barriers between physicians and patients, few studies have investigated the barriers in communication between rehabilitation teams and patients. Rehabilitation team (PTs and OTs) communication with patients is unique and different, as it requires more demonstrations and understanding of patients’ daily livening and habits, which are very individualized. Thus, the communication between patients and the rehabilitation team is quite unique and different and can provide different inside to the impact of communication in patients’ health in general. To our knowledge, this is the first study of its kind to be conducted in the KSA. However, few studies have addressed the barriers to effective communication among rehabilitation practitioners, especially PTs and OT therapists, with their patients to overcome these barriers in practice for better quality care in the literature, especially in Gulf Cooperation Council countries [20, 21]. In addition, the results from the study can direct recourses in overcoming communication barriers and significantly improve the rehabilitation services.

This study aims to identify an initial impression and outline the barriers that negatively affect effective communication between rehabilitation practitioners (PTs/OTs) and their patients from both perspectives in Saudi Arabia by using two self-administered questionnaires. The primary aimed of this study is to identify the perceived barriers to effective communication between patients and PT/OT from both perspectives. Additionally, the study aimed to evaluates the patients’ satisfaction with the rehabilitation services that been provided.

Subjects and methods

Study setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted from September 2022 to January 2023 in the Rehabilitation Services Department at King Abdulaziz Medical City-Riyadh (KAMC-RD). The study included on both outpatient and inpatients receiving rehabilitation care. The questionnaires were distributed to OT and PT therapists, who then provided these questionnaires to their patients prior to discharge. This approach enabled a comprehensive assessment of communication barriers from both the therapist’s and patient’s perspectives within a real-world clinical environment. Importantly, the survey was only given to patients in the last session prior to discharge.

Sampling and sample technique

The sample size for this study was calculated via Epi Info™ v7.2, a trademark of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), which is based on a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. The required sample size was determined to be 385 patients and 67 rehabilitation practitioners. This required sample size was calculated using the average number of outpatients per month and the total number of rehabilitation practitioners within the KAMC-RS setting. Convenience sampling was employed to obtain target simple. For patient participants, the inclusion criteria were adult patients aged 18 years or older who were conscious and fully oriented, who received rehabilitation services either as inpatients or outpatients at KAMC-RD, and who were literate in either Arabic or English. The exclusion criteria included patients who are unable to communicate, patients with poor cognitive and memory abilities. For the rehabilitation specialists, the study included OT/PT practitioners actively practicing in KAMC-RS. This criterion ensured that participants had direct experience with patient care thus, ensuring a diverse and comprehensive assessment of communication barriers from the practitioners' perspective.

Study design

The study utilized a cross-sectional design. By employing two self-administered questionnaires, this research aimed to identify and analyze the perceived barriers to effective communication from both the patients' and the therapists' perspectives. This design allowed for the collection of data at a single point in time, providing a snapshot of the current communication challenges within the rehabilitation setting.

Tools for data collection

The study employed two self-administered questionnaires to assess communication barriers. One questionnaire targets patients' perceived frequency of encountering communication obstacles, whereas the other focuses on physical/occupational therapists' (PT/OT) perceptions of the risk posed by these barriers to effective communication.

The patients' questionnaire and the therapists' questionnaire were primarily based on Part C of the Doctor–Patient Communication Needs Assessment Scale, with additional items incorporated from various sources to better suit the local culture. Both questionnaires were translated into Arabic and piloted by Albahri et al. (2018) in their study on communication barriers in a walk-in center setting in Dubai. We obtained permission from the original authors to use and adapt these questionnaires for our study [19]. For this study, minimum adjustments were made to professional-level titles to match the context of PT/OT professionals:

The senior residents were changed to PT/OT technicians.

The senior residents were changed to PT/OT technicians.

Specialist registrars were changed to junior PTs/OTs.

In the patients' questionnaire, respondents are asked to rate the frequency of encountering each communication barrier on a five-point scale ranging from "never" to "always." The therapists' questionnaire, on the other hand, requires PTs and OTs to evaluate each barrier from two perspectives: frequency of encounter and perceived seriousness, both of which are rated on five-point scales. The frequency scale ranges from "never" to "always," whereas the seriousness scale ranges from "not at all serious" to "extremely serious."

To assess the perceived qualitative level of risk, a risk stratification score is generated by multiplying the frequency score by the seriousness score, resulting in a possible range of 1–25. This score is then used to categorize barriers into five qualitative levels of risk, from very low to very high, as summarized in a frequency‒impact matrix. The frequency‒impact matrix visually represents the combined frequency and seriousness scores, highlighting the overall risk level associated with each barrier (Fig. 1) [19].

Statistical analysis

The data were entered into an Excel® sheet and subsequently analyzed via SPSS software, version 28. Pearson’s chi-square test of independence was employed to assess associations between various variables, including patients' gender, nationality, and first language against potential barriers to communication in the linguistic and cultural factors domain, as well as the associations between therapists’ gender and nationality. Additionally, we examined the associations between therapists’ professional level and communication skills training against barriers related to time limitations and multiple problem presentations, as well as the associations between patients’ gender and age (aged 18–30, and above 30) and the barriers to communication with therapists. In cases involving 2 × 2 contingency tables, Fisher’s exact test with a two-tailed p-value was applied, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. The data were stored on a single computer, ensuring confidentiality and anonymity through the use of unidentifiable information.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted following stringent ethical guidelines to ensure the protection of participants' rights and well-being. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (IRB approval number: IRBC/0937/22), ensuring that the study adhered to all ethical standards required for research involving human subjects. Personal identifiers were not collected, and all the data were anonymized before analysis. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before their involvement in the study, ensuring that they were fully aware of the study's purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits.

When examining the rank of the communication barriers identified by the therapists, most therapists ranked the barriers as moderate risk (3). All barriers related to organizational factors were ranked as moderate risk barriers. In the communication of information domain, only lack of shared management was ranked as low risk (2), whereas the others were ranked as moderate risk. Similarities and discrepancies between perceived communication barriers from patients’ and therapists’ results

The patients’ perceived barriers of communication were not having aid tools, limited time during consultation, not having a therapist of the same gender, provide large amount of information, and not having a therapist of the same culture. Yet on the other hand, therapists’ perceived barriers of communication were patient not following with treatment plan, inconsistent information provided by patient, and patient lack interest in self-care (unable to follow the instructions). Almost all the results suggest a different of perceived barriers in communication between patients and therapists. Interestingly, while patients reported lack of consultation time, therapists ranked not patients not following the treatment plan as the highest barrier. This showed that while, perceived barriers are different between the two groups the ability to overcome the barriers can be implemented when understood. Moreover, of the four domains the communication of information domain had the most frequent barriers with a total of five barriers: two perceived by the patients and three by the therapists.

Association analysis

The Fisher Exact Test was used to examine the associations between therapists' age, first language, years of experience, and professional level against barriers including difficulty understanding language, unfamiliarity with cultural alternatives, time limitations, presentation of many problems, and inadequate translation by interpreters. There was a significant relationship between therapists' first language and their difficulties in language understanding, with monolingual Arabic speakers reporting significantly more challenges (p = 0.02) (Appendix 1 Table S1). Time limitation was associated signifyingly in therapists’ preceptive with the use of second language (i.e. Arabic and or English) when communicating with patients (p = 0.001) (Appendix 1 Table S3). Similarly, age is another factor that is significantly related to therapists' ability to handle multiple problems in their presentations, with younger therapists (ages 25–30) experiencing more difficulties (p = 0.03) (Appendix 1 Table S4). Additionally, gender also plays a role in communication barriers between patients and therapists, although it is minimal overall, with the notable exception of "Pressurizing for decisions," where male patients report significantly more issues (p = 0.04) (Appendix 2 Table S7).

Discussion

This study identifies obstacles to communication between therapists and patients in a Saudi setting to improve outcomes and patient satisfaction. Research shows that better communication enhances patient satisfaction and treatment outcomes [22]. Our findings showed that patients perceived limited consultation time and the use of medical records during the therapy sessions as barriers to communicate with the PT/OT therapist. These barriers illustrate and suggest a structural challenges within the clinical settings. Effective communication between healthcare providers and patients is essential for high-quality care and patient satisfaction. Barriers can lead to misunderstandings, reduced adherence, and poorer outcomes. In physical and occupational therapy, these barriers can significantly impact care effectiveness. Physical and occupational therapy really on building a good rapport with the patients and require effective communication to understand the patient’s symptoms to address them with the right goals.

A significant portion of the patient population consisted of young adults aged 18–30 years, with the majority being Saudi nationals with undergraduate degrees. Notably, a high percentage of patients expressed satisfaction with their therapists, with 69.3% reporting being ‘very satisfied’. One suggested reason for the high percentage of young adults within our patients’ simple is that our study inclusion criteria of relatively healthier sample. Moreover, this simple of patients as young adult who may have fewer co-morbidity, and precautions which, can explain the high percentage of ‘very satisfied’ with the rehabilitation sessions results. Nevertheless, the study findings regarding patients perceived barriers to communication give an inside to identify the barriers and seek improvement in rehabilitation services.

In addition, our current finding show that most of the therapists were young professionals aged 25–30 years, who had 3–5 years of experience, and predominantly held junior positions While these therapists generally reported satisfactory levels of job performance and communication skills, the findings highlight the need for targeted improvements in organizational structures and enhanced training for early-career therapists to address these communication barriers effectively. In addition, from the therapists’ perspective, most of the communication barriers with the patients were ranked as moderate risk, identifying an issue that should be addressed to avoid further threats.

A study conducted by Albahri et al. (2018) in Dubai about barriers to effective communication in primary healthcare revealed several parallels and differences with our findings. The Dubai study also identified time limitations as the most significant barrier, with 23.4% of patients frequently encountering them and 50.6% of physicians perceiving them as moderate to very high risk. This aligns closely with our findings, where 15.8% of patients reported limited consultation time as a frequent barrier. Both studies highlighted issues such as patients presenting multiple problems, which were perceived as a significant barrier by 51.2% of the physicians in Dubai and 50% of the therapists in our study. Additionally, patient nonadherence to treatment plans was a major concern, noted by 35.9% of the physicians in Dubai and 67.4% of the therapists in our setting [19].

Preoccupation with medical records was a lesser concern for patients in both studies, with 12.5% in Dubai and 4.8% in our study reporting it as a frequent issue. Organizational factors, such as the physical setup of the consultation room, were viewed as significant barriers by 47.8% of the therapists in our study, reflecting similar challenges faced in Dubai. Furthermore, the therapists in our study identified poor patient history-taking 56.5% and nonadherence to treatment plans 67.4% as high-risk barriers, mirroring the findings from Dubai, where poor adherence to treatment plans was also a significant issue.

Another study by Lovell and colleagues (2012) investigated interpersonal factors affecting communication in clinical consultations from the perspective of Canadian physicians. This study revealed that communication barriers vary significantly according to patient age, specialty, and other interpersonal factors. Physicians reported more difficulties with younger patients and those using culturally based alternative medicine, while issues related to diagnosis and its explanation were more prominent than in our study. These differences highlight the diverse challenges faced by healthcare providers in different contexts [22].

These comparisons underscore the critical need for improving communication strategies in healthcare settings to enhance patient‒therapist interactions and patient care outcomes. Both studies emphasize addressing time constraints, improving organizational structures, and enhancing training for healthcare providers in effective communication can lead to better patient satisfaction and adherence to treatment plans. Furthermore, both studies reported time limitation in consultation, non-adherence to treatment plan, and organizational factors related to physical set up and availability of tools. Time limitation is one of the most cited barriers in communication the results when intenerated in both contexts may suggest lack of time and busy schedule for the healthcare provided. Non-adherence to treatment plan can be explain in term of culture and lack of awareness from patients’ side about the significations of treatment plan. Lastly, physical set up and lack of tools in the healthcare settings seems to be a potential problem need further exploration [23].

The findings of the present study revealed that therapists' first language significantly impacts their difficulty understanding language, with monolingual Arabic speakers experiencing greater challenges. Additionally, age was significantly associated with the ability to manage multiple problems during sessions, with younger therapists reporting greater difficulty. Time limitations were also found to be a significant issue for therapists whose first language was Arabic. However, other potential communication barriers, such as inadequate translation services, unfamiliarity with cultural alternatives, and professional experience levels, had no statistically significant effect on communication efficacy (p-values ranging from 0.27–0.95). From the patient perspective, gender differences were minimal, although males reported feeling more pressured to make decisions. Overall, these insights highlight the critical influence of linguistic and age factors on communication barriers, which may guide targeted improvements in training and policy adjustments within therapeutic settings in Saudi Arabia. It is worth noting that monolingual Arabic speakers with therapist is viewed as a challenge. A suggested explanation for this challenge is that while, therapists speak Arabic with their patients they most often communicate in English with each other and learn in English in most of the provided courses.

Few studies mostly qualitative in nature examine the barriers and facilitators affecting rehabilitation services [24–26]. Two studies, found that organizational barriers, lack of knowledge about the role of the therapist, and insufficient satisfactions. Maximizing the role of multidisciplinary teamwork, apprenticeship schemes and university engagement in provided training courses can help overcome the barriers [24, 25].

This research provides valuable insights into the specific barriers to communication between physical/occupational therapists and patients in a Saudi healthcare setting. It emphasizes the critical role of organizational factors and cultural and linguistic differences in shaping communication efficacy. The findings of this study illustrate the need to enhance therapists’ communication skills and provide therapists with the necessary support and education to overcome the barriers of communication with patients. Possible suggested solution to overcome communication barriers can be via organizational structures such as providing mentorship programs to junior therapists and provided time to attend training. In addition, training sessions such as peer to peer training, effective communication training can be utilized in the developmental plan for all therapists [24].

Future research should explore interventions aimed at mitigating identified barriers, such as implementing advanced electronic health records systems and enhancing therapist training programs. Longitudinal studies could provide deeper insights into how these barriers evolve over time and their long-term impact on patient satisfaction and health outcomes. Additionally, research into the specific communication training needs of early-career therapists could inform targeted professional development initiatives.

Study limitations

The limitations of this study include its cross-sectional design, which restricts the ability to infer causality between identified communication barriers and patient outcomes. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the potential for response bias, as participants may not accurately recall or may selectively report their experiences. Another significant limitation is the limited availability of comparable studies, as this investigation is among the first to specifically examine communication barriers between physical/occupational therapists and patients in the Saudi healthcare context. The study’s small simple size is another limitation, the small simple size made it difficult to generalize our findings. Lastly, the study's single-center approach may limit the generalizability of the results, as the findings may not fully represent the experiences of therapists and patients in other healthcare settings across Saudi Arabia.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides critical insights into the barriers to effective communication between physical/occupational therapists and patients. The key barriers identified include limited consultation time, structural issues related to the clinic environment, and challenges with patient adherence to treatment plans. The findings emphasize the need for targeted interventions to address these barriers, such as enhancing communication training for therapists, improving clinic organizational structures, and integrating advanced electronic health records systems. Despite the study's limitations, including its cross-sectional design and reliance on self-reported data, the research underscores the importance of effective communication in improving patient outcomes and satisfaction. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies and the development of specific communication training programs to further enhance the quality of care in rehabilitation settings.

Recommendations

On the basis of the study results, extending consultation times for patients who have comorbidities and optimizing the physical setup of consultation rooms to address time limitations and environmental barriers are recommended. Continuous communication skills training for therapists, incorporating visual aids, and ensuring cultural competency can enhance patient interactions. Balancing the use of technology, implementing feedback mechanisms, and using qualified interpreters will help mitigate organizational and linguistic barriers. The development of structured patient education programs and the fostering of strong therapeutic relationships through empathy and genuine interest are crucial to improving patients’ understanding of and adherence to treatment plans.
