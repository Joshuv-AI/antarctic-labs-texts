The Polymatroid Representation of a Greedoid, and Associated Galois Connections
Robert P. Streit, Vijay K. Garg

Abstract
A greedoid is a generalization of a matroid allowing for more flexible analyses and modeling of combinatorial optimization problems. However, these structures decimate many matroid properties contributing to their pervasive nature. A polymatroid greedoid [ 31 ] presents an interesting middle ground, so we further develop this class. First we prove every local poset greedoid for which the greedy algorithm correctly solves linear optimizations over its basic words must have a polymatroid representation. For this, we use relationships between the lattices of greedoid flats and closed sets of a polymatroid to generalize concepts in [ 31 ] . Then, we show our generalization is defined by a Galois connection between the greedoid flats and closed sets of a representation. Finally, we apply this duality to identify a subclass of polymatroid greedoids with favorable properties, which we call strong polymatroid greedoids . As technical tools for our analyses, we introduce optimism and the Forking Lemma for interval greedoids. Both are pervasive in our work, and are of independent interest.

Introduction

Simple algorithms are good, correct algorithms are better, and the harmony intersecting these two is best.
As a result, the greedy algorithm has inspired many axiomatic approaches towards studying when it “works.”
One example is
greedoids
, which are language generalizations of
matroids
.
Specifically, the Edmonds-Rado Theorem
[
14
,
37
]
says the greedy algorithm correctly optimizes any choice of linear function over a downward closed set family if and only if the family is a matroid.
Yet, the greedy algorithm is successful in many settings not captured by this statement.
Postulating the essence of this result lies in a matroid’s
exchange
property, Korte and Lovász propose greedoids in
[
29
]
.
But, such generalization gives up properties elevating matroids to their mathematical ubiquity, so greedoids do not enjoy as prominent a position in combinatorial optimization as matroids.
Still, greedoids show great potential.
Recent works give new applications to network reliability
[
41
]
and generalized linear programs
[
26
]
.
And more abstractly, there is opportunity to use greedoids to model complex structures in discrete programs, possibly to generalize matroids’ role in submodular and discrete convex analysis.
But this requires more basic insights.

Polymatroid greedoids
, whose feasibility structure is encoded by polymatroids we call
representations
, present a middle ground between greedoids and matroids.
So, we develop this class.

First, we generalize the approach from
[
31
]
used to initially define polymatroid greedoids.
Though Korte and Lovász restrict their attention to integral polymatroid rank functions, we require more flexibility.
So, we introduce
aligned representations
satisfying axioms we call
alignment
, which asserts relationships between a greedoid’s flats and a polymatroid’s closed sets.
This meaningfully generalizes
[
31
]
as
Proposition
4.5
proves integral representations are aligned.
Then, we examine linear optimization over greedoids, and show
local poset greedoids
where the greedy algorithm is correct always have aligned representations.

Main Result 1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a greedoid with the local poset property,
and suppose the greedy algorithm correctly solves any linear optimization over the basic words.
Then, there exists an aligned polymatroid representation
\rho:2^{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{R}
of
\Lambda
.

This advances our understanding of submodularity as a necessary condition for the greedy algorithm’s correctness, as Theorem
5.2
shows such local poset greedoids have structure conforming with a polymatroid.
Furthermore, our work and
[
31
]
are evidence to representations being a powerful analytic tool, and by Theorem
5.2
one need only verify the correctness of the greedy algorithm to apply them in analyzing of local poset greedoids.
Such is significantly easier a task than directly constructing an aligned representation, hence easing application to future work investigating discrete programs on greedoids with non-linear objectives.

Alignment is a minimal set of axioms ensuring that a polymatroid representation is “useful.”
Our specificity is needed as a general continuous representation
\rho
does fail to satisfy some properties, such as
optimism
and the
\rho
-closure of the flat supports required for our second main result.
Our second main result shows alignment in a representation
\rho
is equivalent to the existence a special Galois connection between the lattices of flats
\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)
and
\rho
-closed sets
\mathscr{L}(\rho)
, with adjoints determined by the kernel closure operator
\kappa
.
The result is technically surprising; for example, it is not obvious the image of
\kappa
should even be in
\mathscr{L}(\rho)
.
Furthermore, this shows the flat support structure entirely determines aligned representations, exposing a new perspective on polymatroid greedoids and their representations.

Main Result 2
.

Let
\Lambda
be a greedoid possessing optimism and the interval property, and
\rho:2^{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{R}
a polymatroid representation.
The representation
\rho
is aligned if and only if the following are true:

1.

The flat support sets are
\rho
-closed,

2.

The kernel closure operator
\kappa
is cover-preserving,

3.

And,
\kappa:\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)\leftrightarrows\mathscr{L}(\rho):\kappa^{-1}
is a Galois connection.

Finally, we identify a maximum aligned representation
\accentset{\vee}{\rho}
, called the
greatest representation
and describe the subclass of polymatroid greedoids possessing it.
We call this subclass
strong polymatroid greedoids
, and show optimistic interval greedoids with flat supports closed under intersection are exactly this class.
Our method uses the closure and interior operators of the Galois connection to show the
\accentset{\vee}{\rho}
-closed sets are isomorphic to the flats of any greedoids represented by
\accentset{\vee}{\rho}
.
Then, as the flat supports of an interval greedoid are also isomorphic to its flats, one can intuit that lattice of flat supports and
\accentset{\vee}{\rho}
-sets are actually equal (and so the meet of two flat supports is their intersection).
Similar techniques also show these greedoids have flats related via lattice-embedding to
every
aligned representation’s closed sets.
So strong polymatroid greedoids are quite well-behaved, since this makes the flats always isomorphic to a sublattice of the closed sets of any aligned representation.

Main Result 3
.

Let
\Lambda
be an interval greedoid with optimism.
The following are equivalent:

1.

\Lambda
is a strong polymatroid greedoid,

2.

\Lambda
is a polymatroid greedoid such that for all aligned representations
\rho:2^{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma_{\rho}\circ\kappa
is a lattice-embedding of
\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)
into
\mathscr{L}(\rho)
,

3.

The greatest representation
\accentset{\vee}{\rho}
is a polymatroid representation of
\Lambda
,

4.

\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)\cong\mathscr{L}(\accentset{\vee}{\rho})
, i.e. the
\accentset{\vee}{\rho}
-closed sets are isomorphic to the flats of
\Lambda
,

5.

The flat supports of
\Lambda
form a Moore family.

We’ll show that optimistic

After observing the success of the greedy algorithm in a variety of settings incompatible with downward closed set families, Korte and Lovász introduced greedoids as an analytic tool generalizing matroids

Strong exchange is a prototypical manifestation of a

In absence of strong exchange, there does exist a class of

More recently, Szeszlér investigated the polyhedral aspects and applications of

and proved new insights about the greedy algorithm

]
.
Lastly,

Polymatroid greedoids

And our work shows new applications of the lattice theory of polymatroids, an understudied area recently examined in

3.1
Lattices

A partially ordered set (poset)
\mathscr{P}=(\mathcal{X},\sqsubset)
is defined by an irreflexive, asymmetric, and transitive relation over a ground set
\mathcal{X}
.
In this case we say that
\mathbf{x}
is
less than
or
lies below

\mathbf{y}
whenever
\mathbf{x}\sqsubset\mathbf{y}
, and conversely that
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are
incomparable
if all of
\mathbf{x}\not\sqsubset\mathbf{y}
,
\mathbf{y}\not\sqsubset\mathbf{x}
, and
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{y}
hold true.
We denote the reflexive closure by
\sqsubseteq
, and say that
X\subseteq\mathscr{P}
is an (order)
ideal
if
\mathbf{y}\in X
and
\mathbf{x}\sqsubseteq\mathbf{y}
implies
\mathbf{x}\in X
.
An (order)
filter
is defined dually.
For
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathscr{P}
we refer to the
meet
(greatest lower bound) by
\mathbf{x}\sqcap\mathbf{y}
and the
join
(least upper bound) by
\mathbf{x}\sqcup\mathbf{y}
, when they exist.
A poset is a
lattice
whenever it is closed under meet and join, making
\mathscr{L}=(\mathcal{X},\sqcap,\sqcup)
a well defined algebraic structure.
Correspondingly, a
sublattice
is a substructure closed under meet and join.
The
covering relation
, which we denote by ‘
\prec
’ for all partial orders in this work, is such that
\mathbf{x}\prec\mathbf{y}
if and only if
\mathbf{x}\sqsubset\mathbf{y}
and there is no
\mathbf{z}
with
\mathbf{x}\sqsubset\mathbf{z}\sqsubset\mathbf{y}
.
The covering relation allows one to visualize posets with
Hasse diagrams
, which consist of nodes corresponding to the elements of the ground set
\mathcal{X}
and line segments such that there is a line directed upwards from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
if and only if
\mathbf{x}\prec\mathbf{y}
.
A poset
\mathscr{P}
is
graded
whenever there exists a function
g:\mathscr{P}\to\mathbb{Z}_{+}
consistent with the covering relation in the sense that
\mathbf{x}\prec\mathbf{y}
implies
g(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{y})-1
.
A lattice is
semimodular
if and only if
\mathbf{x}\succ\mathbf{x}\sqcap\mathbf{y}
implies
\mathbf{y}\prec\mathbf{x}\sqcup\mathbf{y}
.
An important property of such lattices is they are graded by
submodular
functions, that is
f:\mathscr{L}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying,

f(\mathbf{x}\sqcap\mathbf{y})+f(\mathbf{x}\sqcup\mathbf{y})\leq f(\mathbf{x})+f(\mathbf{y}).

(1)

\mathscr{P}=(\mathcal{X},\sqsubseteq)
and
\mathscr{Q}=(\mathcal{Y},\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\hbox{$\displaystyle\raisedrightarrow$}\cr\kern 5.00108pt\cr\makebox[5.00002pt][s]{$\displaystyle\relbar\hss\relbar$}\cr\kern-5.00108pt\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\hbox{$\textstyle\raisedrightarrow$}\cr\kern 5.00108pt\cr\makebox[5.00002pt][s]{$\textstyle\relbar\hss\relbar$}\cr\kern-5.00108pt\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\hbox{$\scriptstyle\raisedrightarrow$}\cr\kern 3.52174pt\cr\makebox[4.09723pt][s]{$\scriptstyle\relbar\hss\relbar$}\cr\kern-3.52174pt\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\raisedrightarrow$}\cr\kern 2.52853pt\cr\makebox[3.68059pt][s]{$\scriptscriptstyle\relbar\hss\relbar$}\cr\kern-2.52853pt\cr}}}})

\mathbf{x}\sqsubseteq\mathbf{y}

\varphi(\mathbf{x})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\hbox{$\displaystyle\raisedrightarrow$}\cr\kern 5.00108pt\cr\makebox[5.00002pt][s]{$\displaystyle\relbar\hss\relbar$}\cr\kern-5.00108pt\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\hbox{$\textstyle\raisedrightarrow$}\cr\kern 5.00108pt\cr\makebox[5.00002pt][s]{$\textstyle\relbar\hss\relbar$}\cr\kern-5.00108pt\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\hbox{$\scriptstyle\raisedrightarrow$}\cr\kern 3.52174pt\cr\makebox[4.09723pt][s]{$\scriptstyle\relbar\hss\relbar$}\cr\kern-3.52174pt\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\raisedrightarrow$}\cr\kern 2.52853pt\cr\makebox[3.68059pt][s]{$\scriptscriptstyle\relbar\hss\relbar$}\cr\kern-2.52853pt\cr}}}}\varphi(\mathbf{y})

A mapping between lattices is

\varphi(\mathbf{x}\sqcup\mathbf{y})=\varphi(\mathbf{x})\sqcup\varphi(\mathbf{y})

A

When a mapping is both join and meet-preserving, then it is a

An injective lattice-homomorphism is a

3.2
Polymatroids

Introduced by Edmonds
[
16
]
, a
polymatroid
is a polytope associated with a special submodular function.
Specifically, a
polymatroid rank function

\rho:2^{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{R}
is a submodular set function which is
normalized
, i.e.
\rho(\varnothing)=0
, and
monotone
in that
X\subseteq Y
implies
\rho(X)\leq\rho(Y)
.
Here our submodular function is defined on a powerset, so Eq.
1
is equivalent to the
law of diminishing returns
,

X\subseteq Y\implies(\forall z\notin Y)\;(\rho/X)(z)\geq(\rho/Y)(z),

where
\rho/X
is the
contraction
of
\rho
by
X
given by
(\rho/X)(Y)\triangleq\rho(X\cup Y)-\rho(X).

Note we abuse notation by letting
(\rho/X)(y)=0
for all
y\in X
.
A polymatroid is
integral
if the codomain of its rank function is the integers.
The
span
operator
\sigma_{\rho}:2^{\Sigma}\to 2^{\Sigma}
is defined as,

\sigma_{\rho}(X)\triangleq\{y\in\Sigma\mid\rho(X+y)=\rho(X)\},

(3)

i.e. those elements for which the marginal return
(\rho/X)(y)=0
.
The span is a closure operator, and so those sets
X\subseteq\Sigma
such that
X=\sigma_{\rho}(X)
are called
\rho
-
closed
.
Ordering the
\rho
-closed sets by containment makes a lattice (Figures
4
and
7
give Hasse diagrams of such lattices) which we call
\mathscr{L}(\rho)
for rank function
\rho
.
Closure operators on a powerset form
Moore families
, and so the
\rho

\sigma_{\rho}(X)\sqcap\sigma_{\rho}(Y)=\sigma_{\rho}(X)\cap\sigma_{\rho}(Y)
for all
X,Y\subseteq\Sigma
).

3.3
Greedoids

Let
\Sigma
be an alphabet, and
\Sigma^{*}
the free monoid (i.e. all possible finite sequences over
\Sigma
.
Every
\alpha\in\Sigma^{*}
can be written as
\alpha=x_{1}\ldots x_{k}
, where each
x_{i}\in\Sigma
,
so one refers to the elements of

\Sigma^{*}
as
words
and
\Sigma
as
letters
. A collection of words
\Lambda\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
forms a
language
over
\Sigma
which is
simple

whenever no letter appears
twice in any word of the language.
The
support
of
a word
\alpha\in\Sigma^{*}
is,

\widetilde{\alpha}\triangleq\{x\in\Sigma\mid(\exists\beta,\gamma\in\Sigma^{*})\;\beta x\gamma=\alpha\},

i.e. the collection of letters forming
\alpha
, and we refer to the length of a
word via
|\alpha|
.
The longest words of
\Lambda
are called
basic
, and
we denote the identity element of

\Sigma^{*}
by
\epsilon
. Thus,

\alpha\epsilon=\epsilon\alpha=\alpha
for all
\alpha\in\Sigma^{*}
, meaning
\epsilon
holds the privileged position of the
empty word
.
A
greedoid
follows.

Definition 3.3

(Greedoid)
.

A
greedoid

\Lambda
over an alphabet
\Sigma
is a simple language satisfying:

1.

(Non-Empty)
The greedoid possesses at least one feasible word, i.e.
\Lambda\neq\varnothing
,

2.

(Hereditary)
\alpha\beta\in\Lambda
implies the prefix
\alpha
is feasible, i.e.
\alpha\in\Lambda
,

3.

(Exchange)
For all
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
, if
|\alpha|>|\beta|
then there exists
x\in\widetilde{\alpha}
such that
\beta x\in\Lambda
.

Note that non-emptiness and the hereditary axioms together imply that
\epsilon\in\Lambda
, and that the exchange axiom makes all basic words equal in length.
There is an equivalent definition in terms of
accessible
set families making it obvious that greedoids generalize matroids by removing the need for downward closure, see either of
[
5
]
or
[
27
]
for example.
However, we find the language definition more intuitive, and better suited to our proof techniques.

Because a greedoid encodes a set of constraints on valid ways to build a solution in a stepwise fashion, we call the words of a greedoid
feasible
.
Hence, the familiar reader sees that
\Lambda
is a matroid if and only if
\alpha\in\Lambda
implies that every permutation of
\alpha
is also feasible, i.e.
\widetilde{\alpha}!\subseteq\Lambda
(see
[
8
]
for more on this perspective).
A motivating example is
undirected branching greedoids
.

Example 3.4

(Undirected Branching Greedoid)

Let

For all

Verifying the hereditary and exchange axioms is left to the reader.

The (greedoid)

A letter

\Lambda
.
In many of our proofs it will be useful to make use of this.
Specifically, let

F/F^{\prime}\triangleq\{\alpha\in\Lambda/F^{\prime}\mid(\exists\beta\in F^{\prime})\;\beta\alpha\in F\}.

Then, the interval property is defined by the guarantee that a shorter feasible word can always be augmented by a subword of a longer feasible word to achieve length at least that of the longer word.

Definition 3.5

(Interval Property)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a greedoid.
Then,
\Lambda
has the

if for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
with
|\beta|>|\alpha|
, there exists a subword
\beta^{\prime}
of
\beta

|\beta^{\prime}|\geq|\beta|-|\alpha|
with
\alpha\beta^{\prime}\in\Lambda
.

All greedoids under our consideration will satisfy the interval property.
For interval greedoids, the flats and the image of the kernel closure operator are in bijective correspondence because the interval property ensures that a set
X\in\kappa\left(2^{\Sigma}\right)
if and only if the set of feasible words with supports contained in
X
form a flat
[
27
]
.
For this reason we call a set in the
\kappa

\kappa[\alpha]=\kappa(F)=\bigcup\{\widetilde{\beta}\mid\beta\in F\}.

This means that
\kappa(F)
is the flat support of

.
Then, depending on context (that being whether its operand is a set of letters or a flat) either

to
2^{\Sigma}
like Eq.

\Lambda/\mathord{\sim}
to
2^{\Sigma}
in the way described above.
Furthermore, due to this bijection we introduce another abuse of notation by letting
\kappa^{-1}(X)
give the unique flat
F\in\Lambda/\mathord{\sim}
such that
\kappa(F)=\kappa(X)

\kappa^{-1}(X)\triangleq\bigsqcup\big\{[\alpha]\bigm|\alpha\in\Lambda\cap X^{*}\big\}.

(5)

As justification, note
\kappa^{-1}\circ\kappa
is the identity mapping when applied to
\Lambda/\mathord{\sim}
.
Another useful property is that the flats of an interval greedoid are

\alpha\sim\beta

Correspondingly, since the

\Gamma(F)=\Gamma[\alpha]
for

Finally, the flats of an interval greedoid form a semimodular lattice
[

]
.
This lattice, which we refer to by

In light of this, we let

for any
\alpha\in F
.
Moreover, the lattice of flats is isomorphic to the flat supports ordered by containment
[

]
, i.e.
\left(\kappa\left(2^{\Sigma}\right),\subseteq\right)
.
We often use this fact implicitly.
A nice consequence is,

\displaystyle\kappa(F\sqcup F^{\prime})=\kappa(\kappa(F)\cup\kappa(F^{\prime})),

\displaystyle\text{and},

\displaystyle\kappa(F\sqcap F^{\prime})=\kappa(\kappa(F)\cap\kappa(F^{\prime})),

for all

To see the former, first observe
\kappa(\kappa(F)\cup\kappa(F^{\prime}))=\kappa(F)\sqcup\kappa(F^{\prime})
as one can verify
\kappa(\kappa(F)\cup\kappa(F^{\prime}))
is the least flat support containing
\kappa(F)\cup\kappa(F^{\prime})
.
Then,
\kappa(F)\sqcup\kappa(F^{\prime})=\kappa(F\sqcup F^{\prime})
by the isomorphism.
The latter
\kappa(F\sqcap F^{\prime})=\kappa(\kappa(F)\cap\kappa(F^{\prime}))
follows similarly.

Polymatroid Representations

A polymatroid greedoid
\Lambda

\rho:2^{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{R}
encoding the feasible words.
Specifically, we call
\rho
a

if and only if,

\Lambda=\{x_{1}\ldots x_{k}\in\Sigma^{*}\mid(\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\})\;\rho(\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{i}\})=i\}.

(6)

Korte and Lovász define the

as greedoids possessing integral representations.
However, we generalize this.
By inspecting Eq.

However, more interesting is the representation maintains a submodular structure even among sets which

The class of polymatroid greedoid is broad, including matroids, poset antimatroids, undirected branching greedoids, and certain ordered geometries.
See

Polymatroid greedoids belong to a subclass of interval greedoids called

Definition 4.2

)
.

Let

(7)

(
[

)
.

If a greedoid
\Lambda

Our first main result in Section

Though integrality doesn’t seem necessary for defining polymatroid greedoids, general continuous representations do lose important properties.
This includes relationships with the flat supports proven in Main Result

So, in the next section we take an axiomatic approach towards defining what makes a representation “useful,” and we expand our definition of a polymatroid greedoid to be those possessing

Following this, we introduce

Both will be pervasive in our analyses in Sections

4.1
Alignment

Because the lattice of closed sets encodes the combinatorial information of a polymatroid, we define alignment in terms of a special order-preserving mapping between
\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)
and
\mathscr{L}(\rho)
.

Definition 4.4

(Aligned Representation)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a greedoid and
\rho:2^{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{R}
a polymatroid rank function, with
\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)
and
\mathscr{L}(\rho)
being the respective lattices of flats and closed sets.
Then,
\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)
and
\mathscr{L}(\rho)
are
aligned
if and only if there exists an order-preserving
\varphi:\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)\to\mathscr{L}(\rho)
satisfying,

1.

(Agreement)

\rho(\widetilde{\alpha})=\rho(\varphi[\alpha])
for all feasible words
\alpha\in\Lambda
,

2.

(Inclusion)

\widetilde{\alpha}\subseteq\varphi[\alpha]
for all feasible words
\alpha\in\Lambda
,

3.

(Cover-Preservation)

F\prec F^{\prime}\implies\varphi(F)\prec\varphi(F^{\prime})
for all greedoid flats
F,F^{\prime}\in\Lambda/\mathord{\sim}
.

These axioms are a minimal set of conditions necessary for the combinatorial structures of a greedoid and a representation to be compatible in a way that isn’t spurious.
They can be intuited and justified without reference to our forthcoming results:

Agreement
and
inclusion
both ensure the representation maintains information about the greedoid’s flats within the polymatroid’s closure system.
Specifically, agreement preserves the rank information, while inclusion enforces the identities of letters in
\rho
-closed sets map back to the supports of the
\Lambda
-flats.

Cover-preservation
ensures chains of
\mathscr{L}(\rho)
can be associated with feasible words, much like how feasible words encode sets of chains in
\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)
.
Such makes
\mathscr{L}(\rho)
reflect the feasibility structure whenever
\mathscr{L}(\rho)
and
\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)
are aligned.

If
\rho
is a representation where
\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)
and
\mathscr{L}(\rho)
are aligned we call it an
aligned representation
.
Using results from
[
31
]
, verifying integral representations are
always
aligned is straightforward.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\Lambda
be a greedoid and
\rho
a polymatroid rank function.
Suppose that
\rho
is a representation of
\Lambda
.
If
\rho
is integral, then it is an aligned representation.

This requires the following statement about the partial alphabets of the integral polymatroid greedoids examined in
[
31
]
.

Lemma 4.6

(Corollary 4.4 of
[
31
]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a greedoid with integral representation
\rho
.
Then, for all partial alphabets
X
,
\rho(X)=r(X)
.

Proof of Proposition
4.5
.

We show that
\varphi=\sigma_{\rho}\circ\kappa
is a suitable mapping witnessing alignment.
Because a flat support is a partial alphabet, Lemma
4.6
implies
\rho(\kappa[\alpha])=r(\kappa[\alpha])
for all
\alpha\in\Lambda
.
Since
r(\kappa[\alpha])=r[\alpha]
, agreement follows.
One also sees cover-preservation by integrality.
Finally, inclusion follows by our choice of
\varphi
.
∎

4.2
Optimism

Suppose one wishes to build a basic word
x_{1}\ldots x_{r}\in\Lambda
in an iterative fashion.
A subtle property of a normal matroid is that a letter
y
is a continuation of the empty word
\epsilon
, and remains so until the first prefix containing
y
in its span.
However, this dynamic is lost in the greedoid setting.
Because of its usefulness, we introduce a weakening of this dynamic being every letter is a continuation at
some
point when building basic words step by step.
We call this
optimism
.

Definition 4.7

(Optimistic Greedoid)
.

A greedoid
\Lambda
is
optimistic
if and only if for all
y\in\Sigma
which are not loops, and basic words
x_{1}\ldots x_{r}\in\Lambda
, there exists some index
i
such that
y\in\Gamma[x_{1}\ldots x_{i}]
.
Furthermore,
\Lambda
is
strongly optimistic
if and only if there exists an index
i
such that,

y\in\Gamma[x_{1}\ldots x_{j}]\iff i\leq j\text{ and }x\notin\kappa[x_{1}\ldots x_{j}].

For Sections
5
and
6
, it is helpful to observe that strong optimism makes
x\notin\kappa[\alpha]
imply the existence of
\beta x\in\Lambda/\alpha
.
Furthermore, strong optimism also makes
F\prec F^{\prime}
and
x\in\kappa(F^{\prime})\setminus\kappa(F)
imply
x\in\Gamma(F)
.
Now we show that aligned representations imply optimism.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
\Lambda
be a greedoid possessing an aligned representation
\rho
.
Then,
\Lambda
is optimistic.

Proof.

Let
\varphi:\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)\to\mathscr{L}(\rho)
be the mapping satisfying the alignment axioms, and
fix a basic word
x_{1}\ldots x_{R}\in\Lambda
and non-loop
y\in\Sigma
.
Note that inclusion implies
\kappa[\alpha]\subseteq\varphi[\alpha]
for all
\alpha\in\Lambda
.
So, because there is a feasible word containing
y
in its support it follows that
y\in\varphi[x_{1}\ldots x_{R}]
.
Moreover, agreement makes
\rho(\varphi[\epsilon])=0
, and so
\varphi[\epsilon]
can only contain loops of the greedoid.
This makes
y\in\varphi[x_{1}\ldots x_{R}]\setminus\varphi[\epsilon]
, and as cover-preservation implies,

\varphi[\epsilon]\prec\varphi[x_{1}]\prec\varphi[x_{1}x_{2}]\prec\ldots\prec\varphi[x_{1}\ldots x_{R}],

it follows that there exists an index
i\in\{1,\ldots,R\}
such that
y\in\varphi[x_{1}\ldots x_{i}]\setminus\varphi[x_{1}\ldots x_{i-1}]
.
By cover-preservation, we see that
\sigma_{\rho}(\varphi[x_{1}\ldots x_{i-1}]+y)=\varphi[x_{1}\ldots x_{i}],
which makes,

(\rho/\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1})(y)=(\rho/\varphi[x_{1}\ldots x_{i-1}])(y)=(\rho/\varphi[x_{1}\ldots x_{i-1}])(x_{i})=(\rho/\{x_{1}\ldots x_{i-1}\})(x_{i})=1.

Thus
y\in\Gamma[x_{1}\ldots x_{i-1}]
.
As our choices were arbitrary, optimism follows.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

Though they do not use our terminology, optimism was proven for integral representations in Corollary 4.17 of
[
31
]
.
We gave a shorter argument for the more general setting.

Remark 4.10
.

A general continuous representation does not imply optimism.

The relationships between optimistic greedoids and other classes in the greedoid taxonomy are shown in Figure
1
, and proven in Appendix
A
and
B
.
In particular, we show that every strong-exchange greedoid is optimistic.
Furthermore, we use a trimmed matroid construction to show the existence of an optimistic interval greedoid without the local poset property, and adapt a construction from
[
33
]
to make an optimistic local poset greedoid which cannot possess any aligned representation.
Describing polymatroid greedoids by greedoid properties which exist independently of any polymatroid representation is an important open problem and, by the latter construction, optimism and the local poset property cannot do so alone.

as a consequence. By semimodularity
F\sqcup[\mu x]
covers

, so
F\prec\kappa^{-1}(\kappa(F)+x)
(what we just found) and
\kappa^{-1}(\kappa(F)+x)\sqsubseteq F\sqcup[\mu x]
imply
\kappa^{-1}(\kappa(F)+x)=F\sqcup[\mu x]
.

Now we present our Forking Lemma for interval greedoids.
This lemma will be frequently applied in Sections

and
6
for obtaining our main results.
Furthermore, towards the end of the section we use our lemma to show that optimistic interval greedoids satisfy a stronger form of Definition

.

Lemma 4.11

(Forking Lemma)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a greedoid with the interval property, and select two flats
F,F^{\prime}\in\Lambda/\mathord{\sim}
.
Suppose that
\mu\in F\sqcap F^{\prime}
.
Then,
x\in\Gamma(F\sqcap F^{\prime})
and
[\mu x]\sqsubseteq F^{\prime}
implies
x\in\Gamma(F)
.

We imagine the continuation
x\in\Gamma(F\sqcap F^{\prime})
as creating a “fork” in that it leads to a flat beneath
F^{\prime}
but not
F
whenever
F\neq F^{\prime}
, see Figure
5
.
In some sense, the lemma thus shows that concatenation with
x
is partially commutative.
Compare these guarantees to the exchange and interval properties:
Suppose
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
with
|\alpha|<|\beta|
.
Then, the exchange and interval properties guarantee
existence
of
x\in\widetilde{\beta}\setminus\widetilde{\alpha}
with
x\in\Gamma[\alpha]
, but cannot guarantee a
specific
identity of
x
.
In contrast, the Forking Lemma can be applied to witness a specific identity of such a continuation
x
.

To prove this result, we induct over the difference in grade between
F
and the meet
F\sqcap F^{\prime}
.
For this, we will require the following weaker result making a statement similar to the Forking Lemma, but only for words differing by one letter in the same location.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
\Lambda
be a greedoid with the interval property, and
\mu\in\Lambda
a feasible word.
If there exists
x,y\in\Gamma[\mu]
and
\alpha\in\Sigma^{*}
with
\mu x\alpha
and
\mu y\alpha
feasible, then
\mu x\alpha\not\sim\mu y\alpha
implies
\mu y\alpha x\in\Lambda
.

Proof.

Select
x,y\in\Gamma[\mu]
and
\alpha\in\Sigma^{*}
satisfying the premise.
Note that
\mu x\alpha\not\sim\mu y\alpha
ensures that
x\neq y
, making the task of showing
x\in\Gamma[\mu y\alpha]
well-formed.
To do so, first recall that two words in the same flat of an interval greedoid are cospanning.
Hence, via negation we find
\Gamma[\mu x\alpha]\neq\Gamma[\mu y\alpha]
.
First, suppose
\Gamma[\mu x\alpha]\setminus\Gamma[\mu y\alpha]\neq\varnothing
, and select a letter
z
in this difference.
Then because,

\widetilde{\mu x\alpha z}\setminus\widetilde{\mu y\alpha}=\{x,z\},

(8)

and the words of
\Lambda
are simple, the fact that
\mu x\alpha z\in\Lambda
and
|\mu x\alpha z|>|\mu y\alpha|
makes the lemma by way of the exchange axiom.
More precisely, since
z\notin\Gamma[\mu y\alpha]
and Eq.
8
makes

the only letter that can be concatenated onto
\mu y\alpha
from
\widetilde{\mu x\alpha z}
while maintaining feasibility, we conclude that
\mu y\alpha x\in\Lambda
.

To finish, we must also verify that the case of
\Gamma[\mu x\alpha]\setminus\Gamma[\mu y\alpha]=\varnothing
does not pose any problems.
If this is true, then there exists
z\in\Gamma[\mu y\alpha]\setminus\Gamma[\mu x\alpha]
.
Then, by symmetry the preceding arguments show that
\mu x\alpha y\in\Lambda
.
Therefore, by simple exchange it must be the case that
\mu y\alpha x
is feasible as well, because
|\mu x\alpha y|>|\mu y\alpha|
and
\widetilde{\mu x\alpha y}\setminus\widetilde{\mu y\alpha}=\{x\}
.
∎

Let

and

The subword equals

The only remaining case is (2.), where

By applying the Forking Lemma over an inductive structure, we can prove a stronger statement which concerns words rather than single letters.

The Forking Lemma combines nicely with optimism to show that flat supports witness the feasibility of eventually appending a letter

Proposition 4.14

,

b(\kappa(F)\cup Y)-b(\kappa(F)\cup Y-z)=1,

and so
z
lies in the support of any feasible word
\alpha\in\Lambda
such that
|\widetilde{\alpha}\cap(\kappa(F)\cup Y)|=b(\kappa(F)\cup Y)
.
However, as our choice of
z\in Y
was arbitrary, this means,

\alpha\in\Lambda\text{ and }|\widetilde{\alpha}\cap(\kappa(F)\cup Y)|=b(\kappa(F)\cup Y)\implies Y\subseteq\widetilde{\alpha}.

With that, select a basic word
\beta\in\Lambda
achieving
|\widetilde{\beta}\cap(\kappa(F)\cup Y)|=b(\kappa(F)\cup Y)
.
Then, from
Y\subseteq\widetilde{\beta}
and
(b/\kappa(F))(Y)=|Y|
we observe,

|\widetilde{\beta}\cap\kappa(F)|=b(\kappa(F)\cup Y)-|Y|=b(\kappa(F)\cup Y)-(b/\kappa(F))(Y)=b(\kappa(F))=r(F).

(12)

Or put in other terms, there are exactly
r(F)
letters from
\kappa(F)
in the support of
\beta
.
Well, for any
\gamma\in F
, it follows by exchange that there is a simple word
\pi\in(\widetilde{\beta}\setminus\kappa(F))^{*}
which can be appended onto
\gamma
to make a basic word
\gamma\pi\in\Lambda
.
However, the support of
\pi
cannot overlap with
\kappa(F)
, as
\pi\in\Lambda/\gamma=\Lambda/F
by construction.
By this and Eq.
12
, there are exactly
r(\Lambda)-r(F)
to choose from
\widetilde{\beta}\setminus\kappa(F)
to form
\pi
.
But, because
|\gamma|=r(F)
and
\gamma\pi
is basic we also have that
|\pi|=r(\Lambda)-r(F)
.
Therefore
\widetilde{\pi}=\widetilde{\beta}\setminus\kappa(F)
, which implies
Y\subseteq\widetilde{\pi}
.
Then by recalling
\pi\in\Lambda/F
and
Y\subseteq X
,

b_{F}(X)\geq b_{F}(Y)\geq|\widetilde{\pi}\cap Y|=|Y|=(b/\kappa(F))(X),

and we conclude
b_{F}(X)=(b/\kappa(F))(X)
.
∎

By definition, this implies
b_{F}(X)=b(\kappa(F)\cup X)-b(\kappa(F))
.
But, because
\kappa(F)
is a partial alphabet,
b(\kappa(F))=r(\kappa(F))
.
This produces a helpful corollary.

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
\Lambda
be a greedoid with the interval property.
Then, for all subsets
X\subseteq\Sigma
and flats
F\in\Lambda/\mathord{\sim}
, we have
b_{F}(X)=b(\kappa(F)\cup X)-r(F)
.

5.2
An Aligned Representation of Greedily Optimizable Local Poset Greedoids

Recall, Proposition
5.4
shows
\ddot{\rho}
can be used to encode the feasible words of a greedoid à la polymatroid representation.
We must verify
\ddot{\rho}
is a polymatroid rank function.
A key challenge is that because
\ddot{\rho}
is defined as a constrained optimization over
\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)
, it is not easily evaluated on arbitrary sets of letters.
And so, as a preview, we develop technical lemmas for identifying
\ddot{\rho}(X)
-optimal flats.

First, recall that
\lfloor\psi_{F}(X)\rfloor=r(F)+b_{F}(X)
, so it follows that any
\ddot{\rho}(X)
-optimal flat minimizes
r(F)+b_{F}(X)
among other
\ddot{\rho}(X)
-feasible flats.
In the following two lemmas, we identify that a
\ddot{\rho}(X)
-optimal flat actually minimizes
r(F)+b_{F}(X)
among
all
flats, and that the minimizers of this function form a sublattice of
\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)
.
This latter fact will provide an algebraic structure with which we can observe important properties of
\ddot{\rho}(X)
-optimal flats.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\Lambda
be a greedoid with the interval property, and
X\subseteq\Sigma
any collection of letters.
Then,
\lfloor\ddot{\rho}(X)\rfloor=\min\left\{r(F)+b_{F}(X)\mid(\exists\alpha\in F)\;\widetilde{\alpha}\subseteq X\right\}
.

Proof.

Recall
\lfloor\psi_{F}(X)\rfloor=r(F)+b_{F}(X)
for all
F\in\Lambda/\mathord{\sim}
.
It follows then that
r(F)+b_{F}(X)<r(F^{\prime})+b_{F^{\prime}}(X)
implies
\psi_{F}(X)<\psi_{F^{\prime}}(X)
.
Hence, we need only show,

\{F\in\Lambda/\mathord{\sim}\mid(\exists\alpha\in F)\;\widetilde{\alpha}\subseteq X\}\cap\left(\argmin_{F\in\Lambda/\mathord{\sim}}r(F)+b_{F}(X)\right)\neq\varnothing.

(13)

Well, via Corollary
5.6
we have
b(\kappa(F)\cup X)=r(F)+b_{F}(X)
.
Furthermore, monotonicity of the basis rank makes
b(X)\leq b(\kappa(F)\cup X)
, and for the bottom flat
[\epsilon]
it should be clear that
r[\epsilon]+b_{[\epsilon]}(X)=b(X)
.
Combining these observations together shows,

r[\epsilon]+b_{[\epsilon]}(X)\leq r(F)+b_{F}(X),\quad\forall F\in\Lambda/\mathord{\sim},

and so because the empty word trivially satisfies
\widetilde{\epsilon}\subseteq X
, the bottom flat
[\epsilon]
witness Eq.
13
.
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\Lambda
be a greedoid with the interval property, and
X\subseteq\Sigma
any collection of letters.
Suppose the greedy algorithm correctly solves any linear optimization over the basic words of
\Lambda
.
Then, the set of flats minimizing
F\mapsto r(F)+b_{F}(X)
forms a sublattice of
\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)
.

Proof.

This essentially follows by showing
F\mapsto r(F)+b_{F}(X)
is a submodular function over
\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)
.
Once submodularity is shown, a standard technique for showing that the minimizers form a distributive lattice can be applied (see
[
36
]
for a working example over a ring of sets).

Select two flats
F,F^{\prime}\in\Lambda/\mathord{\sim}
.
To show submodularity, first observe because
\kappa(F)\cup\kappa(F^{\prime})
is a partial alphabet, it follows that
b(\kappa(F)\cup\kappa(F^{\prime}))=r(\kappa(F)\cup\kappa(F^{\prime}))
.
However,
r(Y)=r(\kappa(Y))
for all
Y\subseteq\Sigma
, and
\kappa(\kappa(F)\cup\kappa(F^{\prime}))=\kappa(F\sqcup F^{\prime})
since
\kappa(\kappa(F)\cup\kappa(F^{\prime}))
is the join of
\kappa(F)
and
\kappa(F^{\prime})
in the lattice of flat supports.
Thus,

\left(b/\kappa(F)\cup\kappa(F^{\prime})\right)(\kappa(F\sqcup F^{\prime}))=0.

(14)

Now, our assumptions make
b
a matroid rank function.
We leverage this with Corollary
5.6
,

\begin{array}[]{lll}r(F)&+\;r(F^{\prime})+b_{F}(X)+b_{F^{\prime}}(X)=b(\kappa(F)\cup X)+b(\kappa(F^{\prime})\cup X),&\text{by Corollary~\ref{cor:bf-eq}},\\
&\geq b((\kappa(F)\cap\kappa(F^{\prime}))\cup X)+b(\kappa(F)\cup\kappa(F^{\prime})\cup X),&\text{by submodularity},\\
&\geq b((\kappa(F)\cap\kappa(F^{\prime}))\cup X)+b(\kappa(F\sqcup F^{\prime})\cup X),&\text{by Eq.~\ref{eq:783473}},\\
&=b_{F\sqcap F^{\prime}}((\kappa(F)\cap\kappa(F^{\prime}))\cup X)+b_{F\sqcup F^{\prime}}(X)+r(F\sqcap F^{\prime})+r(F\sqcap F^{\prime}),&\text{by Corollary~\ref{cor:bf-eq}},\\
&\geq b_{F\sqcap F^{\prime}}(X)+b_{F\sqcup F^{\prime}}(X)+r(F\sqcap F^{\prime})+r(F\sqcup F^{\prime}),&\text{by monotonicity}.\end{array}

So we have submodularity over
\mathscr{L}(\Lambda)
.
It is easy now to show that the minimizers form a sublattice: Suppose
F
and
F^{\prime}
additionally satisfy,

r(F)+b_{F}(X)=r(F^{\prime})+b_{F^{\prime}}(X)\text{ and }(\forall F^{\prime\prime}\in\Lambda/\mathord{\sim})\;r(F)+b_{F}(X)\leq r(F^{\prime\prime})+b_{F^{\prime\prime}}(X).

(15)

Well, by the averaging principle,

\min\{r(F\sqcap F^{\prime})+b_{F\sqcap F^{\prime}}(X),r(F\sqcup F^{\prime})+b_{F\sqcup F^{\prime}}(X)\}\leq r(F)+b_{F}(X),

which implies
\min\{r(F\sqcap F^{\prime})+b_{F\sqcap F^{\prime}}(X),r(F\sqcup F^{\prime})+b_{F\sqcup F^{\prime}}(X)\}=r(F)+b_{F}(X)
by Eq.
15
.
Therefore, to maintain the submodular law,

\max\{r(F\sqcap F^{\prime})+b_{F\sqcap F^{\prime}}(X),r(F\sqcup F^{\prime})+b_{F\sqcup F^{\prime}}(X)\}\leq r(F^{\prime})+b_{F^{\prime}}(X).

And so,
F\sqcap F^{\prime}
and
F\sqcup F^{\prime}
are also minimizers.
∎

Now we can make a number of observations regarding
\ddot{\rho}(X)
-optimal flats.
These include uniqueness and monotonicity, among other properties.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
\Lambda
be a greedoid with the interval property, and fix
X,Y\subseteq\Sigma
and
z\in\Sigma
.
Suppose the greedy algorithm correctly solves any linear optimization over the basic words of
\Lambda
,
and assume
F_{X},F_{X+z}
, and
F_{Y}
are
\ddot{\rho}(X)
,
\ddot{\rho}(X+z)
, and
\ddot{\rho}(Y)
-optimal flats respectively.
The following are true:

1.

F_{X}
is the unique
\ddot{\rho}(X)
-optimal flat,

2.

If
X\subseteq Y
, then
F_{X}\sqsubseteq F_{Y}
,

3.

X\cap\Gamma(F_{X})=\varnothing
,

4.

If
F_{X}\sqsubset F_{X+z}
, then there exists
\alpha\in(X\setminus\kappa(F_{X}))^{*}
such that
z\alpha\in F_{X+z}/F_{X}
.

Proof.

Now, for 1., let
F_{X}^{\prime}
be another
\ddot{\rho}(X)
-optimal flat.
Lemma
5.7
implies that both
F_{X}
and
F_{X}^{\prime}
minimize
F\mapsto\lfloor\psi_{F}(X)\rfloor
, and so
through Lemma
5.8
we observe the join
F_{X}\sqcup F_{X}^{\prime}
minimizes this function as well.
Without loss of generality, we assume either
F_{X}
and
F_{X}^{\prime}
are incomparable or
F_{X}
and
F_{X}^{\prime}
can be ordered as
F_{X}\sqsubseteq F_{X}^{\prime}
.
Hence, as some
\alpha\in F_{X}
and
\beta\in F_{X}^{\prime}
such that
\widetilde{\alpha}\cup\widetilde{\beta}\subseteq X
are guaranteed to exist by
\ddot{\rho}(X)
-feasibility, we apply the Amplified Forking Lemma to witness the existence of a subword
\beta^{\prime}
of
\beta
such that
\beta^{\prime}\in F_{X}\sqcup F_{X}^{\prime}/F_{X}
.
Thus,
\alpha\beta^{\prime}\in F_{X}\sqsubseteq F_{X}^{\prime}
shows that the join is
\ddot{\rho}(X)
-feasible as well.
Now, because
b_{F_{X}}(X)\geq|\widetilde{\beta^{\prime}\gamma}\cap X|
and
\widetilde{\beta}^{\prime}\cap\widetilde{\gamma}=\varnothing
for every
\gamma\in F_{X}\sqcup F_{X}^{\prime}
, by letting selecting such a
\gamma
satisfy
b_{F_{X}\sqcup F_{X}^{\prime}}(X)=|\widetilde{\gamma}\cap X|
(and recalling
\widetilde{\beta}^{\prime}\subseteq\widetilde{\beta}\subseteq X
),

b_{F_{X}}(X)-b_{F_{X}\sqcup F_{X}^{\prime}}(X)\geq|\widetilde{\beta^{\prime}\gamma}\cap X|-|\widetilde{\gamma}\cap X|=|\beta^{\prime}|.

(16)

Therefore,

\psi_{F_{X}}(X)-\psi_{F_{X}\sqcup F_{X}^{\prime}}(X)=\lfloor\psi_{F_{X}}(X)\rfloor-\lfloor\psi_{F_{X}\sqcup F_{X}^{\prime}}(X)\rfloor+\frac{b_{F_{X}}(X)-b_{F_{X}\sqcup F_{X}^{\prime}}(X)}{r(\Lambda)+1}\geq\frac{|\beta^{\prime}|}{r(\Lambda)+1}.

Optimality requires this equal zero, forcing
|\beta^{\prime}|=0
.
Thus
F_{X}=F_{X}\sqcup F_{X}^{\prime}
, so
F_{X}
and
F_{X}^{\prime}
are comparable as
F_{X}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq F_{X}
.
But, as comparability implies
F_{X}\sqsubseteq F_{X}^{\prime}
by earlier assumptions,
F_{X}=F_{X}^{\prime}
.

Next up we examine 2.
Let
\beta\in F_{X}
with
\widetilde{\beta}\subseteq X
.
One witnesses a subword
\beta^{\prime}
of
\beta
with
\beta^{\prime}\in F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y}/F_{Y}
by way of the Amplified Forking Lemma.
Select
\alpha\in F_{Y}
with
\widetilde{\alpha}\subseteq Y
.
Then it is clear that
\alpha\beta^{\prime}\in F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y}
and
\widetilde{\alpha\beta^{\prime}}\in F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y}
, thereby making the join
\ddot{\rho}(Y)
-feasible.
By repeating the same logic underlying Eq.
16
, one can observe the bound,

b_{F_{Y}}(Y)-b_{F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y}}(Y)\geq|\beta^{\prime}|=r(F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y})-r(F_{Y}).

However, we also have
b_{F_{Y}}(Y)-b_{F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y}}(Y)\leq r(F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y})-r(F_{Y})
:
Take a
\gamma\in\Lambda/F_{Y}
with
b_{F_{Y}}(Y)=|\widetilde{\gamma}\cap Y|
.
By the exchange axiom, there is a word
\gamma^{\prime}\in\Lambda/F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y}
of length
r(\Lambda)-r(F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y})
supported on a subset of
\widetilde{\gamma}
.
This implies there are at most
r(F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y})-r(F_{Y})
letters in
\widetilde{\gamma}\cap Y
which are not contained in
\widetilde{\gamma}^{\prime}\cap Y
.
Hence,

r(F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y})-r(F_{Y})\geq|\widetilde{\gamma}\cap Y|-|\widetilde{\gamma}^{\prime}\cap Y|\geq b_{F_{Y}}(Y)-b_{F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y}}(Y).

So, we’ve identified
b_{F_{Y}}(Y)-b_{F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y}}(Y)=r(F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y})-r(F_{Y})
.
This makes,

\psi_{F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y}}(Y)-\psi_{F_{Y}}(Y)=r(F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y})-r(F_{Y})+\left(1+\frac{1}{r(\Lambda)+1}\right)\cdot\left(r(F_{Y})-r(F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y})\right)\leq 0,

and so
F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y}
is also
\ddot{\rho}(Y)
-optimal.
By 1., it can only be that
F_{Y}=F_{X}\sqcup F_{Y}
, making
F_{X}\sqsubseteq F_{Y}
.

Now, we’ll show 3. and 4. together.
For 4. assume
F_{X}\sqsubset F_{X+z}
.
Let
\beta\in F_{X+z}
be such that
\widetilde{\beta}\subseteq X+z
.
By the Amplified Forking Lemma there is a subword
\beta^{\prime}
of
\beta
such that
\beta^{\prime}\in F_{X+z}/F_{X}
.
Notice that 3. and 4. follow if
\Gamma(F_{X})\cap X=\varnothing
.
Specifically, this is a restatement of 3., and it would force
\beta^{\prime}=z\alpha
for some
\widetilde{\alpha}\subseteq X
by pigeonhole principle.
So by way of contradiction, assume there exists
y\in\Gamma(F_{X})\cap X
.
For convenience, let
F_{0}\triangleq\kappa^{-1}(\kappa(F_{X})+y)

Notice, by fixing
\gamma\in\Lambda/F_{0}
with
b_{F_{0}}(X)=|\widetilde{\gamma}\cap X|
, since
y\gamma\in F_{X}
we have,

b_{F_{X}}(X)-b_{F_{0}}(X)\geq|\widetilde{y\gamma}\cap X|-|\widetilde{\gamma}\cap X|=1.

Thus,

\psi_{F_{X}}(X)-\psi_{F_{0}}(X)=r(F_{X})-r(F_{0})+\left(1+\frac{1}{r(\Lambda)+1}\right)\cdot\left(b_{F_{X}}(X)-b_{F_{0}}(X)\right),\\
\geq r(F_{X})-(r(F_{X})+1)+\left(1+\frac{1}{r(\Lambda)+1}\right)=\frac{1}{r(\Lambda)+1},

and so
\psi_{F_{0}}(X)<\psi_{F_{X}}(X)
.
It is easy to see that
F_{0}
is
\ddot{\rho}(X)
-feasible (since we can append
y
onto the end of any word in
F_{X}
to achieve a word spanning
F_{0}
, and
y\in X
).
So we’ve found that
F_{X}
is not
\ddot{\rho}(X)
-optimal, a contradiction.
As discussed this witnesses 3. and 4.,
and so concludes the lemma.
∎

In what follows we will continue to refer to the
\ddot{\rho}(X)
-optimal flat by
F_{X}
.
Lemma
5.9
.1 justifies this.
Now, with Lemma
5.9
.2 it is straightforward to show that
\ddot{\rho}
satisfies monotonicity.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
\Lambda
be a greedoid with the interval property.
Suppose the greedy algorithm correctly solves any linear optimization over the basic words of
\Lambda
.
Then, for all
X,Y\subseteq\Sigma
,
X\subseteq Y
implies
\ddot{\rho}(X)\leq\ddot{\rho}(Y)
.

Proof.

First we show
b(Z)=b(Z\cap\kappa({F_{Z}}))+b_{F_{Z}}(Z)
for arbitrary
Z\subseteq\Sigma
.
Our assumptions make the basis rank
b
submodular.
So,

b(Z)=b(Z\cap\kappa({F_{Z}}))+(b/Z\cap\kappa({F_{Z}}))(Z).

(17)

Because
{F_{Z}}
is
\ddot{\rho}(Z)
-feasible the existence of
\alpha\in Z^{*}
such that
\alpha\in{F_{Z}}
follows.
This construction implies
(b/Z\cap\kappa({F_{Z}}))(Z)=(b/\kappa({F_{Z}}))(Z)
since by the law of diminishing returns,

(b/\widetilde{\alpha})(Z)\geq(b/Z\cap\kappa({F_{Z}}))(Z)\geq(b/\kappa({F_{Z}}))(Z)=(b/\widetilde{\alpha})(Z).

Thus we see,

b(Z)=b(Z\cap\kappa({F_{Z}}))+b_{F_{Z}}(Z),

(18)

by substituting
(b/Z\cap\kappa({F_{Z}}))(Z)=(b/\kappa({F_{Z}}))(Z)
into Eq.
17
.
Since
b(Z\cap\kappa(F_{Z}))=r(F_{Z})
by way of
\ddot{\rho}(Z)
-feasibility, we observe
\lfloor\psi_{F_{Z}}(Z)\rfloor=b(Z)
as a consequence.
Finally, if
F\in\Lambda/\mathord{\sim}
is such that
F\sqsubseteq F_{Z}
then applying this argument to the minor
\Lambda/F
shows,

F\sqsubseteq F_{Z}\implies b_{F}(Z)=b_{F}(Z\cap\kappa({F_{Z}})+b_{F_{Z}}(Z).

Take note that
F_{X}\sqsubseteq F_{Y}
by Lemma
5.9
.2, so
b_{F_{X}}(Y)=b_{F_{X}}(Y\cap\kappa(F_{Y}))+b_{F_{Y}}(Y)
.
However, because
F_{Y}
is
\ddot{\rho}(Y)
-feasible,
b_{F_{X}}(Y\cap\kappa(F_{Y}))=r(F_{Y})-r(F_{X})
.
Therefore,

\lfloor\psi_{F_{X}}(Y)\rfloor=r(F_{X})+b_{F_{X}}(Y)=r(F_{X})-r(F_{X})+r(F_{Y})+b_{F_{Y}}(Y)=\lfloor\psi_{F_{Y}}(Y)\rfloor,

(19)

i.e.
\lfloor\psi_{F_{X}}(Y)\rfloor=\lfloor\psi_{F_{Y}}(Y)\rfloor
.
This and Eq.
18
is used in what follows.

Now first suppose
F_{X}=F_{Y}
.
Then
\ddot{\rho}(X)\leq\ddot{\rho}(Y)
follows from
X\subseteq Y
,
r(F_{X})=r(F_{Y})
, and
b_{F_{X}}=b_{F_{Y}}
.
In the other case, where
F_{X}\neq F_{Y}
, we see
F_{X}\sqsubset F_{Y}
by Lemma
5.9
.2.
So there exists a nonempty
z\beta\in F_{Y}/F_{X}
with
\widetilde{z\beta}\subseteq Y
.
This makes
z\in\Gamma(F_{X})
,
and by way of Lemma
5.9
.3 we see
z\notin X
.
Now, we claim that any
\beta\in F_{X}
such that
\widetilde{\beta}\subseteq X
is such that
|\beta|=r(X)
, which makes
r(X)=r(F_{X})
.
Suppose this weren’t true.
Let
\gamma\in\Lambda
be a longest word with
\widetilde{\gamma}\subseteq X
.
Then since
\widetilde{\gamma}
is independent in the matroid induced by
b
, it follows by exchange that
b(\widetilde{\gamma})+(b/\widetilde{\gamma})(X)=b(X)
.
Hence, as
b(\widetilde{\gamma})=r[\gamma]
and
b/\widetilde{\gamma}=b/\kappa[\gamma]
, we see
\lfloor\psi_{[\gamma]}(X)\rfloor=\lfloor\psi_{F_{X}}(X)\rfloor
by way of Eq.
18
.
But
r[\gamma]>r(F_{X})
by our assumptions, and so
\psi_{[\gamma]}(X)<\psi_{F_{X}}(X)
which contradicts the optimality of
F_{X}
.
So we have shown
r(X)=r(F_{X})
.
Since
z\in\Gamma(F_{X})
, this implies
z\notin\sigma_{r}(X)
.
By Theorem
5.1
, we have that
\sigma_{r}(X)
is
b
-closed.
Then
(b/\sigma_{r}(X))(z)=1
, and so by
z\in Y
and the law of diminishing returns (using
\kappa(F_{X})\subseteq\sigma_{r}(X)
and Proposition
5.5
) we observe
(b_{F_{X}}/X)(Y)>0
.
The upshot is that this implies
\psi_{F_{X}}(Y)>\psi_{F_{X}}(X)
.
Well, because the increments of
\psi_{F_{X}}
are greater than or equal to one, this also makes
\lfloor\psi_{F_{X}}(Y)\rfloor>\lfloor\psi_{F_{X}}(X)\rfloor
.
Therefore, by applying Eq.
19
we observe,

\lfloor\psi_{F_{Y}}(Y)\rfloor=\lfloor\psi_{F_{X}}(Y)\rfloor>\lfloor\psi_{F_{X}}(X)\rfloor.

But
\lfloor\psi_{F_{Y}}(Y)\rfloor>\lfloor\psi_{F_{X}}(X)\rfloor
implies
\psi_{F_{Y}}(Y)>\psi_{F_{X}}(X)
.
So monotonicity follows in all cases.
∎

What remains is submodularity.
Our strategy will be to show the law of diminishing returns.
This analysis starts with the following technical lemma.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
\Lambda
be a greedoid with the interval property, and fix
X\subseteq\Sigma
and
z\in\Sigma
.
Suppose the greedy algorithm correctly solves any linear optimization over the basic words of
\Lambda
.
Then,
F_{X}\sqsubset F_{X+z}
implies
r(F_{X})+b_{F_{X}}(X)+1=r(F_{X+z})+b_{F_{X+z}}(X+z)
.

Proof.

Because
F_{X+z}
is
\ddot{\rho}(X+z)
-optimal, we require
\lfloor\psi_{F_{X}}(X+z)\rfloor\geq\lfloor\psi_{F_{X+z}}(X+z)\rfloor
.

F_{X}\sqsubset F_{X+z}
implies
\left(b_{F_{X}}/X\right)(z)=1
, so plugging in
\lfloor\psi_{F_{X}}(X+z)\rfloor=r(F_{X})+b_{F_{X}}(X+z)
and
\lfloor\psi_{F_{X+z}}(X+z)\rfloor=r(F_{X+z})+b_{F_{X+z}}(X+z)
this makes,

r(F_{X})+b_{F_{X}}(X)+1\geq r(F_{X+z})+b_{F_{X+z}}(X+z).

(20)

Assume the inequality is strict.
Then,
r(F_{X+z})+b_{F_{X+z}}(X+z)\leq r(F_{X})+b_{F_{X}}(X)
as
\left(b_{F_{X}}/X\right)(z)=1
.
However,
\lfloor\psi_{F_{X+z}}(X+z)\rfloor\geq\lfloor\psi_{F_{X}}(X)\rfloor
must hold to maintain the monotonicity of
\ddot{\rho}
, so we find
r(F_{X+z})+b_{F_{X+z}}(X+z)=r(F_{X})+b_{F_{X}}(X)
, or equivalently
\lfloor\psi_{F_{X+z}}(X+z)\rfloor=\lfloor\psi_{F_{X}}(X)\rfloor
.
Because
F_{X}\sqsubset F_{X+z}
,
r(F_{X})<r(F_{X+z})
.
This makes
b_{F_{X}}(X)>b_{F_{X+z}}(X+z)
.
Hence,

\ddot{\rho}(X)=\lfloor\psi_{F_{X}}(X)\rfloor+\frac{b_{F_{X}}(X)}{r(\Lambda)+1}<\lfloor\psi_{F_{X+z}}(X+z)\rfloor+\frac{b_{F_{X+z}}(X+z)}{r(\Lambda)+1}=\ddot{\rho}(X+z),

which contradicts the monotone structure of
\ddot{\rho}
shown in Proposition
5.10
.

6.1
Strong Polymatroid Greedoids and the Greatest Representation

Define the function
\accentset{\vee}{\rho}:2^{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{Z}_{+}
as one assigning a value to each subset
X\subseteq\Sigma
equal to the minimum rank among flats containing
X
in its flat support,

\accentset{\vee}{\rho}(X)\triangleq\min\{r(F)\mid X\subseteq\kappa(F)\}.

Suppose that
\accentset{\vee}{\rho}
is a representation of a greedoid
\Lambda
.
It is not difficult to argue that
\accentset{\vee}{\rho}
is then the unique maximum aligned representation of
\Lambda
.
In light of this, we call
\accentset{\vee}{\rho}
the
greatest representation
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a polymatroid greedoid.
If the greatest representation
\accentset{\vee}{\rho}
is a polymatroid rank function representing
\Lambda
, then
\accentset{\vee}{\rho}
is the unique maximum aligned representation of
\Lambda
.

Proof.

Observe, if
\accentset{\vee}{\rho}
is both a polymatroid rank function and a representation, then because
\accentset{\vee}{\rho}
is integral Proposition
4.5
would show
\accentset{\vee}{\rho}
is aligned.
With that settled, let
\rho
be any aligned representation of
\Lambda
, and select any subset
X\subseteq\Sigma
.
Fix a flat
F\in\Lambda/\mathord{\sim}
with
X\subseteq\kappa(F)
and
\accentset{\vee}{\rho}(X)=r(F)
.
Then,
X\subseteq\kappa(F)
implies
\rho(X)\leq\rho(\kappa(F))
since
\rho
is a polymatroid rank function.
But
\rho(\kappa(F))=r(F)
because
\rho
is aligned.
Therefore,

\rho(X)\leq\rho(\kappa(F))=r(F)=\accentset{\vee}{\rho}(X),

and we conclude the result.
∎

We will see that possessing the greatest representation implies nice greedoid structure.

Appendix A
Optimism and Strong Exchange

The purpose of this section is to show that every greedoid with the strong-exchange property, greedoids for which the greedy algorithm always solves any linear optimization over its basic words, possess optimism.
Our technique is to use a similar trick as that employed in the proof of Lemma
5.12
to show an instance where the greedy algorithm fails in absence of optimism.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a greedoid with the strong-exchange property.
Then,
\Lambda
is optimistic.

Proof.

Select a basic word
\alpha\in\Lambda
.
To show
\Lambda
is optimistic, it is sufficient to prove that for every

which is not a loop, there is an
\alpha
-prefix
\alpha^{\prime}
with
x\in\Gamma[\alpha^{\prime}]
.
This statement is trivially true for
x\in\widetilde{\alpha}
, so we examine the case of
x\notin\widetilde{\alpha}
.
Proceed by way of contradiction: Suppose there exists no
\alpha
-prefix containing
x
in its set of feasible continuations.
Then, let
\alpha=y_{1}\ldots y_{r}
and construct a weighting
w:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
via,

w(z)\triangleq\begin{cases}\frac{i}{r(\Lambda)+1}-1,\quad&\text{if }z=y_{i},\\
-r(\Lambda),\quad&\text{if }z=x,\\
0,\quad&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We claim that in the
i^{\text{th}}
iteration, the greedy algorithm selects the letter
y_{i}
.
This is true in the first step because
\epsilon
is an
\alpha
-prefix, meaning that
x\notin\Gamma[\epsilon]
by assumption which leaves
y_{1}
as the letter of least weight which is initially a continuation.
The same principle holds for the
i^{\text{th}}
step, as if the greedy algorithm has already formed the word
y_{1}\ldots y_{i-1}
then
x
is not a feasible continuation by our assumptions, and
y_{i}
is thus the continuation of least weight.
So, it is true that the greedy algorithm forms the word
\alpha
under this weighting by the principle of induction.
However, because
x
is not a loop there exists some basic word
\beta\in\Lambda
containing
x
in its support.
Observe,

\sum_{z\in\widetilde{\beta}}w(z)\leq w(x)<\sum_{z\in\widetilde{\alpha}}w(z).

Hence, greedy fails to output the optimal basic word, contradicting strong-exchange.
∎

\widetilde{\alpha}\cup\widetilde{\beta}
.
Likewise, one can observe
(\widetilde{\alpha}\cap\widetilde{\beta})!\cap\Lambda=\varnothing
only if
Y\cap\widetilde{\alpha}\cap\widetilde{\beta}\neq\varnothing
.
However, by the construction of
\Sigma
and
\Lambda_{0}
the letters in
Y
are only feasible continuations (with respect to the greedoid
\Lambda_{0}
) of words
\alpha^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{0}
such that
\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}
either contains one of
A
,
B
, or
C
, or
|\alpha^{\prime}|=5
.
If
|\alpha|=6
, then it follows from
\gamma\in\Lambda
and
\widetilde{\alpha}\subseteq\widetilde{\gamma}
that
\widetilde{\alpha}=\widetilde{\gamma}
.
In such a case,
\widetilde{\alpha}\cap\widetilde{\beta}=\widetilde{\beta}
, which clearly can be permuted into a word in
\Lambda
.
So assume
|\alpha|<6
, meaning that
\widetilde{\alpha}
contains one of
A
,
B
, or
C
.
Because
\widetilde{\beta}
contains
A
, and
\gamma
is a word of length at most 6 containing
\widetilde{\alpha}\cup\widetilde{\beta}
in its support, we see
A\subseteq\widetilde{\alpha}
by pigeonhole principle.
Hence
\alpha\in\Lambda_{A}
, and we’ve reduced this case to one which we’ve already solved.
∎

Claim
.

\Lambda
possesses optimism.

Proof.

Select a basic word
\alpha\in\Lambda
and letter
x\in\Sigma
, and by way of contradiction assume
x\notin\Gamma[\alpha]
for any
\alpha
-prefix.
The letters of
A\cup B\cup C
are continuations of
\epsilon
, hence making
x\in Y
.
Notice, if
\widetilde{\alpha}\cap Y\neq\varnothing
then there is a letter in
\alpha
which can be replaced by
x
without changing the relative ordering of any other letters (thereby making
x
a continuation of the corresponding prefix).
So to maintain our assumptions, we see
\widetilde{\alpha}\cap Y=\varnothing
.
Then let
\alpha^{\prime}
be the length 5 prefix of
\alpha
.
For
\alpha^{\prime}x\notin\Lambda
it must be that
\alpha^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{0}\setminus\Lambda_{A}
, hence we see
f^{\prime}(\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime})=5
.
Notice that
Y\cap\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}=\varnothing
as well, so the number of edges in any spanning tree of the subgraph induced by
\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}\cup Y
is at least one greater than that induced by
\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}
.
This makes,

f^{\prime}(\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}+x)-f^{\prime}(\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime})\geq 1,\quad\forall x\in Y.

But,
f^{\prime}(\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}+x)-f^{\prime}(\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime})\leq 6-5=1
by definition of
f^{\prime}
, and so
\left(f^{\prime}/\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}\right)(x)=1
for all
x\in Y
.
As a consequence
\alpha^{\prime}x\in\Lambda_{0}
for all
x\in Y
, a contradiction.
∎

Proposition B.1
.

There exists an optimistic local poset greedoid for which there is no aligned polymatroid representation.

Proof.

By the preceding claims,
\Lambda
is an optimistic local poset greedoid.
Towards a contradiction, let
\rho:2^{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{R}
be any (possibly non-integral) aligned polymatroid representation of
\Lambda
.
Observe
b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}y_{1},b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}y_{2},b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}y_{3}\in\Lambda
implies,

\rho(B+y_{1})=\rho(B+y_{2})=\rho(B+y_{3})=4.

Notice that the set of continuations for
b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}y_{1}
,
b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}y_{2}
, and
b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}y_{3}
are the same, hence making
b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}y_{1}\sim b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}y_{2}\sim b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}y_{3}
.
This implies
\rho(B\cup Y)=4
.The same is true of
c_{1}c_{2}c_{3}y_{1}
,
c_{1}c_{2}c_{3}y_{2}
, and
c_{1}c_{2}c_{3}y_{3}
, hence
\rho(C\cup Y)=4
as well.
It can be seen that
\rho(B\cup C\cup Y)=6
, since
b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}c_{1}c_{2}c_{3}\in\Lambda
and
y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}\in\kappa[b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}c_{1}c_{2}c_{3}]
as
b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}c_{1}c_{2}c_{3}
is basic and none of
y_{1}
,
y_{2}
, or
y_{3}
are loops.
Then because submodularity makes,

\overbrace{\rho(B\cup Y)+\rho(C\cup Y)}^{4+4}\geq\underbrace{\rho(B\cup C\cup Y)}_{6}+\rho(Y),

it follows that
\rho(Y)\leq 2
.
However, because
a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}y_{1}y_{2}y_{3}\in\Lambda
, we see
(\rho/A)(Y)=3
.
In particular, this means that
(\rho/A)(Y)>(\rho/\varnothing)(Y)=2
, and so
\rho
does not satisfy the law of diminishing returns (a contradiction).
∎

Remark B.2
.

In
[
33
]
, Korte and Lovász limit the length of the feasible words using a truncation operation.
This would make basic words such that no prefix possesses all 3 letters from one of
A,B,C
, like
a_{1}b_{1}c_{1}a_{2}b_{2}c_{2}
for example, such that there is no prefix possessing a letter from
Y
as a feasible continuation.
In contrast, we limit the length by operating on the function
f
measuring the size of spanning trees in the induced subgraphs (effectively by intersecting the corresponding graphic matroid with a
uniform matroid
of rank 6).
Specifically, the function
f^{\prime}(X)=\min\{f(X),6\}
, which is a polymatroid representation of
\Lambda_{0}
, is such that the marginal return of a letter
x\in Y
under such words of length 5 is always equal to one, hence making
x
a continuation of such words in
\Lambda_{0}
for preserving optimism.

Appendix C
Proof of Proposition
4.3

We claimed that the argument given in
[
31
]
for Proposition
4.3
doesn’t require integrality.
So, now we have to make good on that promise.
We follow parts of the original argument from
[
31
]
.
This requires some definitions which are equivalent to the interval property and local poset property which are not used in any of our original proofs (see
[
27
]
for reference).

The interval property is equivalently described by following:
For all
X\subseteq Y\subseteq Z
such that all of
X!\cap\Lambda
,
Y!\cap\Lambda
, and
Z!\cap\Lambda
are nonempty and
x\notin Z
,

(X+x)!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing\text{ and }(Z+x)!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing\implies(Y+x)!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing.

(30)

One can use this to prove the following via the law of diminishing returns.

Proposition C.1

(
[
31
]
)
.

Suppose
\Lambda
is a greedoid with any polymatroid representation
\rho:2^{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{R}
, then it possesses the interval property.

Proof.

The argument from
[
31
]
uses only the fact that the marginal return of any continuation under a feasible word is always one.
Specifically, let
X\subseteq Y\subseteq Z
be such that
X!\cap\Lambda
,
Y!\cap\Lambda
, and
Z!\cap\Lambda
are non-empty.
Then, take any
x\notin Z
satisfying the premise of Eq.
30
.
From the law of diminishing returns,

1=(\rho/X)(x)\geq(\rho/Y)(x)\geq(\rho/Z)(x)=1.

Hence, for all
\alpha\in Y!\cap\Lambda

it follows that
(\rho/\widetilde{\alpha})(x)=1
.
Thus
x\in\Gamma[\alpha]
by definition of representation, so the intersection on the right hand side of Eq.
30
non-empty since
\alpha x\in(Y+x)!\cap\Lambda
.
∎

We also need the following.

Lemma C.2
.

Suppose
\Lambda
is a greedoid with any polymatroid representation
\rho:2^{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Fix
X,Y\subseteq\Sigma
, and suppose
X\subseteq Y
.
Then, if
Y!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing
, it follows that
\rho(X)\geq|X|
, with equality if and only if
X!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing
.

Proof.

Induct over
|X|
.
Clearly this is true when
X=\varnothing
.
Now, let
\alpha\in Y^{*}\cap\Lambda
be a maximal length word satisfying
X\not\subseteq\widetilde{\alpha}
.
Then, by the exchange property there exists
z\in Y\setminus\widetilde{\alpha}
such that
\alpha z\in\Lambda
.
Observe, because
\alpha
is a maximal word satisfying
X\not\subseteq\widetilde{\alpha}
, we have
X\subseteq\widetilde{\alpha z}
.
This makes all of
z\in X
,
X-z\subseteq\widetilde{\alpha}
, and

X\cup\widetilde{\alpha}=\widetilde{\alpha z}
true.
Because
\rho
is a representation, we also have all of
\rho(X\cup\widetilde{\alpha})=|\alpha|+1
,
\rho(X\cup\widetilde{\alpha})=|X\cup\widetilde{\alpha}|
, and
\rho(\widetilde{\alpha})=|\alpha|
in particular.

Our construction also makes
X\cap\widetilde{\alpha}\subset X
, and so
\rho(X\cap\widetilde{\alpha})\geq|X\cap\widetilde{\alpha}|
by inductive hypothesis.
Then with submodularity,

\rho(X)\geq\rho(X\cap\widetilde{\alpha})+\rho(X\cup\widetilde{\alpha})-\rho(\widetilde{\alpha})\geq|X\cap\widetilde{\alpha}|+|X\cup\widetilde{\alpha}|-|\widetilde{\alpha}|=|X|.

(31)

This shows the first part of the result.
Now for the second, first assume
\rho(X)=|X|
.
Because
(\rho/\widetilde{\alpha})(z)=1
and
X-z\subseteq\widetilde{\alpha}
, it follows by the law of diminishing returns that
(\rho/X-z)(z)\geq 1
.
Combining this with our inductive hypothesis,

|X|-1\leq\rho(X-z)\leq\rho(X)-1=|X|-1.

So
\rho(X-z)=|X|-1
, which makes
(X-z)!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing
by our hypothesis.
Therefore, since
(\rho/X-z)(z)=1
, it follows from the definition of representation that
X!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing
as well.
Now suppose
X!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing
.
Then
\rho(X)=|X|
by assuming
\rho
is a representation.
Hence the lemma.
∎

Now, a greedoid is a local poset greedoid if and only if it is an interval greedoid with the
local intersection property
.
That is, for all
X\subseteq\Sigma
and
y,z\in X
,

X!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing\text{ and }(X-y)!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing\text{ and }(X-z)!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing\implies(X\setminus\{y,z\})!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing.

See Theorem 2.2 in Ch. 7 of
[
27
]
, for example.
So, in light of Proposition
C.1
we need only verify the above.

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

Let
\rho:2^{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{R}
be any (possibly non-integral or non-aligned) representation, and select
X\subseteq\Sigma
and
y,z\in\Sigma
with
X!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing
,
(X-y)!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing
, and
(X-z)!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing
.
By Lemma
C.2
and submodularity this construction makes,

|X|-2\leq\rho(X\setminus\{y,z\})\leq\rho(X-y)+\rho(X-z)-\rho(X)=|X|-2.

But then,
|X|-2=\rho(X\setminus\{y,z\})
, so
(X\setminus\{y,z\})!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing
once again by Lemma
C.2
.
∎

Appendix D
Greediness is Closed Under Contraction Minors

First, define the
strong exchange property
:
For all
\alpha\in\Lambda
and basic words
\beta\in\Lambda
with
\widetilde{\alpha}\subseteq\widetilde{\beta}
, if
x\notin\widetilde{\beta}
is such that
x\in\Gamma[\alpha]
, then there exists
y\in\widetilde{\beta}\setminus\widetilde{\alpha}
such that
y\in\Gamma[\alpha]
and
(\widetilde{\beta}-y+x)!\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing
.
In
[
28
,
22
]
, it was shown that this is necessary and sufficient for correctness of the greedy algorithm on linear optimization over basic words.
We prove the following:

Proposition D.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be an interval greedoid and
F\in\Lambda/\mathord{\sim}
.
If the greedy algorithm correctly solves any linear optimization over the basic words of
\Lambda
, then it correctly solves any linear over the basic words of
\Lambda/F
as well.

Proof.

Firstly, examine
\Lambda/F
, and select any
\alpha\in\Lambda/F
, basic
\beta\in\Lambda/F
, and
x\notin\widetilde{\beta}
which is a continuation of
\alpha
in
\Lambda/F
, as in the statement of the strong exchange property.
Well, for any
\gamma\in F
we have
\gamma\alpha
and
\gamma\beta
are feasible.
Furthermore, we also have
x\in\Gamma[\gamma\alpha]
.
This means
x
is not in the support of
\gamma
, so
x\notin\widetilde{\gamma\beta}
.
Then, there exists
y\in\widetilde{\gamma\beta}\setminus\widetilde{\gamma\alpha}
such that
\gamma\alpha y\in\Lambda
and
\widetilde{\gamma\beta}-y+x
can be permuted into a
\Lambda
-feasible word.
This makes
y
a continuation of
\alpha
in
\Lambda/F
.
But also, our construction ensures
y\notin\gamma
.
So, by the exchange property there is some permutation
\pi\in(\widetilde{\beta}-y+x)!
such that
\gamma\pi\in\Lambda
.
Hence
\pi
is basic in
\Lambda/F
, and the strong exchange property holds.
∎
