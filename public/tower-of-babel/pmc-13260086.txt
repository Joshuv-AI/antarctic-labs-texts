Innovative Endoscopic Treatment of Dual Gastric Perforations Using Argon Plasma Coagulation and Hybrid Closure Techniques
Authors: Yanie Oliva, Aya Fadel, Jorge Suarez, Jose A. Gascon, Hussain Hussain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anastomotic leaks and conduit perforations are serious complications following Ivor Lewis esophagectomy. A 65-year-old man with tobacco use, obesity, obstructive sleep apnea, hypertension, and gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma underwent Ivor Lewis esophagectomy complicated by an anastomotic leak. Endoscopy confirmed dehiscence, successfully treated with a 20 mm AXIOS stent. A second full-thickness perforation from a chest tube traversing the gastric conduit staple line was also identified. Multiple endoscopic attempts, including argon plasma coagulation, OVESCO clipping, and OverStitch suturing, failed due to poor tissue apposition. A novel approach using Surgicel packing with fibrin sealant and Dermabond achieved closure. Chest tubes were removed, and the patient recovered fully, highlighting an effective salvage strategy in complex postoperative complications.

Introduction

Anastomotic leaks and conduit perforations remain among the most feared complications following Ivor Lewis esophagectomy for distal esophageal and gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) malignancies.1,2 Despite advances in surgical technique, perioperative optimization, and postoperative monitoring, anastomotic leak rates continue to be reported in the range of 5%–15%, while gastric conduit perforations occur in approximately 1%–3% of cases.2 These complications carry significant morbidity and mortality, often prolonging hospitalization, increasing the need for reintervention, and adversely affecting long-term oncologic outcomes. The underlying etiologies are multifactorial, but conduit ischemia, tension at the anastomotic site, and technical factors during gastric mobilization and pull-up remain the predominant drivers.1, 2, 3 Early recognition and timely management are essential, as delayed intervention can lead to mediastinitis, sepsis, and multiorgan dysfunction.3

The Ivor Lewis esophagectomy involves a 2-stage approach, typically beginning with an abdominal phase in which the stomach is mobilized to create a tubularized gastric conduit.1,2 This step requires meticulous preservation of the right gastroepiploic artery, the principal blood supply to the future conduit.1,2 Division of the short gastric vessels, left gastric artery, and associated lymphadenectomy must be performed with careful attention to maintaining adequate perfusion along the greater curvature.1,2 The conduit is then fashioned usually 4–5 cm in width to optimize vascularity while minimizing redundancy and tension during the pull-up.2

Following conduit creation, the thoracic phase involves mobilization of the intrathoracic esophagus and creation of an intrathoracic esophagogastric anastomosis.4 Multiple anastomotic techniques exist, including circular stapled, linear stapled, and handsewn approaches, each with distinct risk profiles.4 Regardless of technique, the anastomosis is highly dependent on the perfusion of the proximal conduit.4 Intraoperative assessment tools such as indocyanine green fluorescence angiography have increasingly been used to evaluate conduit perfusion and guide the optimal site for anastomosis.4,5

Despite these precautions, ischemia of the gastric conduit remains a major driver of postoperative complications.5 The tip of the conduit furthest from the gastroepiploic arcade is particularly vulnerable to hypoperfusion, predisposing it to partial necrosis, anastomotic breakdown, or full-thickness perforation.6 Additional risk factors include excessive conduit length, tension during intrathoracic positioning, hypotension, vasopressor use, and technical injury during mobilization.6 When leaks or perforations occur, they can rapidly progress to mediastinitis, sepsis, and multiorgan dysfunction, underscoring the need for early recognition and prompt intervention.6

Management strategies have evolved significantly, with a growing emphasis on minimally invasive and endoscopic therapies. Endoluminal vacuum therapy, covered stent placement, internal drainage, and targeted debridement have emerged as effective alternatives to reoperation in selected patients.6,7 These modalities aim to control contamination, promote granulation, and preserve conduit integrity, reducing the need for high-risk surgical revision.7 The complexity of these complications highlights the importance of meticulous surgical technique, vigilant postoperative monitoring, and a multidisciplinary approach to management.

Case Presentation

We present the case of a 65-year-old man with moderately differentiated GEJ adenocarcinoma who previously underwent an Ivor Lewis esophagectomy with jejunostomy tube placement. Due to the complexity of his condition with comorbidities including hypertension, obstructive sleep apnea, diabetes, and obesity, as well as postoperative course, he was transferred to our institution for further management. His initial endoscopy revealed the esophagogastric anastomosis located in the mid-esophagus with approximately half of the circumference dehisced. Two chest tubes were found penetrating directly through the anastomosis. One of these tubes extended through the gastric lumen and perforated the gastric wall distal to the anastomosis, raising concern for a full-thickness gastric pull-up perforation. No endoscopic intervention was attempted at that time, and the surgical team was notified to reposition the chest tubes.

A second endoscopy was performed. By then, the surgical team had retracted the chest tube back into the thoracic cavity. Endoscopy revealed a mature fistula at the gastroesophageal anastomosis (Figure). Argon plasma coagulation (APC) was applied to promote tissue granulation. Attempts to close the fistula using an over-the-scope clip and the OverStitch suturing device were unsuccessful due to limited maneuverability at the anastomotic site. An AXIOS fully covered stent was therefore deployed to cover the defect. A second chest tube was also seen penetrating the gastric pull-up at a staple line. A third endoscopy showed the esophagogastric anastomosis in the mid-to-distal esophagus. The previously placed AXIOS stent was removed, revealing closure of the original defect. Fibrin sealant was injected to reinforce healing. A WallFlex fully covered stent was then placed under fluoroscopic guidance, with the proximal end positioned approximately 6 centimeters above the anastomosis and secured with OverStitch sutures. Within the gastric pull-up, an old OVESCO clip was noted, and just distal to it, 2 additional perforations were identified. These were treated with APC, closed with 2 OverStitch sutures, and reinforced with fibrin injection. A fourth endoscopy revealed a previously placed esophageal stent in the mid-esophagus. The securing sutures were cut with endoscopic scissors, and the stent was removed. Mucosal ulceration was noted at the proximal flange site, but the GEJ appeared to be healing, and the prior defect was no longer visible. Given the mucosal ulceration and the need to protect the healing anastomosis, a 20 mm × 10 mm AXIOS stent was placed across the anastomosis to support continued healing while avoiding further trauma to the mid-esophageal ulcer. The gastric pull-up defect was again treated with APC. Surgicel was packed into the tract, followed by injection of fibrin sealant and Dermabond, achieving satisfactory closure.

The final endoscopy demonstrated a well-healed esophagogastric anastomosis and intact gastric pull-up. No residual defects were identified. At the prior staple line perforation site, a healing gastric ulcer was observed without evidence of perforation. The patient was able to eat successfully and was discharged home.

Discussion

The Ivor Lewis esophagectomy remains a cornerstone surgical approach for distal esophageal and GEJ malignancies.1 Multiple complications can develop as mentioned above, the tip of the gastric conduit is particularly vulnerable to hypoperfusion because it lies farthest from the gastroepiploic arcade.2 Full-thickness perforation of the conduit is rare, and the presence of chest tubes traversing the anastomosis is an especially uncommon and high-risk scenario, as mechanical disruption can perpetuate leakage and impede healing.4

This case is unique for several reasons. First, the patient developed both an anastomotic dehiscence and separate gastric perforations, all of which were successfully managed using a comprehensive hybrid endoscopic approach incorporating multiple advanced therapeutic modalities. APC was applied at 0.6 L/min and 25 W to achieve controlled tissue devitalization and promote a clean margin for closure.8 Following this, both defects were addressed using endoscopic suturing with the OverStitch device, reinforced by placement of an over-the-scope (OVESCO) clip to ensure mechanical stability.9 Fibrin sealant was then injected to enhance tissue adhesion and support mucosal healing. This stepwise, multimodal approach allowed for complete endoluminal management without the need for surgical intervention. To our knowledge, the use of this integrated combination APC for tissue preparation, endoscopic suturing, OTSC reinforcement, and fibrin sealant in the treatment of dual gastric perforations remains uncommon in current gastroenterological practice. While each technique is individually established, their coordinated application in a single session for multiple perforations reflects a high level of technical expertise and highlights the expanding therapeutic potential of advanced endoscopy. This case underscores how minimally invasive endoscopic methods can offer a safe and effective alternative to surgery in select, complex presentations. Third, successful management required a highly coordinated, multidisciplinary approach integrating thoracic surgery, interventional endoscopy, and radiology. Finally, the patient underwent a staged, multimodal endoscopic treatment strategy including APC, over-the-scope clipping, endoscopic suturing, multiple stent placements, fibrin sealant, and tissue-based adjuncts, demonstrating the evolving capability of advanced endoscopic therapy to salvage compromised conduits without reoperation.

This case highlights the importance of individualized, iterative endoscopic management in complex post-esophagectomy complications and underscores the expanding role of minimally invasive therapies in scenarios previously managed surgically.

Conclusion

Anastomotic leaks and gastric conduit perforations after Ivor Lewis esophagectomy remain rare but life-threatening complications. This case illustrates the successful use of a comprehensive, multidisciplinary, endoscopic-based strategy to manage both anastomotic dehiscence and a separate gastric pull-up perforation in the setting of chest tube–associated disruption. Through sequential application of advanced endoscopic techniques and careful coordination with surgical teams, complete defect resolution was achieved without the need for reoperation. This experience reinforces the growing role of endoscopic therapy in managing complex post-esophagectomy complications and demonstrates its potential to preserve conduit integrity even in highly challenging clinical scenarios.
