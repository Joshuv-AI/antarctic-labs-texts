DNMT3A in cancer: from epigenetic writer to oncogenic driver
Authors: Thomas Dupas, Enola Gautreau, Josianne Clavel, Noël J-M Raynal, Serge McGraw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer is the leading cause of mortality, accounting for one in six deaths worldwide. Despite the emergence of new therapies, cancer mortality remains high. Cancer cells share many traits, such as reprogrammed metabolism and exacerbated proliferation. All these alterations are in part orchestrated by changes in gene expression, notably due to the reshaping of the epigenetic landscape, including DNA methylation marks. Over the past decades, the enzymes responsible for DNA methylation, the DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), have emerged as important actors in tumorigenesis and therefore as therapeutic targets of choice. Of particular interest is DNMT3A, which plays a critical role in de novo DNA methylation and has been strongly implicated in the pathogenesis of acute myeloid leukemia. Considering the large number of recent studies, this review aims to provide an up-to-date view of knowledge regarding DNMT3A-dependant DNA methylation and its implications for cancer pathophysiology. This review highlights the central position of DNMT3A in cell proliferation, metabolic reprogramming and drug resistance in tumor cells, reinforcing the need for further studies on this protein, which appears to be a key therapeutic target.Keywords: DNA methylation, Cancer, DNA methyltransferase 3A, Epigenetic

Background

Cancer is the world’s deadliest disease, with 10 million deaths worldwide in 2020. Due to demographic changes, including population growth and aging, the global cancer burden is projected to dramatically increase over the next years, from 19.3 million new cases in 2020 to 28.4 million by 2040 [1]. For decades, surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy were the only cancer treatments for patients. In parallel to these conventional approaches, new therapeutic strategies based on stem cells, gene manipulations and monoclonal antibodies, have emerged [2]. This therapeutic arsenal keeps death rates relatively low for thyroid (7%), prostate (28%), and breast (30%) cancers. In contrast, high mortality rates for pancreatic (94%) and hepatic (93%) cancers, as well as leukemia (71%) drive the overall death rate to 53% [3]. Faced with this high cancer-related mortality burden, the development of new therapeutic targets addressing tumor initiation, proliferation and resistance remains an unmet need.

Regardless of the type of cancer, tumor cells exhibit common characteristics compared to non-cancerous cells. For instance, major cell cycle checkpoints are compromised in cancerous cells, favoring high cellular proliferation [4]. Also, in order to meet the increased energy requirements linked to constant and exacerbated division of tumor cells, glucose uptake increases drastically [5]. This metabolic reprogramming will lead to the production of oncometabolites (e.g., lactate, glutamate), which promote cell survival and disease progression [6]. These cancerous modifications are associated with changes in gene expression, notably involving epigenetic modulation.

In comparison to normal cells, cancerous cells display characteristic DNA methylation patterns. DNA methylation is an epigenetic mark corresponding to the covalent addition of a methyl group to cytosine residues. In cancer, this often manifests as widespread genomic hypomethylation along with locus specific hypermethylation [7, 8]. The reshaping of the DNA methylation landscape contributes to tumor initiation and progression [9–12], notably by promoting oncogene expression (e.g., hypermethylation within the gene bodies of DLX1 and POU3F3) and repressing tumor suppressor genes (e.g., promoter hypermethylation of CDKN2A and TP53) [13–15]. Thus, DNA methylation has naturally emerged as a therapeutic target of choice, as illustrated by current hypomethylating anti-cancer treatments (e.g., decitabine, azacytidine) and the numerous ongoing clinical studies [14, 16–18]. However, these agents show modest (less than 50%) and heterogeneous clinical responses, frequent primary and secondary resistance, and substantial hematologic toxicity, underscoring the need for more selective and targeted epigenetic strategies [19–22].

Considering the multifactorial nature of cancer and given the central role of DNA methylation in regulating gene expression, epigenetic dysregulation is now recognized as a key contributor of cancer development. Exploring the mechanisms related to alterations in DNA methylation in cancer poses a significant challenge yet holds potential for developing new therapeutic strategies and discovering biomarkers. This review provides an overview of recent findings regarding the role of DNA methyltransferase 3A (DNMT3A), a crucial factor in DNA methylation, in the context of cancer.

Basic concepts of DNA methylation

DNA methylation is a heritable epigenetic mark, which occurs preferentially on cytosine (C) preceding guanine (G), called CpG dinucleotides; although it can be affixed to cytosines before other nucleotide. Non-CpG methylation is predominantly present in pluripotent cells (e.g., oocytes, embryonic stem cells) and markedly decreases, almost disappearing upon differentiation, except in brain cell types [23, 24]. While the majority of CpG dinucleotides (about 70–80%) are methylated, CpG islands (CGIs), which are genomic regions with a higher density of CpG sites, typically remain unmethylated [23, 25]. DNA methylation is a regulatory mechanism essential for spatiotemporal gene expression, genomic imprinting, X chromosome inactivation as well as silencing of retroviral elements [26–28]. It governs gene expression on the basis of the following dogma: CGIs promoter methylation silences genes, whereas gene body methylation is associated with increased gene expression [29]. As an epigenetic modulator, DNA methylation plays a pivotal role in many, if not all biological processes, involving a capacity of plasticity. Although stable and inheritable during DNA replication, DNA methylation is a reversible process capable of being enzymatically modulated according to pathophysiological contexts or in response to an environmental exposure or an infection [30–32].

Genomic methylation levels depend on the activity of the DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) family members. In humans, this family comprises a core set of 5 proteins: DNMT1, DNMT2, DNMT3A, DNMT3B, and DNMT3L. These proteins share a common structure with catalytic activity contained in the C-terminal part while the N-terminus includes sequences that facilitate molecular interactions. The canonical DNMTs—DNMT1, DNMT3A and DNMT3B, methylate the DNA on the 5th carbon of the cytosine pyrimidine ring through their C-terminal methyltransferase (MTase) domain, while DNMT2 and DNMT3L without DNMT catalytic activity, are referred as the non-canonical proteins (Fig. 1) [33].

DNMT1 is the enzyme responsible for the maintenance of established DNA methylation patterns during DNA replication, thus preserving genomic imprinting (i.e., an epigenetic process controlled by DNA methylation in which only one parental allele is expressed) [34]. The replication foci targeting sequence (RFTS) is a domain containing the binding site for ubiquitin like with PHD and ring finger domains 1 (UHRF1), which helps to recruit DNMT1. At the replication fork, it binds to hemi-methylated DNA —only one strand of a CpG dinucleotide pair is methylated, while the newly synthesized complementary strand remains unmethylated—, thus promoting a faithful maintenance of DNA methylation during cell division [35, 36]. A zinc finger CXXC domain recognizes and binds to unmethylated CpG dinucleotides while bromo-adjacent homology (BAH1/2) domains are necessary to target DNMT1 to replication foci [37, 38]. DNMT1 interacts with Proliferating Cell Nuclear Antigen (PCNA), an essential factor for DNA replication, through its PCNA-interacting protein (PIP) box motif, thus promoting its accumulation at DNA replication sites [39]. Finally, N-terminal region also contains the DNA methyltransferase 1 associated protein 1 (DMAP1)/Histone deacetylase 2 (HDAC2) binding domain that mediates transcriptional repression (Fig. 1) [40].

DNMT2 is certainly the most enigmatic protein in the DNMT family. By contrast to DNMT1, DNMT3A and DNMT3B knockout mice, which are lethal postnatally or at embryonic stages [41, 42], DNMT2-deficient mice are viable [43], making the elucidation of its precise biological role even more complex. In 2006, Goll et al. revealed that DNMT2 does not methylate DNA, but instead methylates transfer RNA for aspartic acid (tRNAAsp) at cytosine 38 in the anticodon loop [43]. This substrate change may explain the presence of a particular CFT motif only found in DNMT2, which could be involved in the catalytic activity of the enzyme (Fig. 1) [44]. Opening a new field of research, new tRNAs have been identified as DNMT2 substrates. Methylation of these tRNAs has been shown to be involved not only in protein synthesis, but also in protection against stress-induced cleavage by ribonucleases and may also play a role in tumorigenesis [45, 46].

DNMT3A and DNMT3B are de novo enzymes, responsible for the establishment of new patterns of methylation on unmethylated DNA. These enzymes are particularly important throughout embryonic development during which they affix the DNA methylation essential for cell lineage specification [47, 48]. These two enzymes shared a common structure composed of PWWP (Proline-Tryptophane-Tryptophane-Proline) and ADD (ATRX–DNMT3–DNMT3L) domains (Fig. 1). PWWP domain of DNMT3A and DNMT3B, which is involved in the binding to the DNA, does not have clear sequence preference, explaining the considerable redundancy of the targeted DNA regions [49, 50]. Nevertheless, the PWWP domain of DNMT3A exhibits a weaker affinity for DNA compared to DNMT3B, thus potentially explaining distinct functions [51, 52]. The PWWP domain acts as a chromatin reader by recognizing histone H3 lysine 36 when it is either di- or trimethylated (H3K36me2 and H3K36me3), thereby promoting high-affinity binding to methylated nucleosomes rather than naked DNA alone [53]. In mouse oocytes, these epigenetic marks provide a chromatin platform that is essential for directing DNMT3A-dependent DNA methylation and underlie distinct methylation levels, with broadly distributed H3K36me2 in intergenic and weakly transcribed regions associated with moderate methylation, and H3K36me3 at actively transcribed regions associated with high methylation [54–56]. ADD domain allows the interaction of DNMT3A and DNMT3B with DNMT3L, which stimulates their activity through the formation of heterotetramers (Fig. 1) [57–59]. In addition to acting as a cofactor, DNMT3L cooperates with the DNMT3 family to ensure the proper establishment of maternal genomic imprinting [60–62]. The ADD domain of DNMT3A also senses the methylation status of the lysine-4 of the histone H3 tail (H3K4), promoting de novo DNA methylation at H3K4-unmethylated (H3K4me0) chromatin while excluding H3K4-trimethylated (H3K4me3) regions, which remain largely unmethylated at the DNA level [63, 64]. Recent findings indicate that DNMT3B exhibits distinct regulatory mechanisms for its activity, appearing to operate independently of H3K4 methylation status [65].

The catalytic domain of DNMT1, DNMT3A, DNMT3B is composed of two main parts: a large domain that binds S-adenosyl-L-methionine, the methyl donor cofactor, and a catalytic pocket. The amino acid sequence of this catalytic region differs between DNMT3A and DNMT3B and across species, but on the 10 motifs (I to X), 6 (I, IV, VI, VIII, IX and X) are extremely conserved. In addition, a smaller subdomain, termed the target recognition domain (TRD), located between motifs VIII and IX, mediates DNA recognition (Fig. 1). Despite its name, the catalytic domain of DNMT3A does not fulfil a purely catalytic role. Indeed, mutational analysis of the murine DNMT3A catalytic domain has provided a functional map of residues that differentially control cofactor binding, DNA binding, flanking sequence recognition, and catalysis. These data highlight that the catalytic domain is not a uniform “active site module” but a collection of specialized residues whose perturbation can selectively rewire individual mechanistic features of DNMT3A [66].

Genome-wide DNA methylation dynamics relies on a balance between the activity of DNMTs and the demethylation process, which can be through either active or passive mechanisms. Active demethylation refers to an enzymatic process involving the ten-eleven translocation (TET) proteins. The TET protein family consists of TET1, TET2 and TET3, which catalyze the oxidation of 5-methylcytosine into its derivatives 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, 5-formylcytosine and 5-carboxylcytosine. Thymine-DNA glycosylase-mediated base excision repair can then remove 5-formylcytosine and 5-carboxylcytosine, ultimately restoring unmethylated cytosines [67]. The role of these proteins in cancer has been widely reviewed [68, 69]. In contrast, passive demethylation refers to the loss of DNA methylation marks across cell division, independent of enzymatic activity. Notably, even if DNA methylation remains stable, repression or absence of DNMT1 during DNA replication can result in passive demethylation [26, 34] (Fig. 1).

The close relationship between DNMTs and cancer

Considering the reshaping of DNA methylation that occurs in cancer, the contribution of members of the DNMT family has been extensively reviewed [70–73]. In the following sections, the different ways in which DNMTs are altered in cancer are examined—with a particular focus on DNMT3A—including their up- or downregulation, the occurrence of somatic mutations, and the consequent changes in gene expression and DNA methylation patterns, while also introducing the concept that age-related epigenetic drift may arise independently of direct DNMT alterations. DNMT1 has been described as upregulated in several types of cancers and regarded as poor prognostic indicator, making it a therapeutic target of choice and potential diagnostic biomarker [74–77]. Concomitant with this, Cui et al. recently revealed by bioinformatics approaches that DNMT1 expression was upregulated in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, leading to changes in expression of genes involved in cell cycle, proliferation and metabolic related pathways. This reinforces the potential of targeting DNMT1 as a therapeutic strategy [78]. Indeed, DNMT1 shows significant promise as a molecular target in cancer, notably in aggressive ones like the triple-negative breast cancer [79]. In 2021, Pappalardi et al. reported the discovery of a new reversible DNMT1 selective inhibitor (GSK3685032) for acute myeloid leukemia (AML) treatment. They demonstrated that GSK3685032 significantly reduces DNA methylation and inhibits cancer cell growth in vitro. Although some animals did not respond to the treatment, even at a high dose (45 mg/kg), the authors highlighted that GSK3685032 improves survival and tumor regression in a mouse model of AML. This molecule gets more promising as the study demonstrated better in vivo tolerability, compared to decitabine, another DNA hypomethylating drug [80]. However, as suggested by Mehdipour et al., further investigations are needed prior to clinical studies; to explore the persistence of the beneficial effects and to evaluate the potential drug resistance over a prolonged administration [81]. Despite the absence of DNA methyltransferase activity, DNMT2 and DNMT3L have been shown to be modulated in cancer. As for DNMT1 and DNMT3B, the expression of DNMT2 is higher in gastrointestinal stromal tumors compared to non-tumor tissues [82]. Conversely, in ovarian carcinoma, while DNMT1, DNMT3A and DNMT3B are all increased in tumor tissue, the expression of DNMT2 is significantly decreased. Interestingly, DNMT2 levels are further decreased in high-grade ovarian carcinoma, supporting a potential tumor-suppressive role for this enzyme [83]. However, to date, biological impacts of these changes in DNMT2 expression are still poorly understood. It could be linked to protein homeostasis and/or cell death as revealed by Adamczyk-Grochala et al. which found that absence of DNMT2 in cancer cells sensitizes them to endoplasmic reticulum stress-induced apoptosis [84]. Increasing the possibilities of its role in cancer, somatic mutations of DNMT2 have been revealed as impacting its methyltransferase activity in a wide range (almost inactive to twofold increase), pinpointing distinct functional roles in tumorigenesis [85]. Compared to DNMT2, DNMT3L has been less intensively studied in cancer. So far, DNMT3L has been identified as upregulated in gastrointestinal, prostate and testicular germ cell tumors, as well as described as a novel marker essential for the growth of human embryonal carcinoma [82, 86, 87]. Conversely, low expression of DNMT3L is associated with a poor prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma, highlighting its potential as prognostic biomarker and therapeutical strategy [88]. Given DNMT3L acts as both cofactor and regulator of DNMT3A and DNMT3B, comprehensive research into the involvement of DNMT3L in cancer would be highly valuable.

As DNMT1, DNMT3B is considered as a prognostic marker in cancer, as demonstrated by So et al. who showed a positive correlation between high expression of DNMT3B and poor clinical outcomes in breast cancer [89]. This group highlighted that overexpression of DNMT3B altered key signaling pathways in cell survival, proliferation and invasion (STAT3, NFκB, PI3K/Akt, β-catenin, Notch signaling), thus explaining that tumor cells, which strongly expressed DNMT3B had a higher metastatic and tumor invasion capacity than those with low DNMT3B levels [90]. The authors also demonstrated that 5-azacytidine combined with an anti-inflammatory molecule, increased survival in mice. Despite the outstanding results, it is important to nuance them given the non-specific nature of 5-azacytidine, which inhibits all DNMTs. Considering that high levels of DNMT1 and DNMT3A are also positively correlated with poor outcomes in breast cancer, it cannot be ruled out that the effect observed also depends on the inhibition of DNMT1 and/or DNMT3A. Further studies to discriminate against the involvement of different DNMTs in the pathophysiology of breast cancer would help to refine the development of new therapeutic strategies. Interestingly, DNMT3B can also act as a tumor suppressor. In fact, Lopusna et al. mentioned that absence of the DNMT3B catalytic domain was associated with acceleration of lymphomagenesis via upregulation of the Met, Stat3 and Lin28b oncogenic pathways. The authors also pinpoint that, as for DNMT3A, some mutations occurs in DNMT3B in human hematologic malignancies [91].

DNA methyltransferase 3A is a pivotal actor in cancer

Based on the Catalogue Of Somatic Mutations In Cancer (COSMIC) database, DNMT3A is the most mutated DNMT-encoding gene in cancer [92]. DNMT3A has been claimed as mutated or modulated in numerous forms of cancer, including lung, breast adenocarcinoma and more importantly in acute myeloid leukemia (Fig. 2A and B) [93]. From all the somatic mutations distributed throughout the DNMT3A gene, most of these mutations are substitutions (> 50% regardless of the affected domain) and more particularly missense substitutions. Interestingly, while it accounts for only one third of the total protein, almost half of all the cancer related mutations (44.7%) occur in the MTase domain (Fig. 2C). The predominance of mutations in the MTase domain reflects their greater functional impact, which in turn facilitates their detection. It is also possible that this overrepresentation is associated with the selective advantage (e.g., proliferation, cell survival) conferred by such mutations. Strikingly, arginine 882 (R882) represents a hotspot for DNMT3A mutations, accounting for approximately 58% of all mutations within the MTase domain, and nearly 98.5% of R882-mutant events are detected in myeloid malignancies, highlighting a strong bias toward blood cancers rather than a proportional distribution across other tumor types.

DNMT3A in acute myeloid leukemia

From age-related clonal hematopoiesis to acute myeloid leukemia

With age, mutations accumulate in somatic cells, including adult stem cells, leading to somatic mosaicism [95]. This phenomenon is common (~ 10% of persons older than 65 years old) and frequently observed in human tissues throughout ageing, including blood, where it is referred to as clonal hematopoiesis (CH) [96–102]. Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) is a subset of clonal hematopoiesis in which somatic mutations occur in hematologic malignancy–associated driver genes and reach a variant allele frequency above 2%, but are not accompanied by abnormal blood counts or any other manifestations of hematologic disease [103]. However, individuals with clonal hematopoiesis have an increased risk of developing hematologic cancers, as well as cardiovascular diseases (e.g., coronary heart disease, ischemic stroke, chronic heart failure) and chronic liver disease [100, 101, 104–106]. Interestingly, DNMT3A—the most frequently mutated gene—and TET2 represent the principal drivers of clonal hematopoiesis, with roughly 70% of detected mutations occurring in these two genes [107].

Although CHIP reflects a pre-malignant state that confers a growth advantage to mutant hematopoietic cells, with CpG sites altered by DNMT3A mutations partially overlapping with those affected in AML with DNMT3A mutations, the methylation landscapes of these pre-malignant clones differ from those of acute myeloid leukemia [108]. In CHIP, DNA hypomethylation affects regions involved in stem cell reprogramming and transcription factor binding motifs sites implicated in hematopoiesis and proliferative processes, thus creating a DNA methylation landscape that favorizes hematopoietic stem cells self-renewal [108]. AML is marked by extensive methylome reprogramming, combining genome-wide hypomethylation with focal hypermethylation at key regulatory loci, including tumor suppressor genes such as p15INK4b and critical hematopoietic regulators such as CEBPA. These changes generate highly subtype-specific epigenetic profiles that lock cells into leukemic transcriptional programs and enforce a stable differentiation arrest [109–113].

Investigating the DNMT3A R882H hotspot mutation in acute myeloid leukemia

AML is the most common form of acute leukemia and is defined by malignant clonal expansion and differentiation arrest of myeloid progenitor cells [114]. In 2010, Ley et al. reported somatic mutations in DNMT3A in the genome of cells from de novo AML patients, and that such mutations were associated with a decrease in median overall survival. Out of the 281 patients included in the study, 62 (22.1%) harbored DNMT3A mutations. Among these, heterozygous missense mutations affecting arginine 882 within the catalytic domain were the most frequent (60%) and were associated with a reduced DNA methylation, underscoring the hypomorphic nature of the mutation. Interestingly, DNMT3A protein levels remained unchanged in the presence of the mutation, ruling out haploinsufficiency and instead suggesting a dominant-negative effect or potential gain-of-function mechanism [115]. Although recurrent mutations at a single amino acid position often suggest a gain-of-function, this study provided no direct evidence supporting such an effect, highlighting the need for further mechanistic investigations.

Several studies have described the molecular mechanisms linked to DNMT3AR882H, the most prevalent R882 mutation observed in humans. The first evidence of the dominant negative effect of the DNMT3AR882H was reported in vitro. By exogenously overexpressing Dnmt3aR878H (the mutation in mice corresponding to human DNMT3AR882H), Kim et al. reported that the mutation severely impaired mutant’s DNA methyltransferase activity, without affecting its ability to interact with wild-type enzymes (DNMT3A, DNMT3B and DNMT3L). Moreover, even though the underlying mechanism was not fully elucidated, the authors showed that mutant DNMT3AR882H reduced the activity of both wild-type DNMT3A and DNMT3B in murine embryonic stem cells (Fig. 3.1) [116]. This reduction in activity may result from the mutant’s inability to form functional complexes. Indeed, the oligomerization of DNMT3A has been described as controlling its catalytic properties [117]. DNMT3A forms either heterotetramers with DNMT3L (DNMT3L-DNMT3A-DNMT3A-DNMT3L) or homotetramer in absence of DNMT3L [117]. Holz-Schietinger et al. revealed that in absence of DNMT3L, R882H mutation disrupted homotetramer formation, favoring a homodimeric form which reduces the methylation processivity of consecutive CpG, which can be significantly improved with the addition of DNMT3L (Fig. 3.2) [118]. This result might be explained by the position of the arginine 882, which is localized at the interface that mediates the DNMT3A-DNMT3A contact (regulatory domain interface). Although this model cannot explain the results obtained by Kim et al. in mouse embryonic stem cells where DNMT3L is highly expressed, it remains particularly relevant in AML, since DNMT3L is absent or weakly expressed in AML cells [115, 119, 120]. These studies also pinpoint that DNMT3L could be a therapeutic avenue, which was recently reinforced by Li et al. who demonstrated that overexpression of DNMT3L corrected the hypomethylation of Dnmt3aR878H/+ cells [121].

The dominant-negative effect was not shown by Russler-Germain et al. when increasing amounts of DNMT3AR882H were mixed with fixed amount of DNMT3Awild−type. The authors justified this result by explaining that this “in vitro” mix did not reflect exactly what happened in AML cells, in which wild-type and mutant proteins are produced and folded concomitantly. To reproduce physiological conditions, human embryonic kidney (HEK) cells were cotransfected with DNMT3Awild−type and DNMT3AR882H. This “in vivo” mix displayed a reduction of the methyltransferase activity by ~ 80% compared to the normal protein, thus highlighting a dominant-negative effect. In these conditions, Russler-Germain et al. corroborated Holz-Schietinger et al. results, by demonstrating that DNMT3AR882H not only exhibited a failure in homotetramerization but also impeded the formation of the DNMT3Awild−type homotetramer, the most enzymatically active configuration of the methyltransferase in absence of DNMT3L (Fig. 3.2) [117, 120]. This result has been challenged by Emperle et al. as they did not observed a dominant-negative effect of the R882H mutation on complexes formation [122]. Despite the presence of DNMT3AR882H-DNMT3Awild−type complexes, Emperle et al. only assessed the effect of the mutation under conditions close to the “in vitro” mix of Russler-Germain et al.. It would be relevant to assess whether the results would be different in conditions similar to the “in vivo” mix.

Recently, Nguyen et al. further supported the dominant-negative effect, revealing that DNMT3AR882H stabilized large DNMT3A oligomers, which was rescued by DNMT3L (Fig. 3.2) [123]. This study also underscored the reduction in DNA binding of DNMT3AR882H (Fig. 3.3). These two distinct mechanisms explained the lower DNA methyltransferase activity compared to the wild-type (reduction of ~ 70%) [123]. The formation of these oligomers may, in part, be explained by the increased stability of interactions between two DNMT3AR882H monomers, as demonstrated by Mack et al. [124]. The substitution of an arginine by a histidine at position 882 promotes the formation of inter-subunit contacts in the regulatory domain interface, thereby facilitating the assembly of R882H/R882H. These results suggested a dominant gain-of-function effect and explain the lower DNA methyltransferase activity relative to the wild-type (reduction of ~ 60%) (Fig. 3.2).

The subnuclear localization, which is unchanged with the R882H mutation, cannot explain aberrant DNA methylation and gene expression in AML [120, 123, 125]. Beyond its impact on the enzyme’s methyltransferase activity and oligomerization, the DNMT3AR882H mutation also affects its preference for CpG flanking sequences [124, 125]. This result was later confirmed by Anteneh et al., who observed that R882H mutation altered CpG specificity, as evidenced by increased methylation at non-CpG sites; and flanking sequence preference, as highlighted by off-target CpG methylation (Fig. 3.4) [126]. Mutational analyses of DNMT3A have shown that a single amino-acid substitution at position R882 alters DNA binding and flanking sequence preferences without fully abolishing enzymatic activity, thereby uncoupling CpG recognition from local sequence readout. This finding underscores the multifaceted impact of mutations at this position and provides a plausible mechanistic framework for how the R882H DNMT3A mutation reshapes methylation landscapes at specific sets of CpG sites in AML [66].

In conclusion, several mechanisms could explain the changes in DNA methylation patterns observed in AML. Collectively, these findings support a hybrid model in which DNMT3AR882H exerts a dominant-negative effect that reduces overall DNMT3A-mediated methylation capacity, while concomitant gain-of-function properties in sequence preference and local activity actively reshape CpG-specific methylation patterns and distort the epigenetic landscape in AML. Despite the mechanistic contribution of these studies, it would be interesting to validate them in cells from patients with AML carrying the R882H mutation.

DNMT3A as a driver for tumorigenesis and chemoresistance in acute myeloid leukemia

Aberrant DNA methylation patterns due to DNMT3A mutations can drive transcriptional alteration of genes belonging to hematopoietic malignancies, such as Mpl and Hlf, as well as pathways linked to malignant and clonal hematopoiesis pathway (Meis1-Mn1-Hoxa), as reported in murine brown marrow and murine leukemia-initiating stem cell/hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells [127, 128]. By sequencing exome from AML patients, Yan et al. were able to identify 6 missense mutations in DNMT3A present in regulatory region and catalytic domain. Several HOX family genes and IDH1 were upregulated with mutants of DNMT3A, probably linked to a hypomethylation, as demonstrated for HOXB2 [129]. Considering the role of HOX genes in AML pathogenesis and the poor survival associated with their high expression, this could explain the reduced survival observed in patients with DNMT3A mutations [130, 131]. The authors also demonstrated that DNMT3AG543C mutation, situated within a minor hotspot close to the histone H3K4 binding surface, increases the ability of DNMT3A to interact with histone H3 in vitro [129, 132, 133]. Investigating DNA methylation profiles could offer valuable insights into whether this mutation promotes off-target CpG and non-CpG methylation and abnormal gene expression. In addition to upregulate genes involved in clonal hematopoiesis, DNMT3AR882H mutation has been also reported to exhibit high proliferative and anti-apoptotic activities through the decrease in cl-PARP and Bax (pro-apoptotic proteins) and the increase in Bcl2, Bcl-xl and Mcl1 (anti-apoptotic proteins) in KG-1a and THP1, two AML cell lines [134]. These effects on apoptotic and proliferative pathways may underlie the pronounced growth advantage and enhanced self-renewal capacity observed in DNMT3AR882-mutated cells compared with DNMT3Awild−type cells in AML with partial tandem duplication of histone-lysine N-methyltransferase 2A [135].

Cancerous cells exhibited a distinct metabolic signature compared to normal cells [136]. Cellular metabolic reprogramming stands as a crucial hallmark in cancer, involved in the neoplastic initiation and tumor progression [137]. Mice harboring Dnmt3aR878H mutation has been reported to overexpress NAMPT—an enzyme involved in the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) metabolism, due to the hypomethylation of its promoter. This overexpression led to abnormal nicotinamide and NAD synthesis, thus promoting cell cycle progression and leukemia development. Inhibition of NAMPT caused cell apoptosis, cycle arrest and differentiation in U937 mutant cells, and prolonged lifespan and reduced splenic infiltration in tumor-bearing mice (Table 1) [138]. Yang et al. demonstrated that DNMT3AR882H mutant K562 cells exhibited upregulation of genes involved in the glutathione synthesis (e.g., CTH, PSPH, PSAT1, SLC7A11), resulting in a marked elevation in glutathione levels, thereby promoting cell proliferation and chemoresistance. The authors further revealed that a treatment combining sulfasalazine, a specific inhibitor of SLC7A11, and daunorubicin, an anticancer treatment used in AML, promoted greater therapeutic effects compared to daunorubicin alone [139]. Although not directly assessed, considering the well-established antioxidant role of glutathione, the authors suggested that DNMT3AR882H-mutant cells may exhibit enhanced antioxidant capacity, which could contribute to their chemoresistance—a hypothesis that warrants further investigation in future studies.

Biological impact and clinical repercussion of mutation and modulation of DNMT3A in cancer

In addition to direct effects on tumorigenesis-associated genes through hypomethylation at the promoter level, DNMT3A mutations act via other mechanisms. DNMT3AR882H promotes cell survival and resistance to daunorubicin through glycolysis via the activation of the NRF2-NQO1 pathway in KG-1a and THP1 cell lines. Inhibition of the NRF2-NQO1 pathway re-sensitized mutant cells to daunorubicin [134, 140]. Interestingly, the authors highlighted that the mutant DNMT3A increased NRF2 expression not via the hypomethylation of its promoter but by increasing its half-life. The change in DNMT3A partner proteins could be one of the mechanisms explaining this increase in stability. Indeed, several missense mutations have been shown to alter the DNMT3A interactome. DNMT3A mutants interacted more with ubiquitin ligases, potentially accelerating their degradation. Conversely, interaction with DNMT3B3, which is the predominant DNMT3B isoform in AML cells and described as stimulating DNMT3A activity, is reduced [141]. The other hypothesis is an indirect effect mediated by DNA methylation. This is supported by the study of Yang et al. who demonstrated that DNMT3A mutation caused an overexpression of mTOR due to its hypomethylation, leading to an inhibition of the degradation of CDK1 by lysosomes. The authors also revealed that the accumulation of CDK1: (1) promoted cell proliferation due to its direct role in the cellular cycle; (2) blocked cellular differentiation through upregulation of HoxA family genes mediated by a reduction in the repressive mark H3K27me3 due to phosphorylation of EZH2; and (3) interacted directly with DNMT3A, preventing its interaction with EZH2 and therefore its recruitment to target genes to be repressed, such as WNT1, which promotes cell division and migration [142]. Thus, given the different possibilities, it would be relevant to assess the mechanism by which DNMT3A reduces the degradation of NRF2. Moreover, while cancer cells produce large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the NRF2-NQO1 pathway has an antioxidant role, the impact of the DNMT3AR882H mutation on this biological parameter remains unknown. This would be all the more relevant as ROS influence epigenetic modulators such as HDACs or DNMTs [143].

Several studies have shown that AML patients harboring DNMT3A mutations, particularly the R882 hotspot, exhibit resistance to conventional chemotherapy, worse clinical outcomes and lower remission rates, and reduced overall survival compared to patients without these mutations [144–148]. Intriguingly, a recent study reported that azacytidine, when combined with chemotherapy, significantly extended survival in patients with the DNMT3AR882 mutation, relative to non-mutant cases. DNMT3A-mutant cells are sensitized to azacytidine, which promotes hypomethylation at retrotransposons sequences such as endogenous retroviruses (ERVs), leading to translational stress and increased apoptosis [149]. This finding is particularly striking, as it suggests that even though DNMT3A’s methyltransferase activity is already impaired by mutation, the additional hypomethylating effect of azacytidine can still modulate the epigenetic landscape in a way that enhances therapeutic efficacy. These results support the hypothesis that, beyond global hypomethylation, defective or aberrant methylation targeting, as seen with the DNMT3AR882 mutation, plays a pathogenic role in AML, and that DNA hypomethylating drug can exploit these vulnerabilities.

In conclusion, since the discovery of DNMT3A mutations in patients with AML in 2011, considerable research efforts have focused on elucidating their biological consequences. Numerous studies have revealed alterations in key signaling and epigenetic pathways, leading to the proposal of new therapeutic strategies. However, given the involvement of DNMT3A in a very large number of biological processes, continued research is essential to deepen our understanding and to develop more targeted and effective treatments. Moreover, DNMT3AR882 mutations impact the presence of other mutations as highlighted by Bezerra et al. who revealed that NPM1 and FLT3-ITD mutations were significantly enriched in DNMT3AR882 compared with non-R882 mutations or wild-type DNMT3A [150]. The co-occurrence of these mutations could be a source of considerable variability in results, further complicating AML research.

DNMT3A in solid tumor

The ambivalent role of DNMT3A

Highlighting its duality, DNMT3A appears to act as both an oncogene and a tumor suppressor gene. For instance, in the context of KRAS-driven cancers, KRAS—a GTPase involved in signal transduction—is frequently mutated, with the G12D variant being the most common activating mutation that enhances cell proliferation and survival [151]. Recent studies have shown that DNMT3A knockout exacerbates leukemia in KrasG12D mutant cells by altering the expression of genes associated with cell cycle regulation, apoptosis and inflammation, thereby supporting a tumor-suppressive role for DNMT3A in this specific context [152]. Focusing on lung cancer, Gao et al. further demonstrated that conditional pulmonary knockout of Dnmt3a in a murine lung adenocarcinoma model carrying heterozygous KrasG12D mutation led to more advanced and larger tumors compared to wild-type. This accelerated lung tumor growth can be explained by altered expression of genes involved in key stages of tumor progression, such as angiogenesis, cell adhesion and cell motion pathway. Taken together with the reduced survival observed in DNMT3A-deleted mice and the observation that its loss did not affect tumor initiation, the authors concluded that Dnmt3a acts as a tumor suppressor gene [153]. In line with these results, Husni et al. reported that high DNMT3A expression in lung adenocarcinoma patients was associated with more favorable outcomes compared to those with weak expression, identifying DNMT3A as an independent prognostic marker [154].

More recently, Na et al. demonstrated in a murine model of small-cell lung cancer that deficiency of KMT2C, a histone methyltransferase, led to the reduced DNMT3A expression, which, through epigenetic remodeling, simultaneously repressed antimetastatic genes and activated prometastatic genes, including Meis2 and multiples Hoxb genes, through hypomethylation. Using three independent SCLC cohorts, the authors also demonstrated that low DNMT3A expression is associated with poor prognosis in SCLC patients, reinforcing the antitumoral role of DNMT3A [155]. Expanding the scope beyond lung cancer, Yu et al. revealed that in pancreatic adenocarcinoma, among the 11 methylated-5-cytosine regulatory genes identified as mutated, only DNMT3A showed a significant correlation with patients’ clinical grade. High DNMT3A expression was positively associated with good prognosis, while low DNMT3A expression was correlated with poor prognosis and linked to altered ribosomal processing. Considering that cancerous cells are metabolically active compared to normal cells and require increased protein synthesis, these findings suggest that low DNMT3A expression may promote cancer cell proliferation and tumor survival (Table 1) [156].

In an opposite manner, DNMT3A can be associated with adverse effects in cancer. Jing et al. uncovered an unfavorable association between DNMT3A and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, where elevated DNMT3A expression correlated with diminished overall survival. Mechanistically, DNMT3A was found to promote cell proliferation by activating the STAT3 signaling pathway, while suppressing apoptosis through the extrinsic apoptotic pathway [157]. Similarly, in gastric cancer, high DNMT3A expression correlated with unfavorable clinicopathological features and poor patient survival, supporting its role as a prognostic biomarker. These effects may be partly explained by DNMT3A-mediated modulation of the tumor microenvironment, particularly its influence on tumor-infiltrating immune cells. Notably, the knockdown of DNMT3A in gastric cancer cells resulted in reduced proliferation and migration, underscoring its potential as an immunotherapeutic target [158]. Ye et al. revealed that DNMT3A was involved in the hypermethylation of the dermatopontin promoter, which led to downregulation of its expression, thus contributing breast cancer cell growth, migration and invasion [159]. Similarly, Yang et al. recently showed that DNMT3A can silence ADAMTS8 in breast cancer, which in turn activates the EGFR-MEK-ERK signaling pathway and drives cancer cell proliferation and invasion [160]. Consistently, aberrant hypermethylation of tumor suppressor gene promoters (FHIT, p16INK4a, and RARβ) was also reported by Lin et al. in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [161]. However, these results were directly challenged by Sato et al., who revealed that there is no discernible correlation between the promoter hypermethylation of various tumor suppressor genes such as p14ARF, p16INK4a and RASSF1A and the expression levels of any of the DNMTs in samples of NSCLC [162]. In addition to its role in tumor progression, DNMT3A also influences sensitivity to cancer treatment. Jing et al. showed that downregulation of DNMT3A led to increased chemosensitivity to gemcitabine and oxaliplatin by disrupting CHK1 and AKT activation in Panc-1 pancreatic cancer cells [163]. More recently, a new genomically defined subset NSCLC harboring loss-of-function mutations in DNMT3A displayed heightened sensitivity to PD-(L)1 blockade, which was associated improved objective response rates, longer median progression-free and overall survival in patients [164] (Table 1).

In conclusion, the ambivalence of DNMT3A in cancer makes the elucidation of its role even more complex. By impacting gene expression, DNMT3A plays a role in tumor cell proliferation, survival and chemoresistance, ultimately affecting patient prognosis.

The DNMT3A-miRNAs axis

MicroRNAs (miRNAs), a subclass of non-coding RNAs, have a significant influence over gene expression regulation. Typically, miRNAs interact with the 3’ untranslated region of target messenger RNAs (mRNAs), instigating mRNA degradation and consequent translation repression [165]. In the intricate landscape of cancer pathophysiology, the miRNAs-DNMT3A axis emerges as a crucial mechanism. DNA methylation, and more particularly DNMT3A, has been extensively reported as being regulated by miRNAs in breast cancer [166]. Recently, Lu et al. reported that DNMT3A reduced miR-603 expression, leading to the upregulation of hexokinase-2, the first-rate limiting enzyme in aerobic glycolysis, in ovarian cancer cell lines (SKOV3 and A2780). As glycolysis-derived lactate drives the Warburg effect by both supplying energy and acidifying the tumor microenvironment, thereby facilitating cancer invasion, migration, and proliferation, DNMT3A may act as a key contributor to ovarian cancer pathogenesis [167]. Supporting this conclusion, Lu et al. showed in Caov-3, an ovarian cancer cell line, that DNMT3A overexpression led to a decrease in miR-182-induced apoptosis through inhibition of caspase-3 and -9 activity [168]. In pancreatic cancer, Wang et al. revealed that miR-532-3p, which was downregulated, directly targeted DNMT3A. The resulting overexpression of DNMT3A led to downregulation of SOCS2—a gene described as negative regulator of the JAK/STAT pathway, through hypermethylation of its promoter. Interestingly, miR-532-3p overexpression suppressed proliferation, invasion, and migration of pancreatic cancer cells, as well as tumor formation in nude mice, by inhibiting SOCS2 methylation through interacting directly with DNMT3A and negatively regulated its expression [169]. Using a similar approach, Yan et al. presented findings in human lung cancer tissue, demonstrating a negative correlation between miR-101 and DNMT3A expression. Overexpression of miR-101 resulted in in vivo tumor regression, reduced DNMT3A levels, and lower DNA methylation in tumor [170]. More recently, Liu et al. revealed that miR-708-5p directly suppresses DNMT3A translation, reducing DNA methylation and upregulating expression of tumor suppressor CDH1. These results propose a biological axis explaining why lung cancer patients with high miR-708-5p levels show consistent better survival and longer remission [171]. Beyond tumorigenesis, the DNMT3A-miRNAs axis also influences sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents. In cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancer cells (A2780/CDDP), decreased miR-143 levels led to DNMT3A overexpression. This, in turn, increased cisplatin resistance, while silencing DNMT3A restored drug sensitivity. Remarkably, DNMT3A-mediated hypermethylation of the miR-143 precursor gene created a negative feedback loop, leading to miR-143 downregulation—a critical epigenetic mechanism implicated in cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer [172] (Table 1).

In conclusion, the DNMT3A-miRNAs axis appears to play an important role in cancer pathophysiology, and is therefore a promising area of research. The study of other non-coding RNAs such as long non-coding RNAs is also of great interest [173].

Interplay between enzymes regulating DNA methylation

Although they have completely opposed biological functions, DNMT3A and TET2 are both mutated in AML and are both associated with a reduction in patient survival [174]. Making this interaction even more intriguing, DNMT3A and TET2 are co-mutated in several types of cancer, including lung, breast, skin, kidney as well as leukemia [175]. Without elucidating the underlying mechanism, Yang et al. revealed that co-occurrence of TET2 and DNMT3A mutations improves the response of different cancer cell lines to 4’-thio-2’ deoxycytidine. In fact, the authors showed a reduced cell survival in breast (BT549), lung (NCI-H23), melanoma (SKMEL5) and renal (ACHN) cancer lines harboring both deleterious TET2 and nonsynonymous DNMT3A mutations, as opposed to cell lines lacking this specific mutation pattern [175]. Conversely, instead of enhancing sensitivity to a treatment, TET2 and DNMT3A can act synergistically to promote drug resistance. In 2024, Cheng et al. demonstrated that both DNMT3A and TET2 were upregulated in sorafenib-resistant hepatocellular carcinoma cells, associated with an increase in 5mC and 5hmC levels. Overexpression of DNMT3A led to hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes such as p15 and SOCS2, thereby explaining their silencing and the emergence of sorafenib resistance. The authors revealed that simultaneous knockdown of both DNMT3A and TET2 led to a more robust inhibition of cell proliferation and migration than knockdown of either gene alone, thus pinpointing a functional cooperation between these two proteins. Consistent with this result, they showed that DNMT3A, TET2 and HDAC2 interact and that this complex localizes to the promoter of oncogenes such as CDK1, CCNA2 and RASEF. These oncogenes are consistently upregulated in sorafenib-resistant cells explaining the elevated proliferation and migration capacity. Counterintuitively, these expression shifts occur without DNA methylation alterations, suggesting that both DNMT3A and TET2 have DNA methylation-independent molecular functions in this context (Fig. 4) [176]. Nevertheless, this conclusion warrants caution, since the approach employed to quantify DNA methylation (methylation-specific PCR) failed to detect a reduction in DNA methylation following 48 h of decitabine treatment, a known DNMT inhibitor.

TET2 is not the only epigenetic enzyme interacting with DNMT3A in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). In 2024, Yan et al. revealed that high DNMT3L expression in HCC patients predicted good prognosis and limited tumor progression. Through mining cancer databases and conducting biochemical and molecular biology analyses, the authors demonstrated that although DNMT3L is widely recognized as a cofactor of the de novo methyltransferases DNMT3A and DNMT3B, its overexpression paradoxically increased CDO1 expression by reducing its promoter methylation. Based on this unexpected role of DNMT3L, only previously described in embryonic stem cells within a specific genomic context, the authors proposed that DNMT3L competitively inhibits DNMT3A-mediated methylation of the CDO1’s promoter [88, 119] (Fig. 4). Considering that CDO1 acts like a tumor suppressor in HCC tumorigenesis and that its promoter hypermethylation is more frequent in advanced stages (III-IV) compared to early stages (I-II), DNMT3L may represent an promising therapeutic avenue to explore in the future [177, 178].

In conclusion, cancer is a multifactorial pathology associated with profound epigenetic remodeling. The functional interplay between enzymes modulating this epigenetic landscape underscores the need for an integrative perspective to identify novel mechanisms.

Perspectives and future directions

In recent years, many studies have focused on the role of DNMT3A in cancer. These studies have demonstrated that alterations in its expression and mutations play an active role in tumorigenesis, metabolic reprogramming, and cancer cell sensitivity and/or resistance.

In recent years, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) have emerged as a powerful tool in biomedical research, including oncology, offering a unique platform to dissect the molecular underpinnings of cancer and explore novel therapeutic strategies [179]. A key advantage of iPSC technology lies in its ability to retain patient-specific genetic and epigenetic signatures within a standardized and tractable system—an especially valuable feature given the prominent role of DNMT3A in hematologic and solid malignancies. However, Lee et al. demonstrated that AML cells bearing driver mutations are often refractory to reprogramming, raising questions about the feasibility of this approach in certain AML contexts [180]. This resistance may stem in part from profound epigenetic barriers that accompany the reprogramming of somatic cells to pluripotency [181]. Nonetheless, recent findings by Golubeva et al. reported successful reprogramming of AML patient cells into iPSCs, indicating that these barriers may be overcome under optimized conditions [182]. While technical and biological limitations remain, such as low reprogramming efficiency and disease-specific epigenetic constraints, iPSCs represent a promising two-dimensional (2D) culture model for studying cancer initiation and progression in a genetically defined, patient-specific context.

Unlike iPSCs, organoids are three-dimensional (3D) in vitro culture systems that more accurately recapitulate the structural and functional characteristics of native tissues, offering a physiologically relevant platform for cancer modeling [183]. Furthermore, co-culturing tumor cells with non-malignant stromal components enables the reconstruction of the tumor microenvironment, including its complex cellular interactions [184]. This is particularly important given the well-established role of the tumor microenvironment in promoting tumor progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance, as well as its emerging relevance as both a biomarker and a target for cancer therapy [185–189]. In addition to recapitulating the tumor microenvironment and thereby enabling a better understanding of the mechanisms involved in cancer, patient-derived organoids can also be used to predict treatment efficacy. Indeed, in a meta-analysis, Wensink et al. demonstrated that patient-derived organoids could discriminate clinical responses with high sensitivity and specificity [190]. This opens up new perspectives for treatment screening that is personalized and tailored to each patient.

Hypomethylating agents have emerged as promising therapeutic strategies, particularly in AML and other cancers. Nevertheless, as discussed by Agrawal et al., azanucleoside hypomethylating agents (azacitidine and decitabine) achieve only modest overall response rates of about 30–50%, in part because of suboptimal bioavailability and their reliance on nucleoside transporters with heterogeneous expression for cellular uptake [191]. Moreover, these molecules display metabolic instability, which contributes to their low half-lives in patients (approximately 35–40 min for subcutaneous azacitidine and intravenous decitabine) [192–195]. Mechanistically, decitabine and azacitidine are cytidine analogues that become incorporated into DNA (decitabine) or into both DNA and RNA (azacitidine), where they trap catalytically active DNMTs and thereby increase the risk of adverse effects. Decitabine, in particular, induces proteasome-dependent degradation of DNMT1, resulting in loss of DNA methylation through DNA replication and possible reactivation of silenced tumor suppressor genes [196, 197]. Because DNMT plays a central role in DNA damage repair, azanucleosides may also modulate DNA repair pathways [198]. Beyond this, azacitidine perturbs cholesterol and lipid homeostasis, whereas decitabine enhances the expression of enzymes that generate reactive oxygen species [199, 200].

In response to these limitations, second-generation hypomethylating agents (e.g., NTX-301, SGI-110/guadecitabine) have been developed to improve pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties [201, 202]. Ongoing preclinical and clinical studies are evaluating the therapeutic potential of these next-generation molecules [203–206]. Another major avenue involves combining hypomethylating agents with drugs targeting apoptosis (e.g., BCL-2), metabolism (e.g., IDH1, IDH2, glutaminase) or immune checkpoints (e.g., TIM-3, CD47, PD-1, PD-L1). Some of these combination strategies have shown encouraging activity, notably in overcoming primary or acquired resistance to hypomethylating agents [20, 207]. Finally, the development of selective DNMT inhibitors represents a major challenge, not only to directly treat cancers associated with a specific DNMT, but also in cancers that depend on other DNMTs. This is supported by a recent study from Bhogal et al., who showed that mutations in MTase domain of DNMT1 decreased the proliferation of AML cells harboring DNMT3A mutations [208].

Conclusion

In this review, we have highlighted the intricate effect of DNMT3A mutation in AML, with a specific emphasis on the hotspot arginine 882 mutation. This underscores the potential of DNMT3A as a therapeutic target for treating AML patients. While the R882 is the most prevalent mutation and is well-documented, comprehensive investigations of the other mutations are needed to better understand the associated mechanisms. Such insights are crucial for tailoring individualized treatments, overcoming therapeutic resistance, and minimizing the risk of cancer relapse. Furthermore, this review emphasizes the pivotal, yet sometimes opposing, roles played by DNMT3A in cancers, influencing both disease progression and response to therapy. DNMT3A emerges as a potential prognostic biomarker and a candidate for therapeutic strategies, either as a standalone target or in combination with chemotherapy and/or immune checkpoint inhibitors, to develop effective and personalized treatment approaches [209–211].
