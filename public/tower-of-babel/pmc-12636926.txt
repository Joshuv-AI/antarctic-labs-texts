Reducing drug-related harm by triggering proactive outreach
Authors: Lesley Campbell, Bev Fraser, Michelle Beattie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug-related deaths (DRDs) remain a global issue, with Scotland reporting the second-highest rate per million population. Although some areas have seen improvements, DRDs continue to rise in parts of the Scottish Highlands. Proactive outreach to those at highest risk is believed to reduce harm. This project tested and implemented a risk identification tool—the Trigger Checklist (TC)—to initiate assertive outreach in a remote Highland area by September 2023.The Model for Improvement was used to structure the improvement process. This included collaborative exploration of the problem and solution, development of the TC, devising a family of measures and Plan, Do, Study, Act cycles to structure interactive learning and refinement of the TC and outreach process. Data were collected on the number of completed TC, the percentage of those outreached within 48 hours and the number of days between incidents of non-fatal overdoses (NFOD). Timely staff experience feedback was gathered using a visual facial analogue scale.A standardised TC was devised and tested. 48 TCs were received over 8 months. Of those 100% (n=48) were assertively outreached within 48 hours of a TC referral. The median number of days between NFOD increased from 6.5 days (January–August 2022) to 23 days (September 2022–August 2023). There was an increase in the number of days between incidents of NFOD locally, with more than 90 days between two events (previously the maximum number reached was 48). For the duration of the project, the locality did not receive a DRD notification.There is a need to further test and standardise the use of the TC in other areas frequented by those most at risk of drug-related harm, such as the remote and rural emergency department.Keywords: Quality improvement, Checklists, Communication, Community Mental Health Services, Control charts/Run charts

Problem

Drug use is estimated to cause half a million deaths globally each year, with more than 70% involving an opioid.1 Opioid overdoses are rising worldwide, with many European countries being among those with the highest incidences.2 Scotland’s drug death rate of 327 per million population annually is the highest recorded in Europe, with Norway being the next largest at 85 per million.3 Scotland reports the second-highest recorded drug deaths annually per million population, with America recording the highest.2 Despite some improvement in the numbers of drug-related deaths (DRDs) within Scotland in recent years, some areas of the Scottish Highlands have observed a continued rise.4

The rural community for this project has an adult population of 21 291 and the estimated prevalence of drug use in Scotland is 1.62%.5 The average annual referral rate into the local recovery service is 173 people; approximately half of whom are referred due to a drug problem. As 1.62% of 21 291 would equate to 345 people, this suggests that three in four people who use drugs in this rural community are not accessing drug treatment services. The nearest acute hospital is over 100 miles away. Drug use is linked to levels of deprivation,6 and this rural community has areas of high deprivation. Furthermore, the project locality demonstrates the highest rate, per head of population, of drug-related hospital admissions in the Highland board area.7 Thus, supporting the claim that the recovery service is not reaching most people who use drugs.

NHS Highland is well versed in quality improvement (QI) methods and works closely with the Highland Alcohol and Drugs Partnership (HADP), a multiagency strategic partnership with a mission to reduce drug and alcohol related harm in Highland. The local drug and alcohol recovery service consists of a team lead (a nurse and project lead), three registered nurses and four support workers. This project aimed to test and implement a Trigger Checklist (TC) to identify those at high risk of drug-related harm and provide assertive outreach within 48 hours of referral for those living in a remote area of the Scottish Highlands by September 2023. The project commenced in January 2023.

Background

Assertive outreach has increasingly been recognised as a promising approach for engaging individuals at high risk of drug-related harm. Although study outcomes are variable,8 there is growing evidence supporting the effectiveness of the interventions delivered through assertive outreach, such as harm reduction, engagement with services and improved treatment retention. Colledge-Frisby et al9 suggest that assertive outreach may offer a protective element for vulnerable individuals, particularly when combined with timely and targeted interventions. Despite the limited volume of research, UK policy has endorsed assertive outreach as a strategic component in addressing drug-related harm, reflecting its perceived value in reaching populations who may not engage with traditional services. This is based on two premises. First, people who use drugs often have low self-worth partly due to being marginalised by society and therefore do not often reach out for help due to the belief that no one cares. Second, that the contexts in which the TC can be used, such as police custody, is often a time of crisis for the person using drugs, which can initiate a readiness for behaviour change. The aim is that assertive outreach will support those at increased risk of drug-related harm to access harm reduction and be actively supported to engage in treatment. Assertive outreach for this project involves the outreach support workers (in pairs) visiting the person’s home address/known location within 48 hours of receipt of the TC and offering harm reduction interventions and support into the drug and alcohol recovery service.

The Scottish Government developed Medication Assisted Treatment (MAT) Standards, which promote equity of access to treatment across Scotland, placing each person at high risk of drug-related harm at the centre of their care.10 One of these standards states that ‘All people at high risk of drug-related harm are proactively identified and offered support to commence or continue MAT’.10 Public Health England also identifies that engaging those who use drugs in treatment saves lives.11

Although risk assessment is widely used across healthcare, no existing tool specifically identifies individuals most at risk of drug-related harm. However, numerous factors contribute to increased vulnerability, such as polysubstance use (taking more than one substance simultaneously or within a short timeframe), which significantly raises the risk of overdose.

Using empirical evidence and experience, one of the authors (LC) developed the TC in collaboration with key stakeholders, including individuals in recovery (online supplmental additional file 1: Trigger Checklist). The TC includes 2 screening questions to determine illicit drug use, 10 risk assessment questions and a free text section for additional comments from the referrer. The tool uses a low threshold for determining risk of drug harm with assertive outreach initiated for a ‘yes’ response to either of the two screening questions and to any one of the 10 risk questions. Completed TCs are then triaged and risk assessed by a recovery nurse before outreach (see online supplemental additional file 2: Triage Decision Tree). All outreaches are attempted within 48 hours of referral.

Referrals can be made by anyone working in government services, as well as family members or loved ones who can refer a person they are concerned about. The decision to use the TC is based on a belief—through presentation, behaviour or self-disclosure—that the individual is using drugs (excluding cannabis only) and may be at risk.

Due to challenges in recruiting a nurse for the local drug and alcohol recovery service, funding was reallocated to expand the outreach support worker team from two to four. These workers are trained to deliver harm reduction interventions, including naloxone distribution, injecting equipment provision, signposting to internal and external support services and referral facilitation to the drug and alcohol recovery service. Clients who opt for referral are assessed for opioid agonist therapy, detoxification or other appropriate treatments.

The aim of this QI project was to test and implement the TC and assertively outreach those identified as high risk of drug-related harm in the project locality within 48 hours by September 2023.

Measurement

A measurement plan was developed collaboratively with the project team (see online supplemental additional file 3: Measurement Plan). Data were collected on the number of TCs received by the local drug and alcohol recovery service, which were collected by the outreach support workers. The number and name of service organisations of those who had completed a TC was documented by the project lead to establish implementation and areas for improvement. The percentage of those outreached within 48 hours was captured by the project lead and documented monthly. The outcome measures of the number of days between incidents of non-fatal overdoses (NFOD) within the project locality were collected from the Scottish Ambulance Service database (accurate and timely). The number of DRDs was captured from the HADP database. A balancing measure of staff experience was gathered using a visual facial analogue scale to monitor whether an increase in service user access was having a negative impact on staff experience.

Design

The Model for Improvement (MFI) has been used widely in healthcare and its success is thought to be related to the focus on incremental change in a complex system.12 MFI was also familiar to the project lead and QI supervisor and aligned well with the use of a value management board (which was already in use within the recovery service). The model has two parts: a ‘thinking’ part and a ‘doing’ part with iterative movement back and forward between the parts.12 The thinking part includes three questions. The first question seeks to determine the aim, and the team developed a driver diagram (see online supplemental additional file 4: Driver Diagram) to explore the problem and agree the aim. Question two asks how we will know the change is an improvement and focuses on measurement. The measurement plan included a family of measures and the concept of measurement over time with the assumption that any variation inherent within the system would even out over time13 and allow the use of run chart rules for data interpretation (see online supplemental additional file 3: Measurement Plan). Question 3 of the MFI asks what changes can we make that will result in an improvement? The change idea of the TC and assertive outreach process was developed with key stakeholders and there was agreement that small scale Plan, Do, Study, Act (PDSA) testing would take place as to whole scale roll out and that feedback would be integrated into the new tool and process. The team created a current process flow map to understand the pathway (see online supplemental additional file 4: Current and Future State Process Map). The next part of the MFI is the PDSA cycles for which there were multiple. Key PDSA cycles are described in the Strategy section below.

The project was managed in three phases. Phase 1 consisted of introducing the use of the TC with Police Scotland and the health and social care Single Point of Access team, within the project’s locality. It is known that people who use drugs are more likely to get involved in criminality due to their high-risk behaviour and the chaotic lifestyle associated with drug use.14 Copies of the TC have been disseminated widely to police custody, emergency department (ED), community partners, etc. Concerned family/loved ones normally contact the service by telephone and a support worker will complete the TC for them and submit it for triage. The use of the TC with concerned loved ones/family members was added during January 2022 due to service user/family feedback.

Phase 2 commenced in May 2023 and included the locality rural general hospital. Implementation into the rural general hospital was agreed between the project lead, the clinical nurse manager of the hospital as well as the senior change nurses for both the ED and the admissions ward. As the drug and alcohol recovery team already had an embedded hospital liaison process, it was predicted that the new process would be easily adopted. Copies of the TC were disseminated and an informal explanation given on their use from staff from the recovery service. The outreach support workers chose to physically base themselves within the hospital for the first 2 weeks of phase 2, on a rotational basis, to provide consistent support and advice regarding the process to all members of staff.

Phase 3 commenced in August 2023 and included promoting the use of the TC to all community partners. The outreach support workers commenced a roadshow visiting various organisations to inform them of the project and inviting them to use the TC. The outreach support workers continued to collect feedback to further develop and refine the tool and the process (see online supplemental additional file 5: Future State Process Map).

Strategy

The key events in the development of the TC as a standardised mechanism commenced with opportunistic conversations around how to identify someone proactively before formal stakeholders’ meetings were arranged. Due to this, no formal QI tools were used initially to create the TC. However, the early stages of this project could be argued to have followed the model of appreciative inquiry which aims to define and find solutions within a community using an asset-based approach focusing on the positives within a problem.15 Numerous PDSA cycles were conducted throughout the project. Example summaries of PDSA ramps are detailed below.

Adapting the TC PDSA

Following the development of the TC, the drug and alcohol recovery team ran multiple PDSA cycles to test and adapt it. Consultation meetings were arranged with stakeholders to introduce the TC and feedback helped refine it prior to launching the project. The development of the TC and its inclusion in the referral assessment process became the initial PDSA cycle. Stakeholder meetings allowed the team to gain feedback, be aware of any potential challenges and create their initial PDSA cycles for implementation. Further feedback and testing continued to refine the TC until version 7 was used for the project.

Confidence in TC pathway PDSA

The support outreach workers needed to feel confident with the decision making and risk assessment processes of both the TC and outreach. The team lead was also mindful not to overwhelm the team with the additional workload. PDSA cycles demonstrated that the process was working well for the outreach workers, and they could commence the process within the timeframe. However, initial feedback identified that in ‘unusual circumstances’ such as if the person was already under the care of one of the nurses in the team, they would be less confident in their decision-making. This prompted the drug and alcohol recovery team to create and test a triage decision tree with good feedback (see online supplemental additional file 2). Further PDSA testing found outreach support workers developing confidence in the process and beginning to decide outcomes more quickly. Additionally, the cycle also highlighted requests from the outreach support workers to be involved in more aspects of the process, that is, the individual risk assessment and management planning of outreach requests.

Process flow PDSA

Initial testing identified fear and overwhelm from the team that they may be unable to cope with a sudden increase in demand. Learning from this feedback directed the project to be planned over three phases. With a small amount of TCs being received during the first phase of testing and, with growing confidence, testing did not create the increase prediction in workload. This enabled phase three to start in July rather than August. During each PDSA cycle, the outreach support workers collected feedback and adapted the process to ensure ease of access to outreach and harm reduction supplies/support.

Results

Over the 8-month period, 48 TC referrals were received. All 48 (100%) were assertively outreached within 48 hours of TC referral (see figure 1). The median number of days between NFOD increased from 6.5 days (January–August 2022) to 23 days (September 2022–August 2023), indicating a potential shift in the underlying process. This change also suggests a non-random signal, consistent with run chart rules.16 A trend was observed between incident number 19–23, which is statistically unlikely to occur by chance and may indicate a real change in the process being measured. Additionally, the number of days between NFOD incidents increased, with two occasions exceeding 90 days—previously, the maximum interval was 48 (see figure 2). Importantly, no DRD notifications were received in the locality during the project period.

Staff satisfaction was collected daily using an electronic visual facial analogue scale. Throughout the project, the staff satisfaction percentage remained with a consistent median of 75%. The team also collected qualitative feedback on what made the day a ‘good’ or a ‘bad’ day, and this was transferred to Pareto charts which were then used for future PDSA cycles. These results were balanced against the overall referral rate per month for the service, which also remained consistent and unchanged by the project.

Lessons and limitations

This project achieved the aim of developing a high risk of drug-related harm identification tool (TC) and assertive outreach for all services and concerned family for those living in a remote area of the Scottish Highlands by September 2023. During the 8 months of the project, the team used QI methodology to collaboratively learn from the data collected and support continuous improvement. Collaborative working was identified as a strength of the project and anecdotal feedback from those in recovery suggested that they felt ‘cared for’ because of the assertive outreach process.

While the recovery team recognises the complexity of drug problems and the need for earlier intervention, this project observed a reduction in incidence of NFOD and DRD during the project, suggesting that the use of the TC to assertively outreach those at risk of drug-related harm may offer a useful strategy. During the project, the highest number of days since NFOD was 101. Prior to the project, there was an NFOD on median every 6.5 days with the highest number of days between NFODs in 2022 being 48. Furthermore, for the duration of the project, the team did not have a DRD in the area. For the duration of the project, the locality did not receive any DRD notifications.

While early findings from the project are encouraging—particularly the absence of DRDs during the implementation period—the data are not sufficiently robust to establish a causal relationship between the intervention and outcomes. The project team believes that the proactive identification of individuals at high risk and assertive outreach efforts are contributing positively; however, several limitations must be acknowledged. Due to the small size of the locality and low incidence rates, specific DRD data are not accessible at the local level to protect individual confidentiality. Broader Highland-wide data are available but not considered a reliable comparator for assessing the project’s impact. Although data were collected over a 12-month period, this included retrospective baseline data. Given the phased implementation, a longer period of consistent data collection would be required to detect meaningful changes in outcome measures. As with all QI data, the inability to control for external variables limits the strength of conclusions. Changes in outcomes may be influenced by factors outside the scope of the intervention.

Key learning from the project demonstrated the potential to identify those at high risk of drug-related harm by using the TC. Developed as a low threshold proactive mechanism, allowing specialist staff to engage with, build trust and provide evidence-based harm reduction to those at risk of drug-related harm. Furthermore, stakeholders were asking for education around the high risk of drug-related harm. The project team agreed to co-create an educational poster to present to partner agencies beyond the scope of the project. The TC was designed to be educational in its support to identify those deemed at high risk of drug-related harm due to various vulnerabilities. It is therefore possible that the poster request is due to a lack of wider awareness of the rising drug death crisis and of the policy directive to support proactive outreach.

The preconditions relating to national education and local problem identification to create a vision and improvement aims are vital.16 The request for further education indicates that the TC process does not satisfy the need for education relating to drug-related harm. Furthermore, despite the drug and alcohol recovery team already having an embedded hospital liaison process, the assumption that the TC would be easily adopted within the ED was unfounded. Few referrals were received from the ED, and there was anecdotal evidence of missed opportunities. Learning from this project reaffirms what is known in implementation science—that interventions cannot just be ‘lifted and shifted’ from one context to another and expected to work. More focused work is required to tailor the specific implementation needs to the context. The preconditions described by Esain et al17 would support co-creating further PDSA cycles within each referring team. For example, what is needed to embed the TC in the ED is likely to differ from the needs of referrers in police custody.

Initial and ongoing barriers include concerns around patient confidentiality and data sharing from one service to another. While ethical care is paramount, Caldicott Principle 7 is ‘the duty to share information for individual care is as important as the duty to protect patient confidentiality’.18 The TC was designed to not require explicit consent and support from National Health Service (NHS) Highland’s Caldicott Guardian was obtained. One of the adaptations to the TC was making this point explicit on the TC to support an ethos of decision-making to support both risk and confidentiality. Despite this, the team continued to experience resistance from some individuals due to fears around information sharing without explicit patient consent. Information sharing barriers have been experienced in similar projects, such as the Turning Point Scotland and Simon Community Scotland19 project. This project also recommends support nationally to address these barriers.

During each phase of the project, TC referrals were noted to be inconsistent and dependent on individual staff members. Those who were working more closely with the drug and alcohol recovery team were more likely to use the TC than those who were not. This supports the importance of relationships and the necessity of understanding a vision to support improvement. The missed opportunities for identification and outreach were explored at a stakeholder meeting and it was evident that a lack of senior leadership approval and formal information sharing agreements between partners and NHS Scotland were continuing to cause reluctance to pass on information directly.

Despite the barriers regarding information sharing, the service user feedback was supportive of outreach and 96% of those outreached were willing to remain open to outreach in the future. This suggests that overall, those at risk of drug-related harm welcomed being identified as ‘at risk’ of drug-related harm and outreached without giving their explicit consent for the information to be shared. One such case example of this was when someone was outreached twice within the same month due to two separate TCs. The team visited the person and again made them aware of the risks, offered harm reduction supplies and a referral into the team. The person was welcoming of the expressed concern and on the second occasion accepted naloxone training and supply, which they had not previously. Four months later, the person referred to the team stating they felt able to ask for help as they now trusted the team. Prior to this, the person had never attended appointments offered for assessment.

Conclusions

This project demonstrated the potential improvement that can be made to reduce drug-related harm and death in Scotland. There remains much to achieve nationally in terms of information sharing, education and risk identification, but we have made an important step in shifting our care model from a reactive to a proactive approach, ultimately saving vulnerable lives. Sustainability of the project will be dependent on continued funding of the additional outreach support workers and wider adoption by all services, that is, ED, police custody. The project team would support further testing and learning to establish how to make the TC and associated outreach work across service boundaries.
