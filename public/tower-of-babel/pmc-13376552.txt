Inorganic nitrogen metabolic reprogramming of the gut microbiome drives fecal microbiota transplantation in ulcerative colitis
Authors: Yinlong Wang, Qihang Hou, Xinying Lv, Jiongyan Liu, Huimei Wang, Yan Zhao, Haonan Tong, Yanli Liu, Juan Du, Xiaojun Yang, Shengru Wu, Shuixiang He, Xin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) shows promise for ulcerative colitis (UC), its clinical success appears to be contingent upon the degree of donor microbiota engraftment. Using an LD50-based ecological model, our study reveals that inorganic nitrogen utilization capacity (IN-uc) critically determines gut microbial assembly in high oxidative stress environments, which significantly influences microbial engraftment outcomes. Building on this mechanistic insight, we engineer a probiotic-metabolite consortia designed to synergistically increase IN-uc in the gut ecosystem. We find that PM-mix14 alleviates oxidative stress-mediated colonization barriers of donor microbiota by catalyzing the conversion of excess reactive nitrogen species through multi-step reactions, promotes L-glutamate biosynthesis and ATP production, thereby ensuring greater similarity in the structure and function of the recipient microbiota to those of the donor. In multiple male murine models of colitis, PM-mix14 supplementation during FMT significantly improves microbial engraftment fidelity, which is correlated with increased anti-inflammatory responses and attenuated colonic pathology. Network meta-analysis of multiple clinical datasets further substantiates the prognostic value of donor gut microbial IN-uc in UC remission. Our findings establish the gut microbial IN-uc as an ecological driver of microbiota engraftment and present a rationally designed microbial therapy that optimizes FMT efficacy through targeted metabolic reprogramming.Subject terms: Applied microbiology, Ulcerative colitis, Mouse, Microbiome

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease with a rising global prevalence1. Owing to its recalcitrance and limited therapeutic options, UC imposes a substantial burden on healthcare systems. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has emerged as a promising intervention for UC through the restoration of gut microbial homeostasis2. However, its clinical efficacy remains inconsistent. Accumulating evidence indicates that the success of FMT in UC may be closely related to the degree of donor microbial engraftment3–5. However, the principles governing donor microbiota engraftment remain poorly resolved, impeding rational optimization of microbial therapeutics.

Current FMT strategies prioritize establishing donor-like taxonomic and functional profiles over mere diversity restoration6–8. However, longitudinal analyses revealed limited engraftment fidelity, with only 35% of donor-associated operational taxonomic units (OTUs) detected post-FMT (N = 436, SD = 27%), alongside the emergence of 39% novel specific OTUs (N = 295, SD = 28%)9. Consequently, even when recipients manage to achieve sufficient microbial species diversity, the species structure and function deviate significantly from those of the donor. A critical barrier is the severe oxidative stress imbalance in the intestinal lumen of UC patients, characterized by the excessive accumulation of reactive nitrogen species (RNS) and nitrite (a decomposition product of nitric oxide)10,11, whose enrichment either prevents the colonization of certain bacteria12 or alters their structure and function13, resulting in a final colonized species composition that diverges from that of the donor. This ultimately compromises the therapeutic efficacy of FMT and severely restricts its development in treating UC. Conventional UC therapies (e.g., infliximab14, 5-aminosalicylic acid15, ustekinumab16, and vedolizumab17) mitigate oxidative stress but incur prohibitive risks—nephrotoxicity, infusion reactions, and multiorgan complications15,18,19. Thus, developing RNS-scavenging strategies that concurrently increase the gut microbial nitrogen utilization capacity represents an unmet need in UC management.

To address this, we applied the ecological principles of stress-driven community assembly20,21, with the hypothesis that selective pressures could promote the evolution of the gut microbiota toward nitrogen-utilizing phenotypes. The median lethal dose (LD50) originally refers to the minimum number of bacteria required to cause the death of 50% of a certain population of animals with a specified body weight or age within a defined time frame via a designated infection route22. Karina K. innovatively utilized LD50-infected hosts to demonstrate that this method can activate the host’s coordinated defense mechanisms and be employed to uncover the key mechanisms influencing survival23. Additionally, Salmonella enterica Typhimurium (S.Tm) overcomes microbiota-mediated colonization resistance by inducing intestinal inflammation (colitis)24–26, leading to the excessive accumulation of RNS in the gut27,28. Leveraging this characteristic, we disrupted the gut microbiota homeostasis in mice via the use of S.Tm at the LD50 dose. By performing LD50-level S.Tm challenges, we created an evolutionary bottleneck in which only the microbiota capable of RNS detoxification and reactive nitrogen assimilation persisted.

Here, we show that inorganic nitrogen utilization capacity (IN-uc) is a critical ecological determinant of donor microbiota engraftment under oxidative stress, identified through an LD50-based model that applies oxidative stress as a selective pressure. Building on this mechanistic insight, we demonstrate that a rationally designed probiotic-metabolite consortium (PM-mix14) enhances IN-uc, improves microbial engraftment fidelity, and attenuates colonic pathology in multiple murine colitis models. Finally, we validate the translational relevance of IN-uc through network meta-analysis of human clinical FMT datasets. Our findings establish IN-uc as an ecological driver of microbiota engraftment and present a targeted microbial therapy to optimize FMT efficacy.

Results

Survivors-FMT facilitates the establishment of a donor-like functional profile in the recipient gut microbiota despite low α-species diversity

To investigate the factors influencing FMT engraftment, we hypothesized that under the selective pressure of survival in an LD50 model, the profound oxidative stress environment induced by S.Tm infection would prioritize the recovery of specific microbial functions crucial for community reassembly.

To test this hypothesis, we established an LD50 model, defined as the dose that kills 50% of the mice while triggering collaborative defense mechanisms in survivors (Fig. 1A). Specifically, SPF BALB/c mice orally infected with the LD50 dose of S.Tm exhibited 45% mortality with quantifiable clinical symptoms (Fig. 1B), independent of the initial body weight (Fig. S1A). The fecal microbiota from survivors (S-FMT) was transplanted into broad-spectrum antibiotic-treated mice. Upon subsequent challenge with the LD50 dose of S.Tm (Fig. 1A), the S-FMT recipients achieved 85% survival (Fig. 1C). These findings establish a robust FMT efficacy model.

To assess engraftment and ecological restructuring process, we analyzed fecal metagenomes from S-FMT recipients (alongside PBS-negative controls: CON; conventional FMT controls: C-FMT). Compared with those of the CON (P = 0.0075) and C-FMT (P = 0.0168) groups, the microbial species diversity of the S-FMT recipients was significantly lower (Fig. 1D). Consistent with this, richness estimates (ACE and Chao indices) were also lower in S-FMT than in C-FMT recipients (Fig. S1B). However, source tracking analysis indicated that 90.17% of species in S-FMT recipients were shared with CON, a proportion higher than that in C-FMT recipients (79.75%) (Fig. 1E). Moreover, interspecies similarity analysis revealed that the microbial composition of the S-FMT recipients did not significantly differ from that of the CON (P = 0.058), whereas that of the C-FMT recipients differed significantly from CON (P = 0.012) (Fig. 1F), confirming that S-FMT drives a highly selective engraftment process. Functionally, KEGG pathway analysis showed that 97.97% of functional features in S-FMT recipients aligned with those in CON, exceeding the 91.81 observed in C-FMT recipients (Fig. 1G). Concordantly, S-FMT shifted the gut microbiome functional profile significantly closer to that of healthy controls (Bray–Curtis = 0.1279 vs 0.1347; Fig. 1H).

Collectively, these results indicate that S-FMT drives a highly selective ecological restructuring in the recipient gut, promoting a functional profile that closely resembles that of a healthy microbiota, even under reduced species diversity.

S-FMT specifically enhances gut microbial inorganic nitrogen utilization capacity

To further elucidate the selective establishment of gut microbial function by S-FMT in recipients, we performed MaAsLin2 to identify significantly altered functional pathways between groups. KEGG Functional profiling indicated that S-FMT recipients exhibited a more abundant and diverse profile of key microbial metabolic functions (Fig. 2A).

Notably, the Nitrogen Metabolism pathway essential for efficient cellular proliferation29 was significantly enriched following S-FMT intervention (Coefficient = 110.96, FDR = 0.0002; Fig. 2A). Mapping the differentially abundant genes to the KEGG orthology (KO) database demonstrated a significant enhancement in the microbial capacity for gut microbial IN-uc, which is mediated specifically by bacterial nitrate respiration. This enhancement was not confined to isolated increases in single genes but represented broad upregulation across the entire pathway (Figs. S2A and 2B). Key genes involved in the process from nitrate utilization to L-glutamate synthesis consistently increased in abundance, including nitrate reductases such as narG (P = 0.0017) and napA (P = 0.0117); nitric oxide reductases such as norB (P = 0.0422) and nosZ (P = 0.0017); and glutamate dehydrogenases such as gudB (P = 0.0010) and gdhA (P = 0.0029) (Fig. 2C).

To obtain more microbe-specific functional insights, we also annotated the metagenomic data against the MetaCyc database. MaAsLin2 analysis of MetaCyc pathways confirmed the enrichment of specific modules related to nitrogen transformation in S-FMT recipients, including Nitrate reduction I (denitrification) and Ammonia oxidation II (anaerobic) (Fig. S2B). Together, the KEGG and MetaCyc analyses converged on the specific nitrite-L-glutmate metabolic route, reinforcing our mechanistic focus.

Given that metagenomic results reflect only genetic potential, we validated these findings via the use of untargeted metabolomics of fecal samples. This confirmed the significant enrichment of nitrogen metabolism-associated pathways, specifically glutamate metabolism (P = 0.0155) and nucleotide metabolism (P = 0.0038), in the S-FMT-treated mice (Fig. S2C).

To further functionally validate the enhancement of this pathway beyond omics data, we quantified key intermediate and end-product metabolites of L-glutamate synthesis, including nitrite (a stable metabolite of nitric oxide), ATP, L-glutamate, glutathione, glutamine, and oxidized glutathione. S-FMT intervention resulted in a significant decrease in the key pathway intermediate nitrite (P = 0.0038), a characteristic factor associated with intestinal inflammation, there were significant increases in the accumulated intermediate ATP (P = 0.0172), the end-products L-glutamate (P = 0.0019) and glutathione (P = 0.0001), a major derivative of glutamate (Fig. 2D). These biochemical measurements robustly corroborate the metagenomic and metabolomic findings. In summary, our integrated multiomics and biochemical analyses demonstrated that S-FMT intervention specifically enhances the gut microbial IN-uc.

Probiotics and metabolites likely act synergistically to enhance gut microbial IN-uc

To elucidate the mechanism by which S-FMT shapes functional microbiota establishment, we analyzed gut microbial taxonomic composition at the species level. S-FMT induced alterations in community structure, as evidenced by change in the relative abundance of the top 20 dominant species (Fig. 3A). Differential abundance analysis using MaAsLin2 identified specific bacterial species significantly enriched in S-FMT recipients, most notably Lactobacillus murinus (L. murinus) and Bacteroides acidifaciens (B. acidifaciens) (Fig. 3B).

To assess the relationships of these bacteria with gut microbial IN-uc, we performed Spearman correlation analysis between the top 10 species ranked by MaAsLin2 coefficient and key gut microbial IN-uc metabolites. L. murinus and B. acidifaciens showed highly significantly positively correlated with the IN-uc process (Fig. 3C). Validation by species‑specific quantitative PCR showed that the absolute colonization level of L. murinus was significantly increased in S‑FMT recipients (P = 0.0497), while B. acidifaciens exhibited an increasing trend (P = 0.0749) (Fig. 3D).

In addition to bacteria, S-FMT material contains abundant metabolites. To investigate potential interactions, we fractionated the S-FMT material via differential centrifugation into bacterial cells and cell-free metabolites, and the experimental design was the same as that in Fig. 1A (Fig. S3A, B). Both fractions independently conferred partial protection against S.Tm infection and enhanced the expression of IN-uc markers, yet their effects were significantly lower than those of intact S-FMT (Fig. S3C–E), suggesting potential synergy between bacterial cells and metabolites.

In support of these findings, untargeted fecal metabolomics revealed distinct metabolite profiles in S-FMT recipients (Fig. 3E). Hierarchical clustering on the basis of significantly altered metabolites (Fig. 3F) and OPLS-DA VIP scoring revealed the top 10 discriminant metabolites (Fig. 3G). Correlation analysis with key gut microbial IN-uc metabolites identified 3-aminobenzamide (3-AB) and phosphatidylcholine (PC) as strong candidates (Fig. 3H). Semiquantitative analysis of the liquid chromatography peak intensities confirmed the enrichment of 3-AB (P = 0.0329) and PC (P = 0.0170) following S-FMT intervention (Fig. 3I).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that S-FMT profoundly reshapes the gut microbial composition and metabolic processes, revealing a likely synergistic role for bacteria and metabolites in functionally sculpting the microbiota toward enhanced gut microbial IN-uc.

PM-mix14 synergistically enhances gut microbial IN-uc and promotes donor-like functional restoration of the recipient gut microbiota

To efficiently validate and screen the impact of identified differential factors on the gut microbial IN-uc, we established an in vitro gut microbial coculture system on the basis of established methodologies30. Analysis of the 16S rRNA gene data confirmed the system’s feasibility under our experimental conditions, as evidenced by the genus-level community structure and stability metrics (Fig. S4A–C). Crucially, L. murinus and B. acidifaciens thrived within this system (Fig. S4D), validating its utility for studying probiotic modulation of the gut microbiota in vitro.

To comprehensively investigate bacterium-metabolite interactions, we employed a full-factorial experimental design in which 16 combinations of omics-derived factors were tested in the fecal coculture system (Fig. 4A). Measurement of key gut microbial IN-uc metabolites (L-glutamate, ATP, nitrite, and glutathione) revealed that the PM-mix14 formulation (L. murinus, 3-AB, and PC) significantly enhanced the gut microbial IN-uc (Fig. 4B).

To further assess the capacity of PM-mix14 to increase gut microbial IN-uc, we challenged the system with varying sodium nitrite concentrations. PM-mix14 intervention markedly increased microbial nitrite tolerance (Fig. 4C). Notably, PM-mix14 also increased the maximum growth density (Fig. 4D), indicating the conversion of nitrite into a nutritional substrate. Growth subsequently normalized concomitantly with nitrite depletion and ATP reduction (Fig. 4E).

Given the inherent limitations of in vitro models, we validated these findings in vivo by colonizing SPF mice with PM-mix14 combined with FMT (PM-mix14 + FMT) (Fig. 4F). Analysis of key gut microbial IN-uc metabolites demonstrated that compared with FMT, PM-mix14 + FMT enhanced the gut microbial IN-uc (Fig. 4G). Metagenomic assessment of recipient fecal samples revealed that, compared with FMT alone, PM-mix14 + FMT promoted the more stable restoration of microbial diversity (Fig. 4H) and induced a genus-level community structure significantly closer to that of the donor (Fig. 4I). This donor-like convergence was evident in the PCoA of the microbial composition (Fig. 4J). Functionally, while FMT alone enriched diverse specific functions leading to functional deviation from the donor, PM-mix14 + FMT mitigated this deviation, yielding a functional profile more aligned with the donor state (Fig. 4K, L).

In summary, our findings demonstrate that PM-mix14 synergizes with FMT to effectively restore gut microbial IN-uc, reduce interindividual variation in the gut microbial structure/function, and promote the establishment of donor-like functionality. This represents a promising strategy to address the high variability in FMT efficacy observed in UC patients.

Therapeutic potential of FMT + PM-mix14 for ulcerative colitis

Our findings demonstrate that PM-mix14 significantly enhances gut microbial IN-uc and promotes robust functional microbiota establishment, providing a compelling rationale for investigating its physiological relevance. To evaluate the therapeutic potential of FMT + PM-mix14, we employed a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis model in SPF BALB/c mice. Five experimental groups were established: the PBS control, DSS, DSS + FMT, DSS + PM-mix14, and DSS + FMT + PM-mix14 groups. In light of the limited long-term efficacy of a single FMT administration in UC, this study adopted a sequential FMT treatment protocol following the paradigm established by the LOTUS trial31. Specifically, over the 10-day experimental period, mice received a total of eight FMT administrations (once daily, excluding the first and last day), ensuring sustained microbial engraftment and functional modulation. Metabolite analysis revealed profound gut microbial IN-uc impairment in DSS-induced colitis, characterized by excessive nitrite accumulation and significantly reduced levels of L-glutamate, ATP, and glutathione, signatures of exacerbated inflammation. While PM-mix14 alone reduced nitrite levels (potentially attributable to the intrinsic antioxidant capacity of L. murinus), FMT + PM-mix14 intervention produced a more pronounced effect: nitrite was further suppressed, accompanied by significant increases in L-glutamate, ATP, and glutathione, closely mirroring healthy control levels (Fig. 5B).

In the DSS-induced colitis model, FMT + PM-mix14 treatment significantly ameliorated DSS-induced pathology, as evidenced by reduced weight loss (Fig. 5C), lower fecal points (Fig. 5D), decreased fecal occult blood (Fig. 5E), and attenuated colon shortening (Fig. 5F). Histopathological analysis confirmed these findings, demonstrating that FMT + PM-mix14 protected colonic epithelial integrity against DSS-induced damage. Compared with the DSS group, the FMT + PM-mix14 treated group presented significantly improved crypt architecture and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 5G). Furthermore, Alcian blue-periodic acid Schiff (AB-PAS) staining revealed significantly increased goblet cell numbers in the FMT + PM-mix14 group (Fig. 5H), indicating enhanced mucosal protection.

To dissect the synergistic contribution of PM-mix14, we compared FMT combined with individual PM-mix14 components with the full mixture (Fig. S5A). FMT + PM-mix14 demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy across all clinical parameters—weight loss, fecal points, occult blood, and colon length—and significantly outperformed single-component interventions (Fig. S5B–E).

Finally, given the S.Tm infection model origin of these factors, we challenged mice with LD50-dose S.Tm following FMT + PM-mix14 intervention (Fig. S6A). FMT + PM-mix14 modestly mitigated infection-associated survival (Fig. S6B) and weight loss (Fig. S6C).

Collectively, these results establish FMT + PM-mix14 as a promising strategy that significantly outperforms conventional FMT or isolated components in ameliorating experimental colitis, primarily through restoring gut microbial IN-uc and mitigating inflammation.

Improvements in the systemic inflammatory environment and structure of the gut microbiota

To further delineate the scope of the potent therapeutic effects of FMT + PM-mix14, we examined the systemic immune environment via complete blood count analysis on day 9. FMT + PM-mix14 treatment attenuated the dysregulated immune response in DSS-induced colitis, which was characterized by a reduced total leukocyte count, a decreased neutrophil proportion, and a concomitant increase in the lymphocyte proportion (Fig. 6A). These shifts reflect the resolution of intestinal inflammation32. Furthermore, analysis of erythrocyte parameters revealed that FMT + PM-mix14 ameliorated DSS-induced pathological anemia, as evidenced by significant improvements in the total red blood cell count, hemoglobin concentration, and hematocrit level (Fig. 6B, C), indicating enhanced oxygen-carrying capacity33. Platelet analysis revealed that PM-mix14 alone significantly normalized the width of the platelet distribution (Fig. 6D), whereas FMT + PM-mix14 had no significant effect on platelet parameters. These findings suggest that platelet metabolism is not the primary mechanism underlying the therapeutic efficacy of FMT + PM-mix14.

In addition to systemic inflammation, we assessed gut microbiota modulation. 16S rRNA gene sequencing revealed that, compared with the other groups, the FMT + PM-mix14 group presented significantly greater bacterial richness and diversity (Fig. 6E). PCoA revealed that, compared with the other treatment groups, the FMT + PM-mix14 group presented a microbial community structure that was more closely aligned with that of the donors (Fig. 6F). This convergence was recapitulated in the genus-level taxonomic profiles (Fig. 6G). Notably, while PM-mix14 alone increased the relative abundance of putatively beneficial bacteria (Lactobacillus; Fig. 6H), FMT + PM-mix14 did not amplify this effect. Crucially, FMT + PM-mix14 significantly counteracted the DSS-induced increase in potentially deleterious Enterobacteriaceae (Fig. 6I), highlighting its selective suppression of inflammation-associated pathobionts.

Screening donors for gut microbial IN-uc is promising for improving the success of FMT in patients with UC

To assess the translational potential of targeting gut microbial IN-uc, we analyzed human clinical metagenomic datasets (Fig. 7A). First, we compared fecal samples from UC patients (n = 74) and healthy donors (n = 50) from the HMP2 IBDMDB cohort34 (Fig. 7A, Cohort study 1). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that metabolic pathways constituted the primary functional divergence between groups (Fig. 7B). Differential analysis (FC > 2, Q < 0.05) revealed that Nitrogen Metabolism was specifically enriched in HD (Fig. 7C). Mapping this pathway revealed greater upregulation of key gut microbial IN-uc genes (including narB and nirK) in HD microbiomes than observed in the S-FMT model (Fig. 7D). Finally, to validate IN-uc as a potential donor screening criterion, we analyzed differences in UC treatment efficacy among different donors from a clinical and randomized controlled trial of FMT for UC36 (Fig. 7A, Cohort study 3). Collectively, these human clinical data demonstrate that robust gut microbial IN-uc is a critical determinant of successful FMT-induced microbiota remodeling in UC, highlighting its potential as a donor selection biomarker.

Discussion

The therapeutic potential of FMT for UC fundamentally hinges on the successful engraftment of donor-derived intestinal microbes within the recipient’s gut. However, the mechanisms governing how transplanted microbes colonize recipients and drive their effective transfer remain largely elusive. In this study, we leveraged the well-established LD50 phenomenon to disrupt the preexisting homeostasis of the host gut microbiota. Exploiting the selective pressure exerted by the host during recovery to reestablish microbial functionality, we constructed an FMT donor host model. Our results demonstrate that this intervention strategy significantly enhances the similarity of the recipient’s gut microbial species composition to that of the donor. Focusing specifically on gut microbial IN-uc, we found that augmenting donor gut microbial IN-uc via a probiotic mixture (PM-mix14) reduced the heterogeneity of donor microbial engraftment during FMT. Consequently, recipients exhibit microbial communities within recipients exhibiting species structures and functionalities more closely resembling those of the donor, thereby validating our primary findings. Furthermore, we observed that enhancing this capability in the donor microbiota conferred increased resilience against DSS challenge. This finding is further corroborated by analysis of human clinical UC databases, suggesting that screening the donor microbiota for IN-uc holds significant promise for improving FMT efficacy in UC treatment.

FMT aims to establish microbial communities in recipients with structures and functions analogous to those of the donor. Strain-level analyses indicate that Single shot FMT typically achieves only 35% similarity between recipient-colonizing microbes and the donor9, which falls short of therapeutic expectations. Among the myriad factors influencing donor microbe engraftment, nutrient competition, particularly for nitrogen, plays a pivotal role37. In UC, compromised colonic epithelial integrity, inflammation, oxidative stress, and dysbiosis impair nutrient metabolism. This reduces the availability of organic nitrogen sources for transplanted microbes. To survive in this nutrient-poor niche, microbes increase their efficiency in IN-uc to secure essential nitrogen and energy. This process, which is traditionally seen as an adaptive survival mechanism38, has significance here. Our data revealed that FMT from LD50-survived donors promoted a richer repertoire of nitrogen metabolic pathways, especially those related to IN-uc, within the recipient microbiota. Enhancing donor gut microbial IN-uc improved the extent of engraftment in recipients, an effect that was independent of establishing species diversity. This finding points towards a host-induced selection mechanism favoring specific microbial functions over specific taxa.

Notably, dramatic shifts in nitrate respiration capacity (the first phase of dissimilatory nitrate reduction: encompassing nitrate → nitrite → nitric oxide → nitrous oxide → N₂) constitute another gut response triggered during acute UC. This leads to the accumulation of RNS, such as nitrite and nitric oxide (NO), in the gut lumen39, echoing an evolutionary microbial survival strategy observed in vitro. However, owing to disrupted microbial homeostasis, these generated RNS cannot be efficiently utilized, leading to their accumulation. This accumulation causes oxidative/nitrosative stress, exacerbates intestinal epithelial cell damage, and is traditionally considered a key etiological mechanism in UC40. Our study demonstrated that the combination of FMT + PM-mix14 not only enhances nitrate uptake and utilization but also crucially promotes the production of ammonia, L-glutamate, and derivatives such as glutathione. Therefore, by enabling energy production while preventing the accumulation of toxic intermediates, the establishment of this integrated pathway provides a mechanistic basis for the superior therapeutic efficacy. This interpretation is supported by clinical findings of elevated luminal nitrite in active UC patients41, reduced ATP levels42, and significantly lower nitrite levels in patients in remission43. Clinical improvement in UC treatment also coincides with increased L-glutamate metabolic activity44 and elevated ATP levels45.

On the basis of our findings, we propose a mechanistic hypothesis to explain how PM-mix14 enhances the efficient engraftment of FMT. Patients with UC exhibit severe oxidative stress in the intestinal lumen, characterized by excessive accumulation of RNS. In conventional FMT, the donor microbiota encounters RNS-induced engraftment barriers, leading to colonization failure or structural/functional alterations under high RNS stress, resulting in significant discrepancies between the implanted and donor microbiota. The combination of PM-mix14 and FMT not only degrades RNS but also converts them into essential nutritional substrates for microbial colonization (e.g., ATP and L-glutamate), thereby facilitating better engraftment of the donor microbiota and improving the clinical efficacy of FMT in UC patients (Fig. 8).

For clinical translation, our findings point to two principal strategies: screening donors for inherently high IN-uc capacity, or functionally enhancing standard donor microbiota ex vivo using a defined probiotic-metabolite formulation such as PM-mix14. A comparative analysis suggests that the latter strategy holds greater practical and economic appeal. Screening donors entails substantial upfront costs related to recruitment, deep functional phenotyping, and the inherent difficulty of identifying individuals with consistently high IN-uc. In contrast, ex vivo enhancement of readily available donor material minimizes large-scale screening efforts and leverages a standardized, scalable, and potentially industrializable production process. This approach aligns with emerging clinical paradigms, such as the “X-augmented” microbiota transplantation strategy recently validated in a study, where a specific probiotic (Holdemanella biformis) was combined with washed microbiota transplantation to significantly improve outcomes in radiation enteritis46. These independent results reinforce the feasibility and therapeutic potential of augmenting standard donor material with defined beneficial microbes. We therefore consider ex vivo enhancement a more viable and cost-effective strategy for broader clinical implementation.

However, our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the specific probiotic strain used in our mouse model, L. murinus, is a rodent-associated species with no direct human homologue related lactobacilli. For instance, Lactobacillus have been identified as key differential taxa between healthy individuals and UC patients47 and have been shown to improve FMT outcomes when used to precondition donor microbiota48. More importantly, we emphasize that the conservation of microbial function holds greater translational relevance than taxonomic identity. Human metagenomic analyses consistently show that core IN-uc pathways are depleted in UC and restored in FMT responders, independent of the specific bacterial species involved. This functional conservation supports the translational relevance of our findings, even if the orchestrating taxa differ between mice and humans. Second, the ability of enhanced IN-uc to promote microbial engraftment is complex and context-dependent. While boosting IN-uc via substrates like nitrate can alleviate colitis in mice49, overactivation of nitrite/NO metabolism may led to harmful RNS accumulation in UC patients11. Therefore, therapeutic enhancement must coordinate the entire pathway to favor the generation of beneficial end products like ATP and L-glutamate, rather than focusing on isolated steps. This principle is reflected in human dietary studies50,51: the anti-inflammatory benefits of high-vegetable (nitrate-rich52) diets in industrialized settings are mediated in part through enhanced microbial IN-uc. However, in energy-limited environments, redirecting metabolic flux toward nitrate respiration could compromise host and microbial health53–55. Thus, the therapeutic window for IN-uc modulation may vary with nutritional context, underscoring the need to better understand microbial functional adaptations across diverse dietary landscapes.

In conclusion, our study reveals that the gut microbial IN-uc is the key factor influencing the efficacy of FMT in treating UC. It enhances microbial engraftment by increasing the supply of nitrogen sources and ATP production, thereby overcoming colonization resistance in the recipient and ultimately promoting the restoration of gut healthy. Future clinical trials are needed to validate these findings in humans and to explore how differences in host-driven microbial functional adaptations can be leveraged to promote more stable and efficient microbial engraftment. Such knowledge is crucial for optimizing the efficacy of FMT in treating disorders associated with gut microbial dysbiosis.

Methods

Ethics approval

All animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Management and Ethical Review Executive Committee of Northwest A&F University (Approval No. IACUC2023-1202). Sample sizes for all mouse groups were determined based on the minimum required by ethical guidelines, considering the pathogenicity of S.Tm and DSS. SPF-grade 8-week-old male BALB/c mice were purchased from SpeFluor (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and housed in under specific pathogen-free conditions in individually ventilated cages, with 4–5 mice per cage. Mice were provided ad libitum access to irradiated rodent maintenance diet (Xietong Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China, cat# XTCO1WC-001; no custom formulation or special treatment) and sterile water, and maintained under controlled environmental conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 55 ± 5% humidity, 12:12 light-dark cycle). Mice were monitored twice daily for clinical signs, adverse events such as persistent lethargy or inability to eat or drink were recorded, and animals exhibiting such signs for >24 h were euthanized by overdose of pentobarbital sodium. In the murine colitis model experiment, a maximum weight loss of 20% was permitted, mice exceeding this threshold or showing severe clinical symptoms for >24 h were humanely euthanized. Diarrhea was recorded as a clinical indicator and not actively controlled, mice with diarrhea lasting >3 days were evaluated for weight loss and euthanized if meeting the above criteria. To minimize coprophagia, the bottom of the rodent cage was equipped with a wire mesh floor, allowing fecal pellets to drop through freely. cages were inspected twice daily (at 9:00 AM and 9:00 PM) for fecal pellets, which were promptly removed. At the evening inspection, approximately half of the bedding was replaced to reduce stress associated with complete changes.

For experiments involving donor microbiota, fecal samples from donor mice were pooled, homogenized, and processed into a standardized supernatant to ensure consistent microbial and metabolic input across all recipient groups within a given experiment. This study was conducted in strict accordance with the Regulations for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the Guidelines for Animal Ethical Review (China, GB/T 35892-2018).

Survival of mice in the LD50 model as FMT donors

Twenty SPF-grade 8-week-old male BALB/c mice were infected with S.Tm (ATCC®, cat# 29629) at an LD50 dose of 1.7 × 109 CFU in a total gavage volume of 200 µL. At 20 days post-infection, feces were aseptically collected from surviving and recovered mice, homogenized with sterile PBS (Servicebio®, cat# G4202), and centrifuged at 80 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was separated and stored for subsequent use. For the first portion of the supernatant, forty SPF-grade 8-week-old male BALB/c mice (recipients) were randomly divided into two groups and gavaged daily with 0.2 mL of either PBS or a broad-spectrum antibiotic cocktail (penicillin (aladdin®, cat# P433335) at 1 mg/mL, streptomycin (aladdin®, cat# S432672) at 1 mg/mL, neomycin (aladdin®, cat# N432048) at 1 mg/mL, gentamicin sulfate (aladdin®, cat# G302822) at 1 mg/mL and vancomycin (aladdin®, cat# V301569) at 0.5 mg/mL) for seven consecutive days. These mice were subsequently re-randomized within their initial treatment groups into four subgroups: the CON group (PBS for 14 days followed by PBS), the Mod group (PBS for 14 days followed by S.Tm), the S-FMT group (survivor-FMT for 14 days followed by S.Tm), and the C-FMT group (control-FMT for 14 days followed by S.Tm). Body weight and mortality were monitored for 20 days post-infection.

For the second portion of the supernatant, the supernatant was further separated into bacterial biomass and metabolites by centrifugation at 25,000 × g (Eppendorf®, cat# centrifuge 5810R). Twenty SPF-grade 8-week-old male BALB/c recipient mice were randomly divided into two groups, treated with antibiotics (Abx) as described above, and then gavaged with either bacterial biomass or metabolites for 14 days. One day after the final gavage, the mice were challenged with an LD50 dose of S.Tm, and body weight and mortality were recorded for 20 days post-infection.

Bacterial cultures

Cultures were incubated overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 120 rpm in a shaker (herrytech®, cat# hq26x1c). The next day, the OD600 was measured (biotek®, cat# synergy hts) S.Tm cells were then pelleted by centrifugation and resuspended in an appropriate volume of sterile PBS for immediate infection. Cultures were statically incubated at 37 °C for 48 h under anaerobic conditions (shellab®, cat# bactron300). Following the OD600 measurement, L. murinu cells were harvested via centrifugation and resuspended in sterile PBS for immediate therapeutic use. The cultures were statically incubated at 37 °C for 36 h under anaerobic conditions.

In vitro screening of probiotic-metabolite combinations

In vitro coculture system

SPF mouse feces were collected and homogenized in sterile PBS at a 1:10 ratio. After centrifugation, the pellet was resuspended in PBS and inoculated (1:2000 dilution) into 5 mL of fresh mGAM medium. The cultures were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C without shaking for 48 h. The OD600 was measured, and 16S rRNA sequencing was performed to validate the feasibility of the culture method.

Screening of probiotic-metabolite combinations

Two probiotic strains, including L. murinu (1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL, 0.2 mL) and B. acidifaciens (1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL, 0.2 mL), and two prebiotics, including 3-AB (4 mg/mL, 0.2 mL) and PC (0.5 mg/mL, 0.2 mL), were subjected to a full-factorial design. This included single-component interventions (C₄¹), two-component combinations (C₄²), three-component combinations (C₄³), and four-component combinations (C₄⁴), totaling 16 intervention groups with 6 replicates per group. Sterile mouse feces were processed via the 5 mL in vitro coculture system described above, followed by intervention in the 16 groups. The cultures were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C without shaking for 48 h. The contents of microbial nitrite (Jiangcheng®, cat# A03811), ATP (Beyotime®, cat# S0026), L-glutamic acid (Jiangcheng®, cat# A07411), glutathione (Beyotime®, cat# S0025), oxidized glutathione (Solarbio®, cat# BC1185), and glutamine (Solarbio®, cat# BC1455) were measured via commercial kits.

Validation that PM-mix14 enhances FMT engraftment in a mouse model

Thirty 8-week-old male SPF BALB/c mice were randomly divided into three groups: blank control (FMT donor), FMT group, and FMT + PM-mix14 group. The mice in the FMT and FMT + PM-mix14 groups received daily Abx treatment from Day 0 to Day 7. After a 14-day FMT procedure using feces collected from donor mice, fecal samples were aseptically collected from all three groups. Some of the samples were assayed for microbial nitrite, ATP, L-glutamic acid, glutathione, oxidized glutathione, and glutamine levels via commercial kits. The remaining samples were used for intestinal microbiome metagenomic analysis.

S.Tm infection model

Thirty 8-week-old male SPF BALB/c mice were randomly assigned to three groups: the blank control, FMT group, and FMT + PM-mix14 groups. The mice in the FMT and FMT + PM-mix14 groups received daily Abx treatment from Day 0 to Day 7. After the 14-day FMT intervention, all three groups were challenged with an LD50 dose of S.Tm. Body weight and mortality were monitored daily for 20 days post-infection.

DSS-induced colitis model

Sixty-four 8-week-old male SPF BALB/c mice were randomly allocated into 8 groups. From Day 0 to Day 9, colitis was induced by administering 2.5% DSS (Meilunbio®, cat# MB5535) in the drinking water, while non-model control animals (Con) received PBS. Recognizing the limited long‑term efficacy of single‑shot FMT in UC, we implemented a sequential FMT regimen modeled after the LOTUS trial31 to enhance microbial engraftment. From Day 1 to Day 9, the mice were treated daily with one of the following treatments: FMT, PM-mix14, FMT + L. murinu, FMT + 3-AB, or FMT + PC. On Day 9, the mice were euthanized, and peripheral blood was collected (Solarbio®, cat# YA1462). The colon was excised for histological analysis: standard H&E staining was used to assess injury, and AB-PAS staining was used to quantify goblet cells. The colonic contents were collected, and some of the samples were centrifuged for measurement of microbial nitrite, ATP, L-glutamic acid, glutathione, oxidized glutathione, and glutamine levels via commercial kits. The remaining contents were used for 16S rRNA sequencing.

Stool score

Fresh fecal pellets were collected from mice and scored follows23: Normal = 0; Loose = 2; Diarrhea = 4.

Quantitative qPCR detection of L. murinus and B. acidifaciens

Frozen mouse fecal samples (0.2 g) were homogenized in 1 mL of sterile PBS (pH 7.4) by vortexing for 2 min, followed by incubation at room temperature for 5 min. The supernatant was collected, mixed with 0.1 mm glass beads, and vortexed for 3 min to disrupt cell walls. Total microbial DNA was extracted using the HiPure Stool DNA Kit (Magen®, cat# D3018) according to the manufacturer′s instructions. Species-specific primers were used: for L. murinus (Forward: 5′-TCGAACGAACTACTCTTTACACC-3′; Reverse: 5′-CGTTCGCCACTCAACTCTTT-3′)56 and for B. acidifaciens (Forward: 5′-GAGGAAGGTCCCCCACATTG-3′; Reverse: 5′-ACCCATAGGGCAGTCATCCT-3′)57. Quantitative PCR was performed with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Vazyme®, cat# Q311) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real‑Time PCR System. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Melting‑curve analysis was conducted after each run to verify amplification specificity. Absolute bacterial abundance was calculated using standard curves generated from serial dilutions of plasmids containing the target amplicon. Data are expressed as gene copy numbers per gram of fecal sample.

Fecal shotgun metagenomics

Fecal samples were immediately frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen. Total microbial genomic DNA was extracted using the HiPure Stool DNA Kit (Magen®, Cat# D3018) following the manufacturer’s protocol, which includes mechanical lysis with 0.1‑mm zirconia beads to ensure efficient disruption of diverse gut microbes, including recalcitrant Gram‑positive bacteria. DNA concentration and purity were measured, and integrity was assessed via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Sequencing libraries were prepared by fragmenting the DNA to 350 bp using a Covaris M220 (Genomics®, China), followed by size selection and library construction with the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit (Bioo Scientific®, USA). Paired-end shotgun metagenomic sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Raw sequencing reads were processed using fastp (v0.20.0) to remove adapter sequences and trim low-quality bases. Reads shorter than 50 bp after trimming or with an average base quality score below 20 were discarded. The remaining high-quality reads were aligned to the host reference genome using BWA (v0.7.17), and reads with high similarity to the host genome were removed to obtain microbial reads for downstream analysis.

Quality-filtered microbial reads from all samples within a cohort were co-assembled de novo using MEGAHIT (v1.1.2) with the meta-sensitive preset. Contigs with a length ≥ 300 bp were retained for gene prediction. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted on these contigs using Prodigal (v2.6.3). Predicted genes with a nucleotide length ≥ 100 bp were translated into amino acid sequences. To create a non-redundant gene catalog, the amino acid sequences from all predicted genes were clustered using CD-HIT (v4.7) at 90% identity and 90% coverage. The longest sequence within each cluster was selected as the representative sequence.

To quantify gene abundance, high-quality reads from each individual sample were mapped back to the cohort-specific non-redundant gene catalog using SOAPaligner (soap2.21) with a stringent identity threshold of 95%. The number of reads mapped to each gene was counted, and gene abundance profiles were generated for each sample.

The amino acid sequences of the representative genes in the catalog were annotated against multiple databases using DIAMOND (v2.0.13, BLASTP mode, e-value ≤ 1e-5): The NCBI NR database was used for taxonomic assignment. The abundance of a taxon was calculated by summing the abundances of all genes assigned to it. The KEGG database was used to assign KO identifiers, Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers, and map genes to KEGG pathways and modules. The abundance of a KO, pathway, EC, or module was calculated by summing the abundances of all genes annotated to it. This generated comprehensive functional (KO, Pathway, EC) and taxonomic abundance tables for all subsequent statistical and comparative analyses.

16S rRNA

Total genomic DNA of gut microbiota was extracted using the HiPure Stool DNA Kit (Magen®, Cat. No. D3018) following the manufacturer’s protocol, which includes mechanical lysis with 0.1‑mm zirconia beads to ensure efficient disruption of diverse gut microbes, including recalcitrant Gram‑positive bacteria. DNA integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and concentration/purity were quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific®, USA). The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified with barcoded primers 338F/806R, using TransStart FastPfu DNA Polymerase on a T100 Thermal Cycler (BIO-RAD®, USA) (27 cycles: 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s; initial denaturation: 95 °C for 3 min; final extension: 72 °C for 10 min). PCR products were purified via 2% agarose gel electrophoresis and a PCR Clean-Up Kit (YuHua), then quantified with a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific®, USA).

Libraries were constructed using the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit (adapter ligation→magnetic bead removal of adapter dimers→PCR enrichment→magnetic bead recovery of final libraries) and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology®, Shanghai).

Quality control and merging: Trimming (quality score <20, 10 bp sliding window; discard reads <50 bp or with N bases) and merging (minimum overlap 10 bp, max mismatch ratio 0.2) were performed using fastp v0.19.6 and FLASH v1.2.11, respectively. Sequences were assigned to samples by barcodes (0 mismatches) and primers (max 2 mismatches), with orientation correction. Denoising was conducted via the DADA2 plugin in QIIME2 v2022.11 (default parameters) to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Chloroplast/mitochondria sequences were removed. All samples were rarefied to 20,000 reads per sample (average Good’s coverage: 99.09%). Taxonomic classification of ASVs was performed against the Silva 16S rRNA database (v138.2) using QIIME2’s Naive Bayes classifier.

Untargeted metabolomics analyses

Untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed by MajorBio Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Con group (n = 6), the S-FMT group (n = 6), the C-FMT group (n = 6), and quality control (QC) samples. To account for variations in water content, all fecal samples were first lyophilized. Subsequently, approximately 50 mg of dry weight from each processed sample was accurately weighed for metabolite extraction. The detailed procedure was as follows: approximately 50 mg of each fecal sample was accurately weighed. Metabolites were extracted using 400 µL of a methanol: water (4:1, v/v) solution containing five internal standards (L-2-chlorophenylalanine, valine-d8, inosine-15N4, PGE2-d4, 1-dodecanoyl-2-tridecanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine)58,59. The mixture was precipitated at −10 °C and then homogenized for 6 min at −10 °C and 50 Hz using a high-throughput tissue crusher (Wonbio-96c, Wanbo®, China). This was followed by ultrasonication for 30 min (5 °C, 40 kHz). After incubation at −20 °C for 30 min, the samples were centrifuged for 15 min (4 °C, 13,000 × g). The resulting supernatant was collected transferred to a vial with an insert for LC-MS analysis.

Chromatographic separation was performed on a Thermo UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 µm; Waters Milford®, USA). Metabolite detection was carried out using the corresponding mass spectrometer with an electrospray ionization source operating in both positive and negative ion modes. The optimized parameters were: sheath gas flow rate, 50 arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 13 arb; ion source heater temperature, 450 °C; and stepped normalized collision energies of 20, 40, and 60 eV. The ion spray voltage was set to +3500 V in positive mode and −3500 V in negative mode. The full scan ranged was m/z 70–1050.

The raw LC-MS data were processed using Progenesis QI software (Waters Milford®, USA) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment, generating a data matrix containing retention time, mass-to-charge ratio, and peak intensity. Metabolite annotation was achieved by matching the acquired MS and MS/MS spectra against public metabolite databases (HMDB, MetLin) and the MajorBio in-house database.

Further data processing was analyzed on the MajorBio Cloud Platform (www.cloud.majorbio.com). Metabolic features detected in at least 80% of the samples within any experimental group were retained. Missing values were replaced with the minimum positive value from the original matrix. Peak intensities were normalized using the sum normalization method. Variables showing a relative standard deviation > 30% in the QC samples were removed. Finally, the data were log10-transformed to produce the final matrix for statistical analysis.

Principal component analysis and orthogonal partial least-squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed on the preprocessed data matrix using the ropls package (V1.6.2) in R. The stability of the OPLS-DA model was assessed sevenfold cross-validation. Significantly differential metabolites were identified based on the variable importance in projection (VIP) score obtained from the OPLS-DA model (>1) and Student’s t-test P-value (<0.05).

Differential metabolites were annotated against the KEGG database to identify associated metabolic pathways. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using the scipy.stats package in Python. Resulting P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, with Pathways have an FDR-adjusted P-value < 0.05 considered significantly enriched. Fisher’s exact test was further employed to identify the biological pathways most relevant to the experimental treatments.

Assay kit measurements

Fecal samples or in vitro co-cultured bacterial supernatants from the above experiments were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The samples were processed with pre-chilled sterile PBS: centrifuged at 6000 × g, resuspended in PBS, and washed three times. Cell lysis buffer was added, followed by sonication (200 W power, 3 s on/5 s off, 10 cycles) to release the intracellular contents. The levels of nitrite, ATP, L-glutamic acid, glutathione, oxidized glutathione, and glutamine were then measured via commercial assay kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions, with the data used for subsequent analysis.

Microscopic observation

Distal colon tissues (2 cm from the anus) were collected from the mice and fixed in pre-chilled 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. The samples were then dehydrated in gradient ethanol, cleared in xylene, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, stained, and coverslipped. H&E-stained sections were used to assess histoarchitecture and inflammation; AB-PAS was used to quantify goblet cells.

Blood cell analysis

Peripheral blood was collected from the mice in EDTA anticoagulant tubes. The samples were loaded into the inlet of a fully automated five-differential hematology analyzer (mindray®, cat# BC5000) to precisely classify white blood cells into neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, and basophils. Simultaneously, other blood parameters, including red blood cells and platelets were measured.

Bioinformatics analyses of publicly available datasets

For bioinformatics analyses, we first downloaded bulk fecal metagenomic data from the HMP2 IBDMDB database (http://ibdmdb.org)34,58. This dataset comprises the fecal profiles of 50 healthy individuals and 74 UC patients. Second, we retrieved bulk fecal metagenomic data from the NCBI GenBank database under the accession number SRA Bioproject PRJNA1071720, which included fecal samples from 9 responders and 15 non-responders among UC patients undergoing FMT treatment35. Finally, additional fecal metagenomic data were downloaded from the same SRA Bioproject PRJNA107172036, containing samples from effective donors (UC responder rate: 58.3%) and ineffective donors (UC responder rate: 16.7%) during FMT for UC. All aforementioned fecal metagenomic datasets were used for subsequent analyses.

Statistical analysis

Raw data were recorded in Excel. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. Prior to ANOVA, the normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was verified using the Brown–Forsythe test. Only when both assumptions were satisfied was parametric ANOVA applied. If the data violated the normality or equal variance assumption, the non‑parametric Kruskal–Wallis H test was used, followed by Dunn’s post‑hoc comparisons with Bonferroni correction. Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method, and statistical differences between curves were analyzed using the log‑rank test. A P‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.0.

Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) was performed based on the Bray–Curtis distance algorithm to examine the similarity of microbial community structures among samples. Analysis of Similarities (ANOSIM), a non-parametric test, was applied to assess whether differences in microbial community structures between sample groups were statistically significant (R vegan (v2.5.4) and R ade4 (v. 1.7.13)). For significantly differential metabolites (VIP > 1, Q (FDR-adjusted P-value) <0.05), expression levels were normalized using Z-score. Hierarchical clustering of samples was then performed via the average linkage method based on Euclidean distance, and the results were visualized as a circular heatmap (R: ggplot2 (v3.4.4), ComplexHeatmap (v2.10.0), and OmicStudioClassic (v1.45.0)). Loading plots, derived from orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), were generated to identify key metabolites contributing to group separation using R ropls (v1.36.0) and ggplot2 (v3.4.4). For inter-group bubble charts and radar charts, raw data were processed by Min-Max Normalization. Subsequently, one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was applied to obtain inter-group P-values. The results were finally visualized using R ggplot (2 v3.4.4) and GraphPad Prism. The KEGG enrichment factor plot was generated for differentially abundant genes filtered by |log₂ fold change| > 1 and Q (FDR-adjusted P-value) <0.05, and visualized using R ggplot2 (v3.4.4) and OmicStudioClassic (v1.45.0). The KEGG pathway map was generated by overlaying log₂ fold change values from metagenomic gene abundance data onto the nitrogen metabolism pathway template (ko00910) retrieved from the KEGG database, using R ggplot2 (v3.4.4) and OmicStudioClassic (v1.45.0) for visualization. The metabolite enrichment factor plot was generated by calculating metabolite importance via Betweenness/Out-degree centrality. The pathway impact value was derived from the ratio of “the sum of importance of matched metabolites” to “the total sum of pathway importance”. This value was combined with the pathway enrichment P-value (converted to −Log₁₀(P)) for visualization using R ggplot2 (v3.4.4) and OmicStudioClassic (v1.45.0).
