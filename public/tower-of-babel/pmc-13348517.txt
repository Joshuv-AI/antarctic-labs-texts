The influence of pterygium size on corneal refractive properties and higher-order aberrations
Authors: Jianqin Shen, Ying Chen, Yanghong Chen, Bingzhen Gou, Hongguang Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate associations between pterygium size parameters and corneal refractive properties and higher-order aberrations (HOAs).MethodsIn 41 patients with unilateral pterygium, thickness, length, and width were measured by anterior segment optical coherence tomography(AS-OCT). Corneal refractive parameters, irregularity indices, and HOAs were assessed using Pentacam HR. Correlation analyses evaluated relationships between pterygium dimensions and corneal parameters.ResultsPterygium length and width significantly correlated with multiple corneal parameters in the 3–7 mm zone. Moderate correlations were found with K1 (length: |r| = 0.376–0.473; width: |r| = 0.361–0.415; all p < 0.05), and stronger correlations with corneal astigmatism (length: r = 0.582–0.642; width: r = 0.529–0.581; all p < 0.001) and irregularity indices (ISV, IVA, IHD) (length: r = 0.623–0.637; width: r = 0.566–0.596; all p < 0.001). Length positively correlated with total, third-, and fourth-order HOA RMS across the anterior and posterior surface, and entire cornea (r = 0.455–0.691, p < 0.05), primarily driven by vertical trefoil, vertical coma, and tetrafoil. Width also correlated with anterior and total corneal HOAs (r = 0.519 to 0.645, p < 0.05). Posterior surface correlations for width were weaker (r = 0.313 to 0.472, p < 0.05) but remained significant for total RMS, third-order RMS, and several specific aberrations.ConclusionPterygium length and width are key parameters associated with deteriorated corneal refractive status, irregularity, and HOAs. Width, being easily standardized, serves as a practical indicator for predicting pterygium-induced visual quality deterioration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-05009-y.Keywords: Pterygium size, Anterior-segment optical coherence tomography, Cornea, Refractive, HOAs

Introduction

Pterygium is an ocular surface disease characterized by the growth of abnormal fibrovascular tissue from the conjunctiva extending from the limbus toward the central corneal area. Even before invading the optical zone, pterygium can induce significant changes in corneal refractive status, leading to decreased visual acuity and impaired visual quality [1].

Furthermore, given that pterygium and cataracts share common etiological factors such as age and ultraviolet (UV) exposure, their comorbidity is a prevalent clinical scenario [2]. For patients presenting with both conditions, the theoretical gold standard is a staged surgical approach: pterygium excision is performed first, followed by cataract extraction and intraocular lens (IOL) implantation only after the corneal refractive status has stabilized. However, the complexities of clinical practice often diverge from this ideal. A subset of elderly patients, motivated by a desire to shorten the postoperative recovery period, reduce the burden of clinical visits, and minimize healthcare costs, often express a preference for a single-stage, combined procedure. In other clinical situations, when a pterygium is deemed small and to have a limited impact on vision, standalone cataract surgery may be the chosen course of action [3]. Therefore, given these practical clinical decision-making needs, accurately and objectively quantifying the degree of pterygium-induced disturbance to corneal refractive status is crucial for guiding clinicians in developing individualized treatment strategies and selecting optimal intervention timing. Particularly in today’s landscape of widespread refractive cataract surgery and functional intraocular lens implantation, the clinical significance of accurately assessing the potential impact of pterygium invasion on higher-order visual quality is especially pronounced [4].

In this study, AS-OCT was used to accurately obtain morphological parameters of the pterygium, while the Pentacam system was employed to measure corneal refractive power, irregularity index, and higher-order aberrations of the anterior and posterior corneal surfaces. Correlation analysis was conducted to investigate the influence of pterygium size on corneal refractive characteristics and higher-order aberrations.

While a limited body of research has previously reported associations between pterygium size and corneal curvature or higher-order aberrations [1, 5], our study possesses several distinct advantages. Firstly, whereas prior studies have predominantly relied on slit-lamp photography for assessing pterygium parameters, all measurements in our investigation were acquired using AS-OCT. This methodology ensures superior anatomical correspondence and measurement precision. Secondly, to our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically analyze the correlations between pterygium size and the full spectrum of HOAs—including total, ordinal, and individual Zernike terms—across both the anterior and posterior corneal surfaces. Thirdly, our correlational design, as opposed to case-control comparisons, more intuitively reveals the strength of the direct association between the physical extent of a pterygium and the resulting degradation in the cornea’s refractive characteristics and higher-order optical quality.

Subjects and methods

Subjects

A retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical records of 41 patients diagnosed with primary nasal pterygium at our ophthalmology center between March and December 2024. The participant selection process encompassed the following exclusion criteria: (1) recurrent pterygium; (2) concurrent ocular surface pathologies; (3) a history of intraocular surgery; (4) systemic conditions potentially affecting ocular blood perfusion, such as diabetes or autoimmune disorders.

All patients gave informed consent to participate in this study. The research protocol adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine.

Procedure

A standardized ophthalmic assessment protocol was implemented, comprising three components: (1) general clinical examinations; (2) AS-OCT examinations; (3) corneal topographic examinations.

General ophthalmic examinations

logMAR uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA), intraocular pressure, slit lamp, and anterior segment photography were involved.

Anterior segment optical coherence tomography examinations

Structural assessment of pterygia in this study was conducted using the anterior segment imaging module of the TowardPi® optical coherence tomography system (Beijing, China). The scanning protocol was configured with the following parameters: an 18 mm × 18 mm field of view, 6 mm axial scan depth, 400,000 A-scans/s rate, 3.8 μm axial resolution, 10 μm transverse resolution, and a 1060 nm light source wavelength. Detailed definitions and procedures for measuring pterygium length, thickness, and width are provided in Supplementary Material.

A standardized imaging protocol was rigorously adhered to for all sessions. Furthermore, to guarantee data reliability and reproducibility, a certified technician with experience in over 500 anterior segment OCT cases performed all acquisitions.

Corneal topographic examinations

Anterior segment imaging for all subjects was conducted using the Pentacam HR platform (Oculus, Wetzlar, Germany). This system employs a Scheimpflug-based three-dimensional design, performing 3,600 scans within two seconds to yield 50 tomographic images per session. A standardized imaging protocol was implemented in a darkened environment by a sole, qualified technician. Prior to each scan, subjects were directed to execute two blinks to minimize potential tear film artifacts. Only datasets meeting the predefined “OK” quality specification (QS) were deemed acceptable for analysis. The investigated parameters comprised:

(1) Keratometric parameters

flat keratometry (K1), steep keratometry (K2), mean keratometry (Km), and astigmatism in the 3 mm, 5 mm, and 7 mm zones.

(2) Topometric indexes

Topometric indexes from the Pentacam HR include index of surface variance (ISV), index of vertical asymmetry (IVA), keratoconus index (KI), central keratoconus index (CKI), index of height asymmetry (IHA) and index of height decentration (IHD).

(3) Corneal higher-order aberrations (HOAs) parameters

① the root mean square (RMS) of total, 3rd, and 4th HOAs on the anterior corneal surface, posterior corneal surface, and the whole cornea. ② the RMS of the Zernike coefficients from 3rd up to 4th orders on the anterior corneal surface, posterior corneal surface, and the whole cornea.

Data analysis and statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two independent groups: if the data followed a normal distribution with equal variances, an independent samples t-test was applied. If the data followed a normal distribution but variances were unequal, Welch’s t-test was used. If the data did not follow a normal distribution and variances were unequal, the Mann-Whitney U test was employed. For correlation analysis between two variables: if the data followed a normal distribution, Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed. If the data did not follow a normal distribution, Spearman’s correlation analysis was applied. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Affected eyes included 15 right eyes and 26 left eyes. The mean uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA, logMAR) was 0.29 ± 0.27. The average thickness, length, and width of the pterygium were 0.05 ± 0.14 mm, 3.56 ± 1.11 mm, and 5.58 ± 1.12 mm, respectively. Detailed measurements of these dimensions are presented in Table 1.

Conversely, pterygium length demonstrated a strong and widespread impact on HOAs. A significant positive correlation was established between pterygium length and the root mean square (RMS) values of total HOAs, third-order HOAs, and fourth-order HOAs across the anterior corneal surface, posterior corneal surface, and the whole cornea (r = 0.455 to 0.691, all p < 0.05) (Table 5). Analysis of individual Zernike coefficients revealed that vertical trefoil (), vertical coma (), and tetrafoil () from the anterior and posterior surfaces, as well as the total cornea, were significantly correlated with pterygium length (r = 0.337 to 0.699, all p < 0.05) (Table 5). Additionally, several surface-specific aberrations showed strong associations, including horizontal coma (), vertical trefoil (), and tetrafoil () on the anterior surface, and secondary astigmatism ( on the posterior surface (r = 0.368 to 0.548, all p < 0.05) (Table 5).

Pterygium width was also a significant determinant of increased HOAs, although its effect was most pronounced on the anterior cornea. On the anterior surface and for the whole cornea, width was strongly correlated with total HOA RMS, third-order RMS, and fourth-order RMS (r = 0.519 to 0.645, all p < 0.05) (Table 5). This association extended to all individual third-order aberrations and all fourth-order aberrations, with the notable exceptions of spherical aberration ( and secondary astigmatism (. While the correlations on the posterior surface remained statistically significant for total RMS, total third-order RMS, vertical trefoil (), vertical coma (), tetrafoil (), and secondary astigmatism (, the strength of these associations was markedly attenuated (r = 0.313 to 0.472, all p < 0.05) (Table 5).

Discussion

Our analysis of the impact of pterygium size on corneal curvature revealed findings that differ slightly from existing literature. While Tomidokoro et al. [1]. reported a significant negative correlation between mean corneal power (Km) and the percent extension of the pterygium onto the cornea(r = -0.370), our present study found no correlation between Km and pterygium size parameters. Instead, we identified a significant correlation between pterygium length and the flattening keratometry (K1), with a correlation strength consistent with Tomidokoro’s reported association between Km and pterygium extension percentage. In summary, whether using the absolute length of corneal invasion or its relative proportion as an assessment metric, the analysis results consistently and robustly confirm that the extent of pterygium invasion is significantly correlated with corneal curvature flattening. A novel finding of this study is the significant correlation between pterygium width at the limbus and the K1 value. In light of the high morphological variability that complicates consistent length measurements, measuring the width is a more straightforward and reproducible procedure. We therefore propose that pterygium width can serve as a simple yet effective surrogate metric in clinical assessment. Compared to the effect of pterygium on corneal curvature, the visual quality impairment caused by the increased corneal astigmatism induced by pterygium may be of greater concern. Although pterygium-induced astigmatism often reduces significantly after pterygium excision [6–8], a more objective preoperative assessment of the correlation between pterygium size and corneal astigmatism allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of the timing of surgical intervention and visual prognosis. Previous studies have demonstrated that corneal astigmatism in the eye with pterygium is significantly higher compared to the normal contralateral eye [5, 9]. Further research indicates that changes in pterygium-induced corneal astigmatism are closely related to parameters such as the length and area of pterygium invasion into the cornea: Previous studies by Tomidokoro et al. [1] and Igarashi et al. [10] demonstrated robust correlations between pterygium extension and astigmatism. Similarly, Yoon et al. [5] found that pterygium length, width, and area all correlated with the induced astigmatic change. Our results align closely with these reports, confirming a strong positive correlation between pterygium length and astigmatism. Critically, our analysis elevates the importance of pterygium width, demonstrating that it possesses a correlational strength comparable to that of length. The relationship between pterygium thickness and corneal astigmatism remains inconclusive. Contrary to the findings of this study and Yoon et al. [5], which did not identify a significant correlation between pterygium thickness and corneal astigmatism, Xu et al. [9] demonstrated a close association between pterygium thickness levels and the degree of corneal astigmatism. Preoperative high astigmatism may affect postoperative best-corrected visual acuity. Given the significant correlation between pterygium size and corneal astigmatism, this metric can also serve as an important reference for determining the optimal timing for surgery. Mohammad et al. [11] noted that when pterygium length exceeds 2.2 mm, width exceeds 5 mm, or total area exceeds 6.25 mm², it induces 2D corneal astigmatism, at which point surgical intervention should be considered.

Our study reveals that the impact of pterygium invasion on corneal curvature and astigmatism extends beyond the central 3 mm zone. When expanding the corneal observation area from 3 mm to 5 mm and even 7 mm, we found that both the K1 and the corneal astigmatism degree showed significant correlation with the length and width of the pterygium. Moreover, this correlation strength did not diminish with the enlargement of the corneal observation area. This finding, previously unreported in the literature, strongly confirms that the retracting traction forces within pterygium tissue exert a significant influence on corneal morphology even in the mid-peripheral region.

Research on the impact of pterygium size on corneal regularity remains scarce in the existing literature. Only Yoon et al. [5]. reported significant correlations between pterygium length, width, and area with corneal irregularity parameters in 3-mm and 5-mm regions. Our study found that pterygium length and width were significantly correlated with the irregularity parameters ISV, IVA, and IHD as measured by Pentacam. Pterygium width was also significantly correlated with IHA. However, the keratoconus-specific indices CKI and KI showed no significant correlation with either pterygium length or width. The ISV value reflects the deviation of corneal curvature from the mean, while IVA and IHA primarily indicate corneal curvature symmetry based on the horizontal meridian. The IHD value analyzes vertical eccentricity. Increased corneal irregularity is also a key factor inducing certain higher-order aberrations.

Our study further conducted a multidimensional correlation analysis between the morphological characteristics of pterygium and the higher-order aberrations of the anterior and posterior corneal surfaces, as well as whole corneal higher-order aberrations. Consistent with the correlation results for corneal refractive parameters, corneal higher-order aberrations showed strong correlations primarily with pterygium invasion length and pterygium width, while exhibiting minimal association with pterygium thickness. We observed that pterygium thickness showed only a weak association with anterior secondary astigmatism (, consistent with findings from Yoon et al. [5]—pterygium thickness demonstrated no significant correlation with any induced corneal higher-order aberrations. Nevertheless, this statistical finding appears to contradict the common clinical observation that fleshy, thick pterygia exert direct compressive forces on the adjacent cornea. This discrepancy likely arises from our measurement methodology: we assessed thickness using two-dimensional AS-OCT cross-sections at the limbus, which may not objectively capture the comprehensive thickness profile or the absolute maximum thickness of the entire fibrovascular mass. Therefore, future studies incorporating three-dimensional volumetric AS-OCT analyses are warranted to fully elucidate the compressive effects of pterygium thickness.

Simultaneously, we first identified the following characteristics regarding the associations between pterygium size and corneal higher-order aberrations: greater pterygium length is significantly associated with higher total higher-order aberrations and third- and fourth-order aberrations on both anterior and posterior surfaces; while pterygium width significantly correlated with greater total higher-order aberrations, third-order aberrations on both surfaces, and fourth-order aberrations on the anterior surface, with no significant association on the posterior surface. Compared to the strength of the association between length and width for anterior surface higher-order aberrations, the association strength for posterior surface aberrations was relatively weaker.

In our in-depth analysis of the correlation between individual aberrations and pterygium dimensions, our findings align with previous literature [5, 12], indicating that pterygium length and width exhibit significant correlations with most horizontal asymmetric aberrations. More importantly, this study also demonstrates for the first time that pterygium size is significantly associated with vertical aberrations such as vertical coma. These higher-order aberrations induced by pterygium provide a direct physical explanation for the poor visual quality frequently reported by patients with severe pterygium, which cannot be fully corrected with spectacle lenses. Furthermore, no significant correlation was found between spherical aberration and pterygium size, consistent with previous literature [5, 12]. The stability of this parameter holds direct clinical significance: it implies that during cataract surgery for pterygium patients, surgeons may routinely select aspheric intraocular lenses designed to correct mean corneal spherical aberration without concern that the pterygium itself will introduce unpredictable interference to this critical parameter.

In our analysis of the correlation between individual Zernike coefficients and pterygium size, spherical aberration was found to have no significant statistical correlation with pterygium size, a finding that is consistent with previous literature [5, 12]. The stability of this parameter has explicit clinical significance: it implies that when performing cataract surgery on patients with concomitant pterygium, surgeons can routinely select aspheric intraocular lenses (IOLs) designed to correct the cornea’s average spherical aberration, without concern that the pterygium itself will unpredictably alter this critical parameter.

Furthermore, our study not only confirmed the previously reported significant correlation between pterygium length and width and most horizontally asymmetric aberrations [5, 12], but also revealed for the first time that a significant association also exists between pterygium size and vertical coma. This significant increase in higher-order aberrations induced by the pterygium provides a direct physical explanation for the degradation in visual quality frequently reported by patients with severe pterygium, which cannot be fully corrected with spectacle lenses.

Given that astigmatism and trefoil aberrations are critical factors affecting postoperative visual quality in cataract surgery, particularly in multifocal intraocular lens implantation [4], our findings suggest that preoperative assessment for cataract patients with pterygium should extend beyond evaluating the refractive changes induced by the condition. More importantly, careful evaluation of the potential impact of higher-order aberrations caused by pterygium on intraocular lens selection decisions and postoperative visual prognosis is essential.

From a practical perspective, our results offer tangible guidance for clinical management. While the concept that a larger pterygium causes greater corneal disruption is intuitive, objectively quantifying this disruption is critical. Because measuring the exact corneal invasion length can sometimes be hindered by ill-defined apical borders, our finding that pterygium width—a parameter easily and reproducibly measured at the limbus—correlates strongly with HOA degradation provides a valuable surrogate tool. Clinicians can use limbal width as a rapid screening indicator; a significantly increased width should prompt immediate detailed aberrometry. This is particularly crucial for guiding the optimal timing of surgical intervention in the short term. For patients planning to undergo refractive cataract surgery, especially those desiring premium multifocal IOLs, identifying a substantial pterygium width and associated HOAs (such as astigmatism and trefoil) can justify early pterygium excision. Addressing the pterygium before it induces severe, potentially irreversible higher-order aberrations optimizes the long-term optical conditions for precise IOL biometry and improved visual prognosis.

Limitations of the study

We acknowledge several limitations in the present study that warrant careful consideration. First, as a retrospective, single-center analysis with a relatively moderate sample size of 41 eyes, the study is inherently susceptible to selection bias and potential limitations in statistical power. To mitigate these risks, we implemented strict exclusion criteria to eliminate confounding ocular surface conditions, utilized automated imaging systems to minimize subjective grading biases, and ensured that all measurements were obtained by a single masked and certified technician. Second, the study design lacked a comparison with an age- and gender-matched control group or the unaffected normal fellow eyes. While previous literatures have elegantly utilized case-control designs to establish the qualitative consensus that pterygium degrades corneal optics, the primary objective of our study was specifically to evaluate the “dose-response” strength—that is, quantifying the direct correlation between varying dimensional severities of the pterygium and optical degradation. Nevertheless, the absence of a normal baseline control limits our ability to completely isolate the specific optical disruption caused by the fibrovascular tissue from potential age-related corneal variations or pre-existing subclinical refractive anomalies. Finally, the current findings are entirely based on a single preoperative cross-sectional episode. Because the subsequent clinical management of the included patients varied considerably—ranging from surgical excision and topical medical interventions to conservative observation—it was unfeasible to evaluate the longitudinal effects of a unified treatment protocol within this specific cohort. Consequently, our data demonstrate associations but cannot establish causality, nor can they confirm the extent to which these induced higher-order aberrations are reversible postoperatively. In the future, we plan to conduct well-powered, prospective longitudinal studies incorporating standardized surgical interventions to accurately assess the reversibility of these topographic and aberrometric changes following pterygium excision.

Conclusion

This study confirms that the length and width of a pterygium, rather than its thickness, are the key physical dimensional parameters responsible for the deterioration of corneal refractive status, morphological irregularity, and higher-order aberrations. The length and width of the pterygium are not only significantly correlated with the flattening of the central corneal curvature (K1 value) and the magnitude of astigmatism, but this effect also extends to the 7 mm mid-peripheral corneal zone, revealing a broad mechanical tractional effect. Furthermore, the width of the pterygium demonstrates a strong correlation, comparable to that of its length, with its impact on corneal astigmatism and higher-order aberrations. Given that width is more easily standardized for measurement in clinical practice, we propose that it can serve as a simple and effective indicator for predicting pterygium-induced deterioration of refractive status and visual quality. In the era of refractive cataract surgery, preoperative assessment for patients with coexisting pterygium should not be limited to the refractive changes it causes. More importantly, a systematic evaluation of the potential impact of pterygium dimensions on corneal higher-order aberrations is crucial. This is essential for guiding the timing of surgery, optimizing intraocular lens (IOL) selection (especially for multifocal IOLs), and predicting postoperative visual quality.
