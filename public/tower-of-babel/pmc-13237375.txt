Unified spatial-temporal graph aggregation framework for predicting student performance
Authors: Xian Yu, Yifen Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of student performance is crucial for enabling timely interventions and supporting data-driven instructional strategies. Beyond single-term forecasts, it also facilitates early alerts and targeted feedback for learners and instructors. However, many existing models handle spatial relationships and temporal sequences independently, which limits their ability to capture the intricate dependencies embedded in academic data. In this study, we propose a unified spatial-temporal graph aggregation (USTGA) framework that embeds spatial similarity directly into temporal dependency modeling, forming a coherent and dynamically evolving graph representation. Unlike conventional architectures that treat spatial structure as static or auxiliary, the unified graph enables spatial relations to actively modulate temporal information propagation. To further enhance representation learning, an attention-guided aggregation strategy is employed to adaptively highlight informative spatial-temporal neighbors, while stacked aggregation layers progressively refine node representations across multiple scales. By jointly capturing fine-grained spatial-temporal interactions, the proposed framework overcomes limitations inherent in independent modeling strategies. Experiments on an educational dataset demonstrate that USTGA consistently outperforms competitive baselines, achieving reductions in multiple error metrics under repeated evaluations. The results confirm the framework’s effectiveness and robustness, and its design offers high scalability and adaptability, making it well-suited for deployment in diverse educational analytics systems such as early-warning dashboards and program-level advising workflows.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In educational data analysis, student performance prediction is a fundamental task that supports timely academic interventions, informed instructional planning, and efficient allocation of educational resources1. Accurate predictive models enable educators to identify learners at risk, design personalized learning pathways, and evaluate the effectiveness of pedagogical strategies2. Despite its practical importance, modeling student achievement remains challenging due to the complex interplay between temporal learning trajectories and spatially dependent contextual factors. Temporal trajectories reflect how a student’s performance evolves across semesters, assessments, or learning activities, while spatial context captures relational influences arising from peers, classrooms, courses, or institutional environments. These two dimensions are inherently intertwined: contextual interactions can alter learning progress over time, and temporal performance patterns may, in turn, reshape relational structures. The resulting dependencies are often dynamic, heterogeneous, and noisy, making it difficult for predictive models to jointly capture evolving behaviors and contextual correlations in a coherent manner.

To address this challenge, a wide range of predictive strategies has been explored, with early studies predominantly relying on traditional machine learning techniques3. For example,4 compared multiple classifiers on limited e-learning datasets to predict student dropout, achieving reasonable accuracy despite sparse features. Similarly,5 analyzed online learning environments using various algorithms to identify academically at-risk students based on behavioral logs and assessment scores. In addition,6 conducted a comparative study of six commonly used models for first-semester performance prediction, where a multiclass classifier combined with SMOTE and feature selection achieved high predictive performance. Although these methods offer interpretability and perform well under constrained data conditions, they rely heavily on hand-crafted features and lack the capacity to learn expressive representations that simultaneously model temporal evolution and relational dependencies.

Recent advances in deep learning have enabled more flexible modeling of student performance dynamics7. For instance, APP-TGN8 constructed temporal graphs from learning activity logs and employed temporal graph networks with attention mechanisms to capture time-aware interaction patterns. LLM-SBCL9 integrated signed bipartite graphs with large language models to enhance predictive accuracy through multimodal contrastive learning. Furthermore, CGNNP10 combined clustering techniques with graph neural networks to infer performance patterns from behavioral and assessment data. Despite these improvements, many existing deep learning models still treat spatial and temporal information as separate components, often encoding spatial relationships statically while modeling temporal dependencies in isolation. Such decoupled designs limit the ability to capture dynamic spatial-temporal interactions and may overlook subtle academic signals that evolve across both dimensions.

These limitations highlight the necessity of a unified modeling paradigm that can dynamically integrate spatial and temporal dependencies within a single representation. An effective framework should allow spatial relationships to influence temporal information propagation, adapt to evolving learning contexts, and aggregate relational features at multiple levels of abstraction. Motivated by these requirements, we propose a unified spatial-temporal graph aggregation (USTGA) framework that encodes spatial similarity and temporal dependency into a coherent graph structure. Specifically, spatial similarity graphs, which represent relational or contextual proximity among students or learning entities, are embedded into temporal dependency graphs derived from historical performance trajectories. This unified graph formulation enables spatial relations to dynamically modulate temporal feature aggregation, yielding a more expressive and adaptive representation than conventional architectures. Building upon this representation, USTGA employs a multi-level attention-guided aggregation mechanism that selectively emphasizes informative spatial-temporal neighbors and progressively refines node embeddings across stacked layers. Through this design, the proposed framework effectively captures fine-grained spatial-temporal interactions and improves the accuracy and generalizability of student performance prediction. Furthermore, USTGA adopts an inductive learning strategy inspired by GraphSAGE, where node representations are generated through learnable neighborhood aggregation functions rather than fixed embeddings. Compared with our previous MGSTSN11 model, which follows a multi-graph synchronization paradigm under a transductive GCN framework, USTGA provides a more compact unified representation and shifts toward inductive learning. This transition simplifies the model architecture while enhancing its flexibility and adaptability in handling complex and evolving spatial-temporal dependencies.

Related work

Student performance prediction based on feature-driven machine learning methods

Feature-driven machine learning methods have been widely applied to student performance prediction due to their clear modeling assumptions and computational efficiency12. Representative approaches include support vector machines (SVM), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), decision trees (DT), logistic regression (LR), naive Bayes (NB), and Bayesian networks (BN). These methods typically rely on manually designed features extracted from academic records and learning behaviors, and they have been adopted across diverse educational scenarios to analyze and forecast student outcomes.

SVM has been recognized for their robust generalization capabilities and effectiveness in high-dimensional spaces. For instance,13 compared the performance of SVM and KNN on a dataset from the University of Minho and found that SVM achieved a slightly higher correlation coefficient (0.96 vs. 0.95). Similarly,14 developed early-stage performance classification models incorporating SVMs alongside other classifiers. Their experiments indicated that SVM achieved competitive accuracy levels when predicting student performance based on LMS interaction data. KNN has also seen broad applications due to its simplicity and non-parametric nature.15 proposed an enhanced version of KNN by integrating bio-inspired optimization strategies such as the honey badger algorithm and arithmetic optimization algorithm, resulting in remarkable accuracy (0.921) in predicting final mathematics performance.

DT algorithms, known for their transparency and interpretability, have been evaluated in multiple studies. For example,16 assessed DT performance after applying an adaptive feature selection algorithm and found that DT maintained competitive accuracy while benefiting from reduced feature complexity. Moreover,17 reported that DT demonstrated potential generalizability across different types of educational data. LR has also been adopted as a baseline classifier.14 used LR to classify students into at-risk and high-performing categories during various course phases.

NB classifiers have been used for probabilistic reasoning and low computational complexity.18 introduced a machine learning-based assessment platform utilizing NB, which provided a novel and efficient method for predicting student achievements. BN has been utilized to model the probabilistic dependencies between the student-related variables.19 constructed a BN for MOOC performance prediction using hill-climbing and maximum likelihood estimation. Their model quantified uncertain relationships among students, curricula, and environmental factors, achieved accurate forecasts and offered personalized improvement suggestions. In addition,20 included BN as a baseline method in comparative evaluations and showed that while BN performed adequately, it was generally outperformed by more advanced models.

Despite their effectiveness in many educational applications, feature-driven machine learning methods exhibit inherent limitations. Their predictive performance strongly depends on the quality of handcrafted features, and they often struggle to capture complex spatial-temporal patterns embedded in academic data. In addition, these models usually lack sufficient flexibility to represent high-order interactions among students, courses, and learning stages, particularly when temporal dynamics and relational dependencies become prominent. These challenges motivate the exploration of more expressive modeling paradigms that can automatically learn informative representations from raw educational data.

Student performance prediction based on representation learning methods

To overcome the limitations of feature-driven approaches, representation learning methods based on deep neural networks (DNN) have been increasingly explored for student performance prediction21. By leveraging hierarchical architectures, these methods are capable of automatically learning abstract feature representations and modeling complex temporal or relational structures inherent in educational data. Such capabilities make deep learning particularly suitable for capturing evolving learning behaviors and long-term dependency patterns across students and academic stages.

Convolutional neural networks (CNN) have been applied to capture local spatial correlations and interaction patterns in educational data. A prominent example involved transforming online learning sequences into 2D representations using various encoding methods, which were then classified by CNN and FCN-based ensemble models, yielding superior performance over classical techniques22. Some studies combined CNN with other networks to form hybrid models; for example, the CNN-LSTM architecture was used to analyze LMS interaction logs and predict learning outcomes, outperforming other deep learning baselines23,24. Additionally, the MsaCNN model applied multi-scale convolution and attention mechanisms to student performance records to capture both local and global features and course dependencies25.

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) and their variant, long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, have been widely adopted for modeling temporal dependencies in sequential educational data. For instance, an attention-based hybrid RNN was proposed to capture short-term student behavioral sequences, which were subsequently used for classification via an SVM in the SPC framework26. Similarly, a bidirectional LSTM model was employed to encode textual content in exercises, jointly modeling student interaction and content information within the EERNN and EKT frameworks to improve interpretability and performance tracking27. An attention-based BiLSTM model was also explored to extract high-level representations from historical academic records, achieving superior accuracy in performance prediction tasks28. Other studies extended the traditional RNN with optimization strategies and hybrid architectures, such as the bi-gated RNN with feature selection and hyperparameter optimization to achieve high prediction accuracy29, and VR-ODRNN with feature pruning and optimization algorithms30.

General deep architectures have also demonstrated a strong performance. A DNN-based model achieved notable accuracy in early-stage failure prediction for programming-related courses, outperforming several conventional classifiers31. In parallel, generative adversarial networks (GAN) were integrated with deep support vector machines to enhance prediction in data-scarce environments, where synthetic data generation and multi-kernel learning were leveraged to improve specificity and sensitivity32,33.

More recent studies have leveraged graph-based representations to model relational structures among students and learning activities. For instance, MTGNN34 was introduced to construct multiple similarity-driven graphs for semi-supervised performance prediction. Other approaches employed graph convolutional networks on heterogeneous graphs to infer student engagement patterns from multimodal learning data35. In addition, PRISM36 was proposed to model student relationships by constructing similarity graphs using academic performance, enabling improved performance prediction through integrated relational learning. A dual-graph neural architecture was also developed to jointly exploit interaction-level and attribute-level relationships37. In parallel, attention-based sequence models have been introduced to capture long-range dependencies in learning trajectories, using adaptive mechanisms to handle incomplete or irregular observations38.

Our previous work, MGSTSN, further extended graph-based modeling by constructing multiple spatial and temporal graphs and introducing dedicated synchronization modules to align their interactions. While this multi-graph design captured diverse dependency patterns from different perspectives, it relied on separate graph structures and explicit synchronization processes. Moreover, MGSTSN followed a transductive learning paradigm based on graph convolutional networks, where the model was trained and applied on a fixed graph structure, which limited its flexibility in handling unseen nodes or evolving data.

Despite these advances, existing methods still exhibit several limitations. Many approaches focused on either temporal dynamics or spatial structure, and rarely addressed both in an integrated manner. Multi-graph frameworks, although expressive, often introduced additional architectural complexity and relied on post hoc alignment mechanisms. Furthermore, transductive learning schemes restricted generalization capability when the underlying graph structure changed.

To address these issues, we propose a unified spatial-temporal graph aggregation (USTGA) framework. Unlike prior multi-graph synchronization approaches, USTGA constructs a single unified graph in which spatial similarity is directly embedded into temporal dependency modeling. This design simplifies the architecture while enabling continuous interaction between spatial context and temporal evolution. In addition, USTGA adopts an inductive learning strategy inspired by GraphSAGE, allowing the model to generalize to unseen nodes by learning aggregation functions rather than fixed embeddings. Combined with an attention-guided multi-layer aggregation mechanism, the proposed framework enables more flexible and scalable representation learning for student performance prediction.

Spatial similarity graph

Within the proposed unified spatial-temporal graph aggregation framework, graph structures are defined following the standard formulation of graph convolutional networks, where a graph is composed of a set of nodes and their pairwise connections, and the connectivity pattern is encoded by an adjacency matrix. In the context of student performance prediction, each student is treated as a node in the graph, while edges represent relational dependencies among students or across time.

Within the scope of academic achievement forecasting, the spatial dimension refers to the relationships among different students. Unified spatial-temporal graph aggregation

Graph-based neural architectures have shown strong potential in capturing relational patterns within educational data. However, many existing models treat spatial and temporal dependencies separately or rely on static structures, limiting their ability to capture fine-grained spatial-temporal dynamics inherent in students’ academic trajectories. To address this, we propose a USTGA framework, which integrates multi-order spatial correlations and temporal progression within a unified learning paradigm, enabling more expressive and adaptive representation learning.

This framework is composed of two core components. First, the USTGA operation aggregates the features of spatial and temporal peers using attention-guided weights, thereby effectively integrating information across space and time. Second, stacked USTGA layes capture higher-order interactions, progressively enriching the representation of the student nodes. By merging the immediate and extended influences from both dimensions, the model generates robust embeddings that enhance the prediction of future academic outcomes.

Dataset

The dataset employed in this study is sourced from an open simulated learning online platform in China, containing records of 5,011 virtual students, and is publicly available at https://github.com/yxcos/Student-Performance. For each student, we collected academic performance records spanning six consecutive semesters, with three distinct courses per semester. This yields a dataset of dimensions , where all scores are numerical values within the range of 0 to 100.

Each student follows an evolving performance trajectory across semesters, with course-level variations reflecting differences in subject proficiency and assessment difficulty, while moderate stochastic fluctuations are introduced to represent common variability in learning outcomes. Although simulated, the dataset preserves key characteristics observed in real academic records, including bounded scores, temporal continuity, and non-linear performance trends, making it suitable for evaluating spatial-temporal prediction models.

In the context of our predictive modeling, we utilize the academic records from the first four semesters as the input and the subsequent two semesters as the prediction target. Accordingly, the task is formulated as a multi-step, multi-course forecasting problem, where future academic performance is predicted based on historical learning trajectories rather than reconstructed from observed inputs. Experimental results and analyses

Deep learning models such as LSTM and GRU exhibit moderate improvements, reducing MAE to 15.76 and 15.48 and RMSE to around 19.6, while lowering MAPE to approximately 18%. MGSTSN continues this trend by incorporating multi-graph spatial-temporal modeling, achieving an MAE of 9.03, a MAPE of 11.25%, and an RMSE of 12.27, which indicates its enhanced ability to capture complex relational patterns compared to conventional graph-based baselines. USTGA surpasses all baselines, achieving the lowest error rates across metrics: MAE of 8.69, MAPE of 11.03%, and RMSE of 11.59. These results emphasize USTGA’s superior ability to capture and utilize complex spatial-temporal dependencies inherent in student performance data, offering more precise and robust predictions than existing alternatives (Table 8).

The baseline models show several limitations that constrain their predictive capabilities. SVM, as a classical non-parametric model, struggles with high-dimensional temporal data and lacks mechanisms to capture sequential dependencies. BPNN, while flexible, is prone to overfitting and lacks specialized modules to process time-evolving or relational information. LSTM and GRU address temporal aspects but ignore inter-student relationships, limiting their ability to generalize across connected entities. GCN effectively models node interactions but disregards temporal evolution. Similarly, PRISM incorporates similarity-based relational modeling through graph structures, yet it does not explicitly capture temporal dependencies, resulting in limited performance gains compared to models with dedicated temporal components. STGCN improves on this by introducing separate temporal convolutions, yet it still treats spatial and temporal patterns in a decoupled manner, missing their synchronous influence. Although MGSTSN enhances modeling capacity by incorporating multiple spatial graphs and temporal structures, its reliance on predefined multi-graph constructions and separate synchronous modules limits the flexibility of joint spatial-temporal representation learning.

In contrast, USTGA achieves superior performance due to several complementary design advantages. By constructing unified spatial-temporal graphs (USTGs), the framework jointly models relational correlations among students and the temporal evolution of their academic trajectories, rather than treating these factors in isolation. This formulation enables the model to uncover latent spatial connections, such as students exhibiting similar learning behaviors, engagement patterns, or performance trends over time. Moreover, the attention-guided aggregation mechanism refines neighbor interactions by adaptively weighting informative spatial-temporal relationships, allowing the model to focus on critical dependencies while suppressing less relevant or noisy signals. In addition, the multi-layer aggregation architecture facilitates the capture of both short-range and long-range spatial-temporal dependencies, supporting hierarchical representation learning across multiple scales. Together, these characteristics allow USTGA to form a more comprehensive and flexible understanding of student performance patterns, thereby yielding robust, accurate, and generalizable predictions across diverse educational settings.

To provide a clearer illustration of USTGA’s superior performance, we present a bar chart depicting its relative improvements over the baseline models in terms of MAE, MAPE, and RMSE. As shown in Figure 2, USTGA achieves remarkable reductions in prediction errors, outperforming traditional machine learning models such as SVM and BPNN by margins exceeding 59% across all metrics, with the largest improvement observed against SVM, reaching 66.55% in MAE and 67.25% in MAPE. When compared to sequence models like LSTM and GRU, USTGA still shows substantial gains, reducing MAE and RMSE by approximately 40% and MAPE by over 39%. Even against the graph-based baselines GCN and STGCN, which incorporate spatial and temporal structures, USTGA maintains a clear advantage, with error reductions ranging from about 13% to 25%. Compared with the more advanced MGSTSN model, which employs multi-graph spatial-temporal modeling, USTGA continues to achieve consistent improvements, reducing MAE, MAPE, and RMSE by 3.77%, 1.96%, and 5.54%, respectively. This visualization highlights that USTGA’s design not only improves absolute prediction accuracy but also delivers consistent proportional benefits across diverse model families.

More specifically, USTGA consistently maintains the lowest MAE, MAPE, and RMSE across both prediction horizons, indicating strong long-term prediction stability relative to the baseline methods. This stability can be attributed to the unified spatial-temporal graph modeling, which enables the framework to preserve meaningful inter-student relationships and temporal dependencies even as the prediction horizon extends. In addition, the attention-guided aggregation mechanism helps mitigate error propagation by adaptively emphasizing informative spatial-temporal interactions, while the multi-layer aggregation structure supports robust representation learning over longer temporal ranges. As a result, USTGA demonstrates a reliable ability to generalize across multiple future semesters, despite the increasing difficulty inherent in long-term forecasting.

Robustness test

To evaluate the robustness of USTGA under noisy conditions, we conducted experiments by introducing Gaussian noise into the test data. Specifically, we added noise with zero mean and standard deviations of 2 and 4 to simulate potential fluctuations or inaccuracies in student performance records. This experiment aimed to assess whether the predictive performance of USTGA would remain stable when confronted with data imperfections, which are common in real-world educational datasets.

The results, visualized in Figure 6, demonstrate the model’s remarkable resilience. When noise with a standard deviation of 2 is added, the MAE slightly increases to 8.71, the MAPE rises marginally to 11.04%, and the RMSE becomes 11.61. Even under stronger noise (standard deviation 4), the MAE is only 8.74, the MAPE is 11.06%, and the RMSE is 11.63. These minimal changes highlight the model’s ability to maintain consistent accuracy despite input perturbations. Overall, the robustness test confirms that USTGA delivers reliable predictions even when exposed to noisy or imperfect data, reinforcing its practicality for real-world applications.

Conclusions

In this work, we investigate the problem of student performance prediction from a spatial-temporal perspective and propose a unified spatial-temporal graph aggregation framework to address the limitations of existing approaches. Conventional models often treat spatial relationships and temporal dynamics as separate components, which hinders their ability to capture the synchronized and interdependent evolution inherent in academic data. By integrating spatial similarity and temporal dependency within a single graph structure, the proposed framework enables a more coherent representation of complex educational interactions. Moreover, the attention-guided aggregation mechanism, together with stacked aggregation layers, progressively refines feature representations, allowing the model to adapt to evolving spatial-temporal patterns.

Comprehensive experiments conducted on a real-world student achievement dataset demonstrate that the proposed method consistently outperforms eight representative baseline models, including both traditional machine learning methods and recent deep learning approaches. Across multiple evaluation metrics, such as MAE, MAPE, and RMSE, the framework achieves stable and notable performance gains, indicating its effectiveness in capturing subtle spatial-temporal dependencies and generating accurate predictions across different academic terms. Beyond predictive performance, the proposed framework provides practical value for data-driven educational decision-making, including early academic risk identification and targeted instructional planning.

Despite its effectiveness, the proposed framework has several limitations. In scenarios with a relatively small number of students, the performance of USTGA may be constrained, as the construction of the spatial graph depends on sufficient relational signals to support stable aggregation. In addition, the current framework defines spatial similarity primarily based on performance correlation, which captures latent relationships among students but remains a single-dimensional representation. This formulation may not fully reflect the complex factors influencing learning behaviors in real educational environments, such as interaction patterns and contextual attributes. Future work will explore the integration of multi-modal information, including behavioral, contextual, and curriculum-related features, to construct more comprehensive spatial representations and further enhance the model’s expressiveness and applicability.
