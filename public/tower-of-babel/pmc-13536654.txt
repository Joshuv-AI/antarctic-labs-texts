Targeting breast cancer stem cells by restoring RBM38 function: mechanism and protein delivery therapy
Authors: Jun Yin, Ting Wang, Xuedan Han, Ying Chen, Yue Yang, Yifan Yang, Yidong Zhang, Yu Lu, Miaomiao Niu, Shengtao Xu, Haibo Rong, Lei Huang, Chenxi Xiang, Xiaoman Li, Lufeng Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer stemness drives tumor progression and therapy resistance, yet the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. In this study, we observed that RBM38 expression gradually decreases during breast cancer progression in MMTV-PyMT transgenic mice. Using a model with targeted RBM38 expression in breast tissue, we found it suppressed tumor initiation, decreased tumor size and metastasis, and reduced breast cancer stem cells. Multi-omics analyses, including single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, RIP-sequencing, and proteomics, were used to reveal the mechanisms. We identified HOXD10 mRNA as a critical downstream target of RBM38. Phosphorylation of RBM38 at Ser117, Thr120, and Thr132 by SLK and PGAM5 is required for RBM38-mediated stemness suppression, protein complex formation, and HOXD10 mRNA regulation. Based on these insights, we explored RBM38’s therapeutic potential using an activatable supercharged polypeptide (ASCP) delivery platform. In patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, ASCP-RBM38 achieved effective tumor localization, reduced stemness, and protected against doxorubicin (DOX)-induced cardiotoxicity. Clinical analysis of human samples validated this mechanism. Our study underscores the pivotal role of phosphorylation-mediated regulation and protein complex formation in RBM38-mediated control of breast cancer stemness, highlighting its therapeutic potential.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40364-026-00984-2.Keywords: RBM38, RNA binding protein, Oxidative modulation, Phosphorylation, Breast cancer stemness

Introduction

Breast cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in women worldwide and continues to pose a critical public health challenge [1]. The heterogeneity of breast cancer contributes to diverse clinical outcomes and treatment responses. Contemporary oncological research has revealed that specific tumorigenic subclones, clinically designated as cancer stem cells (CSCs)/tumor-initiating cells (TICs), exert pivotal functions in mediating neoplastic dissemination, treatment-refractory phenotypes, and disease recrudescence [2]. Elucidating the regulatory pathways governing mammary carcinoma stem cell plasticity constitutes a fundamental prerequisite for formulating targeted interventions against these therapy-resistant malignant subpopulations. To develop such interventions, it is critical to identify central regulatory hubs that can integrate diverse cellular signals and orchestrate transcriptional or post-transcriptional programs to dictate the stem cell state. RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), given their capacity for dynamic, post-transcriptional control of gene networks, represent prime candidates for fulfilling this integrative role.

Within the molecular landscape of gene regulation, RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) have been recognized as pivotal modulators governing transcriptional dynamics and post-mRNA processing events. Current studies have identified multiple RBP family members - including but not limited to Musashi 2 (MSI2) [3], SYNCRIP (hnRNP-Q) [4], LIN28/MSI2 heterodimers [5], and fragile X-related protein 1 (FXR1) [6] - that molecularly orchestrate cellular pluripotency maintenance. Notably, RNA-binding motif protein 38 (RBM38/RNPC1) exhibits evolutionarily conserved RNP-type domains (designated RNP1/RBD1 and RNP2/RBD2), whose stereochemical configurations confer structural competence for ribonucleoprotein complex formation [7]. Moreover, alternative splicing of RBM38 produces two isoforms, RNPC1a and RNPC1b, which share the same RNA-binding motifs but exhibit distinct functions [8]. However, whether RBM38 functions beyond a canonical mRNA stabilizer to act as a phosphorylation-dependent regulatory hub that actively governs cancer stem cell fate remains unexplored. The previous study has shown that mice deficient in RBM38 are susceptible to accelerated aging and spontaneous tumors [9], establishing RBM38 as a tumor suppressor. However, the lack of transgenic mice with mammary gland-specific RBM38 overexpression has hindered the definitive confirmation of its tumor-suppressive role in breast cancer, particularly in the regulation of breast CSCs.

Post-translational modifications (PTMs) are essential processes that occur after protein synthesis. They involve the addition, removal, or modification of specific chemical groups on proteins, which can significantly impact their structure, stability, and functionality. In the context of RBPs, PTMs play a crucial role in determining their ability to interact with RNA and exert their functions [10, 11]. RBM38 is also a transcriptional target of the p53 family and functions in a negative feedback loop to fine-tune p53 activity [12]. Notably, the serine-195 (S195) in RBM38, which can be phosphorylated by glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3) [13], is essential for RBM38’s roles in controlling p53 activity [14]. However, the PTMs of RBM38 in breast cancer are still unclear.

In this study, we sought to reposition RBM38 from a conventional mRNA-stabilizing factor to a phosphorylation-regulated master coordinator of breast cancer stemness. We hypothesized that RBM38 exerts this control by forming dynamic protein complexes to post-transcriptionally regulate key fate-determining circuits. To test this, we first documented a progressive downregulation of RBM38 expression during tumor progression in the MMTV-PyMT transgenic model. Through integrated multi-omics analyses, including single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, RIP-sequencing, transcriptomics, and proteomics, we identified HOXD10 as a key downstream target of RBM38. We further demonstrated that RBM38 competes with miR-10b for binding to the HOXD10 3′UTR to suppress stemness. This function depends on RBM38’s RNP1 domain and is crucially regulated by phosphorylation mediated by SLK and PGAM5, which controls its ability to form functional protein complexes and stabilize HOXD10 mRNA. The clinical relevance of this RBM38/HOXD10/miR-10b axis was further validated in patient samples. Finally, guided by these mechanistic insights, we developed a tumor-targeted protein delivery system (ASCP-RBM38) and demonstrated its therapeutic potential in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models [15]. Taken together, our work not only redefines RBM38 as an integrative hub in stemness regulation but also highlights its promise as a therapeutic target for attenuating breast cancer stemness.

Clinical samples

Ethical oversight was ensured through formal approval by the Institutional Review Board (XYFY2021-KL038-01), with written informed consent secured from all participants prior to biospecimen acquisition. Data collection

Multimodal omics datasets were retrieved from the NCBI GEO repository (accession codes: GSE225600/GSE213688) under FAIR data principles. The GSE225600 (accession ID: GPL24676) comprises spatially resolved transcriptomic profiles of four primary mammary carcinomas with matched metastatic nodal deposits, acquired through 10X Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3’ v3.1 chemistry. Complementarily, GSE213688 integrates three molecular apocrine-like (CL-classified) carcinomas, four basal-like TNBCs with chromosomal instability signatures (CIN+), and four metaplastic variants, processed via Visium Spatial Gene Expression Slide v1.0.

Single-cell data pre-process and annotation

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis was executed within a standardized computational framework (Seurat v4.3 workflow) incorporating rigorous quality filtration protocols. Cellular integrity thresholds were implemented as: nFeature_RNA ∈ [400,4000] with mitochondrial read contamination ≤ 10%. Processed data underwent log-normalization and variance stabilization using regularized negative binomial regression. To address technical variability, cross-dataset integration was achieved through Harmony v1.2.0’s reciprocal PCA-based manifold alignment. Nonlinear dimensionality reduction via UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) was performed on harmonized principal components (PCs = 30, resolution = 0.8), followed by graph-based Leiden clustering (modularity = 0.7). Cellular taxonomy annotation leveraged lineage-specific transcriptional signatures curated from CellMarker 2.0 database (http://bio-bigdata.hrbmu.edu.cn/CellMarker/) with supplementary validation markers detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Pseudotime analysis and cell communication analysis

For pseudotemporal trajectory reconstruction, stochastic subsampling (n = 5,000 cells) was performed using Seurat’s integrated subsampling algorithm (seed = 42). Cellular differentiation dynamics were modeled through Monocle2 v1.4.0 with expression thresholds (mean > 0.1, dispersion > 1×fit) determined via negative binomial regression. Manifold learning was executed via DDRTree - a discriminative dimensionality reduction algorithm optimizing branch detection sensitivity (λ = 1e-3, σ = 0.01). Trajectory inference leveraged the reduceDimension() function with max_components = 3 and auto_param_adjust = T, while differentiation states were assigned through partition-based graph abstraction (qval < 0.01). Differentiation pseudotime projections were visualized through streamplot rendering (kernel density = 0.7) with RBM38 expression heatmaps superimposed on trajectory branches. Complementarily, ligand-receptor interactome analysis was performed on a matched 5,000-cell subsample using CellChat v1.6.1 with CellPhoneDB v4.1 as curated interaction repository. Communication probabilities were computed through permutation-based null hypothesis testing (n = 100 permutations, p < 0.01) and represented as multiplex networks via igraph’s weighted adjacency matrix (edge weight=LRscore×prob). Significant interactions (FDR < 0.05) were filtered through scCATCH v2.0 prior to Sankey diagram visualization.

CytoTRACE analysis

The stemness of breast cancer cells was evaluated using the “CytoTRACE” package. CytoTRACE provides a method for measuring cell stemness without requiring prior knowledge. It robustly infers the developmental potential of cell clusters. Each selected cell was assigned a CytoTRACE score ranging from 0 to 1, with higher scores indicating greater stemness.

Spatial expression distribution of RBM38

Spatiotemporal gene expression deconvolution of RBM38 was implemented through Seurat v4.3.0’s integrated spatial transcriptomics pipeline, leveraging histoarchitecture-informed spatial domains derived through either: (1) unsupervised graph-based clustering (Leiden algorithm, resolution = 0.8) on UMAP-embedded feature space (n_PCs = 30), or (2) probabilistic registration to HuBMAP reference frameworks. Spatial differential expression analysis (sDEGs) was conducted using SPARK-X v1.6’s non-parametric kernel-based method (bandwidth = 100 μm, FDR < 0.05) to resolve regionalized transcriptional patterns across annotated tissue compartments. The expression distribution of epithelial markers and RBM38 was presented at the spatial transcriptomic level.

Cell culture and reagents

Human mammary carcinoma biospecimens (MCF-7 [ER+], BT474 [HER2+], MDA-MB-231 [TNBC]) and embryonic kidney HEK293T lineages were commercially sourced from ATCC (VA, USA) under strict quality-controlled maintenance protocols. Cellular authentication was performed through multiplex STR genotyping (Promega PowerPlex® 21 system) with annual recertification. The DOX-resistant MCF-7/Adr subline was acquired from KeyGen BioTECH (Nanjing, China) and maintained under pharmacoresistance selection pressure. Culture conditions were standardized as follows: DMEM (KeyGEN #KGM12800-500) for MCF-7/HEK293T systems; Leibovitz’s L15 (#KGM41300-500) for MDA-MB-231; RPMI1640 (#KGM31800-500) for MCF-7/Adr - each supplemented with 10% FBS (BI #04-001-1ACS). Standardized incubation parameters included 37 °C thermoregulation with 5% CO2-controlled atmosphere (except L15-medium cultures maintained at ambient CO2). PGAM5 inhibitor LFHP-1c was synthesized by Prof. Zhangjiang Huang from China Pharmaceutical University [16].

Plasmids, transfection, and Lentivirus infection

Molecular cloning workflows were executed per manufacturer specifications: HOXD10 overexpression (pcDNA3.1(+)-HOXD10), RNPC1b lentiviral expression (PLVX-RNPC1b), and HA-tagged RBM38 variants (pcDNA3.1-N-HA-RBM38) were engineered via Vazyme C112-01 One-Step Cloning. Site-directed mutagenesis of HOXD10 3’UTR elements (PMIR-HOXD10 3’UTR-mut series) and RBM38 RNA-binding domain truncations (ΔRNP1/ΔRNP2) were synthesized using Vazyme C214-01 Mutagenesis Kit. The ASCP-RBM38 chimeric construct (GenScript) was subcloned into pET28a prokaryotic expression vector for BL21(DE3) transformation. Gene modulation experiments employed: 1. RNAi complexes (siRBM38/siHOXD10; GenePharma);2. miR-10b gain/loss-of-function reagents (mimics/inhibitors; GenePharma); 3. Lentiviral vectors (PLVX-RBM38, shRBM38, shHOXD10; GenePharma). Reverse transfection protocols were implemented at 70–80% confluence using JetPRIME (#PT-114-07) per optimized lipid: DNA ratios. Lentiviral particle production involved third-generation packaging systems (psPAX2/pMD2.G) with subsequent transduction of subconfluent monolayers (MOI = 5–10). Stable polyclonal populations were selected under puromycin pressure (8 µg/mL × 96 h) with validation via Western blot (HA-tag detection) and qRT-PCR (miR-10b TaqMan assays). Comprehensive oligonucleotide sequences cataloged in Supplementary Tables 2–3.

Generation of transgenic mouse and genotyping

Mammary-specific RBM38 transgenic founder lines were engineered through piggyBac transposase-mediated transgenesis under the transcriptional control of murine whey acidic protein (WAP) promoter by Shanghai Nanfang model Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) [17]. The transgenesis workflow comprised: (1) Pronuclear microinjection of linearized transgene cassettes into fertilized C57BL/6J oocytes; (2) Embryo transfer into C57BL/6J pseudopregnant recipients; (3) Germline transmission confirmation via multiplex junctional PCR. Complementary MMTV-PyMT transgenic mice on C57BL/6 background (JAX Stock #022974; spontaneous mammary tumorigenesis phenotype) were procured from Shanghai Nanfang Model Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of China Pharmaceutical University (2021-04-009) in strict compliance with ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

Genomic DNA isolation was performed using Vazyme DC102 magnetic bead-based purification system (elution buffer: 10 mM Tris-HCl pH8.0), with aliquots cryopreserved at -20 °C. Genotyping PCR reactions (25 µL) contained: 2× Taq Plus Master Mix II (Vazyme P213), 0.4 µM allele-specific primers (Supplementary Table 4), and 20 ng template DNA. Thermocycling parameters: 95 °C/5 min; 35 cycles of [95 °C/30 s, Tm ± 3 °C/30 s, 72 °C/1 kb/min]; 72 °C/5 min. Products were resolved on 2% MetaPhor™ agarose gels (Lonza, #50080) with EZ-Vision® DNA staining (Amresco, #E733-01ML).

Isolation of lin-NECs (neoplastic MECs) and pNECs (pre-neoplastic MECs)

Organoid isolation protocols were executed per previously established methodologies with technical optimizations [18]. Primary tissue processing commenced with mechanical fragmentation of preneoplastic mammary glands/tumors under sterile laminar flow, followed by adipose depletion via differential centrifugation (500×g, 5 min). Enzymatic disaggregation was performed in a three-phase system: Phase I: Tissue fragments suspended in DMEM/F12 + 5% FBS + 10 ng/ml EGF (Sigma #E9644) + 500 ng/ml hydrocortisone (Sigma #H0888) + 5 µg/ml insulin (Sigma #I0516) + 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Sangon #B540732) + 1 mg/ml collagenase/hyaluronidase cocktail (Sigma #C7657/SRE0032) at 37 °C/5% CO₂ for 60 min with orbital shaking (120 rpm). Phase II: Sequential enzymatic digestion with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA digestion (2 min, 37 °C) under gentle agitation, followed by neutralization with 5 mg/ml Dispase II (Thermo #17105041) + 0.1 mg/ml DNase I (Sigma #D5025) in Ca²⁺-free HBSS (5 min, 37 °C). Phase III: Erythrocyte lysis using 0.64% NH₄Cl (STEMCELL #07850) in hypotonic buffer (5 min, RT). The cellular homogenate was filtered through 40 μm nylon mesh (Corning #431750) and resuspended in HBSS + 0.5% fatty acid-free BSA (Sigma #A8806). Epithelial lineage enrichment was achieved through negative selection magnetic sorting using EasySep™ Mouse Epithelial Cell Enrichment Kit (STEMCELL #19868) per manufacturer’s STEMCELL protocols (Document #1000000689). NECs and pNECs were cryopreserved in Recovery™ Cell Culture Freezing Medium (Thermo #A3536901) for downstream applications.

Whole-mount staining of mammary gland and histology

The methodological framework established in prior investigations was implemented with technical refinements [18]. Mammary gland processing entailed immersion-fixation of the 4th inguinal pairs in Carnoy’s solution (EtOH: CHCl3:glacial acetic acid, 6:3:1 v/v) for 2–4 h, followed by rehydration through graded ethanol series (100%-70%). Overnight chromatic impregnation with 0.2% carmine alum (Sigma #C1022) preceded whole-mount specimen preparation under stereomicroscopic guidance (Leica M205C). Subsequent progressive ethanol dehydration and paraffin infiltration using Tissue-Tek® VIP6 processor (Sakura #4813) enabled serial sectioning (5 μm) followed by H&E staining per H-score protocol.

Metastatic mouse model construction

Orthotopic xenograft models were established via bilateral mammary fat pad injection of RBM38-engineered isogenic variants (1 × 10⁶ cells in 200 µL sterile PBS; pH 7.4) into 5-week-old female BALB/cAnN.Cg-Foxn1nu/CrlNarl immunocompromised mice (Gempharmatech Strain Code: D000521). Following a 14-day tumorigenesis latency period, in vivo multiparametric MRI was performed using Bruker BioSpec 7T/20 USR preclinical imaging system equipped with 72 mm horizontal-bore magnet and respiratory-gated T2-weighted RARE sequence (TR/TE = 2500/33 ms, matrix = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 0.7 mm). Survival kinetics were longitudinally monitored until humane endpoint criteria were met. Following the mouse’s death, the survival time was recorded, and Servicebio performed H&E staining of the lung tissue (Wuhan, China).

In vivo tumorigenic analysis

The orthotopic limiting dilution transplantation assay was performed through tripartite dose escalation (1 × 10⁵, 1 × 10⁶, 1 × 10⁷ cells/site) of differentially modified breast cancer variants xenotransplanted into bilateral inguinal mammary fat pads (4th pair) of 6-week-old NSG mice (Gempharmatech). Following a 14-day tumorigenesis latency period, subjects were humanely euthanized. Tumor initiation capacity was quantified through Extreme Limiting Dilution Analysis (ELDA v1.0) with maximum likelihood estimation (95% confidence intervals). Stem cell frequencies were computationally modeled through ELDA’s modified Poisson regression algorithm (http://bioinf.wehi.edu.au/software/elda/) incorporating Fisher’s exact test for significance (p < 0.05).

Western blot and native-page western blot

Western blot analysis was performed according to our previous procedure [19]. Moreover, the native page was operated as SDS-PAGE, but the procedure was without any SDS to confirm the natural structure of protein. The primary antibodies used in this procedure included: SOX2 (Wanleibio, Cat # WL00982a), Oct3/4 (Wanleibio, Cat # WL01728), β-actin (Proteintech, Cat # 66009-1-lg), RBM38 (Abcam, Cat # ab200403), HOXD10 (Santa, Cat # sc-166235), PGP (Proteintech, Cat # 25081-1-AP), ALDH1A1 (Proteintech, Cat # 15910-1-AP), PGAM5 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat # sc-515880), SLK (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat # sc-434), PKM2 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat # 4053), GAPDH (Proteintech, Cat # 60004-1-lg).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and proteomics

The MDA-MB-231 cells overexpressing RBM38 were washed twice with pre-chilled PBS and then lysed with pre-chilled RIPA buffer. The cell lysate was centrifuged at 4 °C, 14,000 g for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected for protein quantification using a BCA assay kit. To remove non-specific proteins and reduce background, immunoprecipitation procedures were conducted under stringent buffer-exchange conditions. Initial lysate pre-clearance involved incubation with Protein A/G Plus Agarose beads (Thermo Scientific #22851; 100 µL slurry) in lysis buffer (1 mL total protein extract) with end-over-end rotation (4 °C, 30 rpm, 10 min). Subsequent microcentrifugation (4 °C, 14,000 × g, 15 min) yielded clarified supernatant for immunocomplex formation through overnight incubation (16 h, 4 °C) with immunoprecipitation-grade anti-RBM38 monoclonal antibody. Affinity capture of immunocomplexes employed fresh Protein A/G beads (100 µL) with orbital agitation (25 °C, 1 h). Bead-antibody complexes were pelleted via pulse microcentrifugation (14,000 × g, 5 s) and resuspended in 2× Laemmli buffer with 5% β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma #M6250), followed by thermal denaturation (95 °C, 5 min). Denatured samples underwent electrophoretic separation on 10% Tris-Glycine precast gels (Bio-Rad #4561093) under constant voltage (100 V, 90 min). Post-electrophoretic transfer to methanol-activated PVDF membranes (0.45 μm; Millipore #IPVH00010) was performed using Trans-Blot Turbo semi-dry system (Bio-Rad #1704150). Membranes underwent blocking (5% non-fat dry milk in TBST, 25 °C, 90 min) prior to overnight incubation (4 °C) with anti-RBM38 (1:1000) in blocking buffer. Post-primary antibody incubation, three 10-min TBST washes preceded 1-h incubation with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000; Abcam #ab6721) at 25 °C. Finally, protein expression changes were detected using an ECL detection system. The Coomassie Brilliant Blue-stained gel strip was sent to Beijing Novogene Science and Technology Co., Ltd. after co-IP electrophoresis for proteomic analysis of interacting proteins.

To delineate RBM38 phosphorylation landscapes under differential signaling modulation, RBM38-overexpressing MCF-7 lineages subjected to PGAM5 pharmacological inhibition or SLK genetic ablation were lysed in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with phosphatase/protease inhibitors. Immunoprecipitation was performed as per established protocol. Enriched RBM38 complexes were electrophoresed on 10% Tris-Glycine SDS-PAGE (100 V, 90 min) and visualized via Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 colloidal staining. Target bands were excised for phosphoproteomic profiling using nanoLC-MS/MS (Orbitrap Fusion Lumos; Thermo Scientific) with database matching against PhosphoSitePlus (v6.5.1) at OeBiotech’s CAP-accredited facility.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated using TransZol Up phenol-chloroform extraction (TransGen Biotech #ET111-01). cDNA synthesis employed Moloney Murine Leukemia Virus Reverse Transcriptase (Vazyme #R021-01) under standard thermal cycling (42 °C/30min → 85 °C/5min). Quantitative PCR amplification was conducted with ChamQ SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme #Q711-02; containing dNTPs and HotStart Taq DNA Polymerase) on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Technical triplicates were analyzed with threshold cycle normalization (2−ΔΔCt method) against GAPDH endogenous control. Primer sequences (Supplementary Table 4) were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST with optimized annealing temperatures (60 ± 2 °C).

Flow cytometry analysis

The detailed procedure was mentioned in our previous study [20]. Briefly, dissociated single-cell suspensions (cell lines/primary isolates) underwent sequential ice-cold PBS wash cycles. Cells were immunolabeled with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies: anti-CD24-PE (BD #555428; Clone M1/69) and anti-CD44-APC (BD #559942; Clone IM7) in staining buffer (HBSS + 2% FBS) for 30 min at 4 °C protected from light. ALDH enzymatic activity was quantified using ALDEFLUOR™ (STEMCELL #01700) per manufacturer’s protocol, with diethylaminobenzaldehyde (DEAB) inhibitor controls. Post-staining cells were washed and analyzed on BD LSRFortessa™ X-20 using FACSDiva™ software v8.0.1 with multicolor compensation settings. Gating strategies employed fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls for background subtraction.

Cell spheroid formation assay

To assess three-dimensional clonogenic potential, cells were maintained in serum-free MammoCult™ proliferation medium (STEMCELL Technologies #05620; containing epidermal growth factor) using Corning® Costar® ultra-low adherence multiwell plates (#3473). Single-cell suspensions were plated at clonal density (3000 cells/mL) in 500µL aliquots per well. Cultures were sustained under standardized conditions (37 °C/5% CO₂) for 8–10 days with medium replenishment every 72 h. Quantitative morphometric analysis was performed using a Leica DMi8 phase contrast system (LAS X v3.7 software) with automated sphere enumeration (> 50 μm diameter threshold).

MTT assay

Cellular viability assessment was conducted via colorimetric MTT assay. 96-well culture plates were inoculated with 4 × 10³ cells/well in triplicate. Post-attachment (16–18 h), doxorubicin (Sigma #D1515) was administered at indicated concentrations for 48 h continuous exposure. MTT reagent (0.5 mg/mL final concentration; Selleckchem #S0077) was introduced for mitochondrial reductase activity quantification through 4 h incubation (37 °C/5% CO₂). Formazan crystals were solubilized in DMSO (100 µL/well; Sigma #D2650) following media aspiration, with orbital agitation (300 rpm ×10 min). Optical density measurements were acquired at λ = 490 nm using Bio-Rad iMark microplate reader (Model #1681130) with Gen5 v3.11 analysis software.

RNA stability assay

After individual treatments, cells were treated with 5 µg/ml actinomycin D (ActD, Cat # HY-17559, MedChem Express, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). At different time points (0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h), the cells were collected using TransZol Up, and qRT-PCR was performed to analyze the mRNA level of HOXD10. The mRNA half-life was determined from the measurements obtained.

RNA-sequencing assay

Global gene expression profiles of isogenic RBM38-engineered breast cancer variants were analyzed through RNA-seq. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol®/RNeasy hybrid protocol with RNA integrity validated via Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (RIN ≥ 8.0, ISO 17025-certified). Strand-specific sequencing libraries were prepared with NEBNext® Ultra™ II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB #E7760S) incorporating poly-A selection and rRNA depletion, followed by 150 bp paired-end sequencing on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (20 M reads/sample, Q30 > 90%). Differential expression calling was performed via Cuffdiff v2.2.1 (FPKM normalization) with FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg method). Experimental workflow and computational pipelines followed published standards [18, 20, 21]. Raw FASTQ files and processed data were deposited in GEO (Accession: GSE253253).

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)-sequencing assays

Ribonucleoprotein complexes were immunoprecipitated using protein A/G magnetic beads (YEASEN #36403ES03) coupled with RBM38-specific antibody (Proteintech #66720-1-Ig) and IgG isotype control (#66467-1-Ig). Co-precipitated RNAs underwent NovaSeq 6000 sequencing (150 bp PE, Q30 > 90%) to profile RBM38-bound transcripts (GEO: GSE253253). Functional validation in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 models involved transient transfection of miR-10b mimics (Dharmacon #C-310512-05) combined with HA-tagged RBM38 variants (WT/ΔRNP1/ΔRNP2) or siRNA-mediated knockdown. Post-transfection (48 h), cells were lysed in NP-40 buffer (Beyotime #P0013F; 50 mM Tris pH7.4, 150 m NaCl) for RIP using Ago2 antibody (Proteintech #60035-1-Ig). HOXD10 expression was quantified qRT-PCR.

RNA pull-down

MCF-7 cells were transfected with siRBM38 and a Negative control. The cells were then collected and lysed using NP-40. BeaverBeads™ Streptavidin (Beaver, Cat # 22305-1) was employed to bind to biotin, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, a qRT-PCR assay was conducted to measure the level of HOXD10. For in vitro transcription, the Ribo™ RNAmax-T7 biotin-labeled transcription kit (RIBOBIO, Cat # C11002-1) was utilized to generate biotin-labeled RNA. This biotin-labeled RNA was then utilized in the RNA pull-down experiment to capture the RNA-protein complex. Finally, western blot analysis was performed to determine the protein level of RBM38 in the pull-down complex.

Luciferase reporter assay

HEK293T cells were seeded into 24-well plates and transfected with plvx-RBM38 plasmid, luciferase reporter plasmids (HOXD10 3’UTR, HOXD10 3’UTR-mut, HOXD10 3’UTR-1, HOXD10 3’UTR-2 cloned into PMIR reporter plasmids), and renilla reporter control plasmid. After 48 h, the Dual-Luciferase Reporter kit (Promega, Cat # E1910) was used to measure luciferase activity according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Characterization of ASCP-RBM38 recombinant proteins

Electrophoretic Separation and Immunoblotting: Recombinant proteins were resolved via 12% denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (EpiZyme #PG012; Tris-glycine-SDS buffer system) at constant voltage (120 V, 90 min) followed by semi-dry transfer to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore #IPVH00010). Immunodetection utilized a murine monoclonal anti-polyhistidine antibody (1:5,000; Proteintech #66005-1-Ig; clone 4E3) with subsequent probing using horseradish peroxidase-conjugated caprine anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000; Proteintech #PR30011). Chemiluminescent signals were captured using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP imaging system with Image Lab v6.1 software. Intracellular delivery of ASCP-RBM38

ASCP-RBM38 was labeled with FITC-NHS for 12 h at 4℃. After ASCP-RBM38 (FITC, 5 µM) was incubated with cells for 24 h, the intracellular localization was analyzed by laser scanning confocal microscope. DAPI, as a nuclear stain, was added to the dish (NEST Biotechnology, China) prior to observation.

The recombinant RBM38 proteins were expressed and purified. To generate REMSA probes, in vitro transcription utilized to generate biotin-labeled RNA as mentioned in RNA pull-down section. In Vivo Pharmacokinetic Profiling: Female BALB/c nude mice received orthotopic implantation of luciferase-expressing MCF-7 cells (5 × 10⁶ cells in 100 µL PBS) within the subcutaneous dorsolateral region. In vitro hemolytic activity

Whole blood specimens were obtained via cardiac puncture from C57BL/6 mice into K₂EDTA-coated vacutainers (Becton Dickinson #366643). Erythrocyte enrichment was achieved through five sequential isotonic PBS washes (pH 7.4) with centrifugation (300 ×g, 10 min, 4 °C). Washed erythrocyte suspensions were exposed to gradient concentrations (0.1–100 µg/mL) of recombinant fusion proteins for 0.5–8 h at 37 °C with orbital shaking (120 rpm). Triton X-100 (1.5% v/v, Sigma #T8787) served as the positive control hemolytic agent. Post-incubation, cell-free lysates were collected via microcentrifugation (12,000 ×g, 5 min) and hemoglobin release quantified by measuring optical density (OD) at λ = 540 nm using a SpectraMax i3x microplate reader (Molecular Devices) equipped with SoftMax Pro v7.1, with data normalized to positive control lysates.

Evaluation of the anti-tumor effects of human recombinant ASCP-RBM38 protein in vivo

For MCF-7 xenograft studies, three days prior to tumor cell inoculation, female BALB/c nude mice received intramuscular injections of 17β-estradiol (0.1 mg/mouse in sesame oil, once weekly) to maintain stable physiological estrogen levels in vivo. MCF-7 cells (5 × 10⁶ cells/mouse) were mixed with Matrigel (1:1) and subcutaneously injected into the left dorsal flank near the axilla. When tumors reached ~ 100 mm³ (volume = ½ × length × width²), animals were stratified into six cohorts via stratified randomization: (1) saline control; (2) neoadjuvant DOX (0.5 mg/kg; Main Luck Pharma #H20058566); (3–4) ASCP-RBM38 monotherapy (1/5 mg/kg); (5) ASCP-RBM38 (5 mg/kg) + DOX combination. Tumor growth kinetics were tracked biweekly via digital caliper (Mitutoyo #500-196-30). Terminal sacrifice occurred at day 28 post-implantation for tumor gravimetric analysis and organ histopathology (H&E staining via Servicebio #G1102, Wuhan). Metastatic Dissemination Assay: To evaluate antimetastatic efficacy, MDA-MB-231 cells (2 × 10⁶ cells/mouse in PBS) were administered via tail vein injection (27G needle). Cohorts received either ASCP-RBM38 (5 mg/kg i.v., q3d×4) or vehicle control. At study endpoint (day 28), pulmonary metastatic burden was quantified through histomorphometric analysis of paraffin-embedded sections (4 μm) stained with hematoxylin-eosin (Servicebio #G1005). Metastatic foci (> 50 μm diameter) were enumerated across five lobar sections per animal.

Evaluation of the anti-tumor effects of human recombinant ASCP-RBM38 protein through PDX model

For the PDX model, in compliance with the protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University and with the subject’s informed consent (XYFY2021-KL038-01), Primary tumor samples were procured to initiate PDX models. Essentially, primary tumor samples obtained through surgery from patients were gathered, trimmed, sliced into 20–30 mm3 fragments, and promptly implanted under the skin using an 18-gauge trocar in the bilateral fore and/or hind flanks of 6–8 week-old female NOD-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/Nju mice (NCG) (GemPharmatech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). After successfully establishing the initial xenograft generation (referred to as P0) and achieving tumor sizes ranging from 100 to 300 mm3, consecutive implantations were carried out in NCG mice to propagate the xenograft tumors further (designated as P1, P2, P3, and so forth). The IHC assays were performed as per the instructions of manufacturers of primary antibodies (RBM38, Cat # ab200403, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), HOXD10 (Cat # MA5-26485, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA), and high-sensitivity HRP-IgG polymers detection system (Cat # PV-8000, ZSGB-Bio, Beijing, China). Statistical analysis

The data presented in this study were obtained from three independent experiments and expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD). Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Differences between group means were assessed by Student’s t test or Mann-whitney U test (two groups), Kruskas-wallis test, Spearman rank test or one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons tests (more than two groups). One-way ANOVA was conducted only if F in ANOVA achieved P < 0.05, and there was no significant variance inhomogeneity. The univariate and multivariate survival analysis were performed in R (version 4.2.2) using “Survival” and “Survminer” packages. Statistical significance was determined with a significance level of P < 0.05.

Results

RBM38 suppresses the formation, metastasis, and CSCs expansion of PyMT-induced mammary tumor

Molecular characterization of the RBM38 locus demonstrates its dual functionality as an RBP gene, generating two distinct splice variants: the canonical RNPC1a isoform comprising 239-residue architecture and an alternatively processed RNPC1b variant containing 121 amino acid residues [8]. As the previous studies have shown that RNPC1a, but not RNPC1b, can inhibit tumor progression and positively correlates with breast cancer patients’ survival probability [9, 22], this study primarily focuses on RNPC1a, and the terms RBM38 and RNPC1a are used interchangeably. Notably, RBM38 expression progressively decreased during breast cancer progression in MMTV-PyMT transgenic mice (Fig. 1A). To further confirm RBM38-mediated effects on breast cancer progression, we further established mice with targeted expression of RBM38 transgene in the mammary gland of MMTV-PyMT transgenic mice (PyMT-RBM38) (Fig. 1B). Comparative longitudinal analysis of oncogenic progression dynamics in PyMT versus RBM38-modified PyMT cohorts revealed distinct tumorigenic trajectories. The transgenic PyMT cohort reached 100% tumor incidence by day 96 (Fig. 1C). Conversely, the RBM38-modified PyMT cohort exhibited substantial tumorigenic latency, with first detectable lesions emerging post-day 96 and achieving only 50% incidence by day 120 (Fig. 1C). Metastatic profiling six weeks post-tumor detection identified oligometastatic pulmonary foci in RBM38-modified specimens, contrasting with extensive pulmonary colonization in wild-type counterparts (Fig. 1D). Importantly, PyMT mRNA levels were not changed in PyMT-RBM38 mice tumors relative to PyMT controls (Fig. 1E), and the branching morphogenesis during wild-type mice development was not affected by RBM38 overexpression in the mammary gland (Fig. 1F). To delineate the oncogenic regulatory mechanism of RBM38 overexpression in PyMT-driven CSC dynamics, we conducted a comparative histopathological analysis of mammary gland whole-mount specimens harvested during hyperplastic/preneoplastic phases from PyMT and RBM38-transgenic cohorts. As quantified in Fig. 1G, RBM38-overexpressing specimens at 15 weeks postnatal exhibited quantifiable attenuation in both incidence and dimensional parameters of dysplastic foci compared to PyMT controls. Given the established translational relevance of PyMT models to human luminal B breast malignancies [23, 24], we employed FACS to isolate Lin-epithelial subpopulations from preneoplastic mammary tissue. Flow cytometric profiling revealed that CD29loCD24+ luminal progenitor cells – previously established as TIC surrogates in this model [25, 26] – demonstrated reduced expansion in RBM38-modified specimens versus PyMT controls (Fig. 1H and I). Crucially, this suppression was tumor-context specific, whereas WT backgrounds showed no changes in the CD29loCD24+ population. Contrastingly, the CD29+CD24+ basal-enriched compartment [24] – containing multipotent mammary stem cells in select oncogenic contexts [25] – displayed inverse expansion patterns, with PyMT specimens exhibiting an reduction compared to RBM38-modified counterparts (Fig. 1H and I). Subsequent interrogation of the CD29loCD24+ALDH+ TIC subset (validated in advanced PyMT carcinogenesis [27]) through ALDEFLUOR assays confirmed significant depletion within Lin-MECs of RBM38-modified specimens (Fig. 1J and K). These results indicate that RBM38 overexpression could suppress tumor formation, metastasis, and the expansion of cell populations enriched for CSC markers.

RBM38 expression is associated with a differentiated status and negatively correlates with invasion and stemness

We then analyzed RBM38 expression through single-cell data GSE225600, which contains single-cell RNA and spatial transcriptome of 4 primary tumors and 4 paired metastatic lymph nodes in breast cancer. The quality control, normalization, and PCA were conducted on GSE225600 (Figure S1A-D). After dimension reduction, 14 cell clusters were obtained (Figure S1E). According to the canonical markers, the cell clusters were annotated as B cells, Endothelial cells, Epithelial cells, Fibroblast, Myeloid cells, NK cells, Plasma T cells, and Smooth muscle cells (Figures S1F and 2A). Also, the cell distribution of RBM38 was presented in Fig. 2A. The monocle revealed the cell differentiation trajectories of cells in breast cancer. After cell selection, the cell differentiation tracks of cell clusters were investigated after dimension reduction and cell state identification (Figure S1G, Fig. 2B). Epithelial cells were differentiated at the early-middle stage of the whole trajectory. As shown in Fig. 2C, RBM38 was rarely expressed at the early stage of differentiation (State 1), while it was remarkably increased in the later differentiation stage in immune-related cells (State 2), including T and B cells. Additionally, for the exploration of cell stemness of different cell clusters in breast cancer, the CytoTRACE analysis was performed. Figure 2D presented the distribution of cells with different differentiation orders in a t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) dimension reduction plot based on the CytoTRACE score of each cell. Besides, the predicted order of different types of cells was plotted in Fig. 2E, which suggested that T cells showed the lowest stemness, while the fibroblasts expressed the highest differentiation potential. This result suggested that RBM38 expression was positively correlated with cell differentiation and negatively with cell stemness. Replicating this molecular signature, qRT-PCR analysis revealed significant upregulation of mammary differentiation-associated transcripts in RBM38-overexpressing PyMT specimens compared to wild-type counterparts (Figure S2A). Consistently, comparative proteomic profiling and histopathological grading demonstrated that RBM38-modified tumors maintained a more differentiated architecture, with elevated E-cadherin, reduced Vimentin, and a depleted K14⁺ pro-tumorigenic subpopulation at invasive fronts (Figure S2B–D). To further investigate the functional role of RBM38, we established reciprocal gain- and loss-of-function cellular models. Notably, MDA-MB-231 cells, which exhibit higher stemness, expressed substantially lower endogenous RBM38 protein than MCF-7 cells, which exhibit lower stemness (Fig. 2F) [21]. Accordingly, we generated RBM38-overexpressing MDA-MB-231 cells and RBM38-knockdown MCF-7 cells, enabling us to assess RBM38 function in both stemness-enriched and stemness-low contexts. Functional studies demonstrated reciprocal modulation of core pluripotency factors (Oct3/4, Sox2) across breast cancer subtypes, with RBM38 overexpression suppressing and genetic ablation enhancing their expression in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 lineages respectively (Fig. 2F). Profiling of surface antigen expression patterns revealed inverse regulation of the breast CSC-enriched CD44+/CD24− immunophenotype [28] corresponding to RBM38 expression levels (Fig. 2G), while RNPC1b isoform showed negligible regulatory effects on these stemness parameters (Figure S2E). In vitro functional assays using non-adherent culture systems, a well-established CSC enrichment platform [28], showed RBM38-mediated functional attenuation of self-renewal capacity. Mammosphere formation assays revealed altered morphometric parameters of mammosphere formation contingent upon RBM38 expression status (Fig. 2H and I). To rule out the possibility that the observed phenotypes were due to alterations in cell cycle progression or proliferation rates, we examined cell cycle distribution and proliferation in RBM38-overexpressing MDA-MB-231 cells and RBM38-knockdown MCF-7 cells. As shown in Figure S2F and G, RBM38 modulation did not significantly affect cell cycle status or proliferative capacity, confirming that the observed effects on stemness markers and sphere formation reflect genuine regulation of stemness rather than secondary changes in cell growth dynamics. Complementary in vivo limiting dilution transplantation models confirmed RBM38-dependent regulation of tumor-initiating capacity across experimental groups (Figure S2H and I). Expanding to translational relevance, experimental metastasis models employing micro-CT imaging coupled with histomorphometric evaluation demonstrated RBM38-mediated suppression of metastatic competence - a phenotype correlated with improved survival outcomes in preclinical models (Figure S3A-D). Further interrogation of pharmacosensitivity profiles identified RBM38 as a molecular determinant of DOX responsiveness in breast cancer cells (Figure S3E and F). Collectively, these multimodal analyses establish that experimental modulation of RBM38 levels can influence differentiation markers and constrain stemness-associated malignant progression trajectories in breast oncogenesis models.

RBM38 suppresses breast cancer cell stemness through its RNP1 domain and likely mediated by protein complex formation

We investigated which submotifs of RBM38 were essential for its suppression on breast cancer stemness. The RRM in RBM38 consists of two putative RNA-binding submotifs, namely RNP1 and RNP2 [8]. Therefore, we generated HA-tagged RBM38 constructs lacking either RNP1 (△RNP1) or RNP2 (△RNP2) (Fig. 3A). △RNP2 still exerted similar inhibitory effects on breast cancer stemness as RBM38, as evidenced by the change of CD44+/CD24− sub-population ratio (Fig. 3B), the expression of core pluripotency transcription factors (Oct4/Nanog/Sox2 cluster) (Fig. 3C and D), and sphere-formation ability (Fig. 3E and F). In contrast, △RNP1 exerted only a weak influence. These observations suggest that RBM38 primarily suppresses breast cancer cell stemness through its RNP1 domain. Given that RBM38 possesses a single RRM [8] and only the RNP1 sub-domain within the RRM is essential for its effects on the stemness of breast cancer cells, we speculated that RBM38 might function through protein complex formation with other proteins. As expected, our results confirmed the formation of protein complexes involving RBM38 in breast cancer cells (Fig. 3G). Furthermore, overexpression of RBM38 or △RNP2 facilitated the formation of the protein complexes, while overexpression of △RNP1 did not (Fig. 3H). Therefore, our results provide evidence that the effects of RBM38 on breast cancer cell stemness depend on its RNP1 sub-domain, implying the involvement of protein complexes in mediating these effects.

RBM38 suppresses the stemness of breast cancer cells by enhancing HOXD10 mRNA stability dependent on its RNP1 domain

To explore the downstream mechanisms by which RBM38, through its RNP1 domain, suppresses breast cancer stemness, we performed RIP-sequencing and RNA-sequencing to explore the mechanisms underlying the roles of RBM38 in breast cancer stemness, and found that there were six intersecting transcripts commonly pulled down by anti-RBM38 and increased by RBM38 overexpression, including HSP90A, DSP, HOXD10, CELSR2, PCSK9, BCL2 (Fig. 4A). Previous studies have shown that HOXD10 3′UTR can attenuate the migration ability, stemness, and enhance DOX sensitivity in breast cancer cells [21, 29–35]; this effect is consistent with that of RBM38. Based on these findings, we formulated a mechanistic hypothesis positioning HOXD10 as the master regulatory node through which RBM38 exerts stemness-suppressive effects in mammary carcinogenesis. RNA-protein interaction assays, combined with RT-qPCR validation and luciferase-based cis-regulatory element mapping, confirmed specific RBM38-HOXD10 3′UTR ribonucleoprotein complex formation across breast cancer models (Figure S4A-D). Genetic complementation assays were performed via HOXD10 knockdown in RBM38-overexpressing MDA-MB-231 cells and ectopic HOXD10 expression in RBM38-depleted MCF-7 systems. HOXD10 overexpression reversed the dedifferentiation phenotype induced by RBM38 knockdown, as indicated by reduced expression of stemness-associated genes in MCF-7 cells (Fig. 4B). Conversely, HOXD10 ablation in RBM38-modified MDA-MB-231 clones rescued the differentiation-promoting phenotype. Functional assays further confirmed that RBM38 and HOXD10 regulated mammosphere formation in accordance with their molecular effects (Fig. 4C and D) and the CD44+/CD24− sub-population ratio (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, the effect of RBM38 on DOX sensitivity was partially reversed by ectopic expression of HOXD10 (Fig. 4F). Moreover, we constructed MDA-MB-231 cells with stable overexpression of RBM38, knockdown of HOXD10, or stable overexpression of RBM38 combined with HOXD10 knockdown. Additionally, we generated MCF-7 cells with or without knockdown of HOXD10. The limiting dilution analysis confirmed that HOXD10 knockdown promoted the tumor-initiating ability of breast cancer cells (Fig. 4G and H), and RBM38 overexpression-mediated decrease of tumor-initiating ability was reversed by HOXD10 knockdown (Fig. 4I and J). Notably, only △RNP2 was capable of binding to the HOXD10 transcript (Figure S4E). Consistently, △RNP1 failed to increase HOXD10 expression in MDA-MB-231 cells (Figure S4F and G). Importantly, REMSA experiments consistently demonstrated that full-length RBM38 and △RNP2 (but not △RNP1) bound specifically to the 3’UTR of HOXD10 (Fig. 4K). RBM38 competitively binds to HOXD10 3′UTR with miR-10b and thus suppresses the stemness of breast cancer cells

Notably, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis based on RNA-seq data from MDA-MB-231 cells with or without RBM38 overexpression (GSE253253) indicated that the microRNAs in the cancer pathway were significantly enriched in cells with RBM38 overexpression (Fig. 5A). Moreover, RBPs often competitively bind to mRNA 3′UTRs with miRNAs [36, 37], which indicates that miRNAs might be engaged in RBM38-mediated effects on breast cancer stemness. Based on considerable evidence provided by our studies and others, it has been confirmed that HOXD10 is a direct target of miR-10b, and miR-10b level is positively correlated with breast cancer progression [29, 38–44]. Thus, we assumed that RBM38 might competitively bind to HOXD10 3′UTR with miR-10b. To test this hypothesis, miR-10b was knocked down in MCF-7 cells with RBM38 knockdown and overexpressed in MDA-MB-231 cells with RBM38 overexpression. As expected, the effects of RBM38 knockdown on HOXD10 expression were rescued by miR-10b inhibition, and the increase in HOXD10 expression caused by RBM38 overexpression was partially reversed by miR-10b overexpression (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, the decreased HOXD10 mRNA stability resulting from RBM38 knockdown was rescued by miR-10b inhibition (Fig. 5C). Additionally, RIP analysis demonstrated miR-10b-mediated displacement of HOXD10 mRNA from RBM38-ribonucleoprotein complexes in triple-negative breast cancer models (Fig. 5D). Crucially, bidirectional independence was established between miR-10b and RBM38 expression tiers through reciprocal genetic perturbation studies (Figs. 5E-H). Contrastingly, Ago2-centric ribonomic profiling validated miR-10b’s direct engagement with HOXD10 3’UTR cis-elements, with RBM38 deficiency potentiating this miRNA-mRNA interactome (Fig. 5I). Consistently, HOXD10 mRNA was enriched in RNA pulled down by anti-biotin in MCF-7 cells with biotin-miR-10b transfection, and this enrichment was promoted by RBM38 knockdown (Fig. 5J). Then, we wondered whether RBM38 and miR-10b competitively bind to the same site in HOXD10 3′UTR. The luciferase reporter assay showed that RBM38 overexpression had a more substantial effect on the activity of the vector containing HOXD10 3′UTR than that of HOXD10 3′UTR-Mut, HOXD10 3′UTR-1 and HOXD10 3′UTR-2 (Fig. 5K and L). Co-IP experiments using biotin-labeled sequences of HOXD10 3′UTR also yielded a consistent result, demonstrating that RBM38 displayed a more robust interaction with HOXD10 3′UTR than with HOXD10 3′UTR-Mut, HOXD10 3′UTR-1 and HOXD10 3′UTR-2 (Fig. 5M). Mechanistic mapping revealed spatial adjacency between miR-10b’s canonical seed region and RBM38’s RNA-binding motif on HOXD10 3’UTR, with potential auxiliary binding loci suggesting additional cis-regulatory RNA elements that may modulate this interaction. To dissect the miR-10b dependency of RBM38-mediated stemness regulation, genetic complementation experiments demonstrated phenotypic reversion of RBM38-knockdown-induced pluripotency through pharmacological miR-10b suppression in MCF-7 cells, while miR-10b overexpression reversed RBM38-mediated stemness suppression in MDA-MB-231 cells (Figure S5A). Conversely, RBM38-overexpression-driven differentiation was counteracted by functional antagonism via miR-10b reconstitution. Functional validation in 3D mammary spheres formation systems confirmed bidirectional modulation of self-renewal capacity - miR-10b inhibition attenuated RBM38-deficiency-induced hyperproliferation, while miR-10b overexpression circumvented RBM38-mediated suppression of oncosphere formation (Figure S5B and C). Consistent results were obtained from the analysis of the CD44+/CD24- sub-population ratio (Figure S5D). Moreover, ectopic expression of miR-10b also rescued the effect of RBM38 on DOX sensitivity (Figure S5E and F). To further validate the miR-10b-dependent regulation of stemness by RBM38, we generated MDA-MB-231 lineages with constitutive RBM38/miR-10b co-expression systems alongside miR-10b transgenic MCF-7 derivatives. Functional interrogation via orthotopic transplantation models demonstrated miR-10b-mediated potentiation of neoplastic initiation capacity - a stemness-associated trait that was counterregulated through RBM38-mediated transcriptional constraints, with miR-10b co-expression restoring baseline tumorigenic competence in RBM38-modified systems (Figure S5G-H). Furthermore, miR-10b inhibition significantly reduced the CD44+/CD24- sub-population ratio, and miR-10b overexpression decreased the sensitivity of DOX (Figure S6A and B). Moreover, miR-10b expression showed a negative correlation with the survival of metastatic model mice (Figure S6C), and the inhibition of RBM38 knockdown on the survival of metastatic model mice was rescued by suppressing miR-10b (Figure S6D). Our data suggest that RBM38 post-transcriptionally constrains breast oncogenic stemness programs through competitive miRNA-mRNA interactome dynamics, establishing a post-transcriptional regulatory axis mediated by miR-10b sequestration.

RBM38-mediated effects depend on its phosphorylation regulated by protein kinase SLK and PGAM5

Having established that RBM38 suppresses stemness through RNP1-mediated binding to HOXD10 3′UTR and competitive displacement of miR-10b, we next sought to elucidate the upstream regulatory mechanisms governing RBM38 itself. To this end, we performed Co-IP combined with interactome proteomics to identify proteins that associate with RBM38 and modulate its function. Considering that post-translational modification (PTM) events emerge as evolutionarily conserved molecular rheostats that dynamically orchestrate proteome homeostasis through stabilization-destabilization equilibria, we focused on proteins capable of mediating PTMs. Among them, PGAM5, protein kinase SLK, and M2-type pyruvate kinase (PKM2) drew our attention as they directly regulated protein phosphorylation and displayed a relatively high interaction score (Fig. 6A). Consistently, the annotation of functions, including the analysis of the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Gene Ontology (GO), Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) of proteins, and Information of Protein Regions (IPR), revealed a total of 138 proteins which were significantly enriched in Translation-related functions (Figs. 6B-E). We subsequently confirmed the interactions of SLK-RBM38 and PGAM5-RBM38 through co-IP analysis in MCF-7 breast cancer cells, while the interaction between PKM2 and RBM38 was weaker (Fig. 6F). Therefore, SLK and PGAM5 were selected as the main subjects for further investigation. We observed that SLK knockdown significantly increased RBM38 protein level, whereas inhibition of PGAM5 had the opposite effect (Fig. 6G). Due to the unavailability of antibodies specifically targeting RBM38 phosphorylation, we assessed RBM38 phosphorylation in cells with SLK knockdown or PGAM5 inhibitor treatment using Phos-tag-SDS-PAGE. As shown in Fig. 6H, SLK knockdown markedly reduced the phosphorylation level of RBM38, whereas PGAM5 inhibition increased RBM38 phosphorylation. Consistently, modulation of SLK and PGAM5 activity altered RBM38-mediated downstream regulation, as evidenced by changes in RBM38 protein complex formation, HOXD10 mRNA stability, and HOXD10 expression (Figure S6E–H). Moreover, SLK knockdown reduced HOXD10 association with miR-10b, whereas PGAM5 inhibition produced an opposite effect (Figure S6I). Additionally, SLK knockdown enhanced RBM38 protein stability, while PGAM5 inhibition decreased it (Fig. 6I), suggesting that increased RBM38 phosphorylation is associated with reduced protein stability. We further performed PGAM5 knockdown experiments in MCF-7 cells and verified the knockdown efficiency (Figure S6J). CHX chase assays under PGAM5 knockdown conditions showed decreased RBM38 stability, opposite to the effect observed following SLK knockdown, consistent with our observations using the PGAM5 pharmacological inhibitor (Figure S6K). To further determine RBM38 phosphorylation sites, RBM38 proteins were immunoprecipitated from MCF-7 cells with RBM38 overexpression treated with PGAM5 inhibitor or subjected to SLK knockdown, and subjected to mass spectrometry, which identified three phosphorylation sites, including Ser117, Thr120, and Thr132 (Fig. 6J). MS analysis revealed that SLK knockdown significantly reduced the phosphorylation enrichment abundance of all three sites, whereas PGAM5 inhibition enhanced it (Fig. 6K). Furthermore, the regulation of breast cancer stemness by RBM38 indeed depended on SLK and PGAM5, as demonstrated by alterations in the CD44+/CD24- sub-population ratio and sphere-formation ability (Fig. 6 L-N, Figure S6L). Moreover, with the progression of breast cancer in MMTV-PyMT transgenic mice, SLK expression was gradually increased, while PGAM5 expression was decreased (Fig. 6O, Figure S6M). Collectively, these findings establish that SLK and PGAM5 act as upstream regulators of RBM38, controlling its protein stability through opposing phosphorylation and dephosphorylation events, and thereby modulating the RNP1-dependent downstream suppression of breast cancer stemness.

ASCP-RBM38 recombinant protein suppresses the stemness of breast cancer cells

To further demonstrate the crucial role of RBM38 in regulating breast cancer stemness, we utilized a protein delivery platform (activatable supercharged polypeptide, ASCP), which was established by us, to achieve intracellular delivery of RBM38 (Fig. 7A) [15]. First, the recombinant ASCP-RBM38 protein was expressed and purified by E. coli, and its properties were identified (Figure S7). In addition, ASCP-RBM38 can efficiently enter various cell lines and enable efficient endosomal escape (Figure S8, Figure S9A and B). Flow cytometry results showed that ASCP-RBM38 could respond to a specific tumor microenvironment and tolerate a particular concentration of serum (Figure S9C and D). To delineate the tumor-targeting pharmacokinetics of ASCP-RBM38, longitudinal in vivo tracking was performed in MCF-7 xenograft-bearing murine models via time-lapse near-infrared (NIR) imaging. The ASCP-RBM38 (Cy5-labeled) conjugated complex demonstrated progressive neoplastic accumulation commencing at 4 h post-intravenous infusion, achieving peak tumor-to-background ratios by 8 h within a 48 h observation window, with sustained tumor retention persisting beyond 24 h post-administration (Figure S9E). Ex vivo NIR imaging further indicated that ASCP-RBM38 could localize to the tumor site and enter the cytoplasm (Figure S9F and G). Hemolysis experiments also showed that ASCP-RBM38 could achieve safe, targeted delivery (Figure S9H).

Comprehensive interrogation of ASCP-RBM38’s pharmacodynamic impact on BCSC plasticity revealed dose-responsive attenuation of multiple oncogenic signatures associated with stemness through multiparametric analysis. This modulation manifested through: (1) Transcriptional repression of core pluripotency transcription factors (Oct4/Nanog cluster) as per MIAME-compliant quantification standards; (2) Flow cytometric quantitation showing progressive depletion of the BCSC-enriched CD44+/CD24− signature across treatment cohorts; (3) Functional impairment of 3D self-renewal capacity evidenced by mammosphere formation assays with reduced clonogenic potential (Figure S10A-D). Among the stemness markers examined, the reduction in Sox2 protein was less pronounced and did not display a strict linear dose-response relationship (Figure S10A). Nevertheless, the dose-responsive suppression of Oct4 and Nanog, together with the functional impairment of sphere formation and the dose-dependent depletion of the CD44⁺/CD24⁻ population (Figure S10B–D), collectively supports the effective anti-stemness activity of ASCP-RBM38. Furthermore, ASCP-RBM38 exerted a significant suppressive effect on tumor progression derived from breast cancer MCF-7 cells. The inhibitory rate of ASCP-RBM38 was comparable to that of DOX, and it enhanced the tumor-suppressive effects of DOX (Figs. 7B-D). Additionally, ASCP-RBM38 treatment inhibited the metastatic ability of breast cancer cells (Fig. 7E). Importantly, ASCP-RBM38 treatment did not cause obvious toxicity to the organs of mice with the exhibition of some degree of protective effects against DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, as evidenced by the reduction in cardiomyocyte apoptosis (Fig. 7F and Figure S11A). To further confirm the clinical significance of ASCP-RBM38, we established a PDX model and administered a 14-day treatment regimen. Consistently, ASCP-RBM38 exerted a significant suppressive effect on tumor progression of PDX models along with enhancing the efficacy of DOX, as evidenced by the decrease of tumor volume and weight (Figs. 7G-J). Complementary to these findings, IHC quantification demonstrated significant attenuation in the expression of the stemness triad SOX2/OCT4/NANOG following ASCP-RBM38 therapeutic intervention, corroborating the pharmacodynamic effects observed in vitro (Figure S11B and C). Taken together, these results indicated that ASCP-RBM38 recombinant protein suppresses the stemness of breast cancer cells.

The clinical significance of RBM38, HOXD10, and miR-10b in patients with breast cancer

Finally, we evaluated the expression of RBM38, HOXD10, and miR-10b in tissue microarray chips (TMA). As shown in Table 1, the high expression of RBM38 showed no significant association with age ≥ 50 (p = 0.557) or advanced TNM stage (p = 0.778). However, the high RBM38 group was enriched with TNBC subtypes compared to the -/low and medium groups (42% vs. 27%, 37%; rs = 0.269, p = 0.013). Furthermore, we found a positive correlation between the expression of RBM38 and HOXD10 (rs = 0.296, p < 0.001), but not with miR-10b (rs = 0.103, p = 0.109). We then investigated the effects of RBM38 HOXD10 expression on patients’ prognosis. Both high expression of RBM38 and HOXD10 tended to be associated with better overall survival of breast cancer patients, although not statistically significant (p = 0.3, p = 0.076, Fig. 8A and B). Conversely, high expression of miR-10 showed a tendency to negatively affect patients’ survival (p = 0.086, Fig. 8C). And these results were verified by analyzing all online datasets for HOXD10 and RBM38, GSE40627 for miR-10b, respectively, in Kaplan – Meier (KM) Plotter analysis (http://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php?p=background) [45], in which patients with high expression of RBM38 and HOXD10 and low expression of miR-10b tended to have a better prognosis (Figs. 8D-F). We further used multivariate survival analysis to demonstrate whether RBM38/miR-10b/HOXD10 was independently associated with breast cancer patients’ survival. It was shown that high RBM38 expression was associated with lower death risk (HR:0.298 (95%CI: 0.115–0.774), p = 0.0129) (Fig. 8G). Conversely, high miR-10b level indicated increased death risk (HR:2.103 (95% CI: 1.2222–3.169), p = 0.0073) (Fig. 8G). Finally, we observed a negative correlation between the expression of HOXD10 and miR-10b (rs = -0.176, p = 0.012, Fig. 8H). Additionally, the expression of HOXD10 showed a positive association with RBM38 (Fig. 8I). Collectively, these results demonstrate the relationship between RBM38 and clinico-pathological parameters, the correlation of RBM38, HOXD10, and miR-10b with each other, and their potential effects on the prognosis of breast cancer patients.

Discussion

Mammary carcinogenesis is a multifaceted disease fundamentally driven by tumorigenic stem-like subpopulations that orchestrate critical oncogenic processes, including neoplastic initiation, metastatic dissemination, and therapeutic resistance. In this context, our study identifies RBM38 as a factor whose expression is associated with and can functionally impact these critical processes. Based on the PyMT-induced mammary tumors and multi-omics analysis, this is the first study demonstrating that knock-in of RBM38 significantly suppresses the breast CSC population, tumor initiation, size, and reduces the metastatic burden. These findings indicate that RBM38 suppresses tumor-initiating cells through post-transcriptional regulation of stemness-related pathways, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.

The present study suggests that RBM38 can suppress the stemness of breast cancer cells, but RNPC1b has no effect on the stemness. Additionally, only the RNP1 sub-domain is necessary for its mRNA binding and inhibition of the stemness of breast cancer cells. Here, we also found that the AU/U-rich elements (AREs) exist in the 3’UTR of HOXD10 mRNA, and after the essential AREs were mutated, the luciferase activity and the amount of RBM38 binding with HOXD10 3’UTR were reduced. This result is in accordance with the previous study indicating that RBM38 could bind to AREs in its target mRNA 3’UTR and increase mRNA stability [46]. Mechanistically, RBM38 enhanced HOXD10 mRNA stability and competed with miR-10b for binding to the HOXD10 3′UTR. This competitive interaction was required for RBM38-mediated suppression of breast cancer stemness. And although the target of miR-10b on HOXD10 has been revealed in several studies [40, 43, 47, 48], there are also other miRNAs, like miR-23a, being indicated to promote tumor metastasis by targeting HOXD10 [49]. In addition, RBM38 could suppress let-7b from binding to and degrading BIRC5 (Survivin) mRNA [50]. These results suggest that other miRNAs may also engage in RBM38-mediated regulating of HOXD10 mRNA stability and breast cancer stemness.

We also observed in TMA that the expression of both RBM38 and miR-10b was correlated with that of HOXD10, further supporting the idea that RBM38 and miR-10b competitively bind to the HOXD10 transcript. Specifically, through the multivariate survival analysis, we verified in our cohort that patients with high RBM38 expression level independently have a superior survival prospect. Although in univariate survival analysis, RBM38, HOXD10, and miR-10b only tended to stratify patients’ prognosis, this might be due to the confounding effects brought by other clinical parameters and the relatively short follow-up period in this study. With the standardization of breast cancer management, patients’ survival has considerably improved, with a median OS of 8.7 years reported in 2018 [51].

The western blot analysis always reflects the denatured protein, which means it couldn’t reflect the natural forms of protein when it regulates the signal pathway. Therefore, we performed native-page electrophoresis to analyze the real functional style of RBM38. Interestingly, we found that RBM38 could form protein complexes with other proteins rather than existing as a monomer, and this complex formation depended on the RNA-binding motifs, suggesting that RBM38 may exist and function as a protein complex in breast cancer cells rather than acting as a monomer. Specifically, we identified that PGAM5 and SLK could interact with RBM38 and regulate the phosphorylation of RBM38. Furthermore, it was demonstrated that RBM38-mediated effects on breast cancer stemness depend on its phosphorylation regulated by SLK and PGAM5. Notably, PPM1D phosphatase, a target of p53, has been shown to directly interact with and dephosphorylate RBM38 at serine 195 [12]. Glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3) can interact with RBM38 and phosphorylate it at Ser195 [13]. These results suggest that PPM1D and GSK3 are also two critical regulators for RBM38, and Ser195 is the important phosphorylation site for RBM38. However, these two proteins were not identified in our interacting proteomics results, this may be due to the adoption of different experimental techniques with different detection limits. Therefore, future studies are also needed to focus on the detailed phosphorylation sites of RBM38 protein through proteomics. Notably, recent work indicates that RBM38 acts as an oncogene in gastric cancer by stabilizing aurora kinase B mRNA [52], this means that RBM38 might function in different roles in different tumors. Notably, recombinant RBM38 protein had not been previously used to verify its function. Here, we constructed ASCP-RBM38 and employed our established protein delivery system to confirm the tumor-suppressive role of RBM38 in breast cancer stemness through PDX models. Moreover, we assessed the clinical relevance of the RBM38/HOXD10/miR-10b interaction by analyzing clinical samples. The findings from these analyses provided valuable evidence linking the RBM38-mediated inhibition of breast cancer stemness to clinical outcomes, further supporting the clinical relevance of our findings.

Although our current study has established a complete regulatory pathway from SLK/PGAM5-mediated phosphorylation to stemness suppression through the HOXD10/miR-10b axis (Figs. 4, 5 and 6), we acknowledge that the precise contribution of each individual phosphorylation site—Ser117, Thr120, and Thr132—remains to be further dissected. The site-directed mutagenesis of these phosphorylation sites, combined with advanced structural biology techniques such as X-ray crystallography or cryo-electron microscopy, could provide deeper insights into how phosphorylation at specific residues influences RBM38‘s conformation, RNA-binding activity, and protein complex formation. Regarding the relationship between RBM38 phosphorylation and its RNP1 domain, as well as their collective effect on protein complex formation, we acknowledge the importance of further investigation in this area. Our findings suggest that RBM38 phosphorylation, particularly at sites regulated by SLK and PGAM5, may alter the conformation of the RNP1 domain. This conformational change could potentially influence the domain’s ability to bind RNA and interact with other proteins involved in breast cancer stemness regulation. However, the exact role of phosphorylation in modulating these interactions remains to be fully elucidated. It is possible that phosphorylation could either enhance or inhibit the RNP1 domain’s RNA-binding capability, thus affecting the stability and formation of protein complexes. This notion highlights the intricate link between RBM38 phosphorylation and the functional activity of its RNP1 domain. Future studies employing site-directed mutagenesis, advanced structural biology techniques (such as X-ray crystallography or cryo-electron microscopy), and comprehensive proteomic analyses could provide deeper insights into how phosphorylation influences RBM38’s interaction with its binding partners and its regulatory role in stemness-related processes. Although this connection was not fully explored in the present study, we view it as an important direction for future research. Understanding how phosphorylation of RBM38 integrates with its RNA-binding activity and protein complex formation could reveal critical details of how post-translational modifications regulate RBM38’s functional roles in cancer progression.

In summary, our study highlights the crucial role of RBM38 in suppressing breast cancer stemness. We demonstrated that RBM38 acts as a competitive inhibitor of miR-10b through its binding to the 3′UTR of HOXD10 mRNA, suppressing breast cancer stemness. Furthermore, we elucidated the involvement of the RBM38 RNP1 domain and the phosphorylation status of RBM38 in mediating these effects. These findings contribute to a better understanding of the regulatory mechanisms underlying breast cancer stemness and suggest RBM38 as a potential therapeutic target. It is encouraging to note that strategies aimed at increasing the expression of RBM38, through either endogenous or exogenous means, may hold potential for future therapy of inhibiting breast cancer stemness.
