Size of Biomolecular Condensates Dictates Fate in Liquid–Solid Phase Transitions through Amorphous–Amyloid Competition
Authors: Junka Kawakami, Taiki Ozawa, Yoko Maruyama, Honoka Ishikawa, Yuto Oshita, Kota Yamauchi, Koichi Kobayashi, Shinji Kajimoto, Takakazu Nakabayashi, Shunsuke Tomita, Tanushree Agarwal, Tomas Sneideris, Kichitaro Nakajima, Kentaro Shiraki, Hideki Taguchi, Akihide Hibara, Tuomas Knowles, Eri Chatani, Yuta Mizuno, Yumiko Ohhashi, Mao Fukuyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proteins function not only through intramolecular folding and intermolecular complex formation but also through phase transitions driven by intermolecular interactions. Such phases, including liquid-like condensates, amorphous aggregates (AAs), and amyloid fibrils, are linked to distinct biological functions and pathologies. Although the transition from liquid-like condensates to amyloids has been extensively studied, the kinetic relationships between amyloids and other metastable solid states under cell-sized confinement remain unclear, which may hinder the establishment of effective therapeutic strategies. This knowledge gap arises because bulk-scale experiments inevitably lead to the conversion of metastable phases into the most stable phase. We developed a droplet-based microfluidic system that quantifies amyloid nucleation and metastable AA formation. Using the yeast prion protein Sup35, we found that condensates convert into both amyloids and AAs and that AA formation imposes a kinetic barrier that suppresses amyloid formation in a size-dependent manner at the micrometer scale, highlighting the importance of size effects in condensate-to-amyloid transitions. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the well-known amyloid inhibitor (−)-epigallocatechin-3-gallate paradoxically promoted amyloid formation at low concentrations by modulating the AA and amyloid nucleation kinetics. This phenomenon cannot, in principle, be observed in bulk-scale experiments and became apparent only under micrometer-scale confinement in the present system. These findings provide fundamental insights into protein phase transitions in cellular environments and may guide the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting the metastable aggregates of amyloidogenic proteins.

Introduction

Proteins perform diverse functions in living systems through microscopic states and assemblies, such as folding into native structures and formation of protein complexes. Over the past few decades, it has also become increasingly clear that proteins undergo phase transitions into mesoscopic and macroscopic assemblies, including liquid-like condensates, amorphous aggregates, crystals, and amyloid fibrils (Figure A), and that these states are associated with both biological functions and disorders. , For example, the reversible phase transition into liquid-like condensates plays important roles in physiological processes such as genome organization, transcription, and immune responses. , In contrast, irreversible phase transitions into solid-like states, such as AAs, crystals, and amyloid fibrils, not only contribute to normal physiological functions, including intracellular insulin storage and long-term memory formation, but are also associated with a number of diseases called misfolding diseases. , In particular, amyloids, which are thermodynamically stable one-dimensional ordered aggregates formed through cross-β structures, are observed in various diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and dialysis-related amyloidosis. The mechanisms of amyloid formation have been extensively studied, as they are expected to contribute to understanding pathology, advancing drug discovery, and developing diagnostic techniques. ,

(B) In bulk solution, although the kinetically favorable metastable phase predominantly forms at the initial stage, once amyloid fibrils, the thermodynamically stable phase, appears, the metastable phase completely transforms into the stable phase. (C) When condensates are compartmentalized in water-in-oil droplets, protein molecules cannot be transported between droplets. As a result, the metastable phase and amyloids coexist in a steady state.

In recent years, biomolecular condensates have been increasingly recognized as complex reaction environments for amyloid formation rather than simple nucleation accelerators. In early studies, condensates were primarily regarded as environments that enhance amyloid nucleation due to their extremely high protein concentrations. From this perspective, amyloid formation from condensates has been investigated for many amyloidogenic proteins, including α-synuclein, − tau, , fused in sarcoma (FUS), transactive response DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43), heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins A1 and A2 (hnRNPA1/A2), , and Sup35. − More recently, this perspective has been revisited, and condensates are now understood to play a more nuanced and physicochemically complex role in amyloid formation. For instance, it was reported that the interior of condensates can suppress the nucleation of amyloid rather than promote it , while simultaneously providing interfaces that facilitate heterogeneous nucleation of amyloid fibrils. ,

To deepen the understanding relationship between amyloid formation from condensates from a physicochemical perspective, one important aspect is the kinetics of the metastable phases, namely the polymorphism of protein assemblies, in cell-sized confinement. In millimeter-scale solutions (∼10–6 L), metastable states such as condensates and amorphous aggregates initially form according to Ostwald’s step rule, as they are kinetically favored. Once the thermodynamically stable phase appears, the metastable phase dissolves because its solubility is higher than that of the stable phase, leading to complete conversion into the stable phase (Figure B). In contrast, a cell can be regarded as an isolated micrometer-sized solution with a volume on the order of 10–12 L. For nucleation-dependent phenomena such as amyloid formation, , it is well-known that such small, confined systems exhibit rare and stochastic behaviors. Consequently, the kinetics of condensate, amorphous aggregate, and amyloid formation are expected to be size-dependent, and it remains unclear whether insights obtained from millimeter-scale systems can be directly extrapolated to cellular size scales.

To address this challenge, we developed a droplet microfluidic approach to analyze the phase transition from condensates to amyloids and a metastable phase. Droplet microfluidics enables the miniaturization and parallelization of chemical and biochemical experiments by generating uniform picoliter-scale water-in-oil droplets. , This technique has been applied to single-cell analysis, − single-molecule detection, , and, more recently, the study of biomolecular phase separation. ,, Here, we considered that the kinetics of the metastable phase converted from condensates could be quantitatively investigated using droplet microfluidics. In droplet microfluidics, protein molecules cannot be transported between droplets, which is similar to cell environment, because they do not partition into the oil phase separating them. As a result, interdroplet equilibration of protein concentrations does not occur, allowing the metastable phase to persist even when the stable phase appears in neighboring droplets (Figure C). In addition, droplet size can be precisely controlled via channel design and flow control, which in turn enables control over condensate size, a key parameter in nucleation kinetics. Although a single condensate is formed in each droplet, unlike in cells where multiple condensates can coexist within the same intracellular space, this system shares an important feature with cellular environments in that the number of molecules contained in each compartment is limited. Furthermore, the observation of phase transitions in numerous condensates enables the quantification of stochastic amyloid nucleation events, which facilitates the investigation of phase transition kinetics for amyloid and metastable phase formation.

In this study, we conducted kinetic analysis of the phase transition from condensates to amyloids, the thermodynamically stable phase, and AAs, a metastable phase, using droplet microfluidics. As a model amyloidogenic protein, we used the NM domain of the yeast prion protein Sup35 (Sup35NM). Sup35NM is a well-established model for amyloid formation and prion-related protein assembly − and is therefore highly relevant to a comprehensive understanding of protein phase transitions, including not only liquid–liquid but also liquid–solid phase transitions. Sup35 consists of an intrinsically disordered NM domain and a C-terminal domain with translation termination activity. The NM domain is widely used to study amyloid formation ,, and essential for condensate formation. We found that amyloids and AA were formed in a competitive manner, resulting in the suppression of amyloid formation with a reduction in condensate size. In addition, we found that the addition of (−)-epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), a well-known amyloid formation inhibitor, unexpectedly promoted amyloid formation under conditions of low EGCG concentration and small condensate size by altering the kinetic balance between amyloid and AA formation.

Results

Formation of Micrometer-Sized Sup35NM Condensates in Droplets

We used the NM domain of the yeast prion protein Sup35 (Sup35NM) as a model amyloidogenic protein. By mixing Sup35NM with 5 wt % polyethylene glycol (PEG, 20 kDa) at pH 7.4, liquid-like condensates of Sup35NM were formed, as previously reported. PEG is one of the most widely used macromolecular crowders for inducing condensate formation in vitro, mainly through excluded-volume and depletion effects, and has also been used in previous studies of Sup35NM condensates. ,, We confirmed the reversibility of condensate formation with respect to temperature using a mixture of unmodified Sup35NM and Alexa Fluor 488-labeled Sup35NM (AF488-Sup35NM), which was prepared from a C-terminal cysteine-engineered Sup35NM mutant for site-specific labeling. As shown in Figure A, the condensates reversibly appeared and disappeared upon temperature increase and decrease. A phase regime of PEG concentration and temperature was obtained by turbidity measurements (Figure B). Increased turbidity at higher PEG concentrations and lower temperatures indicated condensate formation under these conditions, showing a propensity similar to that of other protein condensates. , In addition, fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) measurements showed no difference in fluorescence recovery times between condensates with and without prior heating at 70 °C for 10 min (Figure C). These results demonstrated that short-term heating did not change the fluidity of the condensates.

Next, we prepared uniform Sup35NM condensates within droplets by exploiting the temperature dependence of condensate formation (Figure D). An aqueous solution containing 40 μM Sup35NM, 10 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), 20 μM thioflavin T (ThT), a fluorescent amyloid indicator, and 5% PEG (20 kDa) was used as the dispersed phase, and fluorinated oil (HFE-7500) with 1% fluorous surfactant was used as the continuous phase. Droplets were generated in a flow-focusing device at 70 °C and subsequently cooled to 30 °C. Ten minutes after droplet formation, multiple condensates appeared within individual droplets, which coalesced into a single condensate per droplet after 1 h (Figure E). Such behavior is commonly observed for condensates confined within micrometer-scale compartments, such as liposomes or microwells. ,, It should be noted that the depletion effect of PEG in the microdroplets is expected to be essentially the same as that in bulk solution, because the characteristic length scale of the depletion region, which is on the order of the radius of gyration of PEG 20 kDa (<10 nm), is much smaller than the droplet radius (15–30 μm). In addition, we demonstrated that Sup35NM condensate size could be controlled by altering the droplet size. As shown in Figure F, larger condensates were formed in larger droplets, even when the same aqueous solution was used, as reported previously. Because the same aqueous composition was used for droplets of different sizes, the observed change in condensate size is attributed to the difference in the total amount of Sup35NM in each droplet, rather than to differences in phase-separation equilibrium caused by droplet size. Indeed, the Sup35NM concentration in the dilute phase was independent of both droplet size and condensate size, indicating that the phase-separation equilibrium was the same across different droplet sizes (Supplementary Figure 1).

Observation of Amyloid Formation from Sup35NM Condensates in Droplets

Amyloid formation from Sup35NM condensates within droplets was observed by monitoring ThT fluorescence. Figure A shows the temporal change in the condensates with a radius (R c) of 7.5 μm. Sup35NM condensates exhibited weak ThT fluorescence even in the absence of amyloids, as previously reported. The number of condensates emitting strong ThT fluorescence gradually increased over time. Condensates with strong ThT fluorescence indicated that at least one amyloid nucleation event occurred within them, followed by amyloid elongation. Because amyloid elongation is much faster than nucleation, the rate of increase in the number of condensates with strong fluorescence reflects the rate of amyloid nucleation. Eventually, amyloid formation occurred in all condensates with R c = 7.5 μm.

On the other hand, under the condition of R c = 3.7 μm, some condensates showed an increase in ThT fluorescence, while others did not even 20 h after droplet formation (Figure B). To distinguish condensates with amyloid nucleation from those without, we defined a threshold fluorescence intensity, F thres = F ave + 5F σ, where F ave and F σ represent the average and standard deviations of the fluorescence intensities of all condensates at t = 1 h, respectively (Figure C and D). As a result, the probability of amyloid (P) reached a plateau (P max) after approximately 5 h for all condensate sizes, and the value of P max was found to depend on R c (Figure E). The results with different thresholds are shown in Supplementary Figure 2.

Next, we examined the physicochemical properties of ThT-positive condensates (amyloids) and ThT-negative condensates. In the confocal fluorescence images, clear fibrillar structures were observed in ThT-positive condensates, whereas such fibrillar structures were not observed in ThT-negative condensates (Figure B and Supplementary Figure 3A). FRAP measurements were performed at 1.0–2.5 h after microdroplet formation and after overnight incubation (Figure F). As expected, ThT-positive condensates exhibited no fluorescence recovery at any time point, indicating a solid-like state. In contrast, ThT-negative condensates showed partial fluorescence recovery at early time points (1.0–2.5 h), although the recovered intensity was lower than that observed in bulk experiments, and no recovery after overnight incubation. The loss of fluorescence recovery after overnight incubation indicates that ThT-negative condensates gradually undergo solidification. It should be noted that the reduced fluorescence recovery observed at 1.0–2.5 h compared to bulk experiments is attributable to the elapsed time after condensate formation. In bulk experiments, FRAP measurements were performed immediately after condensate formation, whereas in the microdroplet experiments, the earliest feasible measurement was 1.0–2.5 h after droplet generation due to experimental constraints.

To compare the structural features of solidified ThT-positive and ThT-negative condensates, Raman spectroscopy was performed (Figure G). ThT-positive condensates (amyloids) exhibited a sharp amide I peak at 1665 cm–1, reflecting the presence of ordered cross-β structures and β-sheets. In contrast, solidified ThT-negative condensates showed a broad amide I peak at 1660 cm–1, suggesting a higher content of random coil structures. Comparison with the amide I peak of condensates indicated that the structure of solidified ThT-negative condensates more closely resembled that of condensates than that of amyloid fibrils (Figure G and Supplementary Figure 4). In addition, multiplex staining experiments were performed under the same conditions described above, using ThT with 8-anilinonaphthalene-1-sulfonic acid (ANS) and ThT with PROTEOSTAT, to detect the time evolution of solidification in ThT-negative condensates. However, no increase in fluorescence intensity was observed with ANS and PROTEOSTAT in ThT-negative condensates (Supplementary Figures 3B–D). We consider that the protein conformation in AAs is similar to that in condensates, and therefore these tracers do not respond detectably to the transition from condensates to AAs, which is consistent with the Raman spectroscopy results shown in Figure H. Moreover, this result indicates that the solidified ThT-negative condensates do not represent amyloid fibril polymorphs with only a weak ThT response. Based on this observation, we hereafter refer to the solidified ThT-negative condensates as amorphous aggregates (AAs). In the following experiments, ThT-negative condensates after overnight incubation were treated as AAs.

To examine the relationship between amyloids and AAs, we disrupted the droplets and placed both species in the same aqueous solution. Long-term observation revealed that the number of AAs decreased, whereas that of amyloids increased over time (Figure H). This result suggests that AAs are a metastable phase, whereas amyloids are the most stable phase. This interpretation is also supported by the Raman results, which suggest that amyloid formation is kinetically unfavorable because it requires conformational rearrangements into cross-β/β-sheet structures, whereas AAs are favorable as they require less structural changes.

Based on these results, the size dependence of amyloid formation from condensates can be explained as illustrated in Figure A. As a premise, amyloid and AA formation are considered to compete during condensate maturation. In general, amyloid formation is a nucleation-dependent process with a higher energy barrier, whereas AA formation is a growth-rate-limited process with a lower energy barrier. Because amyloid is the most stable phase, once nucleation occurs within a condensate, the amyloid becomes dominant and AAs disappear. In contrast, if a condensate is entirely converted into AAs before any amyloid nucleation occurs, Sup35NM loses its fluidity, and amyloids do not form. Under this premise, the expected time (τ) for the first nucleation event is shorter in the larger condensates as the larger condensates contain more Sup35NM molecules. Thus, nucleation is likely to occur before complete conversion into AAs (Figure A, upper). As condensate size decreases, τ becomes longer, reducing the probability that nucleation occurs before complete conversion from condensate into AAs (Figure A, lower). To examine whether this interpretation can explain the experimental data, we next compared kinetic models with and without competition between amyloid and AA formation. For amyloid formation, nucleation was described using a two-step nucleation model, whereas AA formation was described as an additional nth-order amorphization reaction. The model without AA formation failed to reproduce the observed sigmoid-like time courses and the condensate-size dependence of the plateau probability (Figure B). By contrast, the model including the additional nth-order amorphization reaction successfully reproduced the experimental results (Figure C; Supplementary Figures 5 and 6). We also examined whether amyloid nucleation occurred in the bulk of condensates or at the interface. In principle, in a simple system involving only amyloid nucleation, these two models could be distinguished from the size dependence of the nucleation rate. In the present system, however, both bulk-nucleation and interfacial-nucleation models were able to fit the experimental data when competition with AA formation was included (Supplementary Figures 5–8). Importantly, in both cases, incorporation of the competing AA formation process was essential to reproduce the observed condensate-size dependence of the plateau probability.

Amyloid nucleation occurs less frequently in smaller condensates, increasing the population of AAs. (B) Fitting results for the time evolution of the probability of condensates containing amyloids (P) using a two-step nucleation model without AA formation. (C) Fitting results using a model that includes competition between amyloid and AA formation through an additional nth-order amorphization reaction.

Small Molecules Modulate Amyloid Nucleation by Altering the Kinetics of AA Formation

Finally, we demonstrated that amyloid formation could be modulated by small molecules through changes in AA kinetics. (−)-Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG, Figure A) is a well-known inhibitor that suppresses amyloid formation not only of Sup35NM but also of α-synuclein and tau, proteins associated with human neurodegenerative diseases, , typically at high concentrations (>10 μM). Here, EGCG was used to modulate AA formation by adding low concentrations (<10 μM). Sup35NM condensates were prepared in 384-well plates in the presence of EGCG, and ThT fluorescence was monitored over time with a plate reader (Figure B). The fluorescence increase followed a sigmoidal curve characteristic of amyloid formation. Increasing concentrations of EGCG decreased the plateau value of ThT fluorescence, consistent with a previous report. The slight decrease in fluorescence intensity at the late stage of the assay is likely attributable to an artifact, such as precipitation and/or adhesion of fluorescent aggregates to the container wall. The quenching of the ThT by EGCG was not likely occurred in this concentration and volume range. A plot of the ThT fluorescence intensity at 20 h as a function of EGCG concentration exhibited a sigmoidal decrease (IC50 = 47 μM, Figure C). We note that EGCG showed slight absorption at the excitation wavelength used for ThT (445 nm), although the estimated reduction in excitation light was limited under our experimental conditions (<5%) (Supplementary Figure 9A). In addition, plate reader assays using Amytracker 680 showed a similar concentration dependence (IC50 = 73 μM), supporting that the EGCG effect discussed below, particularly at low EGCG concentrations, is not an artifact specific to the ThT readout (Supplementary Figure 9B). Amyloid fibril of Sup35NM was partially observed with 3000 μM EGCG in high-contrast images (Supplementary Figure 10), consistent with the results of the plate reader assay using Amytracker 680 (Supplementary Figure 9B).

Next, we examined amyloid formation within condensates in droplets upon the addition of EGCG. In large condensates, the ThT fluorescence intensity at 21 h decreased with increasing EGCG concentration (Figure D, left). A pronounced reduction was observed around 30 μM, consistent with the bulk-solution measurements (Figure C; Supplementary Figure 11A). The time evolution of the probability of condensates containing amyloids (P) was not significantly altered by the concentration of EGCG (Figure E, left), which is consistent with the trend observed in the bulk measurements (Figure C). In contrast, smaller condensates exhibited partially distinct behaviors compared with the bulk. The ThT fluorescence intensities of amyloid-containing condensates at 21 h decreased with increasing EGCG concentration, consistent with the bulk-scale results (Figure D, right; Supplementary Figure 11B). However, the probability of condensates containing amyloids (P) unexpectedly increased at EGCG concentrations of 0.3–30 μM (Figure E). It should be noted that the same threshold (F thres) as that used in Figure E was applied in this analysis. FRAP measurements (Figure F) and Raman spectroscopy (Supplementary Figure 12) of condensates with and without EGCG, performed prior to the maturation of condensates, revealed no discernible differences. This suggests that the increase in P is not attributable to changes in condensate properties but rather to the interactions of EGCG with AA or amyloid formation. To verify this interpretation, FRAP of the ThT-negative condensates in droplets was measured between 0.8 and 3.0 h after droplet formation (Figure G). The normalized FRAP recovery was calculated as (FI AF488,t – FI AF488,4 s)/(FI AF488,20 s – FI AF488,4 s), where FI AF488,t is the fluorescence intensity of AF488-Sup35 at time t, FI AF488,4 s is the intensity immediately after photobleaching, and FI AF488,15 s is the intensity after recovery (Figure H; Supplementary Figure 13). The results show that the fluorescence recovery was significantly larger with EGCG than without EGCG. This indicates that AA formation in the condensates was suppressed by the addition of EGCG. Furthermore, bulk experiments showed that EGCG at 0.3–3 μM did not affect the lag time of amyloid formation (Supplementary Figure 14), indicating that the addition of EGCG did not accelerate amyloid nucleation. Taken together, these results suggest that low concentrations of EGCG suppress the conversion of condensates into AAs, thereby promoting amyloid nucleation and subsequent elongation (Figure I).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated for the first time the size dependence of amyloid formation from condensates by directly quantifying amyloid formation kinetics and analyzing their competition with a metastable amorphous solid state (AA) using a droplet microfluidic platform. Although it is well known that proteins can occupy multiple phases and that their phase transitions sometimes compete, , how such competitive kinetics influence the liquid–solid transition from condensates to amyloids remains unclear. In conventional amyloid formation experiments, the entire microliter-scale solution behaves as a single system. When metastable and stable phases coexist, the metastable phase inevitably disappears. In addition, the absence of effective molecular probes for AAs hindered the distinction between condensates and AAs, thereby preventing quantitative evaluation of their kinetic contributions.

In contrast, in the present study based on droplet microfluidic system, amyloids and AAs were confined within picoliter-scale droplets, each serving as an isolated solution without cross-talk of protein molecules between droplets. Therefore, metastable AAs could be stably preserved within the droplets, and the probabilities of amyloid and AA formation reflected the balance between their respective kinetics. Consequently, we experimentally and numerically demonstrated that amyloid formation from condensates was suppressed in a size-dependent manner when the condensates were on the micrometer scale. It should be noted that, in principle, the scaling exponent of the size dependence of amyloid nucleation can provide insights into whether nucleation is homogeneous or heterogeneous. However, in the present system, these two models cannot be discussed due to the competition of amyloid nucleation and AAs formation. In addition, our microdroplet system is simplified in that a single condensate is formed in each droplet, whereas multiple condensates can coexist within a cell. Despite these limitations, the present study reveals a gap between liquid–solid phase transitions of proteins in micrometer- and millimeter-scale systems, highlighting a fundamental aspect for understanding the role of phase transitions in biological cells. Further investigation of interfacial effects on condensates and interactions among multiple condensates (e.g., Ostwald ripening) will be important to describe intracellular phase transitions more precisely.

Biological cells are typically a few to several tens of micrometers in size, and protein transport between cells is highly limited. The size of intracellular condensates usually ranges from a few hundred nanometers to several micrometers. Considering these factors, the droplet-isolated condensates used here more closely approximate the intracellular environment than conventional bulk-scale experiments and provide a physiologically more relevant model for studying condensate-to-amyloid transitions. Thus, the behavior observed here, in which AAs compete with amyloids and suppress amyloid formation, may occur in vivo. Because AAs are thermodynamically less stable than amyloids, they are more prone to dissolution, raising the possibility that cells may exploit this property for functional purposes.

It has widely been considered that phase separation of amyloidogenic proteins generally promotes amyloid formation. However, this view leaves a fundamental question unresolved: Why do proteins that drive amyloid formation also act as scaffolds for phase separation with such risks? Recently, multiple studies have attempted to revise this simplistic view by proposing that phase-separated states can both suppress and promote amyloid formation depending on their physicochemical properties and cellular context. , Our findings suggest an alternative perspective in the current understanding: Phase separation may serve to trap proteins in a metastable state, namely AAs, which compete with amyloid formation and thereby lower the risk of amyloid conversion while allowing dense protein storage in condensates. Supporting this idea, a previous study has shown that, in stressed yeast cells, wild-type Sup35 forms liquid-like condensates of several hundred nanometers, which are considered to develop into reversible gel-like particles based on in vitro experiments. These observations indicate that AAs have properties distinct from those of amyloids and are potentially of physiological significance. Future studies, including investigations into the interactions of AAs with other biomolecules and their disassembly mechanisms as well as observations of biomolecular condensates in living cells based on this concept, are necessary.

Furthermore, we found that when metastable states and amyloid formation compete, amyloid formation inhibitors can paradoxically promote amyloid formation. A previous study based on bulk-scale ThT assays and transmission electron microscopy observations has shown that EGCG converts Sup35 amyloids into off-pathway oligomers; however, its effects on metastable AAs have not yet been assessed. In this study, we evaluated for the first time the influence of EGCG on the kinetics of metastable states. We showed that, at low concentrations, where EGCG does not affect amyloids directly, it suppresses AA formation and thereby promotes amyloid formation. Although recent studies have reported that high concentrations of EGCG promote AA formation of immunoglobulin light chains or accelerate amyloid formation of Aβ42, the mechanism identified here is distinct, as it arises from the suppression of metastable states. This phenomenon could not be predicted from conventional bulk-scale ThT assays (Figure B) but was revealed only by isolating micrometer-sized condensates in our system. These findings suggest that compounds facilitating metastable states suppress amyloid formation, offering a potential new strategy for treating protein misfolding diseases.

Conclusion

In this study, we controlled the size of Sup35NM condensates within microdroplets and revealed, for the first time, that amyloid formation depends on condensate size due to competition with metastable AAs. In smaller condensates, the probability of complete conversion of monomers into AAs before amyloid nucleation increased, leading to the suppression of amyloid formation. Furthermore, upon the addition of EGCG, we found that amyloid nucleation was promoted in small condensates at low EGCG concentrations due to the suppression of AA formation. Because protein transport between cells is highly restricted in vivo, and intracellular condensates typically range from 100 nm to 1 μm in size, our microfluidic system more closely resembles the intracellular environment than conventional bulk-scale experiments. Therefore, we expect that these findings will provide a foundation for a deeper understanding of the physicochemical behavior of amyloidogenic protein condensates in cells and may contribute to new therapeutic and drug discovery strategies.

Experimental Section

Materials

Phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), thioflavin T (ThT), Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0), DL-dithiothreitol (DTT), guanidine hydrochloride (GdnHCl), and trichloro­(1H,1H,2H,2H-perfluorooctyl)­silane (TPFOS) were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan). Polyethylene glycol (PEG, 20 000 Da) was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Amytracer 480 was purchased from Ebba Biotech AB (Solna, Sweden). HFE-7500 3 M (TM) and 008-FluoroSurfactant was purchased from RAN Biotechnologies, Inc. (Beverly, MA, USA). Silpot 184 and polyethylene-glycol functionalized silicone (501W additive) were purchased from Dow Toray Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan).

Expression and Purification of Sup35NM Protein

The yeast prion protein Sup35NM domain (Sup35NM) was expressed using the pET29b vector containing a C-terminal 7× histidine tag. A cysteine-incompatible mutation was introduced at the C-terminus by site-specific mutagenesis (Takara Bio Inc., Kusatsu, Japan), and the mutation was confirmed by DNA sequencing.

Sup35NM was overexpressed in bacterial strain BL21 (DE3) and purified under denaturing conditions using a column. The soluble fraction was applied to nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid histidine-tag affinity chromatography (Ni-NTA column, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), equilibrated with 8 M urea and 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), and eluted with 250 mM imidazole. The His-tagged coalescent protein was applied to Cation-exchange chromatography (Cytiva, Tokyo, Japan), and the column was equilibrated with 8 M urea and 10 mM 2-morpholinoethanesulfonic acid buffer (pH 6.0). Target proteins and degradation products were then separated in a 5–20% gradient using 1 M NaCl. Purified Sup35NM was passed through a 300-kDa filter (Sartorius) to remove existing aggregates and stored in a solution containing 6 M GdnHCl and 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) at 4 °C. To purify cysteine mutants, 1 mM DTT was added to the buffer. The obtained Sup35NM solution was stocked in the condition of 4.2 mM Sup35NM, 6 M GdnHCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) at −20 °C. Just before use in the experiment, the stock solution was diluted 10-fold, centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 30 min, and the supernatant was used.

Preparation of AF488-Sup35NM

Sup35NM was reduced by treatment with 20 mM Tris­(2-carboxyethyl)­phosphine hydrochloride for 2 h. The cysteine-modified Sup35NM was treated with a 10-fold excess of Alexa Fluor 488 C5 Maleimide (AF488, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) in 6 M GdnHCl for 2 h at room temperature (20–30 °C). AF488-Sup35NM was purified on a Ni-NTA column. The obtained AF488-Sup35NM solution was stocked in the condition of 4.2 mM Sup35NM, 6 M GdnHCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) at −20 °C. Just before use in the experiment, the stock solution was diluted 10-fold, centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 30 min, and the supernatant was used.

Microfluidic Device Fabrication

A polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) microfluidic device was fabricated by soft photolithography. To prepare the microdevice, the micropattern mold of the KMPR 1035 photoresist was formed on a 4 inch silicon wafer, and PDMS was cured on this wafer at 75 °C over 1 h. Holes were then fabricated on the micropatterned PDMS surface to allow the fluid to pass through (inlets and outlets). The micropatterned PDMS sheet was bonded to a slide glass after plasma treatment using a CUTE-1MP/R vacuum plasma system (Femto Science, Gwangju, Korea) and heated at 75 °C for 60 min. To prepare hydrophilic, PEG-modified PDMS microfluidic devices, a mixture of PDMS prepolymer, PEG functionalized silicone and curing agent at a ratio of 1000:5:100 was used instead of normal PDMS.

Observation of Sup35NM Condensates under Temperature Control

To assess the reversibility of Sup35NM condensate formation, an aqueous solution comprising 37 μM Sup35NM, 3 μM AF488-Sup35NM, 0.1 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4), 60 mM GdnHCl, 10 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), and 5% PEG was prepared at 70 °C. The solution was introduced into a PEG-modified PDMS microfluidic device with 45 μm high straight microfluidic channel with microwells, and the formation of condensates was observed at 27 °C and 60 °C under 10× magnification using an inverted research microscope (IX71, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

To prepare the phase regime of Sup35NM with respect to PEG concentration and temperature, aqueous solutions containing 40 μM Sup35NM, 0.1 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4), 60 mM GdnHCl, 10 mM phosphate buffer, and 2.5–10% PEG were prepared at 70 °C. Turbidity was determined by measuring the absorbance at 500 nm using a microplate reader (EPOCH2, BioTek). Measurements were performed over a temperature range from 65 °C to 25 °C in 10 °C increments. At each temperature point, samples were equilibrated for 20 min, including the time required for temperature adjustment, prior to absorbance recording.

Observation of Amyloid Formation in Sup35NM Condensates in Microdroplets

An aqueous solution containing 40 μM Sup35NM, 20 μM ThT, 0.1 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4), 60 mM GdnHCl, 10 mM phosphate buffer, and 5% PEG was prepared at 70 °C. HFE-7500 containing 1 wt % 008-FluoroSurfactant was gently mixed with an aqueous solution of 10 mM phosphate buffer, and 5% PEG to achieve partition equilibrium. Microdroplets of the Sup35NM solution were generated using a flow-focusing microfluidic device (On-chip Biotechnologies Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), with HFE-7500 containing 1 wt % 008-FluoroSurfactant as the fluorous phase at 70 °C. The size of microdroplets was controlled by adjusting the pressure applied to the aqueous solution. The microdroplet formation was completed within 10 min. The generated microdroplets were introduced into a planar microfluidic device with a channel height of 60–80 μm (microfluidic ChipShop GmbH, Jena, Germany) modified with TPFOS. The droplets were observed using an inverted microscope (IX83, Olympus) equipped with a spinning disk confocal scanning unit (CSU-W1, Yokogawa Electric Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Imaging was performed at an excitation wavelength of 445 nm and a detection range of 454–581 nm, using a 20× objective lens at 30 °C. Time zero (t = 0 h) was defined as the start of droplet formation. In addition to observations using ThT alone, multiplex staining experiments were performed under the same conditions described above, using ThT (5 μM) with 8-anilinonaphthalene-1-sulfonic acid (ANS, 50 μM) and ThT (10 μM) with PROTEOSTAT (300-fold dilution).

Image Analysis

ImageJ and custom Python script were used to analyze micrographs of condensates in microdroplets. The radius of the microdroplets were measured from bright-field images using ImageJ. The radius of the condensates were determined from confocal fluorescence images at 1 h after droplet formation, using a Python script. To detect amyloid formation, a fluorescence intensity threshold (F thres) was defined as F thres = F ave + 5F σ, where F ave and F σ represent the mean and standard deviation, respectively, of the fluorescence intensity of condensates at t = 1 h. At each time point, the fraction of condensates undergoing amyloid nucleation (P) was calculated as P = N F>Fthres /N total, where N F>Fthres is the number of condensates with fluorescence intensity above F thres, and N total is the total number of condensates observed.

Quantification of Sup35NM Concentration in the Dilute Phase in Microdroplets

Microdroplets containing 40 μM AF488-Sup35NM, 0.1 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4), 60 mM GdnHCl, 10 mM phosphate buffer, and 5% PEG were prepared at 70 °C, as described in the previous section. Phase separation in the droplets was observed by bright-field and fluorescence microscopy. After correcting the uneven illumination using a homemade Python script, the images were analyzed with ImageJ. The droplet diameter was determined from bright-field images, and the diameters of the condensates were determined from fluorescence images. The region outside the condensates within each droplet was defined as the dilute phase, and its mean fluorescence intensity was measured. Because the fluorescence intensity of a solution within a droplet is proportional to both its concentration and the droplet diameter, the concentration was calculated from the mean fluorescence intensity divided by the droplet diameter. A calibration curve for concentration determination was generated using droplets containing nonphase-separated AF488-Sup35NM solutions of known concentrations.

Estimation of Sup35NM Concentration in Condensates

An aqueous solution containing 40 μM Sup35NM, 20 μM ThT, 0.1 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4), 60 mM GdnHCl, 10 mM phosphate buffer, and 5% PEG was prepared at 30 °C. After incubating for 10 min at 30 °C, the solution was centrifuged at 20,000 × g to sediment the condensates. The Sup35 concentration in the supernatant was determined by measuring absorbance at 280 nm using a BioSpectrometer (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). The Sup35NM concentration was then estimated from the dilute-phase concentration to be 2.0 ± 0.5 mM by calculating the volumes of the microdroplets and condensates.

Observation of Sup35NM Aggregates Following Microdroplet Coalescence

Microdroplets containing 46 μM Sup35NM, 4 μM AF488-Sup35NM, 0.1 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4), 60 mM GdnHCl, 1,000-fold diluted Amytracker 480, 10 mM phosphate buffer, and 5% PEG were prepared at 70 °C, as described in the previous section. The microdroplets were then incubated overnight at 30 °C. Subsequently, they were coalesced by adding perfluorooctanol in a microtube. The resulting aqueous phase was collected using a micropipette and transferred to a 384-well plate. Fluorescence from AF488 and Amytracker 480 were observed using a spinning disk confocal microscope.

Fluorescence Recovery after Photobleaching (FRAP)

For FRAP assays with and without temperature, aqueous solutions comprising 37 μM Sup35NM, 3 μM AF488-Sup35NM, 0.1 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4), 60 mM GdnHCl, 10 mM phosphate buffer, and 5% PEG was prepared at 70 °C or at room temperature. FRAP measurements were performed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM 710, Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Jena, Germany) with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and a detection range of 493–634 nm, under 100× magnification. Condensates were photobleached by irradiation with 488 nm and 440 nm lasers, and the fluorescence recovery was monitored over time.

For FRAP assays of Sup35NM amyloids and AAs, microdroplets containing 51 μM Sup35NM, 1 μM AF488-labeled Sup35NM, 0.1 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4), 60 mM GdnHCl, 1,000-fold diluted Amytracker 480, 10 mM phosphate buffer, and 5% PEG were prepared at 70 °C, as described in the previous section. The microdroplets were incubated overnight at 30 °C. Subsequently, they were introduced into a microfluidic device with a planar channel. After confirming the presence of aggregates by Amytracker 480 fluorescence, FRAP measurements were conducted as previously described.

For FRAP assays of Sup35NM condensates with and without EGCG in microdroplets, microdroplets containing 51 μM Sup35NM, 1 μM AF488-labeled Sup35NM, 0.1 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4), 60 mM GdnHCl, 20 μM ThT, 10 mM phosphate buffer, 5% PEG, and 0 or 3 μM EGCG were prepared at 70 °C, as described in the previous section. The microdroplets were introduced into a microfluidic device with a planar channel immediately after droplet formation and FRAP measurements were conducted as previously described.

Raman Spectroscopy

To measure Raman spectra of Sup35NM amyloid and AA, the condensates of Sup35NM microdroplets were prepared as described above by using an aqueous solution containing 40 μM Sup35NM, 20 μM ThT, 0.1 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4), 60 mM GdnHCl, 10 mM phosphate buffer, and 5% PEG. After the incubation overnight at 30 °C, the microdroplets were introduced in a glass microfluidic device with a planar channel. Prior to Raman measurements, fluorescence from ThT in the Sup35 condensates and aggregates was confirmed on the Raman microscope with an excitation wavelength of 450 ± 20 nm and a detection range of 492–507 nm with a thermoelectric cooled CCD camera (DU934N-BU2-NX, Andor). Raman spectra were then measured with a 785 nm excitation beam from a Ti:sapphire laser (3900S, Spectra Physics), pumped by a 532 nm DPSS laser (Millennia, Spectra Physics), through a water-immersion objective lens (NA = 1.2, Olympus). The exposure time of each Raman measurement was 1 s, and 100 spectra were averaged.

To measure Raman spectra of Sup35NM condensates with and without EGCG, aqueous solutions containing 40 μM Sup35NM, 20 μM ThT, 0.1 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4), 60 mM GdnHCl, 10 mM phosphate buffer, 5% PEG, 0 or 0.3 μM EGCG were prepared in a 384-well glass-bottom black plate (Greiner Bio-One GmbH, Kremsmünster, Austria). The condensates were measured as the same manner as described above.

ThT Assay Using Microplate Reader

The inhibitory effect of EGCG on Sup35NM amyloid formation was measured by a ThT assay using a microplate reader. Aqueous solutions containing 40 μM Sup35NM, 0.1 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4), 60 mM GdnHCl, 20 μM ThT, 10 mM phosphate buffer, 5% PEG, and 0–3000 μM EGCG were prepared. The samples were placed in 384-well glass-bottom black plates and the fluorescence intensity of ThT was measured using a microplate reader (FilterMax F5, Molecular Devices, LLC, San Jose, CA, USA) at 30 °C.
