Longitudinal Outcomes of Total Hip Arthroplasty in Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip: A National Database Propensity-Matched Analysis
Authors: Malik Al-Jamal, Omar Abdalla, Ahmad Hasan, Abdelrahman Diab, Omar Diab, Muhammad Waheed, Dalia Abudaya, Khaled J. Saleh, Walid Yassir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) complicates total hip arthroplasty (THA) due to altered anatomy and biomechanics, increasing surgical complexity and postoperative risks. Although its effect on THA outcomes has been studied, data on long-term implant performance and revision surgery rates remain limited. This study uses a large, propensity-matched cohort to assess 90-day medical complications and 2-year mechanical outcomes in patients with DDH undergoing THA, addressing critical gaps in the literature.Methods:A retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX Research Network analyzed data from 104 healthcare organizations. Patients with DDH undergoing THA (n = 5908) were matched with control subjects (n = 5908) based on age, sex, BMI, and comorbidities. Outcomes included 90-day medical (infection, sepsis, thromboembolism, myocardial infarction, and mortality) and 2-year mechanical complications (reoperations and implant failure). Analyses included risk differences (RD), risk ratios (RR), Kaplan-Meier survival, and P-values.Results:No notable differences were observed in 90-day medical complications between DDH and control cohorts. However, patients with DDH demonstrated significantly higher risks of 2-year mechanical complications: revision surgeries: RD: 0.011 (95% CI, 0.005 to 0.017, P = 0.000); RR: 1.492 (95% CI, 1.199 to 1.858). Implant failure: RD: 0.012 (95% CI, 0.005 to 0.018, P = 0.001); RR: 1.394 (95% CI, 1.152 to 1.687). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis further confirmed significantly lower revision surgery–free survival (log-rank P = 0.001) and implant survival (log-rank P = 0.002) in patients with DDH compared with control subjects.Conclusion:DDH increases 2-year mechanical complications post-THA, emphasizing the need for tailored surgical planning. The absence of increased 90-day medical complications supports perioperative safety. Future research should optimize long-term outcomes through innovations in implant design and surgical techniques.

Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) encompasses a spectrum of hip abnormalities, including neonatal instability, acetabular dysplasia, hip subluxation, and hip dislocation. In utero, the triradiate cartilage and ossification centers of the ilium, ischium, and pubis form the acetabulum during growth to conform around the femoral head.1 DDH is characterized by an abnormal relationship between the femoral head and the acetabulum. Insufficient coverage of the femoral head by the acetabulum due to shallowness or malorientation prevents concentric reduction. When the femoral head is not in contact with the acetabulum, the acetabulum fails to develop adequately and remains flat-shaped, and malaligned contact leads to hypertrophy of the capsule and ligamentum teres, formation of a neolimbus, and early degeneration.2 Instability of the hip joint in DDH leads to abnormal acetabular development and results in a shallow acetabulum, causing secondary osteoarthritis (OA).1,3 The definitive treatment for OA in these patients is total hip arthroplasty (THA). However, this altered anatomy in patients with DDH presents notable challenges that increase the technical complexity of THA.4,5 Part of the preoperative planning involves classifying the severity of DDH. The Crowe classification stratifies hip dysplasia based on the extent of femoral head subluxation, defined by the distance between the head-neck junction and the inter-teardrop line, ranging from Crowe I (femoral head subluxation < 50%) to Crowe IV (femoral head subluxation > 100%).6 Other classifications such as the Hartofilakidis classification are also used to describe DDH hips and sought to guide surgical management in acetabular reconstruction.7 Failure to adequately address these complexities can increase the risk of implant malposition and early revision surgery.5,8

Mechanical complications in patients with DDH undergoing THA are well-documented, with dislocation rates reported as high as 37% and aseptic loosening occurring in up to 17% of cases.9 Although some studies have explored medical complications such as deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE), and infection in this population, they have largely failed to identify notable differences when compared with other THA patients.4,9 Given the anatomic and procedural challenges unique to DDH, a more comprehensive evaluation of perioperative risks in a larger cohort remains necessary.

Although previous studies have investigated outcomes such as revision rates and implant failure, many were limited by small sample sizes and short follow-up times.9-11 A large-scale analysis of perioperative complications remains a critical gap in the literature, particularly when considering that patients with DDH undergoing THA tend to experience longer surgical times, higher postoperative transfusion rates, and extended hospital stays.4,12

This retrospective study of a multi-institutional cohort serves to evaluate perioperative complications in patients with DDH undergoing THA. Using propensity matching, we analyze outcomes at 30-day, 90-day, and 2-year follow-up intervals to assess progression over time and thus aim to help surgeons anticipate and manage postoperative risks more effectively at each stage of recovery. This study serves as the first comprehensive assessment (both medical and mechanical complications) of THA in the DDH population with a follow-up extending to 2 years.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This study is a matched cohort analysis using data from the TriNetX research network, a federated health database providing access to deidentified electronic medical records from 103 healthcare organizations (HCOs) over an 18-year period. TriNetX is a web-based research platform that enables cohort creation and feasibility queries across a global network of healthcare organizations. Data available to investigators include deidentified electronic health record data from more than 71 healthcare organizations, with updates occurring every 1 to 4 weeks. Patients are counted once, and data exclude individuals with only a medical record number or without codes. TriNetX uses standardized terminologies such as ICD and CPT codes; most of the participating institutions are large academic hospitals in the United States that contribute both inpatient and outpatient data and an average of 7 years of historical information. Postoperative outcomes in patients with developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) undergoing total hip arthroplasty (THA) (cohort A) were compared with a matched control group of non-DDH patients undergoing THA (cohort B). The study was conducted in compliance with institutional and data governance protocols.

Cohort Selection and Matching Criteria

Patients were included in cohort A (DDH and THA group) if they had a documented diagnosis of DDH (ICD-10: Q65) and underwent THA (CPT: 27130, SNOMED: 265158005, ICD-10-PCS: 0SRB, 0SR9). Cohort B (control group) consisted of patients who underwent THA for other indications but did not have a DDH diagnosis. Propensity score matching (PSM) at a 1:1 ratio was done to balance baseline characteristics, including age, sex, race, hypertension, bleeding disorders, chronic kidney disease (CKD), tobacco use, obesity, diabetes, heart failure, liver disease, and osteoarthritis of the hip found in Table 1. ICD codes for each postoperative outcome can be found in Table 2. Patients with less than 2 years of postoperative follow-up were excluded to ensure adequate longitudinal assessment of implant survival and mechanical complications. The Q65 diagnosis category “congenital deformities of hip” includes codes for congenital dislocation of hip (Q65.0–Q65.2), congenital partial dislocation of hip (Q65.3–Q65.5), congenital unstable hip (Q65.6), congenital coxa valga and coxa vara (Q65.81 and Q65.82), other specified deformities (Q65.89), and unspecified congenital hip deformity (Q65.9). The ICD-9 code 754.3 for congenital dislocation of hip was also included for encounters before 2015.

Statistical Analysis

Differences in outcomes between the DDH and control cohorts were evaluated using risk differences, risk ratios (RRs), and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was done to estimate implant survival and time to revision THA. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using the TriNetX analytics platform, which applies standardized methodologies to ensure reproducibility.

Results

Patient Characteristics

To control for confounding variables, 4635 patients undergoing THA for DDH were matched in a 1:1 propensity score analysis with 4635 control patients who underwent THA for other indications. Propensity score matching accounted for age, sex, race, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, tobacco use, obesity, diabetes, heart failure, liver disease, and osteoarthritis of the hip.

Medical Complications

Across all follow-up periods, there were no statistically significant differences in medical complications, including infection (hospital-acquired), sepsis, pulmonary embolism (PE), deep vein thrombosis (DVT), myocardial infarction (MI), renal failure, and mortality. Although minor variations were observed, none met the threshold for significance, indicating similar perioperative risk profiles between DDH and control patients. These findings suggest that mechanical rather than systemic factors likely drive differences in surgical outcomes.

Mechanical Complications

Patients with DDH demonstrated a markedly higher risk of implant failure across all time points. At 30 days, implant failure occurred in 1.6% of patients with DDH versus 1.0% of control subjects (P = 0.018). This risk remained elevated at 90 days (2.8% vs. 2.0%, P = 0.012) and 2 years (5.9% vs. 4.4%, P = 0.001). Similarly, the rate of revision THA was significantly greater in patients with DDH at 2 years (19.5% vs. 15.9%, P < 0.001), indicating a higher burden of mechanical complications requiring secondary procedures. The incidence of periprosthetic fractures in patients with DDH at 2 years did not reach statistical significance (1.0% vs. 0.7%, P = 0.116). However, dislocation rates were significantly increased at 2 years (2.7% vs. 2.0%, P = 0.047), suggesting greater instability after THA in the DDH population.

Discussion

This study of patients with DDH undergoing THA offers novel insights by demonstrating a markedly increased risk of long-term mechanical complications compared with non-DDH patients undergoing THA. Implants in patients with DDH failed at a high rate in each follow-up period likely due to the inherent biomechanical challenges posed by the complex anatomy which characterizes DDH. Postoperative dislocations and the need for surgical revision at 2-year follow-up were also markedly higher for patients with DDH, further highlighting the potential failure of prosthetic implants. These findings may be explained by the increasing severity of acetabular and femoral deformities in DDH, as classified by Crowe and Hartofilakidis classifications. Acetabular deficiencies range from segmental superior wall defects in mild subluxation (Crowe I) to superior migration of the femoral head, false acetabulum formation, and severe anteversion in high-grade DDH (Crowe IV), often with notable bone stock deficiencies.6,7 It must also be considered that limited bone stock of the superior acetabulum and inadequate bony architecture can limit prosthesis support and impair proper fixation.5,13 A potential solution to address anterolateral acetabular bone deficiency is the use of femoral head shelf autografts, as described by Nousiainen et al14 in a retrospective study of 25 DDH patients with a mean follow-up of 14 years. Their findings demonstrated that shelf grafting markedly improved acetabular coverage, with a high rate of graft union and minimal resorption, thereby enhancing implant stability and long-term fixation. The increased acetabular and femoral anteversion can increase the risk of dislocation, while high-riding femoral heads may lead to leg-length discrepancies postoperatively.13

It is therefore important to consider the additional surgeries, such as femoral shortening osteotomies, which are often required to properly reduce the hip to its anatomic origin. A group of 67 patients with Crowe IV DDH undergoing THA subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy demonstrated superior rotational stability compared with trochanteric slide osteotomy.15 In addition, implant selection plays a crucial role in optimizing outcomes in acetabular reconstruction for DDH, particularly in cases with poor bone stock. Jin et al16 reported that smaller acetabular implants in DDH may benefit from metal-inlay polyethylene liners with noncemented fixation, which outperforms cemented fixation. Given these acetabular and femoral deformities, specialized implants with smaller acetabular implants, customized surgical techniques, and meticulous preoperative planning is necessary to achieve durable prothesis.5,8

Our work builds on previous research by Wang et al,9 which analyzed in-hospital outcomes and short-term readmission rates in a large cohort. Although the study identifies an increased risk of surgical readmission for patients with DDH due to aseptic loosening and dislocation, the follow-up does not extend past the first 90 days postoperatively. Our study examines outcomes beyond this 90-day postoperative period and demonstrates higher rates of long-term implant failure and mechanical complications in patients with DDH over a 2-year period, further guiding surveillance and implant optimization in this high-risk population.

In an effort toward personalized preoperative planning for patients with DDH, 3D-printing and 3D-CT imaging have been described in the literature as helpful. Liu et al17 demonstrated that 3D-printed integrated acetabular prostheses were associated with lower loosening rates and superior long-term stability compared with modular acetabular prostheses in Crowe III dysplasia. In a study by Liu et al, the use of a 3D-printed pelvis model was compared with standard 3D-CT imaging for surgical planning in 56 patients with DDH undergoing osteotomy. Their findings demonstrated that patients whose surgical planning incorporated a physical 3D-printed pelvis model had a higher procedural success rate, reduced surgical time, and improved postoperative recovery compared with those using only 3D-CT imaging.18 These results underscore the potential benefits of patient-specific modeling in optimizing preoperative planning and intraoperative decision making, particularly in complex DDH cases requiring precise acetabular and femoral reconstruction.

Although patients with DDH undergoing THA experience relatively poor mechanical outcomes, it is important to consider the notable relief THA provides to patients with DDH. Previous studies such as Boyle et al19 have demonstrated favorable patient-reported outcomes and notable functional improvement compared with OA patients, despite reporting markedly worse preoperative function. Our study did not find notable medical complications in any of our follow-up periods, nor did other studies assessing for increased risk of medical complications in patients with DDH.4,9 A systematic review by Esmaeili et al20 including 2,829 patients which examined the outcomes of patients with Crowe type IV DDH found that patients were at increased risk of DVT (3.6%), infection (3.8%), and nerve palsies (5.6%) caused by sciatic or peroneal nerve damage, although no significance was noted in the associations. Although these findings highlight potential perioperative risks, variability in surgeon experience when managing high-grade dysplasia, particularly Crowe III and IV, should also be considered as a contributing factor to complications.

Given these findings, future research should prioritize implant designs and surgical modalities to improve mechanical stability in patients with DDH. In advanced DDH cases, particularly Crowe III and IV, THA should be approached similarly to a revision acetabular surgery due to the altered anatomy, bone deficiencies, and technical challenges that distinguish it from primary hip replacement. There is also a need to prioritize extended follow-up periods beyond 2 years to assess for long-term implant survival, late-onset complications, and functional outcomes in patients with DDH who have undergone THA.

This retrospective study benefits from a large sample size and a 2-year follow-up, allowing for a comprehensive analysis of both medical and mechanical complications of THA in patients with DDH. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The study's reliance on the TriNetX database necessitated the use of electronic health records (EHRs) for patient identification and outcome assessment, which were dependent on ICD-10 coding. As a result, there is potential for misclassification bias and diagnostic inaccuracies, although this methodology has been validated in previous TriNetX-based studies.21 In addition, deidentification of patient data precluded the assessment of key confounders, such as patient activity levels, socioeconomic status, and rehabilitation protocols, which may influence postoperative outcomes. Similarly, our study does not account for the biomechanical alterations and chronic muscular adaptations commonly observed in patients with DDH. Long-standing hip instability and abnormal loading patterns often result in tight hip abductors and flexors, accompanied by atrophy of stabilizing musculature, which may further affect functional recovery and dislocation risk after THA. The absence of surgery-specific and implant-specific data presents another limitation because details regarding implant type, surgical approach, and intraoperative decision making were unavailable, factors that could affect dislocation rates, implant longevity, and overall surgical outcomes. The TriNetX database does not capture functional outcomes such as hip range of motion, pain scores, or patient-reported quality-of-life measures, limiting the ability to correlate mechanical complications with functional recovery and patient satisfaction. Future studies incorporating long-term follow-up, surgical details, and patient-reported outcomes are necessary to further optimize surgical decision making and long-term management strategies in patients with DDH undergoing THA. Furthermore, we were unable to differentiate patients who underwent bone grafting from those who did not, nor could we identify whether noncemented cups were used with or without screws. The TriNetX platform reports only procedure codes and does not capture granular surgical details regarding grafting, fixation methods, or implant design. Similarly, the database does not record the etiology of implant failure; failures are captured as revision procedures or implant-related codes without specifying causes such as aseptic loosening, dislocation, or periprosthetic fracture. These inherent limitations preclude a more detailed assessment of surgical techniques and failure mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that patients with DDH undergoing THA face markedly higher rates of implant failure, dislocation, and revision surgery compared with non-DDH patients, highlighting the unique mechanical challenges in this population. Despite these increased risks, no notable differences in medical complications were observed. Surgical optimization, including implant selection, proper acetabular positioning, and techniques to enhance stability, remains critical to reducing long-term failure rates. Future research should focus on preoperative rehabilitation to improve the function of hip stabilizing musculature, 3D-printed acetabular prostheses with novel bone grafting techniques, improved surgical training using augmented reality for complex cases, and extended follow-up to better assess long-term outcomes and optimize THA success in patients with DDH.
