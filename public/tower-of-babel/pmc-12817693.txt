Practicability of a malaria rapid diagnostic self-test among artisanal gold miners in French Guiana: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Raphaëlle Le-Querriou, Yann Lambert, Marion Petit-Sinturel, Lorraine Plessis, Teddy Bardon, Carlotta Carboni, Martha Suarez-Mutis, Stephen Vreden, Josiane Muller, Jane Bordalo-Miller, Alice Sanna, Maylis Douine
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn French Guiana (FG), artisanal and small-scale gold mining (ASGM) workers represent mobile, hard-to-reach population with high malaria burden. Limited access to rapid diagnostic test (RDT) delays treatment, favours inappropriate use of artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT), and increases transmission and resistance risk. The Malakit intervention enables ASGM workers to self-diagnose and self-treat using RDTs and ACT in a free kit distributed by community health workers (CHWs) after a dedicated training session. This cross-sectional study assessed the praticability of the Bioline Malaria Ag P.f/P.f/P.v RDT among ASGM workers.MethodsDuring Malakit training sessions, an independent observer evaluated participants’ knowledge of RDT use, ability to perform self-testing, and ability to interpret results. Each assessment was conducted using a specific tool and quantified with a corresponding sub-score, which were then combined in an overall practicability score. Associations with socio-demographic characteristics were explored using multiple linear regressions.ResultsFrom May to June 2024 forty participants were enrolled in Oiapoque, Brazil. The male-to-female sex ratio was 2.64 and median age 37 years [IQR 31.8-48.8]; 27.5% had no formal education. Following training, mean scores were: knowledge 83.9% [CI95 79.6–88.2], practice 84.6% [CI95 78.3–90.9] without assistance (100% with guidance) and interpretation 77.2% [CI95 70.7–83.8]. All RDTs performed yielded valid result. Correct interpretation rates were 96.0% [CI95 92.4–99.6] for positive strips, 70.0% [CI95 55.2–84.8] for negative strips, and 65.8% [CI95 56.6–75.0] for invalid strips. The overall practicability score was 81.9% [CI95 78.3–85.6]. In practice, all obtained a valid test (presence of the control strip).ConclusionsThe study demonstrated the practicability of malaria self-testing among non-professional users after structured training, with ASGM workers achieving an overall practicability score of 81.9%. Participants successfully performed RDTs (100% valid results with guidance) and accurately interpreted positive results (96.0%). This methodology provides a replicable framework for evaluating self-diagnostic strategies in hard-to-reach populations and supports integration of community-based malaria diagnosis into elimination programmes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05719-6.Keywords: Artisanal gold miners, Brazil, French Guiana, Malaria, Oiapoque, Rapid Diagnostic Test, Self-testing, Bioline Malaria Ag P.f/P.f/P.v

Background

Malaria remains the world's leading parasitic disease, affecting 263 million people globally in 2023 [1]. The Guiana Shield, encompassing French Guiana (FG), Suriname, Guyana and parts of Venezuela and Brazil, continues to face significant malaria transmission. Regarding malaria in the region: Suriname reported malaria elimination in 2025 [2]. In Brazil’s Amazon region, approximately 140,000 malaria cases were reported in 2023, with 83–85% due to Plasmodium vivax and 15% to Plasmodium falciparum. In French Guiana (FG), 425 malaria cases were recorded in 2024, representing an incidence of 1.5‰ inhabitants. Plasmodium vivax remained predominant. All P. falciparum cases were imported. This region has rich gold deposits attracting approximately 10,000 artisanal and small-scale gold mining (ASGM) workers. These predominantly Brazilians workers operate in remote, inaccessible sites (4–5 days travel by foot/boat) with limited healthcare access. ASGM workers face compounded health challenges: geographic isolation without essential healthcare, socioeconomic marginalization limiting healthcare-seeking behaviour, legal barriers, and language/literacy barriers in a multilingual region. These factors contribute to delayed malaria diagnosis, inadequate treatment, persistent transmission, and community-wide disease burden. In FG, regulations preventing non-health professionals from performing diagnosis tests restricts timely malaria diagnosis and treatment [3–5].

In 2015, study showed 22.3% of ASGM workers carried malaria parasites, with over half self-treating with inappropriate antimalarials—conditions favouring transmission and antimalarial resistance [6]. Malakit (2018-onwards), a tri-country intervention, enabled ASGM self-diagnosis and treatment using community health workers (CHWs) delivered rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) kits. Follow-up showed 71.7% compliance with kit instructions and a significant reduction in malaria incidence and prevalence [6–8]. Although miners can correctly perform RDTs after appropriate training [9, 10], maintaining diagnostic quality at scale remains a challenge.

Between 2015–2020 P. vivax increased relative to P. falciparum in FG, complicating elimination due to relapses [11]. The new CUREMA project (2023–2024) combines Malakit with targeted drug administration (TDA) for P. vivax [12].

The RDT used in initial Malakit was the CareStart™ Malaria pLDH (pan) two-band test manufactured by ACCESSBIO, which was discontinued in 2023. CUREMA therefore adopted the four-band Bioline Malaria Ag P.f/P.f/P.v RDT manufactured by ABBOTT—a more complex test requiring species-specific interpretation [13]. The World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines emphasize the importance of quality-assured diagnosis for all suspected malaria cases [14, 15], yet no study has evaluated the practicability of complex multi-band malaria RDTs for self-testing among ASGM workers. Recent research highlights persistent challenges with RDT accuracy and interpretation in various settings [16, 17], underscoring the need to assess user competency with new diagnostic tools. This study adresses this research gap by evaluating the ability of ASGM workers to correctly perform and interpret the four-band Bioline RDT following standardized training, to support the regional scaling up of Malakit intervention [18].

Methods

Study design, setting and population

This cross-sectional ancillary study was conducted on a subsample of participants enrolled in CUREMA and receiving the Malakit intervention by a single study investigator, hereafter named “observer”.

The study took place in the Brazilian municipality of Oiapoque on the Brazilian border with FG, at CUREMA inclusion sites as well as during mobile outreach activities at hotels [12]. Although CUREMA was implemented both in Brazil and Suriname, this study was conducted in Brazil only, both for operational feasibility reasons and because the four-band RDT used in Brazil was in theory more complex to interpret than the three-band RDT used in Suriname.

Inclusion criteria

The study's target population were individuals planning to work in gold mining in FG and who participated in CUREMA [12]. Inclusion criteria were: (i) to be over 18 years of age, (ii) to agree to participate in the study, (iii) to be involved in ASGM activities, (iv) to be asymptomatic at the time of inclusion, (v) to weigh more than 35 kg. Specific eligibility criteria for receiving a malakit were: (i) willingness to participate in the training, and (ii) planning to visit a gold mining site in FG or Amapá within one month [12].

Recruitment procedure and sampling

The observer was present for 17 days at Oiapoque sites between May 1st and July 31st, 2024, depending on logistical and transportation constraints to reach the inclusion sites. During these period, all ASGM workers who presented at the distribution site and were enrolled in CUREMA and received a Malakit training were systematically included in the ancillary study. The observer remained in the background to avoid interfering with the training or self-testing process, and recorded the information collected using the three evaluation tools described in section “Evaluation tools and procedures”. All participants met the inclusion criteria, provided informed consent, and were included in the final analysis with no exclusions (participation rate: 100%).

Sampling followed a convenience-based approach, meaning that inclusion occurred continuously and exhaustively throughout the observation days without any prior selection or additionnal eligibility criteria beyond those defined in the CUREMA project. Thus, every participant who received a Malakit while the observer was present was automatically included in the study, ensuring that the observations reflected real-life field conditions and provided an accurate depiction of participant’s practices during training and self-testing with the RDT.

RDT evaluated

The study focused on the Bioline Malaria Ag P.f/P.f/P.v rapid diagnostic test (RDT) manufactured by ABBOTT, a qualitative test designed to detect Plasmodium antigens in human whole blood. It uses strips coated with antibodies targeting Histidine-Rich Protein 2 (HRP2), unique to P. falciparum and Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH), either pan-specific (detecting all Plasmodium species), or specific either to P. vivax or P. falciparum [11]. This RDT was chosen for evaluation because it is widely used by the National Malaria Programme in Brazil, where malaria diagnosis and treatment are offered free of charge.

During training, participants were instructed to interpret the test results simply as positive, negative, or invalid, without differentiating species. This was because the medication delivered by Malakit comprised only blood-stage antimalarials (Coartem) without targeting P. vivax hypnozoites, making species identification unnecessary and potentially confusing [19].

Training delivered by CHWs

The project's CHWs, who had past training as nurses, microscopists, or health technicians, were key actors closely connected to the community of ASGM workers. Beyond their technical role, their strong social proximity, cultural understanding, and the trust they inspire among ASGM workers were essential in strengthening both the feasibility and the acceptability of the Malakit intervention. The human dimension allowed them to adapt messages, overcome language barriers, and foster open dialogue on sensitive health issues in a context often marked by mistrust toward institutions. Their missions comprised: raising awareness, reaching out, collecting consent and training CUREMA participants, conducting safety follow-up visits, managing supply and equipment, and performing digital data collection [20] (Supplementary materials 1).

Evaluation tools and procedures

In addition to the procedures of the CUREMA intervention protocol [12], the observer performed a structured observation of the participants during their training by a CHW and during their practice of the self-test, without intervening. Participant’s knowledge and understanding was assessed with an anonymous ad hoc questionnaire administered by the CHW in charge of training, and answers were noted by the observer. Three assessment tools were developed: their design, relevance and feasibility were improved after a prior phase of formative research conducted with the CUREMA investigation team and CHWs, who were comprehensively informed of the ancillary study objectives. Because the observer was not a native Portuguese speaker, the assessment instruments were designed to enable data collection without requiring language fluency; they required only comprehension of participants’ responses, a capability the observer demonstrated consistently throughout the study.

Knowledge test

The knowledge test consisted of seven true/false questions written on laminated flashcards, already known and used by CHWs, allowing the participant to be questioned. The study investigator completed a grid and noted the participants' answers (Supplementary materials 2).

Observation grid

A non-participant observation grid was developed to document the performance of the self-test. The observer did not interfere with, nor influenced, the behaviour of the participant and the CHWs, in order to collect authentic data under conditions as close as possible to reality. Each step of the self-test was evaluated: the observer checked “yes” if the step was correctly performed, and “no” otherwise. The “assistance” checkbox indicated any spontaneous help given by the CHW, such as verbal guidance or support. Critical steps indispensable for the correct performance and validity of the RDT were identified by an expert panel composed of a research engineer, a pharmacist-biologist, a laboratory technician and four medical doctors (Supplementary materials 3).

Interpretation of RDT results

A set of nine standardized strips representing five positive RDTs, one negative RDT and three invalid RDTs were shown by CHWs to participants to test their ability to interpret the results of an RDT (Fig. 1). The observer completed a grid by noting down the participants' answers (Supplementary materials 4).

Standardized RDT strips used to assess the ability of study participants to interpret RDT results in the practicability study (Curema project, French Guiana, 2024)

Data analysis

All data collected on paper were anonymized using sequential identification numbers and then captured electronically with the use of KoboCollect online form. The resulting database was cleaned and recoded using MS Excel's Power Pivot module. Description of data was performed using MS Excel; Scores calculation, correlations and multiple analyses were conducted using R (v4.4.2) and Rstudio (v1.3.1056-1). No missing data were encountered in this study. All 40 enrolled participants successfully completed all three assessment components (knowledge test, practice observation, and interpretation assessment).

Calculation of practicability score

Practicability was assessed using three scores: a knowledge score, a practice score, and an interpretation score. The overall practicability score was calculated as the average of these three sub-scores. Each score was first calculated individually for every participant, and an average score was then calculated among all participants.

Pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess whether each score represented different dimensions of RDT practicability. To identify individual determinants of RDT practicability, associations between each score (overall practicability score and each one of the sub-scores) and socio-demographic characteristics of participants were explored using multiple fractional logit regressions and quantified with average marginal effects (AME)[21].

Knowledge score

The knowledge score corresponds to the number of correct answers given by a participant out of the total number of expected answers.

Practice score

The practice score corresponds to the number of critical steps completed without assistance, out of the total number of six critical steps. The critical steps were validated by an expert panel composed of medical doctors, a pharmacist-biologist, and a research engineer.

Interpretation score

The interpretation score is composed of three sub-scores corresponding to each type of possible result:

Positive score: proportion of strips correctly interpreted out of RDT strips.

Positive score: proportion of strips correctly interpreted out of RDT strips.

Negative score: correct interpretation of the single negative RDT strip.

Negative score: correct interpretation of the single negative RDT strip.

Invalid score: proportion of strips correctly interpreted out of 3 invalid RDT strips.

Invalid score: proportion of strips correctly interpreted out of 3 invalid RDT strips.

The interpretation score is the average of the three sub-scores.

Ethical and regulatory approvals

This study received ethical approval from Fiocruz Ethics Committee and Brazilian CONEP (protocol 5.507.241, July 5, 2022), complying with Good Clinical Practices and Declaration of Helsinki principles. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in their own language. Data were pseudonymized according to GDPR and CNIL MR-001 methodology; the CUREMA project is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05540470).

Results

Between May and July 2024, 396 ASGM workers were enrolled in CUREMA at inclusion sites in Oiapoque (Brazil). Of these, 40 participants (10.1%) were enrolled during the 17 days observation period when the independent observer was present. All 40 participants (100%) completed the full training sessions delivered by CHWs, including the knowledge test, practice observation and interpretation assessment using standardized RDT strips (Fig. 2).

Socio-demographic characteristics of participants

Among the 40 observed participants, the M/F sex ratio was 2.64 and the median age was 37 years [31.75-48.75]. All participants were born in Brazil, and the median time spent working in gold mining was nine years [Min = 1 year; Max = 40 years] at the time of inclusion. The distribution of participants' level of education shows that 27.5% had received no formal education, 42.5% had reached primary level, and 30.0% had received secondary or higher education. Participants’ occupations were diverse: mainly gold mining workers (30.0%), cooks (22.5%) and travelling vendors (20.0%).

Of the 40 participants interviewed, 77.5% came from a gold mining located in FG and 90.0% (n = 36) reported a past history of malaria. In addition, eight participants (20.0%) had already attended a Malakit training course in the past.

Knowledge score

To assess participants’ understanding of RDT procedures and malaria, a knowledge test was administered. Participants scored an average of 83.9% [CI95 79.6—88.2] correct answers on the knowledge test. None of the participants gave less than four correct answers, and 70% gave a correct answer to at least six of the seven questions asked (Fig. 3).

Participant knowledge scores: Proportion of correct and incorrect answers in RDT test in the practicability study (Curema project, French Guiana, 2024) (N = 40)

RDT practice score

The practical skills of participants in performing a RDT were evaluated by perfomance of each critical steps identified by an expert panel (steps n°6, 8, 9, 10, 11 and 13, see Fig. 4). On average, each participant completed without assistance 84.6% [95%CI 78.3–90.9] of the critical steps. With assistance, all study participants correctly performed all six critical steps. All the RDTs performed by the participants produced a valid result, i.e. with the appearance of the control C band. Among the tasks deemed non-critical by the expert panel, the least frequently performed was reading the RDT expiry date.

Participant RDT practice scores: proportion of critical steps correctly performed in the practicability study (Curema project, French Guiana, 2024) (N = 40)

Interpretation score

Participants’ ability to correctly interpret RDT results was assessed. On average, participants had a correct interpretation score of 96.0% [95%CI 92.4–99.6] for strips with a positive result, 70.0% [95%CI 55.2–84.8] for the strip with a negative result, and 65.8% [95%CI 56.6–75.0] for invalid test strips (Fig. 5). Taken together, these three scores correspond to an overall score of 77.2% [95%CI 70.7–83.8] for the correct interpretation of RDT results, on average for all participants.

Participants interpretation scores: proportion of correct and incorrect RDT strip readings in the practicability study (Curema project, French Guiana, 2024) (N = 40)

The most frequent error was to interpret an invalid test as negative: 42.9%, 76.5% and 90.9% respectively for strips 1, 3 and 9, respectively (Fig. 5).

Overall practicability score of the RDT

The lack of correlation (Pearson coefficients < 0.30, supplementary materials 5) between the values of knowledge, practice and interpretation scores suggests that each explores a different dimension of RDT practicability. An overall RDT practicability score was then calculated by averaging this three independent RDT subscores. Accross all 40 participants in the study, the mean practicability score was 81.9% [95%CI 78.3–85.6] (Fig. 6).

Individual determinants of RDT practicability

Factors influencing RDT practicability were explored using multiple regression adjusted for age and sex. The multiple regression model shows a significant association of the overall practicability score with level of education, but not with prior Malakit training (Table 1). After multivariable adjustment, the average practicability score was higher by 8.7 percentage points [95%CI 0.6–16.9] (p = 0.04) among participants with primary or higher education.

These results apply differently to the different components of the practicability score. After adjusting for age and sex, a strong association was found between the knowledge score and the level of education (19.7 percentage point increase [95%CI 10.2–29.1], p < 0.001), while no association was found with the RDT practice score or the interpretation score. Discussion

This study demonstrates that, even within isolated and low-literacy communities, individuals can successfully perform and interpret malaria self-tests when provided with adequate training and support. The mean score on the knowledge questionnaire was 83.9% [95%CI 79.6–88.2]. Each participant completed 84.6% [95%CI 78.3–90.9] of the critical steps without assistance, and 100% with assistance (i.e. CHW guidance, use of video or drawings). All the RDTs performed by the participants yielded a valid result. The correct interpretation score for the positive RDT strips was 96.0% [95%CI 92.4–99.6], 70.0% [95%CI 55.2–84.8] for the negative strip and 65.8% [95%CI 56.6–75.0] for the invalid test strips. The overall score for the correct interpretation of RDT results was 77.2%.

Strenghts and limitations of the study

This study presents an innovative methodology to evaluate malaria self-testing in remote areas. The integration of the study within a broader interventional research project allowed for the adaptation of tools and methodology to the specificities of ASGM workers, thanks to the expertise of CHWs. The use of interactive flashcards encouraged spontaneous and authentic answers, thereby reducing biases related to the participants’ awareness of being evaluated. Furthermore, the involvement of different pairs of CHWs helped limit training-related biases and contributed to the validity of the findings.

However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The sample size of the study remains small (40 participants), which limits its statistical power. Enrolled participants were potentially the most mobile, accessible, and health-conscious among the ASGM population, introducing a selection bias. Additionally, conducting the study in a single inclusion site in Brazil, due to logistical and regulatory constraints, may have led to another selection bias—although previous studies have shown a similar socio-demographic profile across all inclusion sites [5, 22]. Moreover, logistical constraints and the need to align with a training process reduced the time available to thoroughly explore the difficulties experienced by the study participants. The presence of a third-party observer may have been a methodological challenge, as did the language barrier, which at times complicated interactions. Of note, this study evaluated self-testing using a four-band test, which is more complex than the three-band version used in FG or Suriname. This suggests that the observed performance, particularly regarding result interpretation, could potentially fare better in these other settings.

Strong participant competence in understanding, performing and interpreting valid RDTs results

ASGM workers achieved an average knowledge score of 83.9% on the knowledge test and therefore possessed the necessary understanding to use an RDT after training, although some steps, such as checking the expiry date, were often neglected. The effective training of the participants, quantified by a practice score of 84.6% without assistance and of 100% with assistance suggests that self-testing is a promising strategy for combating malaria in remote areas where access to care is limited. Self-testing with RDTs has been validated for diseases such as HIV and COVID-19. Although the sensitivity of RDTs is generally lower than that of conventional methods such as microscopy, this method has been successfully deployed in other infectious disease contexts. Challenges may vary according to the disease, yet this approach represents an interesting alternative, making it possible to improve access to diagnosis and encourage rapid care uptake [23].

The omission of certain steps, deemed non-essential by the expert panel, did not seem to compromise the validity of the RDTs given the control band was systematically observed. These results underline the importance of using accessible, visual teaching aids during training. The aids currently used in Malakit could be improved with, for example, coloured visual reminders for critical steps, such as checking the expiry date.

The strong association found between the knowledge score and the level of education suggests the importance of improving the readability of kit instructions to participants with lower literacy. On the other hand, the association found specifically between the RDT practice score and a previous Malakit training, and this independently of the level of education, is an encouraging result. Learning how to use an RDT can be made accessible to a wider audience through appropriate training, and practice skills can be maintained through refresher trainings. The involvement of CHWs is also crucial, as they play a key role in supporting patients and improving access to diagnosis. Yet beyond mastering RDT performance, this competency must also encompass accurate result interpretation.

Interpretation of standard RDT strips revealed a higher frequency of errors for invalid results, often confused with negative results. This finding, also reported in other studies, underlines the need to reinforce training on these specific points [24, 25]. However, participants were made aware by the CHWs of the importance of performing a new test in the event of an invalid result or persistence of symptoms after a negative result, thus limiting the risk of inappropriate therapeutic decisions.

Conversely, the participants' average score of 96.0% for the interpretation of positive RDT results highlights their excellent ability to identify a result requiring malaria care and justifies one’s confidence in their ability to initiate appropriate treatment. Finally, the absence of any association between the interpretation score and the level of education suggests that the difficulty of this stage of the Malakit training is independent of literacy level and requires reinforcement. The absence of an association between the interpretation score and a previous Malakit training was expected given the standard RDT strips were introduced (and then retained) as a training tool in the CUREMA project specifically for the purposes of this study.

Future perspectives: self-diagnosis as a promising tool for malaria control

The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated the effectiveness of self-diagnosis as a relevant strategy for infectious diseases [26]. However, the use of RDTs for self-testing requires careful attention to minimize errors and ensure reliable results [27]. In this regard, initiatives such as the Malakit training programme support future users by promoting a controlled and informed use of the tests. This approach aligns with similar initiatives developed for HIV, where self-testing has contributed to improved access to diagnosis and care [28].

The results of this study pave the way for the adoption of RDTs for self-diagnosis in areas remote from healthcare systems, particularly on gold mining sites. The availability of rapid tests for HIV and COVID-19 in Low and Low Middle Income Countries (LMIC) has helped overcome several barriers to testing access by enabling decentralized, rapid, and autonomous diagnosis [29, 30]. For COVID-19, antigen tests were deployed on a large scale, including outside healthcare facilities, enabling early case detection, rapid referral to isolation and care, and improved epidemiological surveillance. These strategies helped limit virus transmission, even in resource-limited settings. When applied to malaria control, such an approach could promote earlier and broader testing, particularly in remote areas or among hard-to-reach populations, such as ASGM workers in FG. This would not only reduce inappropriate treatment practices, but also help limit transmission and better target public health interventions [6].

Beyond technical practicability, this study highlights the importance of equity and community empowerment. Malakit enables ASGM to take greater control of their own health management by enabling them to perform and interpret malaria RDTs autonomously. This self-testing approach enhances both diagnostic access and health literacy among populations often excluded from conventional healthcare services.

The three-component evaluation methodology developed here provides a replicable framework for assessing self-testing interventions in other hard-to-reach populations. As the Guiana Shield region advances toward malaria elimination, this study offers additional evidence on whether community-based diagnostic approaches can be successfully implemented among migrant and mobile populations.

However, several challenges remain, particularly regarding regulatory oversight and authorization for use, in order to enable target populations to fully benefit from these tools. These kits were not available in FG at the time of the study due to the lack of regulatory authorization to use RDTs as self-tests by the French National Agency for the Safety of Medicines and Health Products (ANSM). As a result, their distribution was limited to border areas on the Brazilian and Surinamese banks. However, an exceptional waiver was granted by ANSM to allow their use in the specific context of ASGM.

Finally, although the use of expired tests is not recommended by the WHO, it has been observed that some participants do not consistently check the expiration date of the tests. An in-depth risk analysis, combined with a better understanding of how RDTs degrade over time and under various environmental conditions, could help adapt certain practices to field realities while ensuring the reliability of results.

Conclusion

This study represents a first exploration of the practicability of the RDT among ASGM workers in FG. It demonstrated that, with appropriate training, non-professional users in remote malaria-endemic settings can perform and interpret RDTs reliably, providing valuable insights into the ability of non-expert populations in specific contexts. The three-component methodology developed here-encompassing knowledge assessment, structured performance observation, and interpretation validation-provides a replicable model for evaluating similar self-diagnostic strategies in other low-access populations and remote settings. The results indicate that RDTs represent a promising tool for decentralized malaria diagnosis, with potential to enhance access to care in areas where conventional laboratory services are unavailable. This approach could become an essential component of malaria control programmes targeting hard-to-reach populations.
