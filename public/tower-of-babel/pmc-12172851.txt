Task-related oxygenation in the prefrontal cortex as a function of mask-wearing frequency: An empirical test using functional near-infrared spectroscopy
Authors: Peter A. Hall, Mohammad Nazmus Sakib, Anna Hudson, Alkarim Billawala, Geoffrey T. Fong, Hasan Ayaz
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIntroduction of brain hypoxia by frequent mask-wearing is a concern voiced by some who resist masking mandates. Studies have examined acute effects of one-shot mask-wearing on peripheral and cerebral oxygenation in the laboratory, but not effects of everyday mask-wearing frequencies on task-related functional activation. The objective of the current study was to examine whether frequency of mask-wearing in daily life is associated with lower task-related brain oxygenation levels, and whether the magnitude of any such effects vary by age and sex.MethodsParticipants were 78 community-dwelling adults between the ages of 18 and 84 years, all of whom were vaccinated at the time of participation; 65.4% (n = 51) were female. Frequency of mask-wearing was assessed using survey questions on mask-wearing practice during an active COVID-19 mask mandate. Recordings of task-related cerebral oxygenation were taken during the completion of a simple reaction time task using 16-channel functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS).ResultsThe psychomotor vigilance task elicited reliable increases in cerebral oxygenation within the right mid-frontal gyrus (F(1,61.345) = 15.975, p < .001). However, there was no significant association between everyday masking frequency and performance on the psychomotor vigilance task (b = 0.059, SE = 0.092 (95% CI [-0.122, 0.241]), t = .646, p = .520), nor any association between everyday masking frequency and task-related brain oxygenation on any measurement channel (all ps < .05).ConclusionsHigher mask-wearing frequency in daily life is not associated with significantly lower levels of task-related brain oxygenation, or worse performance on a sustained attention task.

Introduction

Public health advice for infectious disease mitigation generally includes mask-wearing in crowded spaces and hospital settings to prevent the spread of airborne pathogens. In the case of SARS-CoV-2, masking among members of the general population was critical for reducing spread of the virus during the COVID-19 pandemic (Abaluck et al., 2022; Rader et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021; Coclite et al., 2020). Mask-wearing became a standard practise in medical settings around the world following the influenza pandemic of 1918, and remains pervasive today in clinical settings (Strasser and Schlich, 2020). Although masks are of benefit in disease prevention efforts and in clinical environments, they are not universally accepted among members of the general public. Among those living in individualistic societies that prize personal choice and emphasize freedom, there have always been reservations about possible negative effects of masks. Such reservations inevitably impact the perceived balance of costs vs. benefits of personal mask-wearing decisions.

One reservation voiced by those in the general public is based on a hypothesized negative effect of mask-wearing on oxygen supply to the brain (Goodman and Carmichael, 2020). Even post-pandemic, this remains a commonly cited reason for ordinary citizens to object to wearing of masks in high-risk situations, including in clinical settings such as hospitals. Several studies have examined the issue, but the empirical evidence in support of the mask-related hypoxia hypothesis is limited to tests of single-wear effects of masking in a laboratory setting, and in most cases does not employ brain imaging modalities that are specifically designed to assess oxygen concentrations in brain tissues. The latter is important to probe because some adverse impacts of frequent masking on brain oxygenation could be cumulative and only evident under conditions of cognitive challenge, and therefore may not be evident at rest.

These limitations aside, experimental findings from the emerging literature on blood oxygenation effects of single-shot mask-wearing are equivocal. One study from early in the COVID-19 pandemic found no evidence of single-shot mask wearing on peripheral oxygenation using a pulse oximeter fitted to the index finger (Chan et al., 2020); however in this study cerebral oxygenation was not measured. Fischer and colleagues used fNIRS to examine cerebral oxygenation effects of single-shot masking and found evidence of reliable but minor hypoxic effects in a small sample of 13 healthy young adults between the ages of 23 and 33 years (Fischer et al., 2021). One additional study using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) found evidence of mask-related reductions in functional connectivity between a seed region in the salience network and two regions within the prefrontal cortex in a sample of 23 healthy adults (Haller et al., 2022); a second fMRI study involving 37 healthy adults found no significant effects of single-shot masking on resting state, task-related activations, nor functional connectivity indices (Klugah-Brown et al., 2022). Finally, a study employing electroencephalogram (EEG) revealed no effects of single-shot mask wearing on neuro-electrical activity within the brain (Tamimi et al., 2022). While these studies are informative, the findings are not uniform across studies and they share in common a focus on acute effects of single-shot masking on neural indices rather than masking frequency under everyday life conditions. Likewise, the studies are largely uniform in age and, in some cases, sex composition and without examining possible moderating effects of age and sex.

To our knowledge no prior studies have examined the association between everyday life mask wearing frequency and task-related brain oxygenation. Yet examining the possibility of cumulative effects of everyday mask wearing on brain oxygenation may be critical in order to address lingering concerns within the population about brain health impacts of habitual mask wearing. In the current study, we used a sample of male and female adults over a wide age range to examine the association between mask-wearing frequency during a population-wide mask mandate and brain oxygenation during a sustained attention task in order to examine whether or not more frequent mask-wearing is associated with differential oxygen saturation within the prefrontal cortex. Based on prior literature, it was anticipated that few reliable associations between masking frequency and oxygenation would be evident. The focus of this study was on the medial and lateral prefrontal cortex, because of their known sensitivity to environmental and lifestyle perturbations (Lowe et al., 2017; Fuster, 2015; Miller and Cohen, 2001), and the significance of these regions for decision-making as well as behavioral and emotional regulation (Fuster, 2015; Miller and Cohen, 2001; Dixon et al., 2017).

Methods

Participants

Participants were 78 adult volunteers, 65.4% (n = 51) of whom were female and 34.6% (n = 27) of whom were male. The mean age of participants in years was 30.13 (SE = 2.14); 61.5% (n = 48) were between ages 18 and 24, while 38.5% (n = 30) were between ages 25 and 84 years. All participants were fully vaccinated against SARS-CoV-2 as per the requirements of the data collection site at the time. Participants were from a larger study examining determinants of effective health communications (Hall et al., 2022); only those participants with valid brain oxygenation changes and masking frequency data were included in the current analyses. The experimental session was approximately 60 min in length and included the use of eye tracking methods coupled with additional cognitive and self-report measures not for the purpose of the present study. The study protocols and all materials, including the survey questionnaire, received ethical approval from the Office of Research Ethics, University of Waterloo, Canada (ORE# 43569). Informed consent was obtained on the web survey from all study participants in writing prior to completing the survey.

Procedure

Participants attended a laboratory session wherein they were fitted with a headband containing fNIRS light emitters and detectors, while seated at a computer with their chin secured by a chin rest. Participants received task instructions while seated, and then completed a computer-administered psychomotor vigilance task using Inquisit Lab 6 software (Fig. 1). Measures

Sustained Attention. The reaction time task in the present analysis occurred at the beginning of the larger experimental session (Hall et al., 2022). Participants attended to a blank white screen with a fixation cross at the centre, followed by the presentation of a visual stimulus [a red circle] which would randomly appear between an interval of 2 and 8 s. Participants were instructed to respond to the stimulus as quickly as possible by pressing the spacebar on a computer keyboard. The task consisted of 20 trials in one block, with the computer software automatically recording the reaction times and accuracy for each trial. The primary metric was reaction time in milliseconds, with higher values indicating worse performance (i.e., slower reaction times).

Masking Frequency. Frequency of mask-wearing was assessed using the following two items, which were standardized and averaged together as a general index of masking frequency in everyday life: “How consistently do you follow the recommendations by your local or provincial public health officials about mask-wearing?” with the following response options: 1 = I do not follow guidelines, 2 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 4 = most of the time, 5 = all/nearly all of the time, and 6 = I go above and beyond, and “How often do you currently wear a mask when you are in INDOOR public places?” with the following response options: 1 = not at all, 2 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 4 = most of the time, 5 = all of the time. These two items showed a significant correlation (r = 0.47, p < .001). Both items were converted to z-scores and then averaged together such that higher values indicated greater levels of mask-wearing frequency in everyday life.

fNIRS signal acquisition and analysis

Prefrontal hemodynamics were continuously recorded during the sustained attention task using the wearable fNIRS system fNIR Devices Model 203c (fNIR Devices, LLC, Potomac, MD, USA). Prior to the task, participants were fitted with an ultra-thin flat head sensor pad containing near-infrared sensors and detectors, arranged in a pre-set manner to record 16 channels covering the anterior prefrontal cortex and 2 additional channels from superficial skin level with short distance detectors. For each channel, two wavelengths (730 nm and 850 nm) and one ambient light time series data were recorded at 10 Hz sampling rate. Anatomical landmarks were used for sensor placement for each individual participant (as described in (Ayaz et al., 2011)). The light emitting diode (LED) light sources and photo detectors were positioned in a rectangular 2 × 8 grid format within a sensor pad (Ayaz et al., 2012). Due to light interference from the use of an eye-tracking device during the task (unrelated to the present protocol), the bottom row of ventral channels did not yield useable data. For this reason, only the top dorsal 8 channels were used in subsequent analyses (channels 1,3,5,7,9,11,13, 15). Data acquisition, visualization, and processing were accomplished using COBI Studio software (Ayaz et al., 2011). All data were filtered and processed offline after recording. Data were passed through a finite impulse response hamming filter of order 100 and cut-off frequency 0.1 Hz. Data of each participant were checked for any saturation and motion artifact contamination by means of a coefficient of variation based statistical filter known as sliding window motion artifact rejection (SMAR; (Ayaz et al., 2010);). Time synchronized blocks were processed with the Modified Beer-Lambert Law to calculate oxygenation-hemoglobin (HbO) concentrations for each optode with a 10 s local baseline. Average ΔHbO during the reaction time task was extracted in reference to this local baseline. Relationships between mask-wearing frequency and ΔHbO were examined at each optode individually, testing for moderating effects of age and sex separately. Given that no interactions were found, these were used as covariates in a subsequent model, and both raw and adjusted beta weights were displayed graphically. Linear mixed models were used to examine task-related activations in the prefrontal cortex at each optode. Hierarchical linear regression models were then used to examine the extent to which mask wearing frequency predicted magnitude of task-related increases in cerebral oxygenation at each optode. A FDR correction was applied to the results in the event of any significant uncorrected effects.

Results

Among participants 33.3% described exceeding the mask recommendations during the mandate, 39.2% reported meeting recommendations “all/nearly all” of the time, 20.8% reported meeting them “most of the time”, and 5.8% reported meeting them “sometimes” or less often; 0.9% did not respond. Likewise, 35% reported mask-wearing in indoor public places “all of the time”, 40% reported doing so “most of the time”, and 24.2% less often; 0.8% did not respond. The psychomotor vigilance task elicited reliable increases in cerebral oxygenation (HbO) at optodes 11 (F(1,61.345) = 15.975, p < .001) and 15 (F(1,49.387) = 6.346, p = .015; Table 1). Discussion

This study's findings support the conclusion that more frequent mask-wearing in daily life is not associated with lower task-related cerebral oxygenation within the prefrontal cortex. Conditional effects analyses also confirm that the pattern of findings is similar for men and women, as well as for younger and older adults. Several prior studies have examined the association between single-shot mask wearing and important parameters of brain function. The present investigation extends earlier studies examining peripheral and central nervous system function as associated with single-episode mask-wearing (Chan et al., 2020; Fischer et al., 2021; Haller et al., 2022; Klugah-Brown et al., 2022), by expanding to mask-wearing frequency in everyday life, and use of task-related changes in cerebral oxygenation via multi-channel fNIRS.

Importantly, the current study took place during the course of an active mask mandate, which produced sufficient variability in the mask wearing variable, ranging from no wearing up to daily wearing. This contextual factor allows for a stronger test of the effects of mask-wearing frequency on hypoxia than would be possible when masking mandates do not exist (i.e., during non-pandemic time periods).

Additional studies may clarify the effects of specific types of mask, as this was unmeasured in the current study. Associations between mask-wearing and task-related brain oxygenation changes may be stronger among those wearing N95 than medical grade masks, disposable or surgical grade masks. Further, prospective studies may enable better quantification of cumulative effects of increasing durations of mask-wearing on regional brain oxygenation changes over more extended periods of time and rule out reverse causality (Xie et al., 2020). Finally, there may be domains of complex decision-making that might be affected by masking under certain circumstances (Smerdon, 2022), though such effects may not be long lasting. These effects await further exploration.

One strength of the present study is the use of fNIRS methodology for quantifying task-related brain oxygen changes within subregions of the prefrontal cortex, an improvement over some prior studies which have primarily relied on imaging modalities that do not directly assess oxygen saturation within brain tissues, and therefore may not readily show impacts of masking on the most important brain outcomes. A second strength is in utilizing two ways to triangulate mask-wearing habits—both in relation to mandate following and referencing a specific high-risk situation. Finally, the measurement of the above variables during an active mask mandate is potentially critical, because of the likelihood of floor effects during times when masks are not required during daily life. From this perspective, the window of data collection for the current study was ideal for addressing our research question.

One limitation of the current study was the loss of useable optode channels due to light contamination from eye-tracking equipment used for another part of the experimental protocol. There remains the possibility that mask wearing frequency was associated with activations in some of these channels, which were located more inferior to the valid channels. However, it is worth mentioning that the only other fNIRS study examining effects of masking on brain oxygenation (Fischer et al., 2021) consisted of only 2 fNIRS channels measured on 13 participants; by comparison, the current study involved 8 valid channels measured on 78 participants. Another limitation of the current investigation is the use of self-reported mask wearing. Self-report recall of this behavior may have introduced some inaccuracy and bias in the frequency measure; this is a limitation shared by other studies of masking behavior however. Likewise, it is not clear that such inaccuracy or bias would produce stronger associations between mask wearing frequency and cerebral oxygenation.

Finally, some may argue that null associations between mask wearing frequency and cerebral oxygenation may simply reflect a floor effect attributable to lack of task-related engagement of the prefrontal cortex. However, our finding of significant task-related changes in HbO at optode 11 suggest otherwise. Our findings that the psychomotor vigilance task elicited activity in this area—corresponding to the midfrontal gyrus—are consistent with another study using a similar psychomotor vigilance task and fMRI imaging to quantify neural response (Drummond et al., 2005).

Conclusion

In summary, the current investigation found no evidence that more frequent mask-wearing in daily life is associated with lower level of cerebral oxygenation during a sustained attention task. Our findings held for younger and older participants, as well as both men and women in the sample. Public health recommendations for masking may be observed without concern around adverse effects of masking on brain oxygenation if this finding is reliable across studies and datasets.
