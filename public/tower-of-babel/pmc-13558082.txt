Beyond Bulk: Cell‐Type‐Resolved Epigenomics as the Path Forward in Alzheimer's Disease Research
Authors: Lucía Cañizares‐Moscato, Jose Ruiz‐Iglesias, Javier Isoler‐Alcaraz, Ernest Palomer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlzheimer's disease (AD) is a complex neurodegenerative disorder in which most risk variants are noncoding and are enriched at gene regulatory regions, implicating epigenetic mechanisms as central mediators of disease pathogenesis. For most of the history of AD epigenetics research, bulk tissue analysis has dominated, obscuring the fundamentally distinct epigenomic landscapes of individual brain cell types and masking cell‐type‐specific contributions to disease. Advances in single‐cell and single‐nucleus sequencing, fluorescence‐activated nuclei sorting and multiplexed epigenomic platforms have transformed this landscape, enabling cell‐type‐resolved profiling of chromatin accessibility, DNA methylation, histone modifications and transcription across the major neuronal, glial and neurovascular populations of the human brain. Here, we review these advances, structured around the argument that cell‐type resolution is not a methodological refinement but a conceptual necessity. We describe the distinct epigenomic programs disrupted in neurons, microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and neurovascular cells in AD, highlighting how each cell type responds to pathology. We discuss the discovery of epigenomic erosion, the progressive loss of cell‐type‐specific epigenomic identity across virtually all brain cell populations as AD advances, as a unifying disease mechanism linking chromatin dysregulation to cognitive decline. Finally, we identify critical gaps in current knowledge, including the near‐complete absence of cell‐type‐resolved histone modification and DNA methylation data for most brain cell types, the underrepresentation of rare populations in standard preparations and the untapped potential of metabolic acylation marks as indicators of the epigenome–metabolism interface in neurodegeneration.

Introduction: The Case for Cell‐Type Resolution

Alzheimer's disease (AD) represents the most common form of dementia, affecting millions of individuals worldwide and characterised by progressive cognitive decline, memory impairment and the accumulation of amyloid‐β (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tangles (Scheltens et al. 2021). Although over 90% of ad cases are late‐onset with complex genetic architecture, genome‐wide association studies have identified dozens of risk loci, most of which are noncoding and enriched at gene regulatory regions of the genome (Bellenguez et al. 2022). Noncoding risk variants are thought to act by altering gene expression, a process mediated by epigenetic mechanisms. The term epigenetics refers to heritable and potentially reversible modifications that regulate gene expression without altering the underlying DNA sequence (Allis and Jenuwein 2016; Janssen and Lorincz 2021). The principal layers comprise DNA methylation, the addition of a methyl group most commonly to cytosines in a CpG context, which is generally associated with transcriptional repression (Smith et al. 2024); posttranslational modifications of histone proteins (histone marks), such as acetylation, which is linked to transcriptional activation, or methylation, which, depending on the residue and degree of methylation, is associated with either transcriptional activation or repression (Allis and Jenuwein 2016; Janssen and Lorincz 2021; Smith et al. 2024). Together, these layers modulate chromatin accessibility (Klemm et al. 2019), the degree to which DNA is physically available for transcription‐factor binding and transcription, and define a cell‐type‐specific regulatory landscape that determines which genes a cell expresses. To understand how genetic risk translates into neurodegeneration, we should therefore ask not merely what the epigenome does in AD brain tissue but what it does in each specific cell type.

This question has been almost impossible to answer for most of the history of AD epigenetics research. The dominant experimental paradigm has been bulk tissue analysis, in which homogenates of brain are assayed for epigenetic marks. The resulting data represent an average across cells spanning dozens of molecularly distinct populations, but we have recently shown that excitatory neurons, inhibitory neurons, microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) and endothelial cells each possess distinct epigenomic landscapes that govern their identity (Ruiz‐Iglesias et al. 2026). These cell types do not merely differ in their baseline epigenomes; they respond to AD pathology in fundamentally different and sometimes opposing ways. When a mark increases in one population and decreases in another, bulk averaging can produce a null result that masks both changes. Also, when a cell type is selectively lost during disease progression, its disappearance masquerades in bulk data as an epigenetic change in all remaining cells. These limitations should not undermine the contributions of bulk epigenomics, which produced the first genome‐wide maps of AD‐associated chromatin variation and the analytical frameworks on which cell‐type‐resolved work now builds. The first histone acetylome‐wide association study of AD identified widespread H3K27ac variation in the entorhinal cortex enriched for AD risk variants (Marzi et al. 2018), later extended to additional acetylation marks and their transcriptional consequences (Nativio et al. 2018, 2020). Bulk approaches retain real advantages, notably larger sample sizes and greater statistical power, and greater feasibility in longitudinal and clinical cohorts. Crucially, the two are complementary rather than competing: Computational deconvolution of bulk histone data using sorted‐cell references, as implemented in CHAS, recovers cell‐type‐specific signal from existing cohorts at a scale no sorted‐cell study can currently match, and has independently replicated sorted‐cell findings in neurons, microglia and oligodendrocytes (Murphy et al. 2025). We therefore argue not for abandoning bulk epigenomics, but for adopting cell‐type resolution as the standard, with integrative strategies of this kind likely to prove optimal.

In recent years, there has been a transformation in what is technically possible. Single‐cell and single‐nucleus profiling methods now permit epigenetic characterisation of large numbers of individual cells, resolving intra‐ and inter‐cellular heterogeneity at an unprecedented scale. The emerging picture fundamentally revises our understanding of AD epigenetics. This review synthesises these advances, structured around the central argument that cell‐type resolution is not a methodological refinement but a conceptual necessity for advancing understanding of AD. We cover the technologies enabling this resolution, the key findings they have produced across epigenetic modalities, the cell‐type‐specific landscapes now emerging for each major brain cell population and the challenges that still remain.

Technologies for Cell‐Type‐Resolved Epigenomic Profiling

The explosion of cell‐type‐specific epigenetic data in AD has been enabled by rapid technological advances across a range of complementary platforms. These differ in the epigenetic modalities they measure, their throughput, their resolution and whether they require cell dissociation. Understanding their relative strengths and limitations is essential for interpreting the literature and designing future studies.

Cell Sorting Approaches

The conceptually simplest approach to cell‐type resolution is physical separation: sort cells or nuclei into pure or enriched populations, then apply any bulk epigenomic assay to the sorted fraction. Fluorescence‐activated nuclei sorting (FANS) using antibodies against nuclear markers remains a widely used and validated approach that is compatible with postmortem frozen tissue (Nott et al. 2019; Ziegler et al. 2026). The method's primary limitation is that it resolves broad cell categories rather than the fine‐grained subtypes now known to be functionally distinct, and it cannot capture intra‐cell‐type epigenomic heterogeneity. Complementing physical sorting, computational deconvolution of bulk brain histone profiles using sorted‐cell reference datasets has recently emerged as a scalable alternative. This is the case of the Cell‐type‐specific Histone Acetylation Score (CHAS) tool, which applies computational deconvolution to infer cell‐type‐specific H3K27ac signatures from bulk ChIP‐seq data, enabling retrospective cell‐type resolution of existing cohorts (Murphy et al. 2025).

Single‐Cell Sequencing Technologies

Single‐cell approaches are virtually impossible to deploy in postmortem brain samples, as it is exceedingly difficult to obtain viable dissociated cells while preserving proportional representation of all major brain cell types. Largely ramified cells, such as neurons, are particularly fragile and tend to lyse during dissociation, resulting in very low cell viability and recovery. This is less problematic for morphologically simpler cell types such as microglia or endothelial cells, but remains a fundamental limitation for neuronal populations. Moreover, single‐cell approaches cannot be applied to frozen tissue, which represents the most common and widely available source of postmortem human brain material. Therefore, single‐nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA‐seq, Figure 1) has become the standard approach for transcriptomic profiling of frozen postmortem brain samples. Recent snRNA‐seq atlases have provided cell‐type taxonomy and disease‐stage annotations against which epigenomic datasets are validated, and have identified vulnerable cell populations that epigenomic studies must prioritise. In parallel, reference atlases of the non‐diseased human brain now provide the regulatory scaffolds against which disease‐associated changes are interpreted, spanning chromatin accessibility (Y. E. Li et al. 2023), DNA methylation and 3D genome architecture (Tian et al. 2023), and, most recently, H3K27ac and repressive H3K27me3 histone modifications profiled jointly with the transcriptome across more than 750,000 nuclei (Xie et al. 2026). Notably, the Kellis laboratory generated the largest AD single‐cell multiomic atlas to date, comprising 3.5 million cells from 384 postmortem samples across six brain regions in 111 individuals (Liu et al. 2025).

Single‐cell and single‐nucleus sequencing technologies for profiling brain tissue. Brain tissue is dissociated into either single cells (sc approaches) or isolated nuclei (sn approaches), which are then processed through one of three barcoding and library preparation strategies: (a) droplet‐based encapsulation, (b) FACS sorting followed by plate‐based processing, or (c) combinatorial indexing, prior to sequencing and downstream analysis. (a) Droplet‐based methods. Cells or nuclei are co‐encapsulated with barcoded gel beads in nanolitre droplets, enabling massively parallel single‐cell processing. The stage at which droplet encapsulation occurs relative to the enzymatic reaction differs by assay: (1) snRNA‐seq: Cells/nuclei are encapsulated together with oligo‐dT–barcoded gel beads; lysis, reverse transcription, and cDNA synthesis all occur inside the droplet, capturing total RNA transcripts for transcriptional profiling.(2) snATAC‐seq: Open chromatin is first tagmented in bulk (prior to droplet formation) by hyperactive Tn5 transposase loaded with sequencing adapters. Tagmented nuclei are then encapsulated in droplets, where barcodes are ligated/incorporated to link fragments to individual nuclei, profiling chromatin accessibility. (3) scCUT&Tag: Antibody binding to the histone mark of interest and subsequent Protein A/G–Tn5 tagmentation are performed in bulk on intact nuclei before droplet encapsulation. Nuclei are then partitioned into droplets for barcoding, yielding single‐cell epigenetic (histone mark) profiles. (4) snNano‐CUT&Tag (snnCT): A multimodal extension of scCUT&Tag: Two primary antibodies (e.g., mouse and rabbit) against distinct histone marks are bound in bulk, followed by nano‐Tn5 fusion proteins (anti‐mouse/anti‐rabbit secondary nanobody–Tn5 conjugates) directing tagmentation to antibody‐bound sites, whereas free Tn5 simultaneously tags open chromatin regions. All antibody binding and tagmentation steps occur before droplet encapsulation; nuclei are subsequently encapsulated for barcoding, enabling joint profiling of multiple histone marks and open chromatin in the same cell. (b) FACS sorting + plate‐based methods. Single cells are sorted by fluorescence‐activated cell sorting into individual wells (1 cell/well) for low‐throughput but highly multi‐modal profiling: (5) scNMT‐seq: Open chromatin is first labelled in bulk via GpC methyltransferase treatment. Following GpC methylation, cells are FACS‐sorted into individual wells, followed by in‐well lysis and oligo‐dT bead‐based mRNA capture for in situ reverse transcription and RNA‐seq library preparation, whereas the DNA supernatant undergoes bisulphite conversion in a separate tube, together reading out chromatin accessibility (GpC), endogenous CpG methylation, and RNA transcripts from the same cell. (6) scCARE‐seq: Cells are crosslinked, and both the Hi‐C arm (restriction digestion and proximity ligation with a barcoded bridge adaptor to capture 3D chromatin contacts) and the RNA arm (barcoded oligo‐dT reverse transcription and second‐strand synthesis) are performed in bulk. Single cells are then FACS‐sorted into individual wells, lysed and tagmented to introduce the single‐cell barcode by PCR, jointly profiling chromatin architecture and transcription within the same cell. (c) Combinatorial indexing: (7) sci‐CAR‐seq: Nuclei undergo in situ reverse transcription, incorporating a first index, a Unique Molecular Identifier (UMI) and a poly‐T primer, and in situ Tn5 tagmentation in a first‐round indexed plate. Nuclei are then pooled and split across a second plate, where double‐stranded cDNA is synthesised, 3′‐adaptors are added to the cDNA and PCR‐based indexing introduces the plate 2 (i5/i7) indices onto both the cDNA and ATAC fractions, yielding joint chromatin accessibility and transcriptional profiling through combinatorial, pool‐split barcoding rather than physical single‐cell isolation.

Single‐nucleus Assay for Transposase‐Accessible Chromatin‐seq (snATAC‐seq; Figure 1) profiles chromatin accessibility genome‐wide in individual nuclei, enabling simultaneous cell‐type identification and open chromatin mapping without prior knowledge of cell identity. Paired snRNA‐seq and snATAC‐seq from the same samples allow direct linking of chromatin states to gene expression outputs at cell‐type resolution. Multi‐modal technologies extend this further: Single‐cell chromatin accessibility and RNA expression sequencing (sci‐CAR‐seq; Figure 1) (Cao et al. 2018) and single‐cell nucleosome, methylation and transcription sequencing (scNMT‐seq; Figure 1) (Clark et al. 2018) jointly capture chromatin accessibility and transcription or chromatin accessibility, DNA methylation and transcription, respectively. More recently, single‐cell chromatin architecture and RNA expression sequencing (scCARE‐seq; Figure 1) have added three‐dimensional chromatin architecture to this multi‐modal framework (Qu et al. 2023), enabling comprehensive characterisation of the regulatory genome in individual cells.

For histone modifications, single‐cell CUT&Tag (scCUT&Tag; Figure 1) has enabled profiling of individual marks, including H3K27ac, H3K4me3, H3K27me3 and H3K36me3 at single‐cell resolution in the mouse brain (Bartosovic et al. 2021). The more recent nano‐CUT&Tag (nCT; Figure 1) approach uses nanobody‐Tn5 fusion proteins to simultaneously profile up to three epigenomic modalities in a single cell, combining chromatin accessibility with simultaneous antibody‐based detection of any two chromatin‐associated targets (Bartosovic and Castelo‐Branco 2022). This technology demonstrated higher sensitivity than standard scCUT&Tag and introduced the concept of ‘chromatin velocity’, tracking the temporal relationship between chromatin opening and enhancer activation during differentiation (Bartosovic and Castelo‐Branco 2022). Application of nCT to human AD postmortem tissue is a key near‐term priority.

Flow Cytometry‐Based Epigenetic Profiling

Complementing sequencing‐based approaches, flow cytometry enables direct, high‐content measurement of epigenetic marks (Figure 2 ). A recent high‐dimensional spectral approach represents a significant advance in this space, enabling simultaneous quantification of 16 such marks across seven major brain cell types, including excitatory neurons, inhibitory neurons, OPCs, oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, microglia and endothelial cells (Ruiz‐Iglesias et al. 2026). This provides orthogonal validation of sequencing‐based findings while allowing millions of cells to be assayed in a short time. The inclusion of both neuronal subtypes and the full glial complement, together with the often‐overlooked endothelial population, positions this platform as a comprehensive tool for studying the brain's epigenetic landscape.

Flow cytometry‐based epigenetic profiling. High‐dimensional spectral flow cytometry enables simultaneous quantification of a 16‐parameter epigenetic panel spanning histone posttranslational modifications (H3K4, H3K9, H3K14, H3K27, H3K36 and H3K79 methylation and/or acetylation; H4K8, H4K16 and H4K20 acetylation and methylation) and DNA modifications (5‐methylcytosine, 5‐mC; 5‐hydroxymethylcytosine, 5‐hmC), distinguishing permissive and repressive methylation marks from acetylation. Isolated nuclei are co‐stained with antibodies against these epigenetic marks alongside cell‐type‐specific markers and processed in a multi‐well plate format for high‐content, high‐dimensional spectral flow cytometry acquisition. Unmixed spectral signals allow simultaneous, multiparametric readout of epigenetic marks together with cell‐type identity.

Spatial Approaches

A critical limitation of dissociation‐based single‐cell approaches is the loss of spatial information: The relationship between a cell's epigenomic and transcriptional state and its physical proximity to pathological features, such as Aβ plaques, cannot be assessed after dissociation. Spatial transcriptomics methods are beginning to address this, and spatial epigenomics is emerging as the next frontier. Miyoshi and colleagues applied spatial transcriptomics to late‐onset sporadic AD, revealing cortical layer‐preferential pathology accumulation and glial inflammatory programs dysregulated in upper cortical layers (Miyoshi et al. 2024). Emerging spatial epigenomic technologies, including spatial CUT&Tag (Deng et al. 2022), spatial CUT&Tag‐RNA‐seq (D. Zhang et al. 2023) and epigenomic Multiplexed Error‐Robust Fluorescence In Situ Hybridisation (MERFISH) (Lu et al. 2022), promise to add chromatin‐level information to these spatial maps (Figure 3). The application of these approaches to human AD tissue remains in early stages but represents a major methodological priority.

Spatially resolved epigenomic profiling technologies. (1) Spatial CUT&Tag ± RNA‐seq. Antibody‐based histone marking is performed directly on intact tissue sections, together with in situ capture of polyadenylated mRNA transcripts via reverse transcription: A primary antibody binds the histone mark of interest, followed by tagmentation via a Protein A–Tn5 fusion, whereas mRNA is reverse‐transcribed in situ in parallel. In situ CUT&Tag and reverse transcription profiling are coupled with dual cross‐channel microfluidic barcoding, in which spatial barcode X (horizontal) and barcode Y (vertical) are sequentially deposited across the tissue in a grid pattern, encoding the (x,y) position of each tagmented DNA fragment and reverse‐transcribed cDNA molecule. This yields spatially barcoded epigenome (e.g., H3K27ac) and transcriptome (mRNA) libraries, jointly profiling epigenome and transcriptome with spatial barcoding. Tissue sections are then lysed, DNA/cDNA is purified and PCR‐amplified into sequencing libraries and reads are mapped back onto the tissue section, preserving anatomical context. 2. Epigenomic MERFISH. A primary antibody against the histone mark of interest directs a Protein A–Tn5 fusion carrying a T7 promoter sequence to in situ tagment chromatin at antibody‐bound sites. The tissue is gel‐embedded and digested with proteinase K to preserve spatial structure while permeabilising the sample, after which in situ transcription with T7 RNA polymerase amplifies signal from each tagmented locus. Targeted loci are then detected through sequential rounds of multiplexed FISH: In each round, a combinatorial fluorescent barcode (e.g., a binary on/off signal across colour channels) is imaged and decoded, with each round resolving a distinct set of loci (Loci A, B, … N) across the tissue. The resulting multi‐round, multi‐loci signal is computationally decoded and mapped back onto the tissue architecture, preserving anatomical context.

In practice, spatial epigenomic methods must explicitly accommodate the physical and structural constraints of postmortem human brain tissue, which represent the primary operational bottleneck for their application in AD. Most protocols rely on thin sections to ensure adequate penetration of antibodies, transposase and barcoding reagents and are optimised for a narrow range of section thickness and tissue hardness. Applying them to human material therefore requires pre‐screening for sectionability, RNA integrity and the absence of severe autolysis or mechanical damage, together restricting analysis to cases with acceptable sample quality (Chang et al. 2026; Deng et al. 2022; Krassner et al. 2023). Such stringent selection constrains both the anatomical coverage achievable in AD tissue and the cohort sizes that can realistically be assembled, but it also identifies where methodological advances such as improved sectioning and reagent penetration would have the greatest impact.

Cell‐Type Epigenomic Landscapes in AD

The epigenomic landscape of the AD brain is not a single disease signature but a composite of cell‐type‐specific programs, each reflecting the distinct biology of the population in question. Resolving these programs across neurons, glia and neurovascular cells are the requisite for understanding which epigenetic changes drive pathology, which are reactive, and which cell types represent tractable therapeutic targets. Neuronal Vulnerability

Excitatory glutamatergic neurons are the primary cellular targets of neurofibrillary tangle formation and cell death in AD, with selective depletion in the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex preceding substantial loss in association and primary cortices (Mathys et al. 2024). These regional pattern maps correlate with the clinical progression of episodic memory impairment. Single‐nucleus transcriptomic atlases have revealed that vulnerable excitatory populations show early dysregulation of synaptic, mitochondrial and proteostasis gene programs, with the strongest transcriptional changes appearing before substantial cell loss (Mathys et al. 2019, 2024).

Critically, epigenomic changes in neurons appear to precede and likely drive transcriptional collapse, as suggested by the temporal staging of chromatin accessibility changes across AD progression (Xiong et al. 2023). This temporal staging reveals that neuronal regulatory modules show differential accessibility predominantly in early‐stage AD, whereas glial chromatin changes are more prominent at later stages, suggesting that neurons are among the earliest epigenomically vulnerable populations (Xiong et al. 2023). Complementing these accessibility findings, 3D genome profiling of neuronal‐sorted nuclei revealed widespread disruption of cis‐regulatory domains and enhancer‐promoter interactions in AD (Bendl et al. 2022). At the DNA methylation level, Li and colleagues identified over 1000 differentially methylated enhancer regions in sorted AD neurons, predominantly hypomethylated at CpH sites, with integrated transcriptomic analysis pointing to pro‐apoptotic cell cycle reactivation in post‐mitotic neurons. Notably, hypomethylation of BACE1‐regulatory enhancers coupled with increased BACE1 transcription suggests a positive epigenetic feedback loop for Aβ production (P. Li et al. 2019).

In contrast, inhibitory neurons remain comparatively understudied at cell‐type resolution. Although captured in large‐scale snATAC‐seq and single‐cell multiome datasets, inhibitory subpopulations are typically underrepresented in nuclei numbers, limiting well‐powered cell‐type‐resolved analysis (Xiong et al. 2023). Consistent with this, epigenomic information scores decline across inhibitory populations in AD, but specific disease‐associated subtypes remain poorly resolved in standard preparations (Liu et al. 2025).

Few studies have examined histone modifications at cell‐type resolution in AD neurons specifically. Most have assessed histone mark levels by immunofluorescence in NeuN+ neurons, for example, reporting increased repressive H3K9me3 levels (M. Y. Lee et al. 2020). More recently, reduced β‐hydroxybutyrylation at histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9bhb), a novel metabolic acylation mark linking ketone body metabolism to chromatin regulation, was reported in both mouse and human AD brains (Yu et al. 2026). So far, only one study has analysed histone modifications in FANS‐sorted neuronal nuclei, showing comparatively few AD‐associated H3K27ac changes relative to oligodendrocytes and microglia, with lower enrichment for AD GWAS variants, suggesting neuronal H3K27ac is not the primary driver of genetic risk (Ramamurthy et al. 2023). Computational deconvolution of large bulk H3K27ac datasets using CHAS independently confirmed these results, with neuronal H3K27ac showing the least AD‐associated variation among the profiled cell types (Murphy et al. 2025).

An important caveat is that most epigenomic datasets derive from late‐stage AD brains, by which point the most vulnerable neuronal populations may already be lost and therefore underrepresented. The relatively modest neuronal changes detected to date may thus partly reflect selective survival of less vulnerable neurons rather than a true absence of epigenomic disruption (Liu et al. 2025; Xiong et al. 2023). We therefore argue that future studies should prioritise earlier disease stages, where epigenomic changes may prove causal rather than merely consequential.

Glial Epigenetic Reprogramming

Glial cells undergo profound epigenomic remodelling in AD, but the nature of this remodelling differs fundamentally across cell types. Microglia, astrocytes and oligodendrocytes each show distinct patterns of chromatin dysregulation that reflect their divergent functional responses to pathology.

Microglia

Microglia are the primary cellular mediators of AD genetic risk. Early work established that noncoding AD risk variants are preferentially enriched in microglial enhancers, more so than in any other brain cell type, providing a cell‐type‐specific framework for subsequent disease‐focused studies (Nott et al. 2019; Novikova et al. 2021). Building on this foundation, microglia have become the most intensively profiled cell type in single‐cell and sorted‐cell epigenomic studies of AD. The emerging picture is one of profound transcriptional and chromatin heterogeneity, with microglia transitioning from homeostatic to activated and inflammatory states and ultimately reaching exhaustion, each stage marked by distinct gene regulatory signatures.

Single‐nucleus transcriptomic and chromatin accessibility studies of human postmortem brain have defined these states with increasing resolution. Sun and colleagues found that in human AD, microglial transcriptional diversity far exceeds epigenomic diversity, suggesting microglia maintain a relatively permissive chromatin landscape that permits rapid transcriptional switching without requiring large‐scale chromatin remodelling (Sun, Victor, et al. 2023). snRNA‐seq from FANS‐sorted microglia further resolved AD‐specific subclusters within the homeostatic population, which are invisible to standard unsorted approaches in human AD samples (Prater et al. 2023). At the chromatin accessibility level, snATAC‐seq identified microglial open chromatin regions as most strongly enriched for AD GWAS risk variants of any brain cell type (Morabito et al. 2021). Complementing this, chromatin accessibility and transcriptome profiling in sorted human microglia cells refined putative regulatory mechanisms at AD risk loci, demonstrating that genetic risk operates predominantly through microglial regulatory elements rather than coding variation (Kosoy et al. 2022). More recently, using single‐cell transcriptomics and spatial analyses in mouse AD models with human tissue validation, Ayata and colleagues identified a microglial subpopulation concentrated at amyloid plaques that displays lymphoid‐like gene expression and T‐cell‐like chromatin accessibility profiles. Depletion of this rare microglial population exacerbates neuroinflammation and accelerates plaque growth, positioning it as a potential immunosuppressive brake on microglial‐driven pathology (Ayata et al. 2025).

At the level of histone modifications, FANS‐sorted microglia show the strongest enrichment of AD GWAS variants in their H3K27ac landscape of any brain cell type, though paradoxically they display fewer AD‐associated differential acetylation peaks than other cell types, with microglial H3K27ac varying more strongly with age than with amyloid burden (Ramamurthy et al. 2023). Computational deconvolution of bulk H3K27ac datasets using CHAS independently confirmed microglial enrichment for AD GWAS variants across a much larger sample. At the same time, AD‐associated differential acetylation peaks showed no microglial enrichment, reinforcing the paradox that genetic risk and epigenetic dysregulation target different cell types (Murphy et al. 2025). In the metabolic‐epigenetic dimension, H4K12la, produced when lactate acts directly as a histone mark substrate, is specifically elevated in plaque‐adjacent microglia as assessed by immunofluorescence in both AD mouse models and human tissue. This mark is specifically enriched at glycolytic gene promoters and drives a positive feedback loop between glycolysis and neuroinflammation, as shown in sorted microglia from an AD mouse model (Pan et al. 2022).

Astrocytes

Astrocytes, the most abundant glial cell type in the human cortex, undergo profound reactive remodelling in AD, yet their epigenomic reprogramming remains far less characterised than that of microglia or oligodendrocytes. The available evidence is limited and largely predates genome‐wide approaches: Immunohistochemical profiling of the inferior temporal gyrus found that fewer astrocytes carried nuclear 5mC and 5hmC marks in AD than in controls, suggesting perturbed DNA methylation‐dependent chromatin regulation in this population (Phipps et al. 2016). Beyond this, astrocytes are known to display dynamic DNA methylation, histone modification and chromatin remodelling across neurological conditions (Morales Pantoja and Mintz 2026), but no cell‐type‐resolved genome‐wide epigenomic dataset has yet been reported for astrocytes in AD.

At the transcriptomic level, snRNA‐seq resolved three astrocyte subtypes and identified a reactive astrocyte module strongly associated with plaque burden, with astrocyte gene expression changes enriched for metallostasis and lipid metabolism, and reduced expression of mitochondrial energy metabolism genes in advanced AD (Mathys et al. 2024). Using FANS‐sorted astrocytes, Sadick and colleagues identified nine transcriptomically distinct astrocyte clusters in human AD prefrontal cortex. One of these clusters showed a strong upregulation of acute inflammatory markers present across all cortical layers. The remaining clusters displayed subtype‐specific cortical layer localisation (Sadick et al. 2022). Expanding this approach, Serrano‐Pozo and colleagues profiled astrocytes across five brain regions spanning the temporal progression of AD pathology using NeuN‐/OLIG2‐‐FANS enrichment followed by computational identification of astrocytes within the sorted fraction. Using this approach, they identified homeostatic, intermediate and reactive astrocyte subclusters whose proportions varied across brain regions. They also detected two temporally dynamic programmes: a trophic factor‐rich programme that declined progressively with pathology stage and a stress‐response programme that peaked at late‐stage AD before unexpectedly returning to near‐baseline at end‐stage, suggesting exhaustion of the astrocyte response under chronic neuropathological exposure (Serrano‐Pozo et al. 2024).

Oligodendrocytes

Oligodendrocytes and their progenitors are among the most transcriptomically and epigenomically altered cell types in AD, with converging evidence pointing to disruption of myelination programmes, lineage identity and chromatin organisation as core features of the disease. Across snRNA‐seq studies, oligodendrocytes consistently show downregulation of myelination‐related genes alongside the emergence of disease‐associated subpopulations characterised by immune, metabolic and apoptotic signatures (Mathys et al. 2019). Using an independent snRNA‐seq dataset, Sadick and colleagues similarly resolved subtype‐specific oligodendrocyte heterogeneity in AD, identifying disease‐associated populations enriched for cholesterol metabolism and synaptic maintenance/axonogenesis pathways (Sadick et al. 2022). Integrating snATAC‐seq with snRNA‐seq, Morabito and colleagues identified key oligodendrocyte transcription factors (NRF1 and SREBF1) whose motif activity is significantly altered in AD and linked it to cis‐regulatory networks at AD GWAS risk loci, including loci with oligodendrocyte‐specific chromatin co‐accessibility (Morabito et al. 2021). At the largest scale to date, Liu and colleagues demonstrated that oligodendrocytes and OPCs exhibit some of the most pronounced epigenomic erosion of any brain cell type across six brain regions, with repressive chromatin compartments opening and epigenomic information scores declining particularly severely in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus, the regions earliest affected by AD pathology (Liu et al. 2025; Xiong et al. 2023).

At the level of sorted cell populations, FANS enrichment of oligodendrocytes has revealed cell‐type‐specific epigenetic dysregulation in AD. Comparing bulk DNA methylation profiles to FANS‐sorted NeuN+, SOX10+ and NeuN‐/SOX10‐ nuclei populations, Shireby and colleagues demonstrated that bulk AD‐associated DNA methylation changes are predominantly driven by non‐neuronal cells, with SOX10+ oligodendrocyte‐enriched nuclei showing intermediate concordance with the bulk signal, thus contributing to, but not primarily accounting for, the non‐neuronal methylation dysregulation in AD (Shireby et al. 2022). Complementing this study, H3K27ac ChIP‐seq of FANS‐sorted oligodendrocyte‐enriched nuclei revealed that the oligodendrocytes harbour the majority of AD‐associated differentially acetylated peaks, which are located near AD risk genes and myelination‐associated gene regulatory elements (Ramamurthy et al. 2023). Computational deconvolution of bulk H3K27ac datasets using CHAS independently confirmed this oligodendrocyte predominance of AD‐associated differential acetylation on a much larger scale, further strengthening the case that H3K27ac dysregulation in AD is primarily an oligodendrocyte phenomenon (Murphy et al. 2025). Together, these findings position oligodendrocyte epigenomic dysregulation as a prominent and underappreciated feature of AD, driven by both disruption of myelination genes and broader chromatin instability.

Neurovascular Unit

The neurovascular unit (NVU), comprising brain endothelial cells, pericytes, smooth muscle cells, perivascular fibroblasts and astrocytic endfeet, represents a critically undercharacterised cellular compartment in AD epigenomics, as vascular and mural cells are severely underrepresented in standard nuclear isolation protocols. At the transcriptomic level, dedicated vascular snRNA‐seq studies have established that the cerebrovasculature undergoes extensive cell‐type‐specific dysregulation in AD, with pericytes and endothelial cells showing distinct transcriptional changes including loss of blood–brain barrier transport function, upregulation of inflammatory and insulin signalling programmes, and selective vulnerability of extracellular matrix (ECM)‐organising pericyte subtypes (Sun, Akay, et al. 2023; A. C. Yang et al. 2022). Coordinated neurovascular–glial–neuronal co‐expression modules further suggest that NVU dysfunction does not occur in isolation but is coupled to the broader transcriptional reprogramming of the AD brain (Sun, Akay, et al. 2023). At the epigenomic level, progress has been slower. Large‐scale snATAC‐seq studies capture vascular cells as a computationally identifiable population, but their extreme underrepresentation precludes meaningful chromatin accessibility analysis of NVU cell types in AD case–control comparisons (Liu et al. 2025; Morabito et al. 2021). Profiling active histone marks in FANS‐sorted cell types from non‐demented tissue, Ziegler and colleagues showed that AD genetic heritability is concentrated in microglial enhancers, with only modest enrichment in endothelial regulatory regions, whereas cerebral small vessel disease heritability was distributed across the full NVU, defining a disease‐specific partition of genetic risk between the two most prevalent dementias (Ziegler et al. 2026).

Recent work has begun to address the vascular epigenome directly. Reid and colleagues developed MultiVINE‐seq, a single‐nucleus multi‐omic platform that jointly profiles chromatin accessibility and RNA in vascular, perivascular and immune cells from postmortem human brain, and used it to link AD‐associated variants to cell‐type‐specific regulatory programmes in endothelial, mural, fibroblast and immune populations (Reid et al. 2025). Nevertheless, comprehensive AD case–control epigenomic profiling of sorted neurovascular cells remains limited, and direct comparisons across chromatin accessibility, histone modification and DNA methylation are still lacking.

Epigenomic Erosion: A Unifying Disease Framework in AD

The concept of epigenomic erosion emerged progressively across model systems before being demonstrated in human brain tissue. We use the term to denote the progressive degradation of epigenomic identity, defined here as the cell‐type‐specific configuration of chromatin marks, accessibility and methylation that distinguishes one cell type from another. Erosion refers to the phenomenon itself; its causal status is considered separately below. Early work by Mertens and colleagues showed that direct conversion of fibroblasts into induced neurons preserves age‐associated signatures that are largely erased when cells are reprogrammed through an iPSC intermediate, establishing these cells as a tractable model of age‐dependent neuronal states (Mertens et al. 2015). In a follow‐up study, fibroblasts from individuals with AD gave rise to induced neurons displaying an aberrant age‐dependent state consistent with loss of neuronal identity and epigenomic erosion (Mertens et al. 2021). This framework was later extended to human postmortem tissue by the Kellis and Tsai laboratories. Xiong and colleagues first demonstrated the phenomenon in late‐stage AD brain, showing a global shift in chromatin accessibility in which cells progressively lose their cell‐type‐specific identity (Xiong et al. 2023); Liu and colleagues then quantified it, introducing the epigenomic information score as a single‐cell quantitative measure of how well an individual cell maintains its expected epigenomic identity and demonstrating its decline across virtually all brain cell types as AD advances, most severely in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus and in cell types associated with AD vulnerability (Liu et al. 2025). Erosion manifests as simultaneous activation of repressive and silencing of active chromatin regions, disrupting cell‐type‐specific architecture rather than producing coherent directional changes, thereby reconciling apparently contradictory findings across the AD epigenetics literature. Crucially, individuals who maintained cognitive function despite substantial pathological burden showed relatively preserved epigenomic information scores, suggesting that the epigenome may determine whether pathological changes translate into clinical symptoms. A comparable trajectory is evident transcriptionally, where microglia and astrocytes show activation followed by exhaustion, initial reactive states giving way to terminal loss of functional identity under chronic pathological exposure (Liu et al. 2025; Serrano‐Pozo et al. 2024; Sun, Victor, et al. 2023).

Although we highlight epigenomic erosion as a potentially unifying mechanism in AD, the concept still requires careful contextualisation and further experimental support. First, available data indicate that loss of cell‐type‐specific epigenomic identity is not unique to AD: Age‐associated decay of heterochromatin and of cell‐type‐specific chromatin states has been described in other systems and in normal ageing, suggesting that AD may represent an extreme or accelerated manifestation of more general epigenetic ageing processes rather than a wholly disease‐specific phenomenon (S. Lee et al. 2026; J. H. Yang et al. 2023; Y. Zhang et al. 2022). Second, single‐cell epigenomic studies report broadly concordant patterns of epigenomic relaxation and declining epigenomic information scores across multiple brain regions and cell types, but the quantitative magnitude and cell‐type specificity of erosion vary between datasets, and the available human evidence derives largely from overlapping cohorts profiled by chromatin accessibility, underscoring the need for replication in more diverse populations and across orthogonal modalities (Liu et al. 2025; Xiong et al. 2023). Third, some apparent erosion could reflect shifts in cell‐state composition, such as depletion of vulnerable neurons or expansion of activated glial states, rather than intrinsic loss of epigenomic identity within surviving cells; disentangling true within‐cell erosion from compositional change will require further experimental data (Liu et al. 2025). Finally, although erosion is strongly associated with AD pathology burden and cognitive decline, current evidence is largely correlative; direct tests of causality will depend on experimental manipulations that either exacerbate or restore epigenomic identity in defined cell types and assess the consequent effects on neuronal function and behaviour (Liu et al. 2025; Xiong et al. 2023). For these reasons, we regard epigenomic erosion as a compelling integrative framework for organising existing data, but not yet as a fully proven causal mechanism.

Challenges and Future Directions

Despite rapid progress, several critical gaps remain. Standard snRNA‐seq and snATAC‐seq preparations fail to capture rare cell populations in sufficient numbers for meaningful analysis, a problem that affects neurovascular cells, specific inhibitory neuron subtypes and other minority populations. Addressing this limitation requires dedicated enrichment or sorting strategies that increase the number of target nuclei before sequencing. A related challenge is that each major cell type encompasses multiple disease‐ and ageing‐associated sub‐states: Microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and neurovascular cells all follow distinct trajectories from homeostatic to activated, reactive or exhausted, with epigenomic features that are lost when a population is treated as a single homogeneous class. Future work will therefore need not only to expand coverage of rare cell types but also to stratify within‐cell‐type sub‐states along disease and ageing axes, using targeted enrichment and analytic frameworks capable of resolving these fine‐grained programmes. Beyond chromatin accessibility, cell‐type‐resolved epigenomic data across other modalities remain severely limited. DNA methylation has been profiled in a small number of sorted populations, but is predominantly measured by bisulphite, which cannot distinguish 5‐methylcytosine from 5‐hydroxymethylcytosine, leaving the functional interpretation of many AD‐associated methylation changes ambiguous. Classical histone modifications beyond H3K27ac/me3 remain almost entirely uncharacterised at cell‐type resolution in AD, representing a fundamental gap in our understanding of each cell type's epigenomic regulatory state. Metabolic acylation marks, including lactylation, crotonylation, or β‐hydroxybutyrylation, are functionally relevant but are essentially absent from AD cell‐type‐resolved profiling (Qin et al. 2024). New platforms capable of measuring multiple epigenetic marks simultaneously at the single‐cell level, including high‐dimensional flow cytometry, offer a complementary route to resolve some of these gaps, while emerging multimodal sequencing approaches that jointly profile several histone modifications in the same nucleus, ideally at single‐nucleus resolution, will be needed to generate the full epigenomic landscape of each cell type across AD progression.

Conclusion

The transition from bulk to cell‐type‐resolved epigenomics has not merely refined our understanding of AD, but fundamentally reshaped it. These approaches have revealed that the dominant signals in bulk tissue reflect glial rather than neuronal biology. They also showed that each major brain cell type undergoes a distinct and partially independent epigenomic response to pathology and that the progressive loss of epigenomic identity across cell types may represent a unifying framework linking chromatin dysregulation to cognitive decline. The tools to address the remaining gaps now exist. What the field requires is their systematic application across the full cellular diversity of the AD brain, across disease stages and across the full repertoire of epigenetic marks, from canonical histone modifications and DNA methylation to the metabolic acylations that couple cellular energetics to chromatin state. Cell‐type epigenomics is no longer a specialised subfield, it is the necessary foundation for mechanistic understanding and therapeutic progress in Alzheimer's disease.
