Characterizing Comorbidities in Total Joint Arthroplasty in a Safety-Net Hospital Using Age-Adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index
Authors: Matthew Chan, Lily Khabie, Noshin Nuzhat, Melanie Anaya, Gabriela de Queiroz Campos, Ira Kirschenbaum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The joint arthroplasty population has a high number of comorbidities at the time of surgery, which increases the cost of care per episode. The goal of this study was to analyze the distribution of comorbid diseases in patients who have undergone total joint arthroplasty at our institution, which serves the poorest congressional district in the United States. We hypothesized that patients in an urban safety-net hospital would have higher comorbidity rates and higher Charlson Comorbidity Indices and that this would suggest the need for improved risk stratification in payment systems.Methods:A retrospective analysis was conducted by reviewing consecutive patients who underwent total joint arthroplasty between 2008 and 2023 (N = 3011). Relevant comorbidities were used to calculate the age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index. From this, we determined the score distribution and identified the most frequent conditions. Descriptive statistical analysis was conducted to determine the mean and variance.Results:Within the patient population concerning CCI scores, 10.93% of the patient population scored 0, 22.42% scored 1, 27.33% scored 2, 20.86% scored 3, 10.73% scored 4, and 7.74% scored 5 and above. The mean CCI score was 2.25 ± 1.50. The most frequently occurring comorbid conditions according to hospital criteria in this population were hypertension (N = 2078, 69.01%), obesity (N = 1366, 45.37%), diabetes (N = 906, 30.09%), and anemia (N = 694, 23.05%). A total of 19.10% of patients were current smokers. Of all TJA patients, 96.9% had at least 1 comorbidity.Conclusion:The major finding from this retrospective analysis revealed the presence of a high incidence of comorbidities in the our health system's total joint arthroplasty population. We confirmed that this closed urban population is a unique subset of at-risk individuals who are undergoing TJA, which may increase their complication rates. Consideration for proper risk stratification in communities like this need to be considered.

Total joint arthroplasty (TJA) is a frequently performed orthopaedic procedure in the United States, making TJA outcomes and cost efficiency important to hospitals and physicians.1 The rapid increase in the number of TJA procedures performed per year corresponds to an increased yearly cost to the United States' healthcare system. One way to decrease the costs of these procedures is to incentivize physicians to increase quality and decrease the cost and complications of each episode. For example, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services have adopted the Bundled Payment for Care Improvement Initiative to incentivize physicians and hospitals to decrease their costs.2

The TJA population can have a high number of comorbidities at the time of surgery, which can greatly affect the surgical outcomes. The presence of multiple comorbid diseases can increase a patient's length of stay and lead to greater utilization of resources after a total knee arthroplasty.3 It may also delay diagnosis, alter treatment, lead to complications, influence survival, and confound the analysis of outcomes.4 The association of preoperative comorbidities increases the risk of postoperative complications, as well as the total hospital cost per episode of care.5,6

The goal of this study was to analyze the distribution of comorbid diseases in patients who have undergone total joint arthroplasty at our institution, which serves the poorest congressional district in the United States. This area represents a relatively “closed urban setting.” We define this as such because the hospital is the main referral source in the district, and most patients remain in the district for care. We hypothesized that patients in this large, closed urban setting have a high incidence of comorbidities, calculated as both the total number of conditions and the use of the age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). If this hypothesis is true, there should be a shift in the risk-adjusted payment structures to account for the actual resources used as a result of preexisting comorbidities.

Methods

A retrospective analysis was conducted by reviewing consecutive patients who underwent total joint arthroplasty at our health system between 2008 and 2023 (N = 3011). All procedures were performed by four fellowship-trained joint arthroplasty surgeons. The total number of conditions per patient was determined using the department's research database, which catalogs additional comorbidities and risk factors common in our particular patient population proven to increase the risk of complications such as obesity, anemia, or smoking status, but does not weigh the comorbidities by severity. In addition, the relevant comorbidities were used to calculate the age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), which includes fewer comorbidities but weights them based on severity. CCI is a valid and reliable tool that classifies comorbidity by scoring 19 comorbid conditions based on the severity of each condition.5 From this, we determined the score distribution, mean, and variance. Statistical analysis was conducted using MATLAB (version 2023a; The MathWorks inc). Furthermore, the highest incidence comorbidities were identified.

Results

Within the patient population concerning CCI scores, 10.93% of the patient population scored 0, 22.42% scored 1, 27.33% scored 2, 20.86% scored 3, 10.73% scored 4, and 7.74% scored 5 and above (Figure 1). The mean CCI score was 2.25 ± 1.50. The most frequently occurring comorbid conditions according to hospital criteria in this population were hypertension (N = 2078, 69.01%), obesity (N = 1366, 45.37%), diabetes (N = 906, 30.09%), and anemia (N = 694, 23.05%). In total, 19.10% of patients were current smokers. Of all TJA patients, 96.9% had at least 1 comorbidity.

Discussion

The major finding from this retrospective analysis uncovered the presence of a high incidence of comorbidities in the total joint arthroplasty patient population. Total joint arthroplasty is a common procedure performed worldwide, and it is well known that comorbidities can influence outcomes. Patients undergoing joint arthroplasty surgery have a higher rate of comorbidities compared with the general population: In a study by Lan et al,7 83.7% of patients who underwent TKA and THA had at least 1 comorbidity. Furthermore, safety-net hospitals serve a patient population of lower socioeconomic status and an increased rate of comorbidities.5 The present study found that 96.9% of the patients in our safety-net hospital population had at least 1 comorbidity.

We further quantified our patient population's comorbidity by calculating their age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Scores, which is used to predict mortality risk based on weighted comorbid conditions and patient age. CCI has been commonly used in TJA studies to measure the health of patients and stratify risk of infection and revision surgeries. For example, analysis of the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) American Joint Replacement Registry for primary TJA from 2018 to 2020 demonstrated an overall nationwide CCI score of 0.48 ± 1.06.8 A similar study done by McCoy et al using a private healthcare system TJA database showed a mean score of 1.05 ± 1.58.9 In comparison, our patient population had a mean CCI score of 2.25, which is more than quadruple the comorbidity rate of the AAOS registry and double that in the study by McCoy et al.

Furthermore, certain comorbidities pose greater risk in TJA operations, such as diabetes, obesity, and smoking. Diabetes mellitus (DM) has been a well-established risk factor of complications after elective THA or TKA, including infection, longer lengths of stay, and pneumonia.10 Prior large, observational studies of the TKA patient population have estimated rates of diabetes from 8.55% to 21.70%.11,12 By contrast, the prevalence of diabetes in our TJA population was measured to be 30.09%. Similarly, we found that 19.10% of our patients were current smokers, compared with larger and broader TJA population studies that found a rate of 11% of TJA smokers.13 It is important to note that our protocol is to have the patients stop smoking six weeks before surgery. Finally, 45.37% of our patients had obesity, compared with estimates of 11.5% in the broader TKA population.11

Higher rates of comorbidities such as the conditions we reported result in longer lengths of stay, led to postoperative complications, and are associated with total hospital cost per episode of care for TJA patients.3–6 We confirmed that this closed urban population is a unique subset of at-risk individuals who are undergoing TJA. Therefore, risk adjustments should be made for safety-net hospitals to compensate for the increase in cost per episode of care, which is a consequence of the high comorbidity rates of the underserved population.

It is critical that joint arthroplasty centers properly assess the pooled severity of comorbid conditions in their patient populations for two main reasons. First, the awareness of increased comorbidities dictates the care delivery process required to achieve quality outcomes. Spotlighting certain risk-associated comorbidities that were previously unknown also allowed the enactment of special protocols. Second, this information is useful for calculating the total cost of procedures used for value-based healthcare payments.

As total joint arthroplasty becomes even more common, gaining a better understanding of the severity of pooled comorbidities within a hospital's unique patient population can lead to a more accurate assessment for developing efficacious payment systems adopted for the procedure.14 A study by Losina and Katz found that operating on patients with fewer comorbidities was more cost effective.15 It has been reported that bundled payments for total joint arthroplasties are promising for reducing costs and improving quality but require additional attention and risk adjustment of complexities for patients with high comorbidity burdens.16

In our opinion, it is critical that the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Services and commercial payors include these risk adjustments for pooled comorbidities so that hospitals with medically complex patients, such as our health system and other safety-net hospitals, are properly compensated.17 This can be implemented through a region-based pricing system and possibly through subregion-based pricing. In a study conducted by Ellimoottil et al., which analyzed the effect of region-based target pricing, it was found that risk adjustment does affect the cost of care per episode. Specifically, region-based pricing led to reduced reconciliation with hospitals treating medically complex patients, and reconciliation payments were markedly higher for hospitals treating more complex patients.18 This shows that hospitals that treat medically complex patients would benefit from a risk-adjusted payment system.

Because our study showed a high number of comorbidities and high CCI values, pooled comorbidity information can be used to better inform bundled payments for joint arthroplasty. This is based on the severity of comorbidities in the population served by the center. Our suggestion is that joint arthroplasty centers go through the exercise described and conducted to understand the severity of the pooled comorbidities in their patients. Not only is it important for these surgical centers to be more aware of the health problems present in their patients but they should also use this information to better assess the total cost of procedures, which could be helpful in reimbursement contracting.

This study had several limitations. One of our study's limitations is that we did not report on the severity of certain comorbidities, such as perioperative A1C levels for diabetes or BMI for obesity. Therefore, this can lead to a variation in the complication rates of each patient and for each subset of comorbidities. In future studies, we can track the severity of each comorbidity and its effect on the cost of the total episodes of care. This can provide further insight into how best to structure payment reimbursements for patients with TJA with certain comorbidities. In addition, we used one type of index to characterize patient comorbidities. In future studies, additional methods can be used to characterize patient comorbidities, such as the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, Vanderbilt Risk Adjustment Index, and Functional Status Index, to further validate our data. Furthermore, we did not include data on the cost of care per episode for each patient and the comorbidities that had the highest costs. In future studies, this information could allow us to propose a system in which the risk is adjusted based on the type of comorbidity.

Conclusion

The major finding from this retrospective analysis revealed the presence of an incidence of comorbidities in our total joint arthroplasty population higher than previous nationwide studies of comorbidities. We confirmed that this closed urban population is a unique subset of at-risk individuals who are undergoing TJA, which may increase their complication rates. Proper risk stratification in communities like this need to be considered.
