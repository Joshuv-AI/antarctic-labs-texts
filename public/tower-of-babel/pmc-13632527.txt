Identification of PfSR1-MT as melatonin-responsive GPCR-like candidate from the human malaria parasite Plasmodium falciparum
Authors: Maneesh Kumar Singh, Barbara K M Dias, Waheed Ahmed, Juliana dos Santos Oliveira, Erika Cecon, Florence Gbahou, Taffarel Melo Torres, Euzébio Guimarães, Ludivine Houzé, Abhinab Mohanty, Myna Nakabashi, Jeimy AC Vélez, Vitor EMA Giostri, Marcio Weber Paixão, Bernard Masri, Ralf Jockers, Celia R S Garcia

ABSTRACT
Highlights•P. falciparum receptor SR1-MT is heterologously expressed on the surface of HEK293T cells.•PfSR1-MT binds melatonin with nanomolar affinity.•Melatonin binding results in a conformational change in the N-terminus of PfSR1-MT.•Melatonin increases cytosolic calcium in cells expressing PfSR1-MT.•Luzindole blocks the cytosolic calcium increase in response to melatonin.Keywords: Plasmodium falciparum, Malaria, GPCR (G protein-coupled receptor), PfSR1-MT, Melatonin

Introduction

Malaria, the vector-borne parasitic disease caused by Plasmodium parasite, is still a major public health problem, affecting millions of people annually around the world, especially in the tropics. In 2024, the WHO estimated 282 million cases of malaria with 610 deaths reported, with most of the fatalities resulting from infection by P. falciparum (World Health Organization, 2025). Although efforts to combat the disease resulted in the development of two recent vaccines recommended by the WHO for children (Datoo et al., 2024; Theodoridis and Carvalho, 2025), multiple doses are necessary to achieve low efficiency; thus, the disease treatment is mainly based on chemotherapy with artemisinin-based therapy (World Health Organization, 2025). The major challenge in combating malaria is the emergence of parasites resistant to antimalarials from all available chemical classes and decreased susceptibility to artemisinin derivatives (Blasco et al., 2017; Okombo and Fidock, 2025), pointing to the necessity of identifying new targets for developing antimalarials.

The symptomatic stage of malaria infection occurs when asexual parasites develop in red blood cells (Cowman et al., 2016). Most species of Plasmodium coordinate their rhythms with the host's rhythms, with parasites developing synchronously within red blood cells and simultaneously bursting the infected erythrocytes, releasing thousands of merozoites into the bloodstream (Mideo et al., 2013). This synchronous rupture of red blood cells occurs in a circadian fashion, since the duration of parasite intraerythrocytic cycle is multiples of 24 h, according to the species (Mideo et al., 2013; Reece et al., 2017), originating the tertian and quartan fevers characteristic of the disease (Garcia et al., 2001).

The synchronous development and simultaneous rupture of infected erythrocytes benefit the parasite, assuring the infection progression by evading the host immune system (Cabral et al., 2019), and misalignment between parasite and host rythms affects parasite development in vivo (O’Donnell et al., 2011; Carvalho Cabral et al., 2024). Thus, elucidating the mechanisms underlying synchronous parasite development is crucial for dissecting parasite biology, understanding parasite-host interactions, and identifying new interventions to combat malaria.

Although no clock-related genes were identified in the malaria parasite genome, recent advances have shown that the parasite possesses an intrinsic clock (Rijo-Ferreira et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2020), and a host cue is essential to entrain this clock and keep parasite synchronicity and coordination with the host (Lambros and Vanderberg, 1979; Rijo-Ferreira et al., 2020). Studies from different groups, including ours, point towards various factors that could govern parasite synchrony, namely temperature (Kwiatkowski, 1989), the timing of host feeding (Prior et al., 2018), the rhythm of TNFα and food intake (Hirako et al., 2018), and the host hormone melatonin(Hotta et al., 2000).

The host hormone melatonin (MLT) plays a key role in synchronizing Plasmodium asexual development via PLC-IP3-mediated Ca2+ mobilization (Hotta et al., 2000). MLT influences the circadian rhythm of both the human malaria parasite Plasmodium falciparum and the rodent parasite Plasmodium chabaudi by increasing parasitemia and promoting maturation of parasites and increasing the percentage of parasites in the more mature stages of the intraerythrocytic cycle (Hotta et al., 2000). Data from in vitro and in vivo studies show a loss of the synchronous intraerythrocytic developmental cycle when MLT is absent (Hotta et al., 2000). Disrupting MLT production in P. chabaudi-infected mice, or blocking its action with the melatonin receptor antagonist luzindole (LUZ), severely disrupts parasite synchronization, reducing the efficiency of the parasite’s life cycle progression, leading to lower parasitemia (Hotta et al., 2000).

In vitro treatment of P. falciparum with MLT at the throphozoite stage increases cytosolic calcium concentrations ([Ca2+cyt]) by activating phospholipase C (PLC), increasing inositol triphosphate (IP3) levels, and promoting Ca2+ release from the endoplasmic reticulum (Alves et al., 2011; Hotta et al., 2000). Similarly, MLT activates the cAMP/PKA pathway in P. falciparum under these conditions in a PLC-dependent manner, indicating a crosstalk between Ca2+ and cAMP pathways in response to MLT in the parasite (Beraldo et al., 2005). Moreover, in addition to PKA, other kinases were associated with MLT signaling in Plasmodium. The knockout of the P. falciparum kinase PfeIK1 (Dias et al., 2020), involved in protein translation, and the atypical protein kinase PfPK7 (Koyama et al., 2012) abolishes the synchronization effect of melatonin in parasite cultures. Other downstream effects of MLT in the parasites have been shown, such as the transcriptional regulation of a subset of ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) genes(Koyama et al., 2014), mitochondria fission genes (fis1, dyn1, and dyn2) (Scarpelli et al., 2019), and the σ70-like sigma factor ApSigma (Kobayashi, 2023).

In mammals, MLT signals through two 7-transmembrane (7TM)-spanning G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), called MT1 and MT2 (Okamoto et al., 2024). Both receptors modulate the activation of several downstream effectors, including adenylyl and guanylyl cyclases, phospholipase C, and Ca2+/K+ion channels, via heterotrimeric G proteins (Cecon et al., 2018).

In P. falciparum, we have identified four 7TM GPCR-like candidates (PfSR1, PfSR10, PfSR12, PfSR25) using bioinformatic tools (Madeira et al., 2008). All four proteins are differentially expressed in the P. falciparum asexual cycle. PfSR25 turned out to be a monovalent cation sensor that modulates Ca2+ signaling upon sensing a shift in K+ concentration (Moraes et al., 2017), and its knockout is more susceptible to antimalarial drugs (Santos et al., 2021). PfSR10 was also shown to be essential for asexual development and gametocytogenesis (Njila Tchoufack et al., 2020) and to assist the parasites in coordinating with the daily host rhythm (Subudhi et al., 2020). Recently, bradykinin and some antimalarial drugs were proposed as ligands for PfSR10 (Jiang et al., 2025). When expressed in mammalian cells, PfSR12 enhances the trafficking of host GPCRs, and the structure predicted by Alphafold revealed similarities with the GOLgi Dynamics (GOLD)-seven transmembrane helix protein family (Pereira et al., 2020). Not much is known about PfSR1 other than its expression during the parasite’s red blood cell cycle, with lower expression in earlier stages (ring and early trophozoite) and higher expression in more mature stages (late trophozoite and schizonts) (Madeira et al., 2008).

In this study, we investigated the ability of PfSR1 (PF3D7_1131100) and PfSR10 (PF3D7_1215900) to bind the host hormone MLT, as well as the functional properties of these receptors, using a heterologous expression system. We identified PfSR1 as an MLT receptor and therefore refer to it as PfSR1-MT hereafter.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and transfection

Human Embryonic Kidney 293T (HEK293T) cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Media (DMEM) Glutamax (Invitrogen) supplemented with 4.5 g/L glucose, 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin, at 37 °C (95% O2, 5% CO2).

Codon-optimized PfSR1-MT and PfSR10 fused to either N-ter HiBiT and SNAP or to C-ter SmBiT, HiBiT, Nluc, and YFP containing plasmids were transfected using jetPEI reagent according to the supplier’s instructions (Polyplus-transfection, New York, NY, USA) in a proportion of 3 µL per 1 µg of DNA directly in culture media. Cells were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C before luminescence, or BRET experiments were performed.

Immunoblotting

Cells expressing Flag-PfSR1-MT and Flag-PfSR10, (1 µg each) for 24 h, 48 h and 72 h were collected and lysed in Laemmli buffer (62.5 mmol/L Tris/HCl pH 6.8, 5% SDS, 10% glycerol, 0.005% bromophenol blue and 50 mM DTT). Proteins were resolved in 10% SDS-PAGE gel, followed by protein transfer to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat dried milk in TBS (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 150 mM NaCl), and immunoblotted with primary antibodies against the Flag tag (rabbit, dilution 1:1000, cat. F7425; Sigma), diluted in 0.3% BSA in TBS (overnight, 4 °C), followed by incubation with secondary antibodies coupled to 800 nm fluorophores (1:15,000 dilution in 0.3% nonfat dried milk in TBST (TBS with 1% Tween 20), 1 hr at room temperature; LI‐COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA). Membranes were read using the Odyssey LI‐COR infrared fluorescent scanner (LI‐COR Biosciences).

Immunofluorescence assay

HEK293T cells transiently expressing SNAP tag containing PfSR1-MT, PfSR10, and hMT2 were seeded onto sterile poly-L-lysine-coated 24-well format cover glass one day after transfection. Cells were washed, fixed with paraformaldehyde 4% solution (15 min), and blocked for 1 h with 5% BSA. Cells were then incubated with SNAP Green Total (Revvity) for 2 h on ice. Cells were washed, and nuclei were stained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich), then the cover glass was mounted onto glass slides. Images were acquired using a Leica DMI6000 inverted microscope and analyzed by open-source Fiji software.

Total and surface expression measurement

To determine the expression of PfSR1-MT and PfSR10, both genes were tagged with SNAP and HiBiT at the N-terminus. SNAP is an O6-alkylguanine-DNA alkyl transferase that catalyzes its own covalent binding to fluorescent derivatives of benzylguanine such as Lumi4-Tb (Keppler et al., 2003). Similarly, HiBiT is an 11-amino acid-long epitope tag and is a split luciferase component. After complementation with another split Nluc fragment LgBiT, a bright luminescent light is produced in the presence of the substrate furimazine.

For surface expression, HEK293T cells were transfected with 100, 300, 500, or 1000 ng of HiBiT-tagged PfSR1-MT or PfSR10 for 48 h, and the assay was performed using the Nano-Glo HiBiT Extracellular Detection System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For total expression, 0.01% digitonin was used at the final concentration.

For a synergistic effect, cotransfection of SNAP and HiBiT epitope-tagged PfSR1-MT (HiBiT-PfSR1-MT – 500 ng; SNAP-SR10 – 300, 500 ng) and PfSR10 (HiBiT-PfSR10 – 300 ng; SNAP-SR1-MT – 300, 500 ng) was performed for 48 h. Expression was detected with Nano-Glo HiBiT Extracellular Detection System (Promega). Luminescence was read on Infinite F500 Tecan (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). The percentage of cell-surface expression was calculated as the luminescence ratio of surface to total. Similarly, fold-change expression was calculated as the luminescence ratio of HiBiT (PfSR1-MT or PfSR10) to the coexpressed values. Data are presented as means ± SEM from three independent experiments, each performed in triplicate.

The ELISA test was used to determine the total and cell-surface expression of SNAP- tagged PfSR1-MT WT and mutant receptors. Briefly, 48 h after transfection in HEK293T cells (1 µg of each receptor), cells were fixed with PFA 2% (surface) or Methanol/Acetone mix (total) for 12 and 1 min respectively, saturated 60 min with BSA 3% (surface) ± 0.2% triton x100 (total) then incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-flag antibody (1/50 000) (Sigma A8592, Saint Louis, Missouri, USA). After adding the substrate (HRP Luminata Forte, Millipore, Molsheim, France), the plate was read using a Mithras LB940 instrument (Berthold, Bad Wildbad, Germany) and filters with the appropriate bandpass.

Complementation assay

For luciferase complementation assays with PfSR1, 100 ng PfSR1-MT-LgBiT was cotransfected with PfSR1-MT -SmBiT (300, 500 & 900 ng) or PfSR10-SmBiT (200, 300 & 500 ng). Similarly, for PfSR10 complementation, 25 ng PfSR10-LgBiT was cotransfected with PfSR1-MT-SmBiT (250, 500 & 750 ng) or PfSR10-SmBiT (100, 250 & 500 ng). Positive control hMT1-LgBiT (2.5 ng) and hMT1-SmBiT (2.5 ng) were also transfected.

Similarly, a saturation curve was obtained where 100 ng PfSR1-MT was cotransfected with PfSR1-MT-SmBiT (100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600, 700, 800, 900 ng) or 25 ng PfSR10 with PfSR10-SmBiT (25, 50, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 750, 975 ng). After 24 h, cells were seeded to poly-L-lysine-coated white P96 wells plate and incubated for 24 h in a CO2 incubator. The following day, cells were washed with DPBS buffer (Gibco, pH 7.4) and incubated for 5 min with 5 µM of coelenterazine h (CLZN-h). Luminescence was measured in kinetics mode using a spectrophotometer (Tecan plate reader). Data are presented as means ± SEM from three independent experiments, each performed in triplicate.

Nluc complementation and BRET assay

For BRET assays, cells were cotransfected with 20 ng PfSR1-MT-Nluc with PfSR1-MT-YFP (400, 600, 800 ng) and PfSR10-YFP (200, 300, 400 ng). Similarly, 5 ng PfSR10-Nluc with PfSR10-YFP (150, 200, 250 ng) and PfSR1-YFP (500, 750, 1000 ng). After 24 h, cells were seeded in a poly-L-lysine-coated white 96-well plate and placed in the CO2 incubator for another 24 h. Before reading, the media was removed, washed once with DPBS (Gibco), and replaced with DPBS. 5 µM CLZN-h was added and incubated for 5 min on the shaker before initiating BRET measurements using a spectrophotometer (Tecan plate reader) for multiple cycles.

For the N-ter saturation curve, 200 ng HiBiT-PfSR10 was transfected with SNAP-PfSR10 (5, 12.5, 25, 100, 200, 400, 600, and 800 ng). After 24 h, cells were seeded in a poly-L-lysine-coated white 96-well plate and placed in the CO2 incubator for another 24 h. Before reading, the media was removed, washed once with DPBS (Gibco), and labeled with cell-impermeant SNAP-488 (NEB) on ice for 1 h, followed by luciferase complementation using the Nano-Glo HiBiT Extracellular Detection System (Promega) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Luminescence and fluorescence signals were read over several cycles to allow the enzyme/substrate reaction to reach a plateau using the Tecan plate reader. The data acquired at the plateau phase (at 10 min after the addition of the substrate) were used for all graphs unless otherwise stated.

BRET values were evaluated by rationing the fluorescence of the acceptor over the luminescence of the donor. Statistical values were obtained from three independent experiments and data are presented as means ± SEM.

Membrane preparations and 2-[125I]-radioligand binding assay

Cells were transfected with SNAP-PfSR1-MT or SNAP-PfSR10 plasmids for 48 h, and the membranes were prepared using standard lab protocol (Jockers and Cecon, 2022). Briefly, cells were harvested and lysed with a Polytron, followed by high-speed centrifugation at 33500 g for 45 min at 4 °C; membranes were collected. The competition-binding assay was performed by simultaneous incubation of 2-[125I]-iodomelatonin (500 pM for PfSR1 or 100 pM for hMT1) with increasing concentrations of MLT or LUZ. The saturation curve was obtained by adding increasing concentrations of 2-[125I]-MLT in the absence (Total) or presence (non-specific) of MLT. The assay was carried out for 120 min at 37 °C, followed by rapid filtration through GF/C glass-fiber filters (Whatman, Clifton, NJ, USA). Filter-retained radioactivity was determined with a γ-counter LB2111 (Berthold Technologies, Bad Wildbad, Germany). The efficiency of ligands Ki is expressed by the IC50 of the ligand normalized to the concentration of the radioligand by using the Cheng-Prussof formula: Ki = IC50/ [1 + (L/Kd)] where L represents the ligand concentration and Kd the dissociation constant. Kd values were 59 and 20 pM for PfSR1-MT and MT1 receptors, respectively.

TR-FRET conformational assay

The conformational TR-FRET assay was performed on HEK293 cells transiently expressing PfSR1 variants (WT, ΔN508, or K681A), each fused to an N-terminal SNAP tag. Transfections were performed with JetPEI (Polyplus, New York, NY, USA) once cultures reached roughly 60–70% confluency. After 24 h, the cells were transferred to white 96-well plates and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ until the time of recording. At 48 h post-transfection, receptor-expressing cells were rapidly cooled to reduce ligand-independent trafficking events. Following a gentle wash, the SNAP substrate (Lumi4-Tb, 100 nM; Cisbio Bioassays) was added to Tag-lite medium and allowed to bind under cold conditions for 1 hour. Excess label was removed through three sequential PBS washes, and cells were resuspended in HBSS. Donor emission at 620 nm was first recorded solely to confirm the efficiency of SNAP labeling. To mark the plasma membrane compartment, the ratiometric dye NR12S (40 nM) was applied for 10 min before baseline acquisition. TR-FRET recording began with the establishment of a stable pre-stimulation signal, followed by automated addition of agonists or antagonists-melatonin (10 or 20 µM), luzindole (20 or 100 µM), ARL34 (10 or 100 µM), or their combinations. Kinetic monitoring resumed immediately after drug delivery and continued for approximately 10–15 min without any further manipulation of the plate. Measurements were obtained using a PerkinElmer EnVision Multilabel Reader (PerkinElmer, Ltd., Connecticut, USA). Time-resolved emissions at 620 nm (donor) and 665 nm (acceptor) were collected with a 150-µs delay and a 500-µs integration window. TR-FRET values are reported as the 665/620 nm ratio, expressed as ligand-induced ΔmFU relative to the corresponding control. Quantification was restricted to the designated post-ligand analysis window (207.5–904.7 s), indicated in each figure by the corresponding bounding region.

Live cell calcium assay

Cells were transfected with 1 µg PfSR1-MT, 1 µg PfSR1-MT-K681A, 1 µg PfSR1-MT-ΔN508, 100 ng AT1R and 1 µg empty vector as described before for 48 h. Calcium assays were performed in HBSS buffer (pH 7.4, Gibco) containing the organic anion transport inhibitor 200 μM probenecid to increase Fluo4-AM retention. Briefly, on the day of the calcium assay, transfected HEK293T cells were trypsinized and seeded onto Cell-Tak (Corning)-coated 25-mm borosilicate glass coverslips in complete DMEM for 1–2 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator to enable cell adhesion. Cells were then loaded with staining solution (1 ml DMEM; 5 µM Fluo4-AM; 0.02% Pluronic F-127) for 1 h at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator. After 1 h, cells were washed, mounted on an autofluor chamber, and carefully washed with HBSS buffer to remove non-adherent cells. The autotfluor chamber was filled with 3 ml of HBSS buffer containing 200 μM probenecid and placed on a thermostatically regulated microscope. For calcium measurement, 1 µM MLT, 250 nM Ang II, 10 µM LUZ, 5 µM thapsigargin (THG), and 5 µM ionomycin (ION) were used. Fluo4-AM fluorescence images for calcium (excitation, 488 nm, emission 520 nm long pass) were acquired at 3 s intervals with a cooled charge-coupled device camera coupled to an epifluorescent Nikon Eclipse Ti2-E inverted microscope. Data were analyzed with FiJi (NIH) (Schindelin et al., 2012) and GraphPad Prism v9. Each experiment was performed at least three times.

BRET assay for diacylglycerol (DAG) production in HEK293 cells expressing PfSR1-MT

HEK293T cells were transiently transfected with plasmids encoding PfSR1-MT (100 ng),

MT1 (100 ng), or the empty vector pcDNA (100 ng), together with Gαq (50 ng) and the DAG BRET biosensor (150 ng). Forty-eight hours after transfection, cells were harvested and transferred to white 96-well plates. Cells were pre-incubated with 10 µM luzindole for 10 min at room temperature before BRET acquisition. BRET measurements were performed using a TriStar² S Multimode Reader (Berthold Technologies) equipped with 400/70 nm (F1, donor emission) and 515/20 nm (F2, acceptor emission) emission filters. Baseline BRET signals were recorded, and 1 µM melatonin (MLT) was automatically injected 22 s after the start of the acquisition. Changes in DAG production were monitored as variations in the BRET signal (BRET ratio) over time. The BRET response was quantified as the area under the curve (AUC) of the ligand-induced BRET signal for subsequent statistical analysis.

Methyl (tert-butoxycarbonyl)-L-alanyl-L-tryptophyl-L-leucinate (ARL34) synthesis

To a 50.0 mL round-bottom flask equipped with a magnetic stir bar, (tert-butoxycarbonyl)-L-tryptophan (1.2 eq., 1.8 mmol) was dissolved in dichloromethane (0.50 M). The solution was cooled to 0 °C, and TBTU (1.5 equiv) was added in one portion, followed by DIPEA (3.5 eq.). The mixture was stirred for 30 min, after which methyl L-leucinate hydrochloride (1.0 eq., 1.5 mmol) was added and stirring was continued for 10 min. The reaction was allowed to warm to room temperature and stirred for 24 h. The mixture was then partitioned between 10% HCl and dichloromethane, and the organic layer was collected and washed with 10% HCl (2 × 40.0 mL), saturated NaHCO3 (2 × 40.0 mL), and saturated brine (1 × 40.0 mL). The organic phase was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and the solvent was removed under reduced pressure by rotary evaporation to afford crude methyl (tert-butoxycarbonyl)-L-tryptophyl-L-leucinate.

The crude product was dissolved in a mixture of dichloromethane/trifluoroacetic acid (7:3 v/v, 10 mL/g) and stirred at 0 °C for 30 min. The mixture was then concentrated under reduced pressure, and residual trifluoroacetic acid was removed by addition of toluene followed by rotary evaporation, leading to the deprotection of the N-terminal position of the dipeptide.

Separately, (tert-butoxycarbonyl)-L-alanine (1.2 eq., 1.8 mmol) was dissolved in dichloromethane (0.50 M) in a 50.0 mL round-bottom flask equipped with a magnetic stir bar. The solution was cooled to 0 °C, and TBTU (1.5 equiv) was added in one portion, followed by DIPEA (3.5 eq.). After stirring for 30 min, the crude deprotected dipeptide was added, and stirring was continued for 10 min. The reaction was allowed to warm to room temperature and stirred for 24 h. The reaction mixture was then partitioned between 10% HCl and dichloromethane. The organic layer was collected and washed with 10% HCl (2 × 40.0 mL), saturated NaHCO3 (2 × 40.0 mL), and saturated brine (1 × 40.0 mL). The organic phase was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and the solvent was removed under reduced pressure by rotary evaporation. Spectrofluorimetric measurement of cytosolic Ca²⁺ dynamics in Plasmodium falciparum PfGCaMP3 parasites

Cytosolic Ca²⁺ dynamics in Plasmodium falciparum were quantified using the PfGCaMP3 reporter line expressing a genetically encoded calcium indicator (Borges-Pereira et al., 2014). Parasite cultures were tightly synchronized, and trophozoite-stage parasites were isolated by saponin (Merck/Sigma-Aldrich). Isolated parasites were resuspended in MOPS-buffered saline (116 mM NaCl, 5.4 mM KCl, 0.8 mM MgSO₄, 5.5 mM D-glucose, 50 mM MOPS, pH 7.4) supplemented with 2 mM CaCl₂. Spectrofluorimetric measurements were performed using a Shimadzu RF5301PC spectrofluorometer with isolated parasites at a density of 1 × 10⁷ cells mL⁻¹ in a 1-mL continuously stirred quartz cuvette at 37 °C. GCaMP3 fluorescence was excited at 488 nm, and emission was recorded at 530 nm. Parasites were stimulated with melatonin (1 µM) or ARL-34 (500 nM), with DMSO (v/v) used as the solvent control. Cyclopiazonic acid (CPA; 5 µM) and ionomycin (ION; 5 µM) were applied as positive controls to induce maximal cytosolic Ca²⁺ elevations. Fluorescence traces were recorded in real time, and cytosolic Ca²⁺ responses were quantified by calculating the difference between peak fluorescence (Fmax) and baseline fluorescence prior to stimulation (F0). Data were expressed as ΔF = Fmax – F0 or normalized to the maximal ionomycin response, as indicated. All measurements were repeated in at least three independent biological experiments.

ARL34 phenotypic and cytotoxic screening in P. falciparum

ARL34 activity was assessed in asexual blood stages, late-stage gametocytes, and mammalian cells. Plasmodium falciparum 3D7 cultures (0.3% parasitemia, 1% hematocrit) were exposed to ARL34 (0.1–10 µM) for 72 h under standard culture conditions (RPMI-1640 supplemented with 0.5% AlbuMAX I; 5% CO₂, 5% O₂, 90% N₂). Asexual growth inhibition was quantified by flow cytometry using SYBR Green I and MitoTracker Deep Red staining (10,000 events/sample; Accuri C6; FlowJo analysis), with DMSO as control.

Activity of ARL34 in sexual stages was assessed using stage IV–V gametocytes (NF54-GFP-Luc), enriched by magnetic separation and standardized to 1% gametocytaemia and 2% hematocrit. Gametocytes were treated with ARL34 (0.1–10 µM) for 72 h under hypoxic conditions (37 °C). Viability was determined by luciferase-based bioluminescence using the Promega Luciferase Assay System and a Berthold Tristar 5 reader.

Cytotoxicity was evaluated in HEK293T cells (10⁴/well) treated with ARL34 (0.1–100 µM) for 72 h using the MTT assay, with absorbance measured at 570 nm (FlexStation® 3). DMSO (v/v) and digitonin served as negative and positive controls, respectively. Selectivity indices were calculated as CC₅₀ (HEK293T)/IC₅₀ (P. falciparum).

All experiments were performed in triplicate across three independent biological replicates, and results are presented as mean ± SD.

Bioinformatics analysis: post-translational modification prediction, molecular modeling and docking

The PfSR1-MT (Uniprot: Q8II53) and PfSR10 (Uniprot: Q8I5P8) sequences were retrieved from the Uniprot database. Thereafter, they were submitted to the MusiteDeep program (“MusiteDeep: a deep learning-based webserver for protein post-translational modification site prediction and visualization”) for the prediction of post-translational modification sites. Also, the signal peptide was identified by SignalP 6.0 (“SignalP 6.0 predicts all five types of signal peptides using protein language models”), and the processed final sequences were submitted to protein structure prediction modeling. The models were obtained by applying a hybrid approach in the ROBETTA program, using the RoseTTAFold method (Baek et al., 2021), and subsequently refined in the Chiron program (Ramachandran et al., 2011).

The protein models were evaluated using various quality scores. The Confidence Score, indicating the confidence in the predicted model, was 0.5446 for PfSR1-MT and 0.4740 for PfSR10. The MolProbity Score, assessing the model's overall quality, was 1.1 for PfSR1-MT and 1.5 for SR10. The Clash Score, measuring the number of atomic clashes, was 0.9 for PfSR1-MT and 2.35 for PfSR10. The Ramachandran Outliers, indicating the percentage of residues outside the preferred regions of the Ramachandran plot, were 0.65% for PfSR1-MT and 2.57% for PfSR10. The Rotamer Outliers, representing the percentage of rotamers outside the favored conformations, were 0% for both PfSR1-MT and PfSR10. The C-beta deviations, evaluating deviations in the position of C-beta atoms, were 0.1294% for PfSR1-MT and 0.1527% for PfSR10. The Bad Bonds, measuring the number of poor bonds, were 0.0735% for PfSR1-MT and 0.1322% for PfSR10. The Bad Angles, assessing the number of poor bond angles, were 0.2408% for PfSR1-MT and 0.3487% for PfSR10. The Cis Non-Proline, indicating the number of cis non-proline residues, were 0.3974% for PfSR1-MT and 0.3257% for PfSR10. Finally, the Twisted Non-Proline, evaluating the number of twisted non-proline residues, was 0.1325% for PfSR1-MT and 0% for PfSR10.

Next, the validated models were blindly docked with melatonin and luzindole on the Achilles server. Following the docking simulations, the poses were optimized based on scores obtained from PLANTS (Korb et al., 2009) and AutoDock Vina (Eberhardt et al., 2021) established protein-ligand docking scoring functions.

Results

Codon-optimized PfSR1-MT and PfSR10 are expressed in mammalian cells

Interrogation of the AlphaFold Database (Jumper et al., 2021; Varadi et al., 2022) confirmed the previously predicted 7TM spanning α-helices organization resembling the typical GPCR architecture and provided predicted structural models for the large N-terminal domains of PfSR1-MT and PfSR10 (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Figure 1A). To obtain a more accurate prediction of the 3D structures of PfSR1-MT and PfSR10, we used a hybrid approach in the ROBETTA program, employing a three-track neural network RoseTTAFold method (Baek et al., 2021), which was subsequently refined in the Chiron program (Ramachandran et al., 2011) by minimizing steric clashes in protein structures. The models suggest that PfSR10 displays a signal peptide (initial 30 residues) while PfSR1-MT lacks a signal peptide but contains an additional N-terminal αhelix (Supplementary Figure 1B, C). In addition, predicted structures show that both proteins are composed of a bulky N-terminal domain and a small C-terminal domain, both potentially oriented towards opposite sides of the membrane harboring the 7TM domain (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Figure 1A). Classical motifs known for class A and B mammalian GPCRs (i.e. DRY, PIF, CWxP, NPxxY motifs (class A GPCRs); PxxG, NPGQ motifs (class B GPCRs) were not identified at potentially relevant positions of the SR1 and SR10 sequence.

To functionally characterize these GPCR-like candidates, we investigated their heterologous expression in human HEK293T cells by transiently transfecting cells with expression vectors containing codon-optimized PfSR1-MT and PfSR10 genes fused to SNAP and flag tags at their N-termini. Expression of SNAP-PfSR1-MT and SNAP-PfSR10 fusion proteins was observed at the predicted molecular weight after 48 h of transfection (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Figure 2A). Labeling with the cell-permeant fluorescent SNAP-488 substrate revealed predominant intracellular localization of SNAP-PfSR1-MT and SNAP-PfSR10 (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Figure 2B). To determine the fraction of PfSR10 and PfSR1-MT potentially expressed at the cell surface vs the total receptors in a quantitative manner, we used the nanoluciferase (Nluc) complementation assay, which is based on the complementation of the small HiBiT (11 amino acids) fragment and the large LgBiT (18 kDa) fragment (Dixon et al., 2016; Hall et al., 2012). N-terminally HiBiT-tagged PfSR1-MT (HiBiT-PfSR1-MT) and PfSR10 (HiBiT- PfSR10) were expressed, and Nluc complementation was measured by adding recombinant LgBiT to intact or permeabilized cells (Fig. 1E, G; Supplementary Figure 3A, B). Predominant intracellular localization of HiBiT-PfSR1-MT and HiBiT-PfSR10 was confirmed, with approximately 10% of the expressed receptors detectable at the cell surface (Fig. 1D, F). Of note, detection of receptors on the cell surface implies that the N-termini of HiBiT-SR1 and HiBiT-SR10 are oriented towards the extracellular milieu. Whereas total expression of HiBiT-PfSR10 was comparable to the human MT1 reference receptor, total expression of HiBiT-PfSR1-MT was lower (5–11%) (Supplementary Figure 2A, 3C), albeit the used amount of MT1 was 100–1000 fold less than HiBiT-PfSR1-MT and HiBiT-PfSR10.

Determination of the oligomerization state of PfSR1-MT and PfSR10 in HEK293T cells

As PfSR1-MT and PfSR10 expression coincide during the trophozoite and schizont stages of the parasite (Bozdech et al., 2003; Smith et al., 2020), we studied the impact of their receptor coexpression on their surface expression. We observed that surface and total HiBiT-PfSR1-MT expression increased by 5- to 7-fold in the presence of SNAP-PfSR10 without modifying the proportion of cell surface expressed HiBiT-PfSR1-MT. Coexpression of HiBiT-PfSR10 with SNAP-PfSR1-MT had no effect on surface nor total HiBiT-PfSR10 expression (Fig. 1H, Supplementary Figure 3D,E).

Receptor oligomerization has been proposed to promote receptor folding, maturation, and transport to the cell surface (Bulenger et al., 2005). We therefore evaluated possible physical interactions between PfSR1-MT and PfSR10 with several proximity-based protein interaction assays in intact cells avoiding potential oligomerization/aggregation artifacts associated with receptor solubilization necessary for co-immunoprecipitation experiments. We first applied the split Nluc complementation assay using C-terminally SmBiT- and LgBiT-tagged proteins (Fig. 1I). The SmBiT fragment is a variant of the HiBiT fragment with lower affinity for LgBiT thus avoiding spontenous complementation and only occurs when SmBiT- and LgBiT-tagged fusion proteins are in close proximity (Supplementary Figure 4A) (Dixon et al., 2016; Hall et al., 2012). A slight complementation was observed in cells expressing the two PfSR1-MT fusion proteins or the two PfSR1-MT/PfSR10 combinations (Fig. 1I). On the other hand, robust complementation was observed in cells expressing the two PfSR10 fusion proteins, similar to MT1 (Fig. 1I, Supplementary Figure 4B, C), the positive control for receptor oligomerization (Ayoub et al., 2002). These results indicate that PfSR10 can form homomers in HEK293T cells, whereas PfSR1-MT does not form homo- nor heteromers with PfSR10. PfSR10 homodimerization was confirmed with another proximity assay, namely the NanoBRET (bioluminescence resonance energy transfer) assay. This assay is based on the energy transfer between the full-length Nluc (energy donor) and the yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) (energy acceptor), both fused to the C-terminus of the target proteins in our case. Energy transfer occurs when donor and acceptor are in close proximity (<10 nm), indicating that the receptors are in close proximity (Supplementary Figure 4D). Similarly to the split Nluc complementation assay, only formation of PfSR10 homodimers was detectable in the NanoBRET assay (Fig. 1J, K). The advantage of the BRET assay compared to the complementation assay is that receptor expression levels can be easily quantified by measuring luminescence and fluorescence levels of the respective fusions protein (Supplementary Figure 4E-H). As the two assays described above cannot distinguish between dimerization at the cell surface and in intracellular compartments, we designed a surface-split NanoBRET assay that captures only cell-surface dimers. In this assay, the Nluc activity is achieved by adding the recombinant LgBiT fragment to cells expressing the HiBiT-tagged PfSR10 at the cell surface. Proximity between the reconstituted Nluc energy donor and the SNAP-tagged PfSR10, labeled with a fluorophore serving as energy acceptor, is then measured as energy transfer. Donor saturation experiments with increasing amounts of SNAP-tagged PfSR10 unambiguously show that PfSR10 forms homomers at the cell surface (Fig. 1K). In conclusion, in the absence of any significant PfSR1-MT/PfSR10 heteromer formation, the increased surface and total PfSR1-MT expression in the presence of PfSR10 is unlikely to be due to a heterodimer-dependent mechanism. Homodimerization of PfSR10 is a unique feature of this protein of unknown functional impact that remains to be explored in future studies.

MLT binds with high affinity to PfSR1-MT but not to PfSR10

We next investigated the ability of PfSR1-MT and PfSR10 to bind MLT at different plasmid amounts (Supplementary Figure 5) by initially performing three independent binding experiments at a single concentration (500 pM) of the radioactively labeled 2-[125I]-MLT tracer (Supplementary Figure 6A-F). The positive control, the human MT1 receptor, showed robust binding, as expected (Fig. 2A). No specific binding was observed with PfSR10 membranes and lower but significant binding with PfSR1-MT membranes (Fig. 2A). Saturation binding experiments with a range of -[125I]-MLT concentrations revealed a dissociation constant (Kd) of 59 pM (pKd = 10.24 ± 0.07) for PfSR1-MT (Fig. 2B) and competition binding experiments an inhibition constant of 2.4 nM (pKi = 8.64 ± 0.16) for MLT in PfSR1-MT membranes (Fig. 2C). The pKi value of MLT for the human MT1 determined in parallel was 10.0 ± 0.21, which corresponds to reported values (Legros et al., 2013) (Fig. 2C). Interestingly, luzindole (LUZ), a melatonin receptor antagonist, was also able to compete 2-[125I]-MLT binding at PfSR1-MT with similar affinity than at MT1 (pKi = 6.90 ± 0.10 vs 6.14 ± 0.27, respectively) (Fig. 2C). These data classify PfSR1-MT as a melatonin-responsive GPCR-like candidate.

Docking identifies a shared putative MLT and LUZ binding site on the PfSR1-MT structure

To identify the candidate binding pocket for MLT and LUZ in PfSR1-MT, we performed in silico docking using the optimized 3D structure of PfSR1-MT. First, blind docking of MLT and LUZ against the validated models was performed on the Achilles server, yielding several putative binding sites on the PfSR1-MT structure. Notably, top-ranked poses for LUZ and MLT were predominantly located near or within the transmembrane domain (Fig. 2D). Using interaction energy and intermolecular bond data, one potential binding site in the core of the TM5 domains was selected, allowing occupancy by either ligand individually (Fig. 2E). This pocket exhibited multiple hydrogen bonds with MLT and cation-pi interactions with both ligands, accompanied by various hydrophobic interactions, with K681 emerging as a potentially important interacting residue with MLT (Fig. 2F). Quantitative analysis of this interaction yielded an AutoDock Vina score of -6.5 kcal/mol, indicating a favorable binding free energy. Additionally, the PLANTS score of -59.9 further corroborates the high predicted affinity for melatonin binding to the pocket involving K681.

Lysine 681 is part of the MLT binding site at PfSR1-MT

To evaluate the importance of the K681 residue, we generated the corresponding alanine (K861A) mutant which was tested in 2-[125I]-MLT binding experiments. Complete loss of MLT binding was observed with PfSR1-MTK681A when compared to the wild-type PfSR1-MT, despite comparable total and surface expression (8 to 12%) of both proteins (Fig. 2G). To understand the dynamics and accessibility of the ligand binding pocket, we performed a detection and characterization of channels (pores and tunnels) analysis using MOLEonline(Pravda et al., 2018). Based on this analysis, seven putative access tunnels were identified either from the extracellular or TM domains, all converging on the same central pocket of the transmembrane domains (Fig. 2H). This observation is compatible with the amphiphilic character of MLT and with the existence of different MLT access channels in human melatonin receptors (Johansson et al., 2019; Okamoto et al., 2024; Stauch et al., 2019). In conclusion, we show that PfSR1-MT binds MLT with low nM affinity at a putative binding pocket that involves TM5 and in particular lysine 681 that also accommodates the binding of LUZ in a competitive manner.

PfSR1-MT expressing cells elicit a cytosolic calcium rise to MLT that is antagonized by luzindole

In P. falciparum, the MLT signal transduction mechanism involves activation of a PLC, which induces the formation of IP3, ultimately leading to an increase in cytosolic calcium ([Ca2+cyt]) (Alves et al., 2011; Garcia et al., 2017). To investigate whether MLT increases calcium levels in HEK293T cells expressing PfSR1-MT, we established a live-cell calcium imaging assay (Fig. 3A), which was validated with cells expressing the prototypical angiotensin receptor AT1 and stimulated with angiotensin II (Ang II) (Supplementary Figure 7A; Sup Video 1). In cells expressing PfSR1-MT, a rapid and transient increase in intracellular Ca2+ was observed (approximately 15–20% cells) after MLT addition, which was absent in vehicle-treated cells (CTL) and in non-transfected cells (Fig. 3B,C,G; Supplementary Figure 7B; Sup Video 2–4). Importantly, the MLT-induced calcium rise was blocked by LUZ pre-incubation, qualifying LUZ as an antagonist of PfSR1-MT (Fig. 3D,G; Sup Video 5).

In agreement with our 2-[125I]-MLT binding data, HEK293T cells expressing the MLT-binding-deficient PfSR1-MTK681A mutant failed to elicit a detectable cytosolic Ca2+ increase upon MLT stimulation (Fig. 3E,G; Supplementary Video 6). These findings further support the importance of K681 for MLT binding to PfSR1-MT at the functional level. Next, we investigated the contribution of the large N-terminal region of PfSR1-MT to the MLT-induced Ca2+ response by generating a truncated variant lacking the first 508 amino acids (PfSR1-MTΔN508). Cells expressing PfSR1-MTΔN508 did not display any MLT-induced Ca2+ response, suggesting that the N-terminal domain is important for the activation and signaling of PfSR1-MT (Fig. 3 F, G; Supplementary Video 7). Expression levels of PfSR1-MT and PfSR1-MTK681A and PfSR1-MTΔN508 mutants were comparable as determined by western blot (Supplementary Figure 8A, B).

To establish that the melatonin-induced calcium response is mediated specifically through the canonical PLC/IP₃ signaling pathway, we monitored the production of diacylglycerol (DAG), which is generated concomitantly with IP₃ following phospholipase C activation (Fig. 3H). BRET experiments with the DAG biosensor expressed together with PfSR1-MT in HEK293T cells indicated a robust increase in DAG production upon melatonin stimulation (Fig. 3I, J). This response was completely abolished by pre-treatment with LUZ, confirming the involvement of a receptor-mediated process as observed in Ca2+ measurement. Likewise, melatonin-induced DAG production was abolished in cells expressing either the melatonin-binding deficient PfSR1-MTK681A mutant or the N-terminal deletion mutant PfSR1-MTΔN508, demonstrating that both the conserved Lys681 residue and the N-terminal domain are required for receptor signaling (Fig. 3 I, J).

MLT and LUZ cause ligand-specific extracellular domain movements of PfSR1-MT at the plasma membrane

To better understand the role of the N-terminal domain in the activation mechanism of PfSR1-MT, we set up a time-resolved (TR)-FRET assay to monitor potential ligand-induced conformational rearrangements. The assay uses N-terminally SNAP-tagged PfSR1-MT labelled with the Terbium donor fluorophore (Lumi-4-Tb) to measure changes in the PfSR1-MT conformation upon ligand binding by FRET (Fig. 4A). A similar approach has been recently applied to the glucagon-like-peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor, which showed a ligand-induced movement of its extracellular domain (ECD) relative to the PM (Lucey et al., 2021).

MLT and LUZ showed a concentration-dependent change in TR-FRET signal (Fig. 4B-D). When added together, (LUZ+MLT), an additive effect was observed (Fig. 4C). The specificity of these signals was further confirmed with the PfSR1-MT∆N508 deletion mutant, which was unresponsive as expected (Fig. 4C). To test the exclusivity of MLT towards PfSR1-MT, we performed similar experiments using PfSR10 and observed no change in the TR-FRET signal upon addition of MLT as expected (Supplementary Figure 9A). We then performed the assay with the PfSR1-MTK681A mutant, which did not show any MLT-induced change in TR-FRET, consistent with the MLT binding deficiency of this mutant (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, the change in TR-FRET induced by LUZ persisted in the PfSR1-MTK681A mutant indicating that binding of LUZ to PfSR1-MT does not rely on the K681 residue. Taken together, this suggests that LUZ binds to the same site as MLT but does not interact with K681. Both ligands induce a movement of the ECD of PfSR1-MT towards the PM.

Indole-based compound ARL34 blocks P. falciparum asexual and sexual stages, triggers cytosolic calcium mobilization in PfGCaMP3 parasites and the movement of the extracellular domain of PfSR1-MT

We next searched for a compound that binds specifically to PfSR1-MT and impairs the human malaria parasite cycle (Fig. 5A).

Based on the biological relevance of the indole scaffold in modulating parasite asexual progression and using a structure-oriented strategy aimed at investigating indole-containing molecular architectures as modulators of Plasmodium signaling, we sought to investigate whether indole-containing structures distinct from melatonin could also influence parasite signaling. In this context, we identified compound ARL34 which provides an indole-containing molecular framework in a different structural and conformational environment, allowing us to probe the contribution of this motif to molecular recognition and PfSR1-MT activation. More broadly, indole-containing structures, including tryptophan-derived architectures, offer the possibility of systematically modulating physicochemical and conformational properties while preserving a biologically relevant scaffold (Schuck et al., 2014; Luthra et al., 2019; Dias et al., 2020; Mallaupoma et al., 2022; Mohanty et al., 2026).

Our results showed that compound ARL34, presented a half-maximal cytotoxic concentration (CC50) of 31.3 µM on HEK293 cells proliferation (Fig. 5B). We further tested ARL34 against the asynchronous wild type (3D7) of P. falciparum and obtained an IC50 of 859.2 ± 0.07 nM (Fig. 5C, D), implying a selectivity index (SI) of 36.4 towards the parasite. The antimalarial drug chloroquine was used as positive control (IC50=10.8 ± 2.9 nM) (Fig. 5E). The gametocytocidal activity of ARL34 was tesed at late-stage (stage IV-V) P. falciparum NF54-GFP-Luc gametocytes (Fig. 5F) and showed a progressive reduction in gametocyte viability, with 26.09% inhibition at 10 µM (Fig. 5G).

We next examined whether ARL-34 induces cytosolic Ca²⁺ signaling in P. falciparum. We used a Plasmodium falciparum strain expressing GCaMP3 (PfGCaMP3) (Fig. 5H) (Borges-Pereira et al., 2014), to monitor cytosolic Ca²⁺ dynamics in real-time within the parasite. Representative bright-field/DIC, GCaMP3 fluorescence, merged images, and Panótico®-stained smears confirmed parasite stage specificity and robust PfGCaMP3 expression (Fig. 5I). Both, MLT and ARL-34 significantly increase cytosolic Ca²⁺ levels (Fig. 5J, L, M). Control DMSO-treated parasites showed no detectable change in GCaMP3 fluorescence and cyclopiazonic acid (CPA, 5 µM) and the calcium ionophore ionomycin (ION, 5 µM) induced increase in cytosolic calcium as expected (Fig. 5K).

Consistent with previous reports (Hotta et al. 2000), MLT displayed kinetics characterized by an abrup rise in fluorescence intensity (Fig. 5L). Treatment with ARL-34 (500 nM) elicited a delayed and robust elevation of cytosolic Ca²⁺ (Fig. 5M), indicating efficient mobilization of intracellular calcium stores. Together, these data establish that ARL-34 is a potent inducer of cytosolic Ca²⁺ mobilization in trophozoite-stage P. falciparum parasites.

To investigate whether ARL34 acts through PfSR1-MT, we used the TR-FRET assay monitoring ligand-induced conformational changes in PfSR1-MT expressed in HEK293 cells. ARL-34 induced a robust decrease in TR-FRET signal over time suggesting a similar movement of the large ECD of PfSR1-MT away from the PM as seen for MLT (Fig. 5N). The specificity of the signal was shown by the absence of ARL34-induced signal for the PfSR1-MT∆N508 deletion mutant of the ECD (Fig. 5N) and control experiments using PfSR10 and the GLP1R (Supplementary Figure 9). Similarly to LUZ, the conformational change observed after addition of ARL34 persisted in the PfSR1-MTK681A mutant, indicating that the binding of this compound does not rely on the K681 residue.

To evaluate the selectivity of ARL34 binding to PfSR1-MT vs. human MT1 and MT2 receptors, we performed 2-[125I]-MLT competition binding experiments with HEK293 cells expressing MT1 or MT2. ARL34 was unable to compete with 2-[125I]-MLT binding to both receptors (Supplementary Figure 10A, B) indicating that ARL34 has no affinity for mammalian melatonin receptors and is a specific ligand for PfSR1-MT.

Discussion

Malaria’s parasite's adaptability to the host's rhythm and synchronous development have been known for over a century due to the timely, recurrent fever attacks characteristic of the disease (Hawking et al., 1968), but the molecular mechanism remains debated (Singh et al., 2020). Data presented in this study provide crucial evidence of MLT recognition by the human malaria parasite P. falciparum-encoded protein PfSR1-MT. Using an in silico approach combined with in vitro assays, we identified the MLT binding pocket. Additionally, we revealed that MLT, by acting on PfSR1-MT, promotes intracellular Ca2+ release, an effect that is antagonized by LUZ in a competitive manner. Furthermore, binding of MLT, LUZ and the PfSR1-MT-selective ARL34 compound to PfSR1-MT induce a movement of its large N-terminal domain that is most likely involved in an allosteric activation mechanism that could resemble the well-established activation mechanism of mammalian GPCR involving ligand binding and receptor activation through a series of conformational changes (Weis and Kobilka, 2018).

In higher vertebrates, melatonin secretion is regulated by the circadian master clock in the suprachiasmatic nucleus, which synchronizes the sleep-wake cycle and regulates body temperature (Brown, 1994). In malaria parasites, MLT synchronizes the red blood cell cycle of P. falciparum and P. chabaudi (Hotta et al., 2000). Interestingly, MLT does not synchronize the 18-h parasite cycle of murine P. berghei and P. yoelii (Bagnaresi et al., 2009). Furuyama et al. showed that inhibiting the MLT pathway with LUZ inhibits P. falciparum growth in vitro, impairing parasite maturation (Furuyama et al., 2014).

Anti-malarial compounds with structures related to melatonin/indoles have been investigated extensively (Pacheco and Santos, 2022; Surur et al., 2020). Our group investigated a series of indolic compounds and identified three compounds that inhibit parasite growth, with IC50 values in the micromolar range, including the lowest IC50 of 2.93 µM (Schuck et al., 2014). Luthra et al. reported indolic analogs of tryptamine able to impair parasite synchronization and proliferation with IC50 values in the low micromolar range (approximately 0.7 µM) against wild type and parasites resistant to the antimalarial chloroquine, and showed the ability of the most potent compounds of the series to bind to the human melatonin MT1 receptor (Luthra et al., 2019). These studies indicate that decoding the role of melatonin in Plasmodium together with structure-activity relation studies should contribute to the development of new chemotherapeutics to combat the disease. We identified the indolic compound ARL34 as a potential antimalarial candidate targeting the PfSR1-MT pathway in Plasmodium. The compound inhibited the inthraerythrocytic asexual progression of P. falciparum 3D7 in vitro, with IC50 value of 852 nM and exhibited 26% inhibition of gametocytes, the sexual stages transmitted to the mosquito vector. In addition, we observed that ARL34 evokes a cytosolic Ca2+increase in the parasite, similar to MLT, and causes a conformational change in the N-terminal domain of PfSR1, implying that the compound can be acting through the receptor. ARL34′s selectivity for PfSR1-MT over human melatonin receptors highlights its potential as a parasite-directed antimalarial strategy. Such selectivity may allow effective targeting of the parasite while minimizing interference with host melatonin signaling. ARL34 provides a foundation for the development of compounds targeting PfSR1-MT. Future studies aimed at better understanding the structure and function of PfSR1-MT will help guide the identification, testing, and optimization of compounds with improved potency and selectivity. Together, these findings identify PfSR1-MT as a novel antimalarial target, with ARL34 serving as a starting point for PfSR1-MT-directed drug development.

Our modeling study suggested that the N-terminal and C-terminal domains of PfSR1-MT and PfSR10 face opposite sites of the membrane. The Nluc complementation assay indicated that both N-termini are oriented towards the extracellular milieu. The majority of the two proteins was retained intracellularly, with approximately 10% reaching the cell surface in HEK293T cells. It is important to consider that the heterologous expression system used in this study highly differs from the unicellular organism in which the receptors are naturally expressed, and the molecular machinery in the parasite is different from mammalian cells, affecting the exportation of PfSR1-MT and PfSR10 to the cell membrane.

GPCR oligomerization is a general feature of GPCRs that can regulate cell surface expression and functional properties of receptors (Ferré et al., 2014). This has also been observed for mammalian melatonin receptors, which form melatonin MT1/MT2 receptor heterodimers in the retina (Baba et al., 2013; Cecon et al., 2018) and MT2 receptor heterodimers with the serotonin receptor 5-HT2C in the brain (Gerbier et al., 2020; Kamal et al., 2015). In this context, we compared the expression profiles of PfSR1-MT and PfSR10 using available transcriptome data from the asexual cycle (Bozdech et al., 2003; Smith et al., 2020) and found that both genes co-occur during late schizogony. Interestingly, our study shows that co-expression of PfSR1-MT with PfSR10 improves the surface expression of PfSR1-MT, not vice versa. Our Nluc complementation and BRET assays did not reveal any PfSR1-MT / PfSR10 heteromers that might be involved in the improved expression of PfSR1-MT in the presence of PfSR10. In contrast, these studies gathered strong evidence for the formation of PfSR10 homomers at the cells surface of HEK293 cells. The molecular basis of this striking difference in the oligomerization properties of PfSR10 and PfSR1-MT remains to be determined to better understand its biological significance for the parasite's function.

Our radioligand binding experiments with 2-[125I]-MLT showed significant binding in membranes prepared from PfSR1-MT-expressing HEK293T cells and revealed a Kd of 59 pM (pKd = 10.24 ± 0.07). Competition binding showed an inhibition constant (Ki) of 2.4 nM (pKi = 8.64 ± 0.16) for MLT. We performed in silico docking using the optimized 3D structure of PfSR1-MT, identifying a putative binding pocket fitting both MLT and LUZ in the TM5 domain. Apart from this central overlapping part, MLT and LUZ appear to explore different parts of this large central binding pocket, as suggested by molecular docking studies and as illustrated by the K681 residue which is essential for 2-[125I]-MLT binding and the MLT-induced movement of the ECD of PfSR1-MT but not involved in the LUZ-induced movement of the ECD. The profile of ARL34 resembles the one of LUZ as the movement of the ECD is not impacted by the PfSR1-MTK681A mutant. Our preliminary data suggest that the binding mode of ARL34 is unique as it can not compete with 2-[125I]-MLT binding (data not shown) by binding further away in the large central binding pocket. Taken together, our data suggest that MLT, LUZ and ARL34 all activate PfSR1-MT by binding to the large central binding pocket in a slightly different pose. This flexibility and the large seize of the binding pocket of PfSR1-MT is very different from the pockets of MT1 and MT2 which are very small and confined (Okamoto et al., 2024). It is remarkable that PfSR1-MT binds LUZ with an IC50 similar to that of MT1, and that LUZ acts as an antagonist of MLT in the Ca2+ assay. While MLT behaves as an agonist and LUZ as an antagonist on both MT1 and PfSR1-MT, the molecular basis for this behavior is likely to be different in light of the differences in the two binding pockets and binding modes of both compounds. We also identify seven putative access tunnels converging on the central MLT/LUZ pocket in PfSR1-MT which is similar to the two ligand access tunnels described in MT1 (Stauch et al., 2019). Altogether, these results indicate that the parasite expresses a novel melatonin-responsive GPCR-like candidate that is evolutionarily divergent from the described high-affinity mammalian MLT receptors (Liu et al., 2019).

MLT-induced intracellular calcium mobilization in HEK293T cells expressing PfSR1-MT validated previous findings of MLT-induced calcium efflux in Plasmodium, which is blocked by the melatonin receptor antagonist LUZ (Hotta et al., 2000). We previously showed that the MLT-induced signal transduction pathway lies upstream of PLC/IP3 activation and an increase in cytosolic Ca2+ concentration. This is the first time we identified PfSR1-MT as the MLT sensor of this pathway. There is growing evidence that parasites' signaling pathways form integrated signaling networks akin to those observed in mammalian cells. A canonical G protein has not been identified in the Plasmodium Genome. Some studies report the identification of Ras-like and Gα subunits of heterotrimeric GTP-binding proteins in P. falciparum extracts (Kaiser et al., 2015; Lu et al., 1994). Further supportive evidence for the role of heterotrimeric G proteins in P. falciparum comes from labeling studies with the Gαi- and Gαs-selective pertussis and cholera toxins, respectively (Dyer and Day, 2000), and treatment studies, where pertussis toxin promotes growth and cholera toxin inhibits the development of the asexual parasite (Harrison et al., 2003). Another study revealed that parasites may use the host’s β-adrenergic receptors and Gαs, which are recruited to the parasite vacuole and facilitate invasion. In conclusion, despite some evidence for heterotrimeric G proteins in P. falciparum, further evidence is needed to determine whether parasite receptors such as PfSR1-MT use signaling machineries similar to those of mammalian GPCRs or employ alternative signaling modes.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, heterologous expression can limit the proper expression and function of P. falciparum proteins, as specific parasite proteins may not be expressed in heterologous systems. Second, despite several attempts, we were unable to generate a PfSR1-MT knockout for phenotypic studies.

Despite the differences between the heterologous and parasite systems, the concordant responses observed with ARL34 in both models support the functional relevance of PfSR1-MT. The ability of ARL34 to modulate receptor-associated responses in transfected cells and produce corresponding effects in P. falciparum strengthens the evidence for its activity through PfSR1-MT. However, the precise endogenous role of PfSR1-MT in the parasite remains to be validated.

Together, the findings demonstrate the utility of this approach for identifying and characterizing parasite-selective ligands with potential antimalarial activity. However, it remains an open question what the effect of PfSR1-MT knockout is in P. falciparum.

Altogether, this study highlights the potential role of melatonin, among other environmental synchronizing cues, in the integration of host-parasite circadian rhythms. Our results pave the way for PfSR1-MT to become a new target for malaria drugs.
