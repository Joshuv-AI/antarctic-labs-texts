Human papillomavirus vaccination among childhood cancer survivors: A scoping review
Authors: Pragya Gautam Poudel, Julia Brown, Tara M Brinkman, Casey L Daniel, Melissa M Hudson, James L Klosky, Kirsten K Ness, Heather M Brandt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChildhood cancer survivors are at increased risk for human papillomavirus (HPV) cancers yet are less likely to be vaccinated against HPV than the general population. This scoping review identified studies describing barriers, facilitators, and strategies to improve HPV vaccination in this vulnerable population. We searched PubMed, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), and Web of Science to identify full-length studies, published in English, from July 2006 to June 2024. We excluded survivorship studies unrelated to HPV vaccination and non-empirical studies (e.g. commentaries). Twenty studies were identified. Multi-level barriers reported occurred in patients- (e.g. vaccine safety-concerns), provider- (e.g. inconsistent guidelines), and system-levels (e.g. lack of vaccine availability). The primary facilitator was the health care provider’s recommendation. Potential strategies for increased vaccination included clear recommendations and digital reminders. This review provides valuable insights for developing strategies to improve HPV vaccination coverage and reduce the risk of HPV-related cancers among childhood cancer survivors.KEYWORDS: HPV vaccination, childhood cancer survivors, barriers, facilitators, health care providers

Introduction

The five-year survival rate for childhood cancers has increased from 58% in the 1970s to 85% today.1 This improvement in survival is primarily attributed to the development of innovative and effective cancer-directed therapies. It has been estimated that more than 500,000 childhood cancer survivors (cancer survivors, hereafter), who have survived five or more years after diagnosis of cancer, are currently living in the United States (U.S.).2 Concurrent with improvement in survival, cancer survivors face an elevated risk of developing treatment-associated chronic health conditions, including subsequent cancers. They also have an increased risk of developing human papillomavirus (HPV)-associated cancers in adulthood, presumably attributed to treatment-related compromised immune systems.3 Both male (oropharyngeal, anal, penile cancer) and female (cervical, oropharyngeal, anal, vaginal, and vulvar cancer) cancer survivors are vulnerable to HPV-related malignancies. Compared with the age- and sex- matched general U.S. population, female and male childhood and young adult cancer survivors have 1.4-fold and 2.5-fold, respectively, higher risk of developing HPV-associated malignancies.3 Evidence shows that the median age for HPV-associated subsequent malignant neoplasms among these cancer survivors is 31 years, and ranges from 10 to 56 years,4 which highlights the persistent threat of HPV-associated cancers into adulthood. Fortunately, the majority of these HPV-associated malignancies can be prevented through timely HPV vaccination.5

The Children’s Oncology Group (COG) Long-Term Follow-Up Guidelines for Survivors of Childhood, Adolescent, and Young Adult Cancers recommend a three-dose HPV vaccine series for all childhood cancer survivors, regardless of their age at the first dose of the HPV vaccine, beginning 6 months after the completion of cancer-directed therapy.6 This recommendation aligns with the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) guidance, which recommends a three-dose series for immuno-compromised individuals.7 Evidence shows that immunogenicity and safety of the three-dose series of the HPV vaccine among cancer survivors are similar to the general population.8 However, HPV vaccination coverage among childhood cancer survivors is lower compared to the general population (i.e., 23.8% vs. 40.5%, respectively).9 Some reasons for lower HPV vaccination in cancer survivors include vaccine hesitancy, safety concerns, lack of adequate health insurance coverage, and lack of health care provider recommendations.10 Lower HPV vaccination coverage among these cancer survivors is highly concerning, particularly in the context of their elevated risk for subsequent malignancies.

Prior reviews evaluating health behaviors among cancer survivors have largely focused on diet, physical activity, alcohol use, and smoking.11–13 In contrast, few studies have described cancer survivors’ behaviors related to HPV vaccination as an effective cancer prevention measure.14,15 Previous reviews, conducted only a few years after the HPV vaccine, was formally approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in the U.S. (2006 for females and 2009 for males), did not systematically focus on behavioral determinants, namely barriers and facilitators, pertaining to HPV vaccination.11–13 Furthermore, comprehensive strategies to increase HPV vaccination coverage among cancer survivors were not and have not been addressed. Understanding the barriers and facilitators to HPV vaccination will inform the development of tailored strategies for improving HPV vaccination coverage among cancer survivors. Thus, our study aimed to address this gap by conducting a systematic scoping review of the scientific literature and identifying studies describing barriers and facilitators of HPV vaccination among childhood cancer survivors. Additionally, we aimed to identify evidence – based interventions, programs, or strategies that improved in HPV vaccination initiation and completion among these cancer survivors. The results of this review will best position clinicians and researchers to develop, test and implement vaccine interventions for cancer survivors, with the goal of reducing late-stage HPV-associated cancers in this high-risk group of cancer survivors.

Methods

We performed a scoping review to comprehensively explore and identify available studies on barriers and facilitators to HPV vaccination, as well as existing programs or strategies aimed at improving HPV vaccination coverage among cancer survivors. A scoping review approach was chosen as it provides a systematic and replicable strategy to rigorously evaluate the available evidence and identify any knowledge gaps as targets for future research.16 We adhered to the scoping methodology outlined by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA), ensuring a rigorous and transparent approach to guide the review process in selecting the relevant studies.17 This included defining eligibility criteria, conducting a comprehensive search of multiple databases, selecting relevant studies, and extracting key data on barriers, facilitators, and strategies related to HPV vaccination.

Search strategy

We comprehensively searched PubMed, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), and Web of Science between 7/1/2006 (the year of FDA licensure of HPV vaccine in the U.S.) to 6/14/2024 to identify qualifying studies. The combination of keywords and MeSH terms were used to search the original studies as shown in Table 1. Briefly, we used following keywords and MeSH terms: (((((Human papillomavirus) OR (Human papillomavirus vaccines)) OR (HPV vaccination)) OR (vaccination [MeSH Terms])) AND (((((((Childhood cancer survivors) OR (Survivors of childhood cancer)) OR (Pediatric survivors)) OR (Cancer survivors)) OR (Young survivors)) OR (Adolescent and young adult survivors)) OR (AYA survivors))) AND (((Interventions) OR (Programs)) OR (Strategies)). The last search was performed on June 14, 2024.

Eligibility criteria

We included original quantitative and qualitative studies focused on barriers and facilitators influencing HPV vaccination among cancer survivors (diagnosed with cancer before age 18 years and completed cancer-directed therapy), studies focusing on programs or strategies to improve HPV vaccination coverage among cancer survivors, studies conducted in the U.S., and full text studies published in the English language. We excluded articles that focused on cancer survivorship other than HPV vaccination, publications that did not report original research results (e.g., commentaries, editorials, protocols, reviews), or were not full-length articles, such as conference meeting abstracts.

Selection procedure

Studies were selected for inclusion using the following: In step 1, the literature searches provided 388 studies (189 in PubMed, 122 in CINAHL, and 77 in Web of Science); In step 2, 45 duplicates were removed providing 343 studies for further assessment; In step 3, titles and abstracts were screened, with 32 studies relevant to the inclusion criteria retained. In step 4, full-text studies were assessed, and 20 studies were included in this review (shown in Figure 1). We found only two meeting abstracts that focused on programs or strategies.18,19 However, we did not find full-length, peer reviewed, studies focusing on strategies to improve HPV vaccination coverage among cancer survivors. As a result, we only included studies focusing on barriers and facilitators influencing HPV vaccination coverage among cancer survivors. Two independent reviewers (first and second authors, PGP and JB, respectively) screened the titles and abstracts of all studies that met the eligibility criteria. The full text of the potentially relevant studies were also independently screened by these two reviewers (PGP and JB). Any disagreements during the review process were resolved by discussion and the opinion of the senior author (HMB).

Data extracted from the original studies included the authors’ details, publication year, study aims, demographic characteristics of the participants, types of cancer diagnosed, age at cancer diagnosis, duration of cancer-therapy completion, methods used including study design, study findings related to barriers, facilitators and potential strategies, and other key study highlights (Tables 2 and 3). The first author (PGP) conducted the data extraction, and another author (JB) carefully reviewed the extracted data using the full texts of respective studies.

Results

Twenty studies were included in this review (Tables 2 and 3). Descriptive characteristics of the participants in the original studies included are shown in Table 2 and Figure 2. Across the 20 studies, 169,10,20–33 (80%) included cancer survivors and/or their caregivers to understand factors influencing HPV vaccination and four studies34–37 (20%) included health care providers to understand their perspectives and roles pertaining to HPV vaccination among cancer survivors. Participants in the original studies were mainly non-Hispanic White followed by Black and Hispanic populations. The included studies covered a broad range of childhood cancers, most commonly leukemia, followed by lymphoma and other solid tumors, including central nervous system (CNS) tumors. Within four studies (20%) that focused on health care providers perspectives,34–37 the majority of the providers were non-Hispanic White, and were mainly pediatric oncology or hematology providers; one study included primary care providers along with pediatric oncology clinicians. Childhood cancer survivors and caregivers

Barriers for HPV vaccination

Several barriers to HPV vaccination were endorsed by cancer survivors and caregivers including insufficient communication from health care providers (included in eight studies),9,25–29,32,33 vaccine knowledge gaps (included in seven studies),10,25,26,29,31,32,35 worries about vaccine side effects (included in four studies),23,35,31,32 vaccine safety concerns (included in three studies),10,31,32 vaccine misperception (included in three studies),26,29,36 vaccine hesitancy (included in two studies),10,23 uncertainty about benefits of HPV vaccine in cancer survivors (included in two studies),31,32 financial barriers (included in two studies),9,26 survivors from rural location (included in two studies),24,30 and family decision-making processes (included in one study).10

Cancer survivors and caregivers expressed concerns about the HPV vaccine safety and side effects.10,23,25,31,32 They also expressed hesitancy to receive the HPV vaccine and the need for more time before making decisions regarding vaccination.10,23 Additionally, they relayed knowledge gaps, such as the misperception that the HPV vaccine was indicated only for sexually active individuals.26,29 Caregivers who perceived financial and/or religious barriers, or fear of the vaccine promoting sexual activity, were less likely to initiate the vaccination for their daughters.26,37 Cancer survivors and caregivers’ perceptions that HPV vaccination was only for females or that the vaccine was linked to sexual activity hindered vaccination efforts.29 Health beliefs and family decision-making processes played critical roles in HPV vaccine acceptance.10 Family decision-making processes were more likely to influence the receipt of vaccination for female cancer survivors than male cancer survivors.10 Furthermore, male cancer survivors took longer time to initiate the HPV vaccination than females.20,22,24 Lack of understanding about the cancer prevention benefits of HPV vaccination was identified.31 Caregivers also reported insufficient information about the appropriateness of HPV vaccination for cancer survivors.31,32 Lack of effective communication and recommendations regarding HPV vaccination from health care providers were also observed as barriers in several studies.9,25–29,32,33

A few studies observed relationships between HPV vaccination in cancer survivors and cancer-specific factors.22,30,31 One study observed a lower prevalence of HPV vaccination in cancer survivors of CNS tumors and those who received blood transfusions.22 Another study showed cancer survivors treated with chemotherapy had higher vaccination missed opportunities.30 Additionally, the time elapsed since a cancer diagnosis also influenced HPV vaccination, with longer time since cancer diagnosis correlating with greater vaccine intention.22,31

Facilitators for HPV vaccination

Across the included studies, facilitators for HPV vaccination also were identified. Facilitators were identified by cancer survivors and caregivers, most notably health care provider recommendation along with access to information about HPV vaccination.

A strong predictor of HPV vaccination was the recommendation from a health care provider, which drove acceptance of HPV vaccination among cancer survivors and caregivers.9,21,28 Adequate and acceptable HPV vaccination information, such as information about the HPV vaccine and its potential side effects, coupled with a provider recommendation for vaccination after completion of cancer treatment showed greater intention among caregiver to agree to HPV vaccination for their child.21,25,26,28 Additionally, positive attitude toward HPV vaccination and perceived severity of HPV-related illnesses, including HPV-associated cancers, facilitated uptake among cancer survivors.21 Cancer survivors and their caregivers who perceived HPV vaccine benefit were most likely to complete HPV vaccination.28

Health care providers’ perspectives

Barriers for HPV vaccination

Healthcare provider perceptions regarding the HPV vaccination for cancer survivors revealed several barriers that influenced vaccine uptake including the combination of access to and understanding of clear and consistent guidelines, time constraints, competing priorities, patient-level concerns, and system-level challenges. Several studies observed that unclear and inconsistent guidelines for HPV vaccination for cancer survivors created challenges to effective communication about HPV vaccination among health care providers.35–37 Health care providers endorsed inconsistency in providing recommendations for HPV vaccination due to the lack of clarity and consistency in HPV vaccination guidelines after cancer treatment, and inadequate communication between oncology and primary care clinicians.35–37 Other barriers included gaps in provider knowledge regarding HPV vaccine guidelines specific to cancer survivors.34 Additionally, time constraints and competing clinical priorities prevented oncology health care providers from discussing HPV vaccination with cancer survivors and caregivers.34,36,37 Limited time during appointments often hindered detailed communication about HPV vaccination.36 Additionally, oncology health care providers reported inadequate resources, such as insufficient medical/nurse staff to focus on vaccination, which further limited their ability to recommend and provide HPV vaccine to cancer survivors during appointments.36 Furthermore, oncology clinicians perceived that the vaccination was within the scope of care of primary care clinicians.35,36

Health care providers also acknowledged that cancer survivors’ hesitancy surrounding HPV vaccination may be related to the perception that HPV vaccination promotes sexual activity.36 Providers also reported barriers, such as patient fears and worries about HPV vaccine side effects.36 Health care providers recognized that the cancer survivors’ concerns need to be addressed to improve HPV vaccination uptake but reported challenges to finding effective ways to alleviate fears and misconceptions during clinical interactions.37

Some studies described perspectives of oncology health care providers about barriers at the system-level.34,36,37 Providers reported systematic challenges, such as difficulties accessing vaccination records and lack of availability of HPV vaccine in oncology clinical settings compared to other vaccines for COVID-19 or flu.34,36 Issues with the availability of the HPV vaccine and inadequate space for HPV vaccination storage were also mentioned.37

Facilitators for HPV vaccination

Facilitators for HPV vaccination from the perspectives of health care providers were identified in the included studies. When oncology care providers are generally positive about HPV vaccination for young cancer survivors and are willing to communicate about HPV vaccine with cancer survivors and their caregivers, vaccine uptake improves.34 Additionally, health care providers knowledge, understanding, and beliefs about the importance of HPV vaccination for cancer survivors facilitated HPV vaccination for cancer survivors.36

Recommended potential strategies to improve HPV vaccination

Eleven included studies (55%) reported potential strategies to improve HPV vaccination coverage among cancer survivors. These included vaccine recommendation from health care providers,9,23,25,27,28,29,31,33,34,35 particularly from trusted members of the oncology care team,25,29,31 and sending reminders, preferably digital messages for timely vaccination.32,34 Health care providers also emphasized the importance of system-level solutions, such as having the HPV vaccine readily available during cancer survivor visits, integrating reminders into the electronic medical record, and mechanisms to foster timely collaboration and communication between oncology and primary care providers.34

Discussion

This scoping review study identified barriers and facilitators to HPV vaccination among cancer survivors. According to results published in included studies, cancer survivors and their caregivers identified key barriers for HPV vaccination, which included concerns about inadequate information about vaccine safety data, vaccine hesitancy, an understanding of the influence of health beliefs and family decision-making on vaccine uptake, and the lack of HPV vaccination communication and recommendation from health care providers. HPV vaccination uptake was also associated with cancer-specific factors, such as cancer type, cancer treatment received and time since cancer treatment completion. Cancer survivors with a history of brain cancer, for example, and those who received chemotherapy were less likely to be vaccinated. However, longer time since cancer treatment was linked to greater vaccine intention. Facilitators of HPV vaccination included health care provider recommendations, positive caregiver attitudes and awareness of HPV-related illness and severity. From the perceptive of health care providers, the results of included studies identified barriers for HPV vaccination among childhood cancer survivors, such as unclear and inconsistent HPV vaccination guidelines, competing clinical priorities, time constraints, and system-level challenges, including ready access to HPV vaccine doses for administration. Unclear post-treatment vaccination protocols have caused confusion, making it difficult for providers to communicate effectively about HPV vaccination. Though the providers acknowledged the importance of HPV vaccination, they faced challenges in effectively communicating HPV vaccination information to cancer survivors, suggesting a need for standardized and clear protocols/guidelines regarding HPV vaccination specific to the cancer survivors.

In included studies, oncologists also reported system-level barriers, such as lack of vaccine availability and limited storage, hindering HPV vaccine implementation within oncology care settings where survivors and caregivers emphasize high levels of trust. These data highlight the need for supportive infrastructures within the clinical care setting to promote timely receipt of the HPV vaccine for cancer survivors. Furthermore, primary care clinicians were acknowledged by other providers for their important role in vaccination. However, studies showed primary care clinicians lack adequate guidelines for HPV vaccination for cancer survivors. Enhanced collaboration between oncology and primary care providers, along with shared guidelines and clear communication, could help to improve HPV vaccination coverage in this under-vaccinated population.

Health care providers also acknowledged patient-level concerns, such as vaccine safety, hesitancy, and the misconception that HPV vaccination promotes sexual activity. These concerns were also prevalent among cancer survivors and their caregivers, emphasizing the need for tailored communication strategies to effectively address patient and caregiver-level concerns. As the cancer survivors and their families trust the oncology care team for health-related guidance, addressing these concerns directly by the oncology care team could potentially increase HPV vaccination coverage. Strategies to improve awareness by framing conversations about HPV vaccination as cancer prevention could alleviate caregivers’ concerns and reduce HPV vaccination hesitancy.

The included studies highlighted potential strategies to improve HPV vaccination coverage among cancer survivors. Most emphasized the importance of strong recommendations from trusted health care providers, particularly from oncology care teams. Cancer survivors and their caregivers trust their oncology care team to provide health information and prefer to visit their oncologist even for follow-up care.38,39 Utilizing the trusted oncology care team to directly convey HPV vaccination information is a promising potential strategy to increase vaccination coverage among cancer survivors. Additional potential strategies include digital reminders, integrated into electronic medical records, and fostering timely collaboration and communication between patients, caregivers, oncology providers and primary care providers regarding HPV vaccination. The role of oncology care providers emerged as pivotal in influencing HPV vaccination among cancer survivors. Furthermore, after completion of cancer treatment, cancer survivors also visit primary care clinicians within their community.40 This transition from oncology care team to the primary care providers can be challenging without proper coordination. Despite the increased health risk of HPV-associated cancers and other conditions among cancer survivors, there is limited discussion about their cancer and survivorship care plan with their primary care providers.41 As a result, this might create gaps in adequate utilization of available health care services. Effective communication about survivorship care plans, including HPV vaccination, would help cancer survivors to make timely decisions in obtaining health preventive measures.

Once HPV vaccination has been initiated, the HPV vaccine series must be completed to ensure maximum protection against HPV-related infections and cancers.42 The updated COG guidelines recommend a three-dose HPV vaccine series for these cancer survivors, starting 6 months after the completion of cancer treatment, regardless of their age at the first dose of the HPV vaccine.6 This change was informed by the robust evidence from the study by Landier et al. which demonstrated the immunogenicity and safety of the three-dose HPV vaccine series in cancer survivors.43 Prior to the guideline updates for HPV vaccination in October 2023, the COG guidelines were to follow the CDC recommendations (i.e., two doses of the HPV vaccine in children who start the vaccine series before their 15th birthday and three doses among those who start the vaccine series on or after 15th birthday and individuals with immunocompromised conditions).44 These HPV vaccination guidelines changes could lead to confusion among young cancer survivors and their caregivers who considered their HPV vaccination series complete after two doses. Thus, this indicates the need for dissemination of clear and detailed guidelines on doses and timing of HPV vaccination for cancer survivors.

This review identified the need for multifaceted strategies to address barriers experienced by survivors, their caregivers, and health care providers to improve HPV vaccination coverage among cancer survivors. Understanding the barriers and identifying tailored strategies at multiple levels, including cancer survivors and caregiver-level (such as, addressing knowledge gap, vaccine hesitancy), health care provider-level (such as, clear HPV vaccination guidelines, proper communication and coordination), and system-level (such as, ensuring vaccine availability and adequate storage), are crucial to improve HPV vaccination coverage among cancer survivors and reduce HPV-related cancers among this vulnerable and under-vaccinated population.

Childhood cancer survivors often require long-term follow-up care. Implementing comprehensive support programs that address their unique needs, including HPV vaccination, could bridge the gaps during transition from oncology to primary care settings. Multidisciplinary teams, including oncologists, primary care providers, nurses, and other health care professionals, can ensure continuity of care and support informed decision-making for preventive measures like HPV vaccination. Addressing knowledge gaps and misconceptions among cancer survivors and caregivers through targeted communication and counseling can provide clarity on vaccine safety and benefits. Additionally, training health care providers on HPV vaccination guidelines specific to cancer survivors would be valuable.

This study provides valuable insights into challenges and potential strategies for improving HPV vaccination among cancer survivors; however, we also acknowledge some limitations. First, we initially intended to include original studies describing actual interventions, programs, or strategies to improve HPV vaccination coverage among childhood cancer survivors. Due to limited studies focusing on interventions to improve HPV vaccination coverage among cancer survivors, we included studies focusing on barriers and facilitators to HPV vaccination among cancer survivors. Second, we only included the original research articles but did not include the gray literature and may have missed important relevant information. Third, the included original studies may not represent the entire population of cancer survivors, as they were limited to specific geographical regions and health care settings, which may limit the applicability of the findings to the broader population of cancer survivors. Fourth, the heterogeneity among the included studies in terms of their outcome measures and sample sizes makes it difficult to directly compare the findings across the studies. Fifth, we included only the studies conducted in the U.S., which might limit the generalizability of the findings to other countries.

Conclusion

This review study identified key barriers and facilitators influencing HPV vaccination among cancer survivors, emphasizing the critical role of health care providers, particularly oncologists, in promoting HPV vaccination through strong recommendations and clear communication. Findings support the importance of health care providers considering patient and caregiver safety concerns, health beliefs, and family decision-making strategies when communicating the importance of HPV vaccination. Implementation work is needed to broadly distribute the current HPV vaccination guidelines to both oncologists and primary care providers, and to determine how best to address competing clinical priorities, time constraints, and system-level challenges. Addressing challenges through coordinated and multidisciplinary strategies is essential to improve HPV vaccination coverage and reduce the risk of HPV-related cancers among this vulnerable and under-vaccinated population.
