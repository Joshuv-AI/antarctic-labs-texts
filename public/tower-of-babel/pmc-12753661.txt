Socio-demographic and occupational factors influencing expatriate construction workers’ musculoskeletal pain
Authors: Ahmad H. Alghadir, Talal Alghadir, Faisal Alghadir, Amir Iqbal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Musculoskeletal pain (MSP) is a major occupational health problem, and construction workers are particularly at high risk. Understanding the association of MSP with age, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, and job type among these populations can help in designing targeted preventive measures. To investigate the association between MSP and factors such as age, BMI, smoking, and job type among expatriate construction workers in Saudi Arabia. A structured English questionnaire was administered to 175 male expatriate construction workers aged 20–60 years, representing nine job categories. Data were collected on demographics, BMI, smoking status, job type, and MSP occurrence in the past 12 months. Chi-square tests examined the relationship between age, BMI, smoking, job, and MSP, while logistic regression assessed their predictive influence. The top three nationalities were Indian (40%), Bangladeshi (16.6%), and Jordanian (13.1%). MSP was reported by eighty-five (48.6%) participants. Chi-square analysis revealed no significant associations between age, BMI, smoking, job type, and MSP among the workers (95% CI; p > 0.05). Regression analysis confirmed that none of these factors significantly predicted MSP among the workers. Socio-demographic and occupational factors did not significantly influence musculoskeletal pain (MSP) among expatriate construction workers in Saudi Arabia, suggesting other unmeasured factors contribute to pain risk. Larger, longitudinal studies are needed to identify broader MSP determinants and guide targeted ergonomic interventions. The observational and modestly sized sample (N = 175) may have limited statistical power to detect moderate associations, potentially explaining the non-significant findings.

Introduction

Musculoskeletal pain (MSP) remains one of the most prevalent and disabling occupational health concerns globally, particularly in physically demanding sectors such as construction1–3. Construction work is characterized by repetitive manual tasks, awkward postures, heavy material handling, and exposure to ergonomic and environmental hazards, making workers particularly vulnerable to musculoskeletal disorders4–9. Numerous studies have reported that the global prevalence of MSP among construction workers is substantially higher (ranging from 33 to 89%) than in other occupational groups10–12. Although studies from Saudi Arabia construction indicate a relatively lower prevalence than some international estimates, MSP still represents a significant burden requiring targeted prevention and management1,2,10.

Previous research has demonstrated that half of construction workers experience recurrent or persistent MSP, which negatively affects productivity, functional capacity and carrier longevity13. For instance, in Saudi Arabia, widespread MSP has been reported in various body regions—lower back pain (16.5%), leg (23.9%), shoulder (10.5%) feet (13.4%), neck (7.5%), and upper back pain (6.2%)—highlighting the pervasive nature of these disorders14. Globally, the prevalence of MSP among construction workers may reach 77–80%, largely influenced by factors such as ageing, prolonged working hours and cumulative biomechanical strain15,16.

Among the numerous determinants of MSP, socio-demographic and occupational characteristics—including age, body mass index (BMI), smoking behavior, and job category—have consistently emerged as key contributors17–21. These variables were prioritized in the present study because they are individual-level risk indicators that are easily measurable, modifiable, and commonly cited in occupational health surveillance. Evidence suggests that aging and elevated BMI may increase susceptibility to chronic pain due to degenerative and metabolic mechanisms22, while smoking has been linked to impaired tissue oxygenation and delayed musculoskeletal recovery23. Moreover, occupational role strongly influences exposure patterns—manual laborers face higher biomechanical loads, whereas supervisory or technical workers may be affected by prolonged static postures or psychosocial strain2,24–26.

Although several studies have explored multiple ergonomic and psychosocial determinants, findings regarding demographic and job-related predictors remain inconsistent across populations2,8,18–21. Recent investigations, including Kowe et al.27 in Social Sciences & Humanities Open, have emphasized the need for focused models that isolate core demographic and occupational variables to better understand their independent and interactive effects on MSP risk. Such an approach enhances interpretability, statistical precision, and applicability to workplace screening programs, especially in contexts where workers differ widely in origin, body composition, and job exposure.

Given that expatriates constitute the majority of the construction workforce in Saudi Arabia—with diverse cultural backgrounds, health behaviors, and occupational exposures—understanding how these selected factors (age, BMI, smoking, and job type) contribute to MSP is crucial for designing evidence-based prevention strategies.

Aims

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between age, BMI, smoking, and job categories with musculoskeletal pain among expatriate construction workers in Saudi Arabia.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a descriptive cross-sectional design with semi-structured face-to-face interviews to investigate musculoskeletal pain (MSP) prevalence and related factors among male construction workers in Saudi Arabia. A cross-sectional design was adopted as it effectively allowed researchers to assess MSP prevalence and its association with various demographic, occupational, and lifestyle factors simultaneously at a specific point in time.

Study setting

The study was conducted at multiple construction sites distributed across several urban regions in Saudi Arabia, representing diverse construction operations in terms of activities, scope, and size. Specifically, construction sites were selected based on diversity in job categories and accessibility for conducting research, ensuring a representative sample from the nine primary occupational categories prevalent in Saudi Arabia’s construction industry: masonry, carpentry, electrical works, plumbing, painting, scaffolding, steel fixing, welding, and general labor.

Participants were approached directly at their workplaces during designated breaks or after shifts, ensuring minimal disruption to work responsibilities and enhancing cooperation. The on-site interview setting enabled the researchers to observe real-time working conditions, facilitating the identification of ergonomic risk factors closely related to MSP. Interviews were carried out privately and quietly on-site whenever possible, thereby improving participant comfort and ensuring the reliability and accuracy of responses. The study was conducted over an eight-month period, from March 18, 2019, to November 10, 2019.

Outcome variables

The primary outcome of interest was the prevalence of musculoskeletal pain (MSP), defined as self-reported pain or discomfort in muscles, joints, tendons, ligaments, nerves, or bones during the past 12 months. This was evaluated using the Standardized Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (NMQ), a validated tool for assessing musculoskeletal symptoms across occupational settings28. The NMQ documented the presence or absence of pain across nine anatomical regions: neck, shoulders, upper back, lower back, elbows, wrists/hands, hips/thighs, knees, and ankles/feet. A binary classification (presence = 1, absence = 0) was used to comprehensively assess and describe the distribution of MSP among participants.

Secondary outcomes included demographic, physiological, lifestyle, and occupational variables potentially associated with MSP prevalence. Demographic data encompassed age, nationality, and smoking status. Age was categorized into predefined brackets (20–30, 31–40, 41–50, and 51–60 years) to allow subgroup analyses. Smoking status included classifications of current smokers, ex-smokers, and non-smokers. Physiological data included body mass index (BMI), calculated from self-reported height and weight and categorized according to World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines into underweight, normal, overweight, or obese29. Occupational data included participants’ job categories, which were classified into manual, skilled, or supervisory groups based on primary job roles and physical task demands30.

Study procedures

Male expatriate construction workers aged between 20 and 60 years were invited to participate, with a total sample size of 175 workers across nine main construction job categories. Recruitment involved direct approach by researchers at construction sites. Workers expressing willingness to participate were scheduled for face-to-face interviews conveniently timed to their work breaks or end of shifts, thus maximizing participation rates.

Data were collected via a structured English-language questionnaire specially designed for this study. This questionnaire systematically addressed demographic details (e.g., age, nationality), lifestyle behaviors (e.g., smoking), physiological parameters (BMI), occupational factors, and MSP prevalence within the previous year. To enhance clarity and response accuracy, bilingual interviewers proficient in the participants’ languages verbally translated each question during interviews.

Interviewers underwent standardized training to ensure uniformity in administration and response recording, significantly improving data reliability. Post-interview, data underwent thorough verification and cross-checking processes, resolving inconsistencies or omissions to ensure high-quality data integrity and accuracy prior to analysis.

Ethical considerations

All participants were informed about the study’s purpose, procedures, risks, confidentiality, and their right to withdraw at any time. Written informed consent was obtained. The study was approved by Ethics Sub-Committee, King Saud University (approval file ID: RRC-2018-006 on 10/04/2018) and followed the Declaration of Helsinki (2010). Confidentiality was ensured through anonymized, securely stored data with restricted access. Regular internal audits were conducted to ensure ethical compliance and uphold the study’s integrity throughout its duration.

Sample size estimation

The required sample size was estimated a priori using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) for association analyses between socio-demographic and occupational factors (age, BMI, smoking, and job category) and musculoskeletal pain (MSP). Parameters for a two-tailed chi-square test included a medium effect size (w = 0.3), α = 0.05, and power = 0.80, yielding a minimum of N = 122. Logistic-regression estimation (Hsieh et al. 1998) assuming an odds ratio = 2.0, MSP prevalence = 61%12, and four predictors indicated N ≈ 130. Allowing 25% attrition, a target of 173 participants was set to ensure 80–90% power to detect medium associations.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0 software (SPSS, IBM Inc.). Descriptive statistics (proportions, means, standard deviations) were calculated for age, smoking history, BMI, job, and presence of MSP. The chi-square test was used to investigate the association of age, BMI, smoking history, and job with MSP. The participants’ ages were divided into four groups (20–29 years, 30–39 years, 40–49 years, and 50–59 years). The BMI of the participants was also divided into four groups (underweight, normal weight, overweight, and obese). Smoking history was also divided into four groups (never smoking, ex-smokers, < 20 cigarettes/day, and > 20 cigarettes/day). Furthermore, regression analysis was carried out to assess the variable’s subgroups impact on MSP among laborers. The p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 175 participants (mean age 36.72 ± 7.22) were recruited, yielding a 50.43% response rate. Of the 172 excluded, 110 did not meet inclusion criteria, 47 lacked time, and 15 declined without reason. Most were Indian (40%), Bangladeshi (16.6%), or Jordanian (13.1%). Manual laborers comprised 25.7% of the sample, and 39.4% reported a history of smoking. Descriptive characteristics are presented in Table 2.

Table 4 presents the multivariate logistic regression analysis, offering insights into the factors influencing musculoskeletal pain (MSP). While several trends were observed, none of the predictors demonstrated statistical significance. Age groups 30–39 (β = 0.7285, p = 0.203) and 40–49 (β = 0.8422, p = 0.349) showed positive coefficients, indicating a potential increase in MSP risk compared to the reference group (20–29 years), though these effects were not statistically significant. Similarly, obesity (β = 0.4659, p = 0.593) and overweight status (β = 0.1342, p = 0.742) exhibited slight positive associations with MSP relative to normal BMI, but without significance. Smokers showed a marginally higher likelihood of MSP (β = 0.3268, p = 0.412) compared to non-smokers, and skilled jobs displayed a protective trend against MSP (β=−0.6389, p = 0.103) relative to physical labor. However, neither of these reached statistical significance. Gender (β=−0.0452, p = 0.875) was not a significant predictor of MSP. These results suggest potential trends but indicate a need for further research with larger sample sizes to confirm and refine these findings.

Discussion

Expatriate workers form the backbone of the construction industry in Saudi Arabia, performing the most physically demanding and ergonomically challenging tasks. Awareness, knowledge, and a better understanding of musculoskeletal pain (MSP) among these populations are critical for implementing effective preventive and ergonomic strategies tailored to their work environment17. Due to increased physical demands, repetitive manual work, and limited control over workplace conditions, these workers are particularly vulnerable to MSP and other occupational injuries31–33.

The present study aimed to determine the association between age, BMI, smoking, and job type with MSP among expatriate construction workers in Saudi Arabia. The observed MSP prevalence (49%) was lower than the global average reported among construction workers (approximately 77%)16. This relatively low prevalence may reflect differences in ergonomic practices, work organization, or cultural factors influencing pain perception and reporting among expatriate workers.

Despite the high physical demands of construction work, no significant associations were found between MSP and age, BMI, smoking status, or job category. This finding aligns with studies that have similarly reported non-significant relationships between these predictors and musculoskeletal disorders23,34,35. However, other studies have reported contradictory evidence, showing significant associations between MSP and demographic or lifestyle factors such as age, smoking, and BMI18,19,20,2122,36. These discrepancies likely stem from variations in sample size, occupational exposure, ergonomic standards, and population characteristics. For example, larger studies (e.g., 21, N = 3567) provided greater statistical power to detect small effects, whereas this study’s smaller sample (N = 175) limited the ability to detect subtle associations.

Furthermore, the high variability in reported MSP prevalence across countries may be influenced by differences in job security, psychosocial stress, socioeconomic conditions, and cultural attitudes toward pain reporting37,38. Many expatriate workers in Saudi Arabia are low-skilled laborers, often with limited access to health services or ergonomic interventions, which could contribute to underreporting or normalization of pain symptoms. This highlights the need for targeted awareness programs and routine musculoskeletal screening among expatriate workers, aiming to promote early detection and workplace adaptation.

The trends observed in this study suggest that age, BMI, smoking status, and job type may influence MSP risk, but none reached statistical significance in the multivariate model, likely due to overlapping effects or limited sample size. Nevertheless, identifying individual risk characteristics remains essential for informing tailored interventions. Preventive strategies such as ergonomic training, work-rest cycle optimization, and behavior-based health promotion (e.g., smoking cessation, weight management) could reduce the cumulative strain on musculoskeletal structures and enhance long-term productivity.

Practical implications

Although statistical associations were not significant, the findings carry relevant implications for occupational health practice. They underscore the need for preventive ergonomic interventions, including optimizing workstation design, reducing repetitive lifting, and encouraging safe posture and task rotation. Employers and policymakers should also promote regular musculoskeletal health screening and educational initiatives to enhance awareness of MSP risk factors among expatriate workers. Health-promotion programs targeting weight control and smoking cessation could further reduce modifiable risks. Integrating such measures into workplace safety policies may improve worker well-being, reduce absenteeism, and enhance productivity in the Saudi construction industry.

Limitations and future recommendations

This study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, only male participants were included, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to female construction workers, who may differ in body composition, physiological tolerance, and exposure patterns. Second, the relatively small sample size (N = 175) may have limited the statistical power to detect moderate associations, thereby increasing the risk of type II errors and potentially contributing to the non-significant findings observed for variables such as age, BMI, smoking, and job type. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, as temporal relationships between potential risk factors and MSP could not be established. Fourth, the use of self-reported data may introduce recall or reporting bias, particularly regarding pain experience and lifestyle factors such as smoking. Furthermore, the exclusion of psychosocial and ergonomic factors—such as work posture, repetitive movements, job strain, and work duration—represents another limitation, as these are well-documented contributors to MSP.

Future research should employ longitudinal designs with larger and more diverse samples, including both genders and multiple job categories, to enhance statistical power and generalizability. Incorporating objective ergonomic assessments and psychosocial risk evaluations would further strengthen understanding of the multifactorial influences on MSP among expatriate construction workers.

Conclusions

Musculoskeletal pain (MSP) among expatriate construction workers in Saudi Arabia was not significantly associated with age, BMI, smoking status, or job category. This suggests that other unmeasured factors—such as ergonomic load, psychosocial stress, or environmental conditions—may play a greater role in influencing MSP risk. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the cross-sectional design and modest sample size (N = 175), which may have limited the statistical power to detect moderate associations. Future longitudinal studies with larger and more diverse samples are warranted to identify causal pathways and comprehensive determinants of MSP in this vulnerable workforce.
