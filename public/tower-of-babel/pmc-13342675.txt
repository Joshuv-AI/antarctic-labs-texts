Simultaneous PET/fMRI distinguishes the separate and synergistic effects of T2DM and MCI on cerebral glucose metabolism and fALFF
Authors: Cuihua Zhao, Jiao Liu, Ruitian Cheng, Zheng Li, Zhi Zou, Zhonglin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a recognized risk factor for mild cognitive impairment (MCI), yet the combined effects of T2DM and MCI on cerebral metabolism and neural activity remain poorly characterized. Fifty-four participants were categorized into four groups based on the presence of T2DM and MCI. Simultaneous positron emission tomography (PET) and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) was used to assess cerebral glucose metabolism and the fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF). The main and interactive effects of T2DM and MCI were evaluated using the two-way analysis of variance. Partial correlation analyses were conducted to examine the relationships between clinical variables and cognitive scores and neuroimaging measures. Changes in glucose metabolism in the T2DM-MCI group were primarily attributable to T2DM, with significant alterations observed across frontal, occipital, and temporal lobes, as well as subcortical areas. Both main and interactive effects of T2DM and MCI were identified for fALFF. A synergistic interaction between T2DM and MCI was found specifically in the right amygdala, where their combined presence was associated with amplified intrinsic neural activity. Moreover, in the right angular gyrus (ANG), T2DM emerged as the predominant factor underlying concurrent reductions in glucose metabolism and fALFF. Clinically, fALFF in the left superior frontal gyrus-medial orbital showed a significant negative correlation with Montreal Cognitive Assessment scores in both MCI and T2DM-MCI groups, while fALFF in the right amygdala was positively correlated with Mini-Mental State Examination scores in the T2DM-MCI group. This PET/fMRI study establishes T2DM as a key driver of coupled metabolic and functional deficits in the right ANG and reveals a synergistic interaction with MCI that amplifies neural activity in the amygdala. These findings position the ANG and amygdala as potential early biomarkers for T2DM-related cognitive decline, offering new insights into mechanisms and potential therapies.

Introduction

Diabetes, with its high prevalence, significantly impairs health and quality of life while increasing the healthcare burden1. Projections indicate that the number of individuals living with diabetes will surpass 1.3 billion by 2050, with the majority diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)2. Chronic hyperglycemia is known to induce damage to the central nervous system, often initially presenting as mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and subsequently increasing the susceptibility to vascular dementia, mixed dementia, and Alzheimer’s disease (AD)3. A growing body of evidence further links T2DM directly to aberrant brain function1,3–5. Therefore, clarifying the shared neural mechanisms underlying T2DM and cognitive decline is critical for developing targeted interventions and slowing neurodegenerative progression1,3–5.

Positron emission tomography (PET) and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) remain among the most widely utilized non-invasive modalities for mapping human brain function6,7. Consequently, an increasing number of studies have adopted these imaging techniques to explore cerebral alterations in T2DM patients8–10. For instance, Natalia et al. used PET to demonstrate that middle-aged individuals with T2DM exhibit reduced glucose metabolism in fronto-temporal regions, with these metabolic deficits being significantly correlated with elevated glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) levels8. Similarly, the co-occurrence of MCI and T2DM has been linked to diminished glucose metabolism, reflected by decreased 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) uptake in the frontal lobe, sensorimotor cortex, and striatum9. Li et al. examined the interactive effects of aging and T2DM on cerebral metabolism, identifying three key alterations in elderly T2DM patients: glucose hypometabolism, enhanced functional integration, and elevated information transfer efficiency. These alterations were predominantly localized within cognition-related brain networks10.

In fMRI studies, multiple analytical approaches have been widely employed to delineate spatiotemporal patterns of neural dysregulation associated with cognitive impairment in T2DM1. For instance, studies applying degree centrality (DC) mapping have identified distinct disruptions in functional connectivity (FC) density within networks responsible for cognitive control, visual processing, and memory consolidation among T2DM patients with comorbid MCI11–13. Relative to healthy controls (HCs), individuals with T2DM demonstrate elevated regional homogeneity (ReHo) in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), alongside reduced ReHo in the medial superior frontal gyrus. Seed-based FC analyses further indicate impaired frontal-parietal connectivity, reflecting disruptions in both local and long-range neural synchronization that may underlie T2DM-related cognitive decline14. Additionally, multiple investigations have documented alterations in the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) across various brain regions in T2DM, which correlate with deficits in multiple domains, including cognitive performance, visual information processing, executive function, and emotional regulation15–17. Further supporting these observations, decreased ALFF and ReHo in occipital and postcentral areas have been linked to poorer cognitive outcomes, with specific reductions noted in subregions such as the cuneus and lingual gyrus18.

In summary, multimodal PET and fMRI studies have systematically characterized brain functional abnormalities in individuals with T2DM and those with comorbid T2DM-MCI, yielding substantial insights. Nevertheless, several key limitations have not been adequately addressed. First, the lack of rigorous experimental designs, such as groups for T2DM-only, MCI-only, T2DM-MCI, and HCs, has prevented a precise quantification of the distinct and interactive effects of T2DM and MCI on cerebral metabolic and functional activity. Second, it remains unclear whether MCI and T2DM exert synergistic or antagonistic effects on brain metabolism and function. Third, existing PET and fMRI evidence is constrained by cohort heterogeneity and inconsistent data acquisition protocols, which introduce temporal and state-related confounds, thereby limiting the establishment of reliable spatiotemporal coupling models across imaging modalities.

Integrated PET/fMRI has emerged as a state-of-the-art neuroimaging platform that enables simultaneous measurement of cerebral glucose metabolism (via PET), functional activity (via fMRI), and brain structure (via MRI), thereby overcoming fundamental issues of multimodal data misalignment19,20. This integrated approach offers a unique opportunity to investigate both independent and synergistic effects of T2DM and MCI on neural activity in terms of metabolic–functional coupling, with potential implications for clarifying disease mechanisms and guiding early intervention. In parallel, the ALFF and its normalized variant, fractional ALFF (fALFF), serve as widely used fMRI metrics for quantifying intrinsic brain activity15–17,21,22. ALFF reflects the total signal power within the low-frequency range (e.g., 0.01–0.10 Hz), whereas fALFF measures the relative contribution of this frequency band to the full frequency spectrum, thereby offering improved specificity to physiological noise15–17,21,22. Given its computational efficiency, sensitivity to metabolic and pharmacological modulation, and closer concordance with PET-derived metabolic patterns, fALFF is increasingly regarded as a robust metric for probing metabolic–functional coupling in cerebral disorders such as T2DM21,22.

To address these limitations, we used integrated PET/MRI to obtain synchronized neuroimaging data from four well-matched groups: HCs, patients with MCI only, patients with T2DM only, and those with T2DM-MCI comorbidity. Using cerebral glucose metabolism and fALFF maps, we applied a 2 × 2 factorial design and a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to quantify the main effects of T2DM and MCI, as well as their interaction effect. We further conducted partial correlation analyses to examine the relationships between clinical indicators and the observed metabolic and fALFF alterations. This approach aimed to construct a comprehensive network linking metabolism, neural function, and clinical measures. We expect that this multimodal framework will provide neuroimaging evidence conducive to the identification of early biomarkers for diabetic encephalopathy. Based on prior evidence, we hypothesized that T2DM and MCI interact to alter intrinsic neural activity, and that these neural alterations would be associated with specific clinical indicators and neuropsychological performance.

Materials and methods

Participants

From December 2023 to July 2024, patients diagnosed with T2DM were recruited from the Department of Endocrinology at The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. The T2DM group inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) meeting the World Health Organization 1999 criteria10,14,16 for T2DM: fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or 2-hour postprandial blood glucose (2 h-PBG) ≥ 11.1 mmol/L during an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT); (2) a disease duration of > 1 year; (3) age between 18 and 70 years; (4) right-handedness, as assessed by a Chinese-specific handedness inventory comprising 10 items; (5) no history of alcohol or substance abuse or dependence; (6) absence of intracranial abnormalities or significant head trauma, as confirmed on T2-weighted dark-fluid and T1-weighted imaging (T1WI); (7) no psychiatric or neurological disorders (e.g., depression, dementia); (8) no metabolic conditions such as hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism; (9) no use of medications affecting cerebral function or perfusion within the preceding three weeks; (10) no contraindications to MRI; and (11) absence of severe diabetes-related complications. HCs were required to meet the following criteria: (1) FBG < 6.1 mmol/L and 2 h-PBG < 7.8 mmol/L10,14,16; and (2) fulfillment of criteria 3–10 listed for the T2DM group.

Participants were categorized into four groups based on Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA, Beijing version) scores and FBG levels11,12,23 as follows: HCs: MoCA ≥ 26 and FBG < 6.1 mmol/L; MCI: MoCA score of 18–25 and FBG < 6.1 mmol/L; T2DM: MoCA ≥ 26 and FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; T2DM-MCI: MoCA score of 18–25 and FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L.

Demographics, clinical characteristics, and neurocognitive testing

We collected general characteristics, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and years of education. Fasting blood samples were drawn in the morning (between 7:00 and 8:00 AM) after an overnight fast of at least 10 h. The following biochemical parameters were measured by the hospital clinical laboratory: FBG, HbA1c, fasting insulin (FINS), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), free triiodothyronine (FT3), free thyroxine (FT4), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), serum creatinine (SCR), and uric acid (UA). An OGTT was performed to assess 2 h-PBG, along with postprandial C-peptide (PC-P) and insulin levels. The HOMA-IR index was evaluated using the HOMA2 calculator (University of Oxford, Oxford, UK). In addition, participants completed several standardized assessments, including the Chinese Handedness Questionnaire, the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test, the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence, and an MRI safety screening form. Cognitive function was evaluated using the MoCA, a brief and sensitive tool widely used for MCI screening23. To more comprehensively reflect cognitive impairment, we also performed the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)23. All neuropsychological tests were administered on the same day as the PET/MRI imaging by trained research staff.

Data acquisition

All participants were instructed to abstain from insulin, hypoglycemic medications, alcohol, caffeine, tea, and any other substances known to influence cerebral blood flow (CBF) for at least 12 h prior to the examination. After a fasting period of no less than 6 h, each subject received an intravenous injection of the radioactive tracer ¹⁸F-FDG at a dose of 3.7 MBq/kg. Following tracer administration, participants rested supine for 25–30 min in a dimly lit, quiet environment to minimize extraneous sensory input and allow for consistent cerebral ¹⁸F-FDG uptake. Before imaging, all subjects were asked to empty their bladder, and blood glucose levels were confirmed to be below 11.1 mmol/L using a portable glucometer. ¹⁸F-FDG was synthesized on-site in the hospital’s PET/CT Center on the day of the examination. Production was performed using a CFN-100 synthesis module and an HM-20 cyclotron (Sumitomo Heavy Industries, Japan), and all batches confirmed a radiochemical purity of > 95%.

PET/MRI data were acquired at the hospital’s medical imaging center using a 3T Siemens Biograph mMR system (Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 16-channel head coil. To enhance comfort and minimize motion artifacts, participants were provided with earplugs and foam padding, and medical tape was gently applied across the forehead to further restrict head movement. PET data were acquired in list-mode for a duration of 10 min, starting 50 min after tracer injection. During both the PET and fMRI acquisitions, participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed. PET data were reconstructed in 3D mode using the ordinary Poisson ordered-subset expectation maximization algorithm with the following parameters: 3 subsets, 21 iterations, 2-mm Gaussian filter, 344 × 344 matrix size, 2.0-mm slice thickness, and 2.3 mm × 2.3 mm × 2.0 mm voxel size.

High-resolution T1WI were acquired with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2300 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.96 ms, field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm², matrix size = 256 × 256, 176 slices, slice thickness = 1 mm, and flip angle = 9°. Prior to further processing, all structural images were reoriented to align with the anterior commissure–posterior commissure (AC-PC) plane using a center-of-mass approach. Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) data were acquired using an echo-planar imaging sequence with 270 volumes acquired over 540 s. The acquisition parameters were as follows: TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, FOV = 224 × 224 mm², matrix size = 64 × 64, 37 slices, slice thickness = 3.5 mm, and flip angle = 90°.

PET and MRI data processing

PET imaging processing

PET data were processed using SPM12 (implemented in MATLAB 2018a) as follows10: (1) Conversion of PET and 3D T1WI data to NIFTI format. (2) Coregistration of PET data to the corresponding 3D T1WI structural image. (3) Anatomical segmentation. (4) Spatial normalization using parameters derived from segmentation. (5) Spatial smoothing with a 10-mm full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) kernel (FWHM)24. (6) Calculation of the regional standard uptake value ratio (SUVr) using the whole cerebellum as the reference region25. Visual quality control was conducted on all NIFTI and normalized PET images; subjects with images of unsatisfactory quality were excluded from subsequent analysis.

fALFF calculation

Rs-fMRI data preprocessing and fALFF analyses were conducted using the Data Processing and Analysis of Brain Imaging (DPABI) toolbox, running under MATLAB 2018a. The preprocessing pipeline included the following steps: (1) Data Conversion and Stabilization: Raw rs-fMRI and 3D T1WI were converted to the NIFTI format. The first 10 time points of the rs-fMRI data were removed to allow for magnetic field stabilization. (2) Slice Timing Correction: Temporal differences in slice acquisition times were corrected. (3) Head Motion Correction: Functional volumes were realigned to correct for head motion artifacts. (4) Coregistration to Structural Image: Rs-fMRI data were coregistered to each participant’s high-resolution 3D T1WI structural image. (5) Tissue Segmentation: Anatomical images were segmented into gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid. (6) Nuisance Covariate Regression: Several potential confounding signals were regressed out, including those from cerebrospinal fluid and white matter, 24 head motion parameters (as per the Friston-24 model), and the global mean signal. (7) Spatial Normalization: Both functional and structural images were normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using the transformation parameters estimated during the segmentation step. (8) Spatial Smoothing: A Gaussian smoothing kernel with a 6 mm FWHM was applied21,22. (9) fALFF Calculation: The ALFF was calculated for each voxel by transforming the time series to the frequency domain using a Fast Fourier Transform, obtaining the power spectrum, and taking the square root of the power averaged within the low-frequency band (0.01–0.1 Hz). The fractional ALFF (fALFF) was then computed as the ratio of the power within the 0.01–0.1 Hz range to the total power across the entire frequency spectrum15–17,21,22. Of note, no band-pass filtering was applied during fALFF computation. Finally, to facilitate cross-subject comparison, each voxel’s fALFF value was scaled by the individual’s global mean fALFF value.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical data analysis

Statistical analyses of demographic and clinical data were conducted using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed data were summarized as median and interquartile range. For normally distributed continuous variables with homogeneous variance, group differences were assessed using a one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc tests; otherwise, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was employed. Categorical variables (e.g., sex) were compared using the Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test, which is appropriate when expected cell counts are less than five26.

PET and fMRI data analysis

To investigate the effects of MCI and T2DM on cerebral glucose metabolism, a two-way ANCOVA was performed in SPM12, controlling for age, sex, and education. In this study, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied at the peak level for multiple comparisons, using a threshold of q < 0.05. Regions that showed significant alterations after FDR correction were identified using the Automated Anatomical Labeling 3 atlas template27, and their SUVr values were extracted for post-hoc analysis. We further extracted fALFF values from these regions derived from the glucose metabolism analysis to test for similar effects on neural activity. A subsequent two-way ANCOVA was then performed on the fALFF values using SPSS 26.0, controlling for the same covariates. Multiple comparisons across the extracted regions were addressed by employing FDR correction (q < 0.05) in MATLAB 2018a.

Brain-behavior partial correlation analysis

We identified brain regions showing significant group differences in the two-way ANOVA and extracted their mean SUVr and fALFF values for each group. Next, we performed partial correlation analyses, controlling for age, sex, and education, to evaluate the associations between these imaging measures (SUVr and fALFF) and clinical or blood biomarkers (e.g., MoCA, HbA1c). The significance of all reported correlations was determined using FDR correction for multiple comparisons (q < 0.05) in MATLAB 2018a.

These participants were categorized into four groups: HCs (n = 14), MCI (n = 13), T2DM (n = 12), and T2DM-MCI (n = 15). Demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. The groups differed significantly in age, education, FBG, HbA1c, 2 h-PBG, MoCA, and MMSE scores, while no significant differences were observed in the remaining variables. Given these baseline differences, age, sex, and education were included as covariates in all subsequent statistical analyses.

PET analysis

The two-way ANOVA revealed neither a significant interaction between MCI and T2DM nor a significant main effect of MCI (q < 0.05, FDR-corrected). However, T2DM showed a significant main effect in multiple brain regions (q < 0.05, FDR-corrected). Group differences in mean SUVr were assessed by comparing the T2DM and T2DM-MCI groups against the HCs and MCI groups. Decreases in SUVr were localized to four primary clusters. These included regions in the right hemisphere, including the middle frontal gyrus (MFG), superior frontal gyrus-dorsolateral (SFG), inferior frontal gyrus (triangular, opercular, and orbital parts; IFGtriang, IFGoperc, IFGorb), middle/superior/inferior occipital gyri (MOG, SOG, IOG), cuneus, angular gyrus (ANG), and calcarine fissure and surrounding cortex (CAL); while left-hemisphere regions comprised the SFG, MOG, superior frontal gyrus-medial (SFGmedial), IFGtriang, superior frontal gyrus-medial orbital (PFCventmed), cuneus, CAL, and SOG. Increases in SUVr were identified within three clusters, encompassing the left parahippocampal gyrus (PHG), amygdala, temporal pole (superior and middle temporal gyri; TPOsup, TPOmid), and inferior temporal gyrus (ITG); the right amygdala, TPOsup, and PHG; as well as bilateral regions including the subgenual and pregenual ACC (ACCsub, ACCpre), PFCventmed, and olfactory cortex (OLF). These patterns of increased and decreased SUVr suggest underlying neuropathology specific to the T2DM and T2DM-MCI groups relative to the HCs and MCI groups. The spatial distributions of the altered cerebral glucose metabolism are illustrated in Fig. 1, and detailed information on the significant clusters is provided in Table 2.

fALFF analysis

Based on fALFF values measured in regions with altered cerebral glucose metabolism, the two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of both MCI and T2DM, as well as a significant MCI × T2DM interaction. The main effect of MCI was characterized by decreased fALFF in the right SFG, right SOG, left SFG, and left PFCventmed in the MCI and T2DM-MCI groups relative to the HCs and T2DM groups. The main effect of T2DM was reflected in decreased fALFF in the right ANG in the T2DM and T2DM-MCI groups relative to the HCs and MCI groups. A significant MCI × T2DM interaction was identified in the right amygdala. Brain regions exhibiting significant effects are displayed in Fig. 2, color-coded for the main effect of MCI (green), the main effect of T2DM (orange), and the MCI × T2DM interaction (blue) on fALFF. Figure 3 illustrates the fALFF values in this region across the four groups. Specifically, individuals with MCI but without T2DM exhibited increased fALFF compared to HCs. Similarly, individuals with both T2DM and MCI showed increased fALFF compared to those with T2DM alone. This pattern indicates a synergistic interaction between MCI and T2DM on neural activity in the right amygdala. Discussion

Using an integrated PET/fMRI approach, this study characterized the independent and interactive neurobiological effects of T2DM and MCI, and explored their associations with clinical measures. We demonstrated that cerebral metabolic disturbances in T2DM-MCI patients were primarily associated with T2DM, showing a regional pattern involving frontal, occipital, and temporal lobes, alongside specific subcortical structures. Analysis of fALFF further revealed significant main effects of each condition and a significant interaction effect in the right amygdala. The right ANG was identified as a key region where T2DM served as the principal factor affecting both metabolic and fALFF profiles. Importantly, clinical metrics such as MoCA, FBG, and 2 h-PBG showed significant correlations with the observed metabolic and fALFF changes in brain regions showing significant main or interaction effects.

T2DM-driven alterations in brain metabolism

Our study reveals a distinct spatial pattern of cerebral glucose metabolism in T2DM, characterized by concurrent regional reductions and elevations. Metabolic reductions were predominantly observed in the frontal lobe, including the bilateral SFG, bilateral IFGtriang, left SFGmedial, right MFG, right IFGoperc, and right IFGorb; the occipital lobe, including the bilateral SOG, bilateral MOG, bilateral cuneus, bilateral CAL, and right IOG; and the right ANG. In contrast, metabolic increases were identified in dorsomedial frontal areas such as the bilateral PFCventmed, bilateral ACCsub, and right ACCpre; subcortical structures including the bilateral PHG and bilateral amygdala; and the temporal lobe, encompassing the bilateral OLF, left TPOsup, left TPOmid, left ITG, and right TPOsup.

Regarding regions of hypometabolism, previous studies have reported reduced CBF in the occipital lobe among T2DM individuals, which correlates with impairments in visuospatial function and visual memory28,29. Consistent with these findings, our results revealed that MoCA scores showed significant positive correlations with SUVr values in the left MOG and left SOG across all participants. Moreover, within the T2DM and T2DM-MCI groups, MoCA scores were also positively correlated with SUVr in the bilateral cuneus, CAL, and right SOG. The ANG has been recognized as a hub for diverse cognitive processes and may serve as a neuroimaging biomarker for T2DM-related cognitive decline30. A previous PET study further demonstrated that a lower regional cerebral glucose metabolic rate in the ANG is associated with an increased risk of AD in individuals with pre-diabetes or T2DM31. In addition, cognitive impairment in T2DM (particularly executive function deficits) has been linked to decreased frontal lobe metabolism8,9. Our study not only corroborates this association but also identifies significant negative correlations between 2 h-PBG and SUVr in the right IFGtriang and right MFG, thereby providing further neurobiological evidence for this relationship. In summary, the spatial pattern of diminished cerebral glucose metabolism in T2DM patients reflects a neurobiological substrate underlying domain-specific cognitive deficits.

Regions of elevated metabolism, such as the amygdala, parahippocampal gyrus, and ACC, which are key components of the limbic system, play critical roles in emotional regulation and memory processing32. The amygdala, in particular, is involved in emotion, cognition, and information relay, and may contribute to central nervous system dysfunction in T2DM32. Altered metabolic activity in the amygdala has been associated with FBG and HbA1c levels, and heightened activity may predict increased risk of disease progression33,34. Intervention-induced metabolic reductions in this region have also been reported in some T2DM cohorts33. In amyloid PET-positive participants, individuals with diabetes showed significantly higher tau PET SUVr in the medial temporal lobe, including the bilateral amygdala, hippocampus, and entorhinal cortex, compared to non-diabetic controls25. Furthermore, Queralt et al. observed that patients with T2DM exhibited increased metabolic activity in the left amygdala and hippocampus at baseline relative to during a hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp35. Increased CBF, referred to as hyperperfusion, in the ACC has also been reported in T2DM patients compared to HCs. The ACC is a region essential for cognitive control36. The WMS-III Text Recall subtest has been correlated with glucose metabolism in the bilateral subgenual ACC, orbital prefrontal cortex, right parahippocampal gyrus, and right ACC37. In our study, FBG was positively correlated with SUVr in the bilateral subgenual ACC and right pregenual ACC among patients with T2DM and T2DM-MCI. It has been well-established that patients with T2DM exhibit decreased brain volume, gray matter density, and ALFF in the temporal lobe, structural and functional alterations linked to cognitive decline38,39. Paradoxically, studies focusing on diabetic retinopathy have revealed increased CBF in the left MTG within this patient subgroup38,39. Taken together, we propose that elevated cerebral metabolic activity in T2DM may reflect a compensatory neural mechanism that serves to mitigate mitigating cognitive and emotional impairment.

Independent and interactive effects of MCI and T2DM on fALFF

The pathophysiological signature of MCI is characterized by a reduction in fALFF, predominantly localized to the bilateral SFG (part of dorsolateral prefrontal cortex), and left PFCventmed (medial orbital region of the SFG). These areas are integral nodes of the default mode network (DMN), which underlies higher-order cognitive processes such as working memory, emotional regulation, and decision-making40–42. Further corroborating prior evidence, we identified a significant negative correlation between fALFF in the left PFCventmed and MoCA scores among individuals with MCI and T2DM-MCI. The apparent discrepancy between the group-level reduction in left PFCventmed fALFF and the negative correlation between left PFCventmed fALFF and MoCA scores within the MCI cohort is consistent with a non-linear, inverted U-shaped trajectory of functional compensation43. At the group level, the MCI cohort may be predominantly composed of individuals who have passed the peak of compensatory hyperactivation and entered a phase of functional decline, which could account for a reduced mean fALFF relative to HCs. However, within the MCI spectrum, higher fALFF in the left PFCventmed may reflect residual, albeit inefficient, compensatory drive. Individuals with higher cognitive scores (i.e., higher MoCA scores) exhibit greater local synchrony in this anterior default mode network hub, likely as a response to posterior network failure44. This negative intra-group correlation therefore captures the transient, exhausting effort of the prefrontal cortex prior to eventual network collapse, rather than contradicting the overall disease-related decline.

Notably, these functionally impaired regions also demonstrate structural degeneration, as reflected by gray matter volume loss in the SFG, particularly in the left hemisphere, in patients with Parkinson’s disease-related MCI45,46. The coexistence of functional and structural deficits suggests that cognitive decline in MCI may arise from synergistic deterioration of critical prefrontal nodes within the DMN. The specificity of SFG impairment is further reinforced by independent investigations. For instance, Yang et al. documented decreased fALFF in the right SFG among patients with amnestic MCI47, a finding that echoes the regional susceptibility observed in our cohort. Moreover, the therapeutic relevance of prefrontal regions is underscored by neuromodulation studies. A meta-analysis by Pievani et al. identified the MFG and SFG as key stimulation targets in AD and frontotemporal dementia48, while a systematic review by Chou et al. demonstrated that high-frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation targeting the left dorsolateral PFC significantly enhances memory performance49. In summary, convergent evidence from neuroimaging and neuromodulation research solidifies the role of the SFG and associated prefrontal DMN regions as pivotal hubs in MCI pathophysiology. These consistent results underscore their potential as biomarkers for early diagnosis and longitudinal tracking of disease progression.

The primary pathophysiological effect of T2DM is reflected in reduced fALFF, predominantly observed in the right ANG. As a pivotal hub for cross-modal integration, the ANG supports a wide spectrum of cognitive processes50. It plays a central role in language comprehension and processing50, and is further implicated in memory, attentional control, and spatial cognition51. Damage to this region is well-established as a cause of diverse cognitive impairments, including aphasia, finger agnosia, spatial disorientation, acalculia, and agraphia30,52–54. Notably, structural and functional abnormalities in the ANG have been identified in T2DM patients even in the absence of MCI30,52,55. For instance, Wang et al. reported reduced ReHo in this area52, while another study has documented impaired FC involving the ANG30. Furthermore, Wu et al. demonstrated that whole-brain FC patterns anchored in the ANG significantly contributed to distinguishing between T2DM-MCI, T2DM, and HCs groups55. Collectively, these findings indicate that T2DM is associated with functional disruptions in the ANG, reflected by both aberrant local neural activity and impaired integration within broader brain networks. Thus, the ANG represents a promising neuroimaging biomarker for the early detection of T2DM-related cognitive decline.

The pathophysiological interplay between MCI and T2DM exerts synergistic effects on the fALFF in the right amygdala. As a core region of the salience network, the amygdala is instrumental in detecting behaviorally relevant stimuli and coordinating dynamic interactions among large-scale functional networks56. It supports the identification and integration of emotionally salient and sensory information, directs attentional resources, and regulates the shift between internally and externally focused cognitive states56. Consistent with earlier discussion, the amygdala coordinates emotional processing, cognitive functions, and information transfer, and is closely linked to central nervous system impairments in T2DM33. Previous multimodal neuroimaging studies have further linked structural and functional abnormalities of the amygdala to cognitive impairment in amnestic MCI, schizophrenia, and late-onset depression55,57,58. In the present study, individuals with MCI but without T2DM exhibited increased fALFF in the right amygdala compared to HCs. Notably, volumetric changes in AD-related brain regions can emerge more than a decade before clinical symptom onset, with the hippocampus and amygdala being the earliest and most severely affected structures1. In the early stages of MCI, as hippocampal function declines, the amygdala, which is anatomically adjacent to the hippocampus and functionally overlaps with it (particularly on the right side), initiates “functional compensation” to partially assume its duties59. Studies indicate that intrinsic functional connectivity between the basolateral amygdala and the hippocampus, as well as between the basolateral amygdala and frontoparietal regions, progressively weakens from MCI to the dementia stage60. Furthermore, a study confirmed that as task difficulty increased, the activation intensity of the amygdala in MCI/AD patients was significantly higher than that observed in healthy controls61. Thus, we speculate that elevated right amygdala fALFF in MCI reflects exhausting compensation rather than functional superiority, wherein heightened local neuronal synchrony preserves emotional memory and network homeostasis amid hippocampal decay and diminished connectivity. Similarly, among T2DM subjects, those with MCI also showed elevated fALFF in the same region relative to non-T2DM individuals. This pattern of hyperactivation aligns with the findings of Zhang et al., who reported increased fALFF in the same region among patients with amnestic MCI62. Collectively, these results suggest that fALFF in the right amygdala may represent a promising functional biomarker for the early identification of cognitive decline in individuals with T2DM.

Significant main effect of T2DM on brain metabolism and fALFF

Notably, T2DM emerged as the primary driver of coupled reductions in metabolic activity and fALFF within the right ANG. These alterations are likely attributable to the combined effects of multiple pathological mechanisms. First, chronic hyperglycemia and insulin resistance can trigger a cascade of responses in the brain, including aberrant insulin signaling, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation, which collectively impair the structure and function of a neurovascular system, leading to dysregulated neuronal metabolism, reduced synaptic transmission efficiency63–65, and ultimately disrupting normal metabolic activity in the ANG32. Second, as a critical hub integrating multiple large-scale brain networks such as the default network and the executive control network, the ANG is responsible for demanding cross-modal information integration32. Consequently, it exhibits exceptionally high energy metabolism and blood supply demands, which renders it particularly vulnerable to pathological insults induced by T2DM. Under T2DM conditions, impaired synaptic function, altered white matter microstructure, and neurotransmitter dysregulation further compromise the region’s high-level integrative capacity1,5. This results in reduced intrinsic neural activity, decreased metabolic demand, and ultimately manifests as synchronized declines in fALFF and the cerebral glucose metabolic rate. In summary, fALFF and metabolic measures in the right ANG represent promising neuroimaging biomarkers for assessing the risk of cognitive decline in T2DM and elucidating its underlying neural mechanisms. Future studies should employ long-term longitudinal tracking to further validate the clinical utility of these biomarkers in predicting individual cognitive trajectories and explore targeted intervention strategies aimed at preserving the functional integrity of this brain region.

Lateralization differences in the brain and behavior are widespread in the animal kingdom and play an important role in sensory, cognitive, and motor efficiency66. Studies have shown that the degree of right hippocampal atrophy is more pronounced than that of the left in patients with T2DM, which may represent one of the potential mechanisms underlying their cognitive dysfunction67. Long et al. found that both type 1 and type 2 diabetes exert detrimental effects on the right hippocampus, suggesting that diabetes may contribute to cognitive decline by promoting neurodegeneration of the right hippocampus in the European population68. The ANG and the hippocampus are closely connected in both structure and function, and their coordinated activity is a key component of the visuospatial memory network69. Based on these observations, it is plausible that structural damage and reduced functional output of the right hippocampus could, via neural connections, affect its downstream target region (the right ANG), leading to decreased synaptic input and reduced energy demand. This, in turn, may ultimately manifest as a compensatory or even pathological reduction in the synchrony of glucose metabolism and spontaneous neural activity (fALFF). Together, these changes might be involved in the development and progression of common cognitive dysfunctions associated with T2DM, such as visuospatial memory impairment. It should be noted, however, that these interpretations await further support from additional experimental evidence.

Conclusion

This simultaneous PET/fMRI study establishes T2DM as a key contributor to coupled disruptions in cerebral glucose metabolism and fALFF, with core impairments localized to the right ANG. Furthermore, we uncovered a synergistic interaction between T2DM and MCI on fALFF in the right amygdala, indicating that their co-occurrence is associated with amplified intrinsic neural activity in this region. Our analyses also demonstrated that fALFF in the left PFCventmed is inversely correlated with MoCA scores in both MCI and T2DM-MCI groups, whereas fALFF in the right amygdala is positively correlated with MMSE scores specifically in the T2DM-MCI group. By integrating metabolic (PET) and functional (fMRI) neuroimaging, this work systematically delineates the interplay between MCI and T2DM, highlighting alterations in the ANG and amygdala as potential early neuroimaging biomarkers of T2DM-related cognitive decline. These findings offer novel mechanistic and therapeutic insights into these comorbid conditions.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the absence of a systematic evaluation for cerebral small vessel disease (e.g., white matter hyperintensities, cerebral microbleeds) hinders our ability to disentangle metabolic alterations specifically related to T2DM from those associated with concomitant microangiopathy. Second, diabetic complications such as retinopathy and nephropathy may synergistically exacerbate cerebral metabolic dysfunction; future studies should therefore incorporate adjusted analyses to control for these potential confounders. Third, longitudinal data are necessary to establish causal relationships between the observed metabolic/fALFF changes and cognitive progression. Fourth, the limited sample size (n = 54) may have reduced statistical power and generalizability, highlighting the necessity of validation in larger, independent cohorts. Fifth, it should be noted that the traditional FDR correction applied in this analysis assumes mutual independence among voxels. While this approach controls the expected proportion of false discoveries, it may be more susceptible to Type I error in the presence of spatial correlation. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with this caveat in mind. Finally, we primarily used glucose metabolism and fALFF as localized measures of diabetes-related cerebral dysfunction without directly examining FC. Future longitudinal studies should integrate both localized measures and FC to investigate the causal pathway from focal damage to network disintegration and ultimately to cognitive impairment.
