Resolving mesoscale brainstem-prefrontal-striatal pathways underlying decisions upon salient events using submillimeter-resolution fMRI
Authors: Yuhui Chai, Joshua Oon Soo Goh, Bradley P. Sutton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Value-based decision-making engages brain-wide motivational, cognitive, and motor processes. Yet, information integration and gating that culminate in immediate decisions upon salient events likely occur within small neural nuclei and cortical layers at the mesoscale not resolved with conventional human neuroimaging. Using submillimeter-resolution 7 T functional MRI with acquisition-matched anatomical references and a lottery choice task incorporating salient superhigh stakes, we dissociated mesoscale operations spanning a brainstem-prefrontal-striatal pathway during choice and outcome processing. The locus coeruleus, caudate, and prefrontal cortex showed enhanced activity during superhigh-stake choices, while the substantia nigra/ventral tegmental area and nucleus accumbens additionally distinguished gains from losses. In contrast, gray-matter bridges between caudate and putamen were associated with faster responses. Laminar analyses revealed deeper prefrontal layers predominating during choice selection and superficial layers during outcome evaluation. Here, we show a mesoscale framework integrating brainstem modulation, striatal gating, and laminar cortical computation in human decision-making upon salient events.

Introduction

When making decisions in response to encountered stimuli, the brain must integrate upstream sensory information from the environment with internally stored knowledge to calculate the values of potential downstream outputs1–3. Such valuation processing unfolds through a sequence of interlinked stages, spanning sensory salience detection and perception, contextual integration and deliberation, action selection and motor execution, and finally outcome updating4,5. Importantly, these processes have been attributed to key neuronal mediators within the brainstem, striatum, and prefrontal cortex (PFC) that form the value-based decision-processing pathway6–9. At present, however, the joint systemic contributions of these regions to a given immediate decision in humans remain under-specified, constraining our understanding and control of the neurocomputational outcomes.

A key limitation is that conventional in vivo human neuroimaging typically operates at voxel sizes of approximately 27 mm3, corresponding to a unit of >400,000 neurons10, and spanning several cortical layers. Yet, pertaining to the hierarchical neural coding of stimuli value, the critical neural substrates gating value information that determine response selection after stimuli recognition might theoretically require neurons only in the order of tens of thousands11 confined to specific cortical layers or small subcortical nuclei. In particular, the human locus coeruleus (LC), which gates phasic and tonic attentional salience to events6, consists of ~30,000 neurons12. In the PFC, long-range projections and local feedback signals are differentially processed across distinct layers7,13,14 within a gray matter sheet around 3 mm thick15 before transmission to other regions. Another intriguing yet understudied element is the caudolenticular gray matter bridge (GMB), a tract ~1 mm thick connecting the caudate and putamen16,17, whose location suggests a role in intra-striatal gating from internal deliberative processes to decision execution18. Likewise, dopaminergic brainstem hubs essential for reward-related gating are confined to small dimensions: the substantia nigra (SN) is ~5 mm thick19, whereas the ventral tegmental area (VTA) spans less than 3 mm between SN and red nucleus20. These instances suggest that critical gating computations for immediate decision responses, where only integrated inputs that surpass a threshold induce nuclei to generate outputs, occur at the mesoscale, which is spatially and functionally intermediate between local microcircuits and large-scale brain systems. As such, validation of the differential processing roles of the above structures during a single decision computation instance remains beyond the resolving power of standard neuroimaging.

Here, we leverage recent advancements in ultra-high field (7 Tesla), submillimeter-resolution functional MRI (fMRI) paired with acquisition-matched anatomical imaging21,22 to simultaneously dissociate the in vivo functional responses of the small gray matter nuclei and laminar structures implicated in the decision processing pathway with greater spatial precision. Specifically, we integrate across several studies to propose and validate a brainstem-prefrontal-striatal framework (Fig. 1) that links systems-level decision processes to functional signatures of key mesoscale structures as follows. We expect that when encountering a given salient stimulus, (1) the LC globally modulates the brainstem-prefrontal-striatal pathway and large-scale networks when high stimuli value salience is detected6,23; (2) the GMB facilitates translation of evaluative decision signals in the caudate for motor execution processing in the putamen18; (3) striatal subregions (NAc, caudate, and putamen) serve dissociable decision-related roles in processing motivational and goal evaluation, motor implementation, and outcome reward8,9,18; and (4) PFC layers generally exhibit task-context specific roles, with deep layers dominating during choice deliberation and selection, and superficial layers during outcome evaluation13,14,24.Fig. 1The integrated brainstem-prefrontal-striatal framework underlying decision-making examined at the mesoscale in this study.In this framework, different cortical layers in the prefrontal cortex are involved in distinct stages of decision-making, while striatal subregions serve specialized functional roles: the caudate in motivation, the putamen in motor execution processing, and the nucleus accumbens (NAc) in processing reward signals from the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and the substantia nigra (SN). Thin gray matter bridges (GMB) connect the caudate and putamen and are hypothesized to facilitate rapid communication essential for swift decision execution. This pathway is globally modulated by the locus coeruleus (LC) for particularly salient events during decision-making.

The integrated brainstem-prefrontal-striatal framework underlying decision-making examined at the mesoscale in this study.

In this framework, different cortical layers in the prefrontal cortex are involved in distinct stages of decision-making, while striatal subregions serve specialized functional roles: the caudate in motivation, the putamen in motor execution processing, and the nucleus accumbens (NAc) in processing reward signals from the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and the substantia nigra (SN). Thin gray matter bridges (GMB) connect the caudate and putamen and are hypothesized to facilitate rapid communication essential for swift decision execution. This pathway is globally modulated by the locus coeruleus (LC) for particularly salient events during decision-making.

To simultaneously drive these different decision subprocesses, we adapted a previously established value-based lottery choice task (LCT)25,26 that requires participants to decide on lottery stakes of varying magnitudes and win probabilities to maximize accumulated points (Fig. S1A). Critically, we randomly interspersed superhigh (SH) stake trials—featuring exceptionally large reward/loss magnitudes, balanced win/lose probabilities and shortened response windows—among regular stake trials, with outcome feedback provided. This design reliably elicited strong engagement of the brainstem-prefrontal-striatal pathway and enhanced the detectability of neural responses in small mesoscale structures that are often difficult to resolve with conventional high-resolution fMRI and regular stake decision-making. Also, by such biasing of low-latency salient decision processing, this method enforces a more linear progression of processing stages that minimizes contributions from more complex deliberation. Together, these task features induced a salient, high-stake decision context that simultaneously engaged stimulus salience processing, decision evaluation, response commitment, and outcome monitoring. Accordingly, our analyses focused on mapping mesoscale functional signatures of neural gating across these subprocesses during the LCT, primarily under the superhigh-stake context, at submillimeter spatial resolution. By doing so, we provide a more complete and powerful framework for understanding healthy decision processing in the brain and guide future research toward addressing its disruption in neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric disorders.

For RTs (Fig. S1C), responses were significantly slower at medium probability compared to both low (t(19) = 3.03, p = 0.007) and high probabilities (t(19) = 12.24, p = 5.5 × 10−10). Overall, these patterns align with previous findings from similar LCT paradigms25–27.

Functional brain response to regular and superhigh-stake choices

We then evaluated neural responses related to detecting salience in stake choices during the LCT. To this end, we considered the more salient MSH trials as superhigh (SH) stake trials in contrast to regular (RG) stake trials, which are ML and MM trials collectively collapsed across probability. Note that the SH vs. RG contrast reflects a composite high-stake manipulation (heightened salience through extreme magnitude, rarity, and shortened response window) rather than an isolated manipulation of magnitude value. As shown in Fig. 2A and consistent with salience processing, SH choices elicited significantly greater activation than RG choices across several key decision-making regions-of-interest (ROIs; see “Methods” for definition and examples in Fig. S2) including the LC (t(19) = 8.44, p = 5.2 × 10−7), VTA/SN (t(19) = 6.02, p = 2.0 × 10−5), PFC (t(19) = 6.11, p = 2.0 × 10−5), NAc (t(19) = 5.31, p = 5.6 × 10−5), and caudate (t(19) = 5.62, p = 3.6 × 10−5). Moreover, the LC exhibited the strongest enhancement under SH relative to RG stake (Fig. S3), compared to all other regions within the brainstem-prefrontal-striatal pathway (paired t-tests: LC vs. VTA/SN, t(19) = 4.31, p = 5.7 × 10−4; LC vs. PFC, t(19) = 4.95, p = 1.8 × 10−4; LC vs. NAc, t(19) = 3.16, p = 0.005; LC vs. caudate, t(19) = 3.67, p = 0.002; LC vs. GMB, t(19) = 7.20, p = 2.0 × 10−6; LC vs. putamen, t(19) = 7.82, p = 1.4 × 10−6), underscoring its specialized role in coding the salience of superhigh-stakes. In contrast, neither the GMB (t(19) = 0.97, p = 0.40) nor the putamen (t(19) = 0.03, p = 0.97) showed significant differences between stake conditions, suggesting their functional roles are distinct from direct stake evaluation. Similar results were obtained when using an anatomical definition of LC (Fig. S4). Representative participant-level voxel-wise contrast maps of SH choices against baseline fixation from general linear model (GLM) analysis (puncorrected < 0.05) in Fig. 2B illustrate spatially localized LC activation in the dorsal pons near the fourth ventricle, midbrain activation encompassing the VTA/SN, widespread PFC response, and pronounced engagement of striatal subregions, including a distinct activation line along the GMB. In addition to high-stake effects within the brainstem-prefrontal-striatal pathway, SH choices were also accompanied by coordinated modulation of large-scale functional networks (Supplementary Result 1 and Fig. S7), including increased engagement of limbic, salience, and frontoparietal networks and heterogeneous modulation within the default-mode network, providing complementary systems-level context for the focal mesoscale effects described above.

Response time-dependent neural activity during superhigh-stake choices

We next examined neural correlates of facilitated and therefore faster decision response by comparing short versus long RT trials during SH stake choices (Fig. 3 and see “Methods”). Short RT trials elicited significantly greater activation specifically within the GMB (t(19) = 3.17, p = 0.036), supporting its role in gating more rapid decision motor execution. In contrast, no significant RT-dependent activation differences were detected in the LC (t(19) = 0.91, p = 0.44), VTA/SN (t(19) = 1.51, p = 0.35), PFC (t(19) = 1.23, p = 0.38), NAc (t(19) = 0.03, p = 0.97), caudate (t(19) = 1.13, p = 0.38), further supporting their primary involvement in motivational evaluation (Fig. 2) rather than motor initiation. The putamen showed an uncorrected RT effect (t(19) = 2.34, puncorrected = 0.03) but this did not survive multiple-comparison correction (p = 0.11). Figure 3B shows GMB activation clearly visible (voxel-wise GLM, puncorrected < 0.05) as thin, contiguous lines between the caudate and putamen across representative participants. Note that the functional activation maps are directly overlaid on MT-weighted EPI in native spaces of each individual fMRI session, demonstrating precise spatial correspondence with the anatomically defined GMB contour (ROI examples in Fig. S2). This high spatial specificity, together with anatomical-functional correspondence and specific modulation of activity by RTs, underscores the robustness and functional relevance of GMB engagement in gating decision motor responses. In a control ROI analysis of V1 (Fig. S6A), the response difference between fast and slow conditions was nonsignificant (t(19) = 0.15, puncorrected = 0.88).

To examine potential contributors to the RT-related effects in the GMB observed during superhigh-stake decisions, we performed two additional control analyses. First, we tested whether uncertainty-related processing could account for the observed RT dependence, given that higher uncertainty (medium win probability) trials were behaviorally more time-demanding (Fig. S1C). Consistent with prior 3 T findings25,26, comparison of medium win probability (PM; higher uncertainty) versus low and high win probabilities combined (PL + H; lower uncertainty) at the medium magnitude (MM) condition revealed stronger fMRI responses in several frontostriatal regions (Fig. S8), including the PFC (t(19) = 4.65, p = 0.001), caudate (t(19) = 2.60, p = 0.03), GMB (t(19) = 2.71, p = 0.03), and putamen (t(19) = 2.68, p = 0.03). Importantly, this uncertainty-related pattern, in which longer RTs for higher uncertainty were accompanied by stronger neural responses, was opposite to the SH-specific RT effect observed in the GMB (and nominally in the putamen), where shorter RTs were associated with stronger activation. Second, to test whether the RT effect in Fig. 3 generalized beyond the SH context—which combined high salience, high uncertainty, and a constrained response window—we performed parallel RT-split analyses in regular-stake trials matched for winning probability (medium probability, low and medium magnitudes; Fig. S9). In contrast to the SH condition, no significant RT-related activation differences were observed in the GMB or any other ROI (all p > 0.1; full statistics in Fig. S9 captions) under regular-stake conditions. Taken together, these results indicate that the RT-dependent GMB effect and trend in putamen observed during SH trials is unlikely to reflect generic decision uncertainty or stimulus evaluation difficulty. Instead, it appears to be specific to highly salient decision contexts and may reflect transient decision-state processes associated with rapid response commitment under such constrained processing conditions.

Outcome-related neural activity after superhigh-stake choices

We further evaluated neural activation dissociating decision outcomes (gain vs. loss), particularly following SH stake choices (Fig. 4). Significant outcome-related modulation was detected exclusively in the NAc (t(19) = 4.03, p = 0.005) and VTA/SN (t(19) = 2.86, p = 0.03), with gains eliciting greater activation than losses. In contrast, no significant outcome-dependent differences were found in the LC (t(19) = 0.35, p = 0.73), PFC (t(19) = 0.65, p = 0.73), caudate (t(19) = 0.64, p = 0.73), GMB (t(19) = 1.48, p = 0.36), or putamen (t(19) = 0.41, p = 0.73). These findings dissociate the specialized roles of NAc and VTA/SN in processing reward outcomes, particularly under superhigh-stake conditions. Neural activity for accepted versus rejected superhigh-stake choices

To additionally distinguish neural activity modulation related to evaluation of anticipated rewards culminating in decision responses from the above activity distinguishing experienced reward versus loss outcomes, we compared decision phase responses during accepted versus rejected choices, focusing on SH stake trials (Fig. 5). Unlike outcome-related effects above, which were restricted to the NAc and VTA/SN, accepted SH choices elicited significantly greater activation than rejected choices in the LC (t(19) = 3.45, p = 0.004), PFC (t(19) = 3.10, p = 0.007), NAc (t(19) = 5.16, p = 3.9 × 10−4), caudate (t(19) = 3.89, p = 0.003), GMB (t(19) = 3.48, p = 0.004) and putamen (t(19) = 3.51, p = 0.004), whereas no significant difference was observed in the VTA/SN (t(19) = 0.55, p = 0.59). This result indicates a distributed ROI involvement across the brainstem-prefrontal-striatal pathway in integrating anticipatory reward signals under salient events and subjective risk preferences towards deciding responses to accept risky outcomes or to reject them for the status quo. Importantly, VTA/SN dissociation of reward from loss was specific to experienced outcomes, whereas the NAc showed sensitivity to reward-related signals in both cases. Laminar effect in the prefrontal cortex during superhigh-stake decisions

Discussion

Using 7 T fMRI with acquisition-matched anatomical references, we pinpointed dissociable operations in neural substrates at submillimeter spatial scales across the subcortical microstructures and the layered cortical architecture of the prefrontal cortex that support different stages of information gating during complex value-based decision processing in the human brain. The differential functional responses observed across these structures during the LCT correspond with the current conceptualization of their separable roles, established across many studies as reviewed in the introduction, as well as additional roles in the decision-making process as outlined in our proposed framework. These include orienting neurocomputations to stimuli salience, arbitrating and facilitating chosen value-based actions, and registering the outcomes of those actions. Critically, we demonstrate that using 7 T to probe functional responses at a more appropriate neuronal scale can provide deeper insight into known as well as additional functional roles and mechanisms previously inaccessible and understudied at conventional neuroimaging resolutions.

As an initial component of this framework, our results point to brainstem neuromodulation by the LC as a key driver of salience-dependent engagement during choice processing. Our finding of higher LC response to superhigh relative to regular-stake trials in the LCT (Fig. 2) is consistent with its well-established role in upregulation of neuronal activity to salient, unexpected, or intense stimuli6,28. Consider that before the central nervous system can effectively begin value-based decision processing, the brain needs to select when and for which stimuli downstream processing resources should be allocated. Inappropriate regulation of system-wide neurocomputational resources in tandem with environmental stimuli dynamics will result in either insufficient processing of necessary information or over-engagement of irrelevant signals. Accordingly, relative to its tonic baseline activity, the onset of salient stimuli induces phasic LC neuronal firing which, through norepinephrine release, increases the response gain of post-synaptic targets to their concurrent inputs6,23. These LC projection targets include the somatosensory, motor, visual, thalamic, medial temporal, basal ganglia, ventral frontal, and cerebellar systems identified in animal studies and validated in humans29. In addition, studies have traced projections from the orbitofrontal and anterior cingulate cortex to the LC6, which is suggested to be a pathway for top-down selection of specific object targets that require LC-related gain modulation.

In support, LC activity in our study robustly distinguished stimuli signaling superhigh versus regular stakes during the choice phase, when a response commitment was required, despite modest differences in AR behaviors (Fig. S1B and Table S1). This stake-related modulation persisted when superhigh-stake trials were compared with probability-matched regular-stake trials (Fig. S5), indicating sensitivity to stake context beyond probability alone. Note that superhigh-stake trials in our LCT combined several features to induce greater salience, including extreme reward/loss magnitudes, contextual rarity, and shortened response windows. These features that defined the salience of our SH trials might also implicate oddball or surprise processing6,28.

However, we found that LC activity did not differentiate actual gain versus loss of rewards during the outcome phase, when no immediate action was required (Fig. 4). Thus, it is difficult to fully capture LC processing from a perspective based purely on reward receipt, prediction error, or post-outcome surprise. Instead, the LC activation measured using our task peaked during the choice period when stake information and urgent action alternatives were evaluated. Moreover, LC activity contributed to differentiating accepted versus rejected choice alternatives during superhigh-stake trials (Fig. 5), but not during probability-matched regular-stake trials (Fig. S10). If LC engagement merely reflected generic stimuli oddball processing, accept–reject differences (indicating subjective valuation) would not be expected at either stake level, when objective stimuli salience is held constant. Thus, our findings on LC engagement support an interpretation of LC activation as reflecting high-stake salience-driven modulation during subjective decision formation, rather than reflexive reward or oddball processing of stimuli per se.

The above results highlight the role of the LC-norepinephrinergic nuclei in mobilizing downstream cerebral neural resources upon salient events that subserve the processing of subsequent action decisions. Indeed, LC-mediated modulation is well positioned to support increased attention, arousal, and cognitive control during salient decision contexts through scaling neural resources necessary to compute timely and adaptive action selection30,31. Matching this view, pronounced LC activation observed under superhigh relative to regular-stake conditions was accompanied by coordinated modulation across large-scale brain networks (Supplementary Result 1 and Fig. S7). These included corresponding upregulation in the limbic, frontoparietal, and salience networks, consistent with heightened motivational drive, greater attentional allocation, and increased cognitive control demands typical of superhigh-stake contexts32–35. In contrast, the default mode network (DMN) displayed a heterogeneous modulation pattern. Specifically, DMN regions, such as superior frontal, medial orbitofrontal, and precuneus, displayed stronger suppression under superhigh-stakes, indicating amplified salience-induced deactivation. Such suppression aligns with prior accounts that elevated cognitive demands and focused attention under uncertainty suppress internally oriented processes, such as mind-wandering and self-referential thought—core functions of the DMN36–38. On the other hand, the rostral anterior cingulate and posterior midline regions (posterior cingulate and isthmus cingulate) showed an increased positive activation during superhigh relative to regular stakes. We note that these cingulate territories lie at the anatomical boundary between the canonical DMN and the salience network, with their network affiliation varying across atlas choices; the Yeo et al.39 atlas in our case. Nevertheless, individual precision mapping studies have revealed that parallel distributed networks, including salience, parietal memory, and DMN subsystems, interdigitate within medial prefrontal and cingulate cortices that differentially express default-related processing in a task-specific manner40–43. Importantly, the heterogeneous DMN modulation pattern observed in this study is consistent with animal findings showing that LC activation induces both DMN suppression and enhancement, indexed by reductions in cerebral blood volume and glucose uptake, greater low-frequency fMRI synchrony in frontal DMN, and elevated calcium activity in the anterior cingulate cortex44.

Our submillimeter data also refine prior accounts of LC’s influence on the basal ganglia by functionally identifying selective exceptions within the striatum. Early tracing studies reported that the LC neurons do not innervate the hypothalamus and caudate-putamen, albeit without specifics about striatal subterritories6. Later human MRI at 1.6–2 mm resolution reported possible LC-basal ganglia connectivity29, but again without subregional specificity. Our findings suggest a more precise view of differential LC modulation of the human NAc, caudate, putamen, and GMB. Specifically, whereas the NAc and caudate nucleus showed high-stake modulation consistent with possible LC post-synaptic modulation, neither the putamen nor the GMB exhibited such effects.

Beyond LC-related salience modulation, our mesoscale results provide evidence elucidating the distinct functional roles of subregions within the striatum, which has long been implicated in value integration for decision-making45,46 and in our prior LCT studies25,26. Essentially, we considered the striatum as a central hub that computes when system-wide evidence integration crosses threshold levels and evaluation is completed (i.e., a decision is obtained), then commits to and coordinates appropriate motor response programs5,6. This describes the drift diffusion model procedure for deciding between options based on extant input information47,48. Correspondingly, modulation of the NAc and caudate nucleus by the high-stake condition (Fig. 2A) but not decision RTs (Fig. 3A) identified their selective sensitivity to motivational salience, which delineates their involvement in integrating subjective values about stimuli prior to a decision (i.e., a “drift” of cumulative stimuli-based evidence towards a motor decision boundary). By contrast, GMB and putamen activity distinguished differential decision RTs (Fig. 3A; albeit nominal, the putamen effect was not significantly different from that in the GMB) but was not modulated in the SH vs. RG stake contrast (Fig. 2A), suggesting their involvement in gating post-decision motor execution rather than stimuli value processing (i.e., registering when evidence accumulation crosses the threshold for a motor decision boundary). These findings strongly agree with many prior reports of the functional distinctions between the caudate and putamen8,18,49–51. Critically, we now further localize RT-linked activity to the caudolenticular GMB, a thin (~1 mm) bridge spanning the caudate and putamen, whose functional role has remained largely unexplored in humans until now. Going further, our additional control analyses indicated that the above RT-related GMB effect is unlikely to reflect generic decision uncertainty or stimulus evaluation difficulty. Across regular-stake trials, higher uncertainty (medium win probability) was associated with longer RTs (Fig. S1C) and stronger frontostriatal (and GMB) responses (Fig. S8), consistent with prior 3 T findings25,26, but opposite to the SH-specific RT effect observed here, in which GMB responses were stronger to shorter RTs instead (Fig. 3A). Moreover, when regular-stake trials were matched for uncertainty, no RT-dependent modulation was observed in the GMB or other striatal regions (Fig. S9). Together, these findings indicate that the RT-linked GMB effect observed (Fig. 3A) reflects processes associated with rapid response commitment under constrained processing conditions specific to highly salient decision contexts, rather than uncertainty-driven deliberation per se.

Anatomically, the GMB comprises neuronal cell bodies that span across the internal capsule from caudate to putamen, exhibit distinct diffusion properties from the white-matter sections of the internal capsule, and have been described as receiving input from frontal sources16,17,52–54. In terms of embryological development, the caudate and putamen are initially merged and gradually separate as descending cortical fiber bundles constituting the internal capsule grow through it, leaving GMBs that maintain partial structural continuity and may reflect graded connectivity or neuromolecular functional dissociations between striatal divisions16,17,28,52,55. Prior anatomical studies in non-human primates have also demonstrated substantial convergence of corticostriatal projections, particularly from prefrontal regions, within central striatal territories53,56,57, corresponding to the anatomical location of the GMB, although GMBs were not explicitly delineated in those studies. Despite its consistent anatomical presence, the GMB is rarely incorporated into models of striatal organization or value-based decision making, and, to our knowledge, has not been functionally characterized in humans using fMRI.

Our findings, therefore, provide evidence linking GMB activity to post-evaluative processes associated with rapid response commitment and action implementation. Note that the GMB should not be construed as an exclusive caudate-putamen communication conduit or a functionally independent striatal module. Rather, we view our findings as localizing response-time-related activity to a specific gray-matter structure within the central striatum that forms part of an integrated corticostriatal continuum. Specifically, we propose that the GMB is best understood as part of a mesoscale striatal territory in which motivational, evaluative, and executive signals from various upstream striatal and extra-striatal regions converge to facilitate more rapid post-evaluative response commitment. Subsequent studies that combine submillimeter dynamic functional data with high-resolution diffusion tractography and task-related computational modulation are required to more deeply resolve the different neuromodulatory sources of GMB activity.

This functional profile of the GMB has important implications. Disruption of GMB integrity could impair the timely relay of motivational signals to motor output systems, contributing to slowed or inefficient action selection—features observed in Parkinson’s disease and other basal ganglia disorders50,58. Meta-analysis of striatal structures also suggests that increased lenticular gray matter and caudate volume in patients with obsessive compulsive disorder relative to normal controls might underlie their maladaptive propensity to engage motor actions regardless of internal belief conflicts59. Future work could combine ultra-high resolution functional and structural MRI to further map the GMB as a target gating region for where internal valuation transitions to motor processing, test whether its role generalizes across different decision-making contexts, and examine how it degrades in aging and clinical populations.

Unique from the above striatal regions, the NAc, along with the VTA/SN in the brainstem, upregulated to gains relative to losses during outcomes (Fig. 4) and superhigh-relative to regular-stake conditions during the decision phase (Fig. 2), with the NAc further differentiating accepted versus rejected superhigh-stake choices (Fig. 5). This response pattern is consistent with the coding of prediction error during outcomes via VTA/SN dopaminergic modulation of the striatum, particularly the NAc32,46, as formalized in reinforcement or temporal difference learning model60–62. Specifically, dopamine signaling encodes mismatches between predicted and experienced outcomes to support learning and policy updating. Our findings further suggest that, at least in high-salience decision contexts, NAc activity encodes multiple forms of deviation from the status quo apart from experienced rewards versus losses at outcome—namely salient versus regular stimuli events and decisions to accept versus reject risk for anticipated rewards. Each of these cases are informative for computing subsequent motivation and action policies in response to future stimuli. Moreover, neural activity increase to accepted versus rejected trials in the NAc was specific to the superhigh-stake condition, involved the LC, PFC, and other striatal regions, and was absent for probability-matched regular-stake trials (Fig. S10). Thus, such system-wide engagement that included the NAc distinguished neural resources involved in decision computation that selects risky actions upon salient events from more generic choice selection modes as detected in our study. Together, these results position the NAc as a key mesoscale integrative hub linking motivational salience, subjective decision commitment, and outcome evaluation in human value-based decision-making. Important questions naturally arise for future work regarding how NAc signals are routed to downstream striatal territories, such as the caudate, GMB, and putamen, to support value learning and action selection.

These mesoscale findings support a view of the striatum as a functionally differentiated yet integrated system. Within this framework, the caudate is preferentially linked to motivational and value-related processing, the putamen and the GMB to rapid post-evaluative response and motor processing, and the nucleus accumbens to anticipated reward and outcome evaluation. Importantly, these subregions do not operate in isolation: each striatal sub-region receives and projects extensive cortical connections53,56,57 and through these shared corticostriatal circuits integrate motivation, valuation, and action (consistent with reviews by Graybiel8; Schultz9; and findings in Brovelli et al.18). By resolving these distinctions at submillimeter resolution, our present study refines existing models of striatal organization and highlights how the previously under characterized sub-regions contribute to human decision processing.

Finally, we leveraged submillimeter resolution to examine whether prefrontal processing differentiates across cortical depths over distinct decision phases. Motivational signals are encountered at both choice and outcome phases of decision processing. As such, the brain needs to appropriately distinguish dynamic changes in task contextual demands that require integrating these signals either to decide motor actions or to update internal neural representations, or both. Studies of decision-making have noted neural responses across broad PFC regions associated with processing stimulus-action rules with strategic representations that guide choice behavior45 as well as outcome-related feedback essential for adaptive learning63,64. However, the neural operational dynamics of a given PFC region over different computational phases of a task remain unclear due to the limitations of previous imaging techniques. To this end, our submillimeter data, which yields approximately 2–3 independent observations across cortical gray matter tissue depths, enables investigation of laminar-specific neural processing dynamics across superficial to deep layers within the same PFC region13,14,65.

Crucially, within ventral prefrontal cortex (vPFC), prefrontal-limbic areas, and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), we found clear laminar functional differentiation across distinct decision-making phases under superhigh stakes (Figs. 6 and S12). Specifically, deeper cortical layers in these regions showed significantly greater response enhancement during the SH choice-selection phase, consistent with their anatomical role in communicating information with motor and subcortical structures13,14,65. Conversely, superficial cortical layers showed significantly greater response enhancement during the SH outcome evaluation phase that further distinguished gains from losses (Fig. 7), consistent with their involvement in feedback integration and engagement of local circuit computations. Note that this layer-dependent pattern would be undetected in the ROI-averaged PFC signal combined across layers since it reflects an interaction that cancels out the diametric laminar effects across decision phases.

Notably, these decision-phase specific laminar effects under superhigh stakes were selective to the vPFC, dlPFC, and our prefrontal-limbic definitions, as ventrolateral and medial prefrontal regions did not show reliable laminar interactions (Fig. S11). Thus, layer-specific operations that separate choice-outcome contexts are not ubiquitous across the prefrontal cortex but evince functionally differentiated laminar computations across distinct decision-relevant subregions. Overall, unlike previous studies localizing separate frontal regions differentially involved in decision processing, we resolve laminar PFC function and extend these prior macroscopic models of prefrontal involvement in decision-making to more dynamic sub-processes.

Beyond the mesoscale findings outlined above, several limitations remain. While the current results delineate spatially distinct mesoscale processes across subcortical nuclei and cortical layers, they do not directly resolve the functional communication or causal interactions among these components. In particular, although we identify laminar differentiation in ventral and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex across decision phases, the present study does not directly measure laminar-specific connectivity with downstream or upstream targets. Future work combining submillimeter fMRI with layer-resolved effective connectivity, tractography, or concurrent electrophysiological approaches will be essential for testing whether the deep- and superficial-layer effects observed here correspond to distinct projection pathways and causal interactions during decision making. More broadly, integrating mesoscale fMRI with structural metrics (e.g., neurite density, fiber architecture) and targeted manipulations of neuromodulatory systems, such as LC noradrenergic or VTA/SN dopaminergic signaling, will further clarify how anatomical substrates and neurotransmitter dynamics jointly support value-based decision computations across different nucleic subregions.

In conclusion, our study supports a coherent mesoscale framework integrating LC-mediated global neuromodulation, GMB-facilitated striatal communication, subregional specialization within the striatum, and laminar-specific processing in the prefrontal cortex for human decision-making. This framework elucidates how motivational salience triggers LC-driven reorganization of large-scale networks, how evaluative signals in the caudate can be rapidly transmitted to motor execution systems via the GMB, and how laminar computations within the PFC coordinate choice-selection and outcome-feedback phases. Compared with previous fMRI studies limited to region-level associations with broad cognitive domains, the present submillimeter 7 T approach enables stronger mechanistic conclusions at the level of gating, task context switching, and nucleus- and laminar-specific computations. This spatial precision reveals fine-grained pathways and functional boundaries that were previously inaccessible, bridging microcircuit mechanisms and systems-level decision models. These advances underscore that resolving the mesoscale architecture of decision-making is not only technically feasible but conceptually transformative. Beyond informing theory, this framework identifies specific mesoscale nodes—such as the LC, GMB, and PFC layers—as candidate biomarkers and intervention targets for translational research in aging, neurodegenerative, and neuropsychiatric disorders where valuation and action selection are disrupted.

Methods

Participants

Twenty healthy volunteers (age = 29.2 ± 3.5 years old, 12 males) gave their informed consent to participate in this study, which was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign.

Task design

During the fMRI scan, participants performed a value-based lottery choice task25,26 (LCT; Fig. S1A). Each trial consisted of a choice phase and an outcome phase. In the choice phase, participants were presented with stakes of varying magnitudes and probabilities of winning (Table 1). Within a RT window of 1.5 or 3 s, they chose to accept or decline the offered stakes via corresponding button presses, aiming to maximize accumulated points. In the outcome phase (1.5 s), feedback was presented on the screen indicating a gain or loss based on the participant’s decision and the predetermined winning probabilities. If the offer had been accepted, the outcome was determined by applying the stake probability, and points were added or subtracted accordingly. If the offer was declined, the point total remained unchanged, and the missing outcome was displayed in parentheses. Inter-trial intervals were jittered (choice-outcome: 1–5 s; outcome-next choice: 1–8 s) to optimize sampling of the hemodynamic response.

The current study introduced an additional superhigh-stake (SH) condition into the original LCT design. SH trials were characterized by exceptionally superhigh magnitudes (1000–1500 points; regular-stake trials <100 points), a medium winning probability (balanced chance of gain or loss), and a halved RT window (1.5 s) to increase urgency. These combined features created a consistently high-salience context that served two main purposes. First, the SH condition was designed to target LC engagement, as the LC is highly sensitive to motivationally salient, superhigh-stakes contexts. This allowed investigation of its role in modulating the brainstem-prefrontal-striatal pathway and large-scale brain networks involved in value-based decision-making. Second, the SH condition amplified neural responses during both choice and outcome phases, facilitating activation detection in small subcortical and laminar structures, such as the LC, GMB, and cortical layers, at submillimeter resolution. In contrast to SH trials, trials with low or medium magnitudes across all probability levels are collectively referred to as regular-stake (RG) trials.

All task instructions—including button mappings for accept and reject choices, RT constraints, the structure of choice and outcome phases, point accumulation rules, and the goal of maximizing total points—were presented to participants via instruction screens at the beginning of each run. Participants were required to confirm their understanding through corresponding button presses. All participants completed practice runs prior to scanning to ensure task comprehension; these practice runs did not include superhigh-stake (SH) trials. To reinforce competitive and motivational engagement, at the beginning of each experiment, participants were also shown a ranked leaderboard of accumulated points. The leaderboard used synthetic scores spanning a wide range to preserve score variability and competitive salience for early participants, and was later updated with real participant scores as data collection progressed. Points accumulated across runs, such that the total score at the end of each run carried over to the next.

In this study, varying levels of probability and magnitude were also applied to engage participants in computing stimuli values in a trial-wise fashion and also afforded a means to behaviorally validate stochastic dominance in LCT performance. RG trials spanned low, medium, and high probability levels to establish a valid probabilistic experimental context and to confirm normative decision-making behavior. SH trials were restricted to the medium-probability level by design, because pairing extreme magnitudes with low or high win probabilities would render outcomes largely predictable (near-certain loss or gain) and thus diminish the decision-relevant stake of the trial. By embedding SH trials sparsely among regular trials spanning a wide probability range, their rarity and unpredictability—together with balanced win-loss probability, extreme magnitude, and increased urgency—jointly define the high-stake nature of the SH condition.

Behavioral data analysis

Behavioral analyses focused on two primary indices derived from the LCT task: RT and AR. RT was measured as the latency to make decisions during the choice phase. For SH trials, individual RT distributions were median-split into short and long RT groups. This classification enabled examination of RT-dependent fMRI activation patterns across ROIs. AR was computed as the proportion of accepted trials within each condition, stratified by probability (low, medium, high) and magnitude (low, medium, superhigh).

Functional and anatomical brain imaging data acquisition

All scans were performed on a Terra 7 T scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) using a 8-channel transmit/32-channel receive head coil (Nova Medical, Wilmington, MA, USA). Each participant completed one scanning session consisting of six functional runs (9.6 min each) and an 8–10 min anatomical acquisition, divided into four or five segments interleaved between the functional runs (e.g., functional-anatomical-functional, etc.).

Functional data were acquired using a 3D-EPI sequence66,67 with parameters of repetition time (TR) = 3.083 s, echo time (TE) = 18.3 ms, flip angle = 20°, 86 slices to cover the brainstem-prefrontal-striatal pathway, 0.8 mm isotropic resolution, 224 × 224 matrix, partial Fourier 7/8 in both phase encoding directions, and CAIPI 3 × 2 (kz shift 1) with 1 shot per kz-partition.

To ensure precise localization of functional signals in small structures, such as the GMB, LC, and cortical layers, the anatomical reference imaging was achieved with a magnetization transfer (MT)-weighted EPI using an identical acquisition design and parameters as the functional scans21,22. MT-weighted imaging leverages the substantial difference in macromolecular proton content between gray and white matter (WM)68 to provide high-contrast anatomical references. fMRI data preprocessing

Motion correction was applied to the images of all functional and anatomical runs together within each session using SPM12 (Wellcome Trust Center for Neuroimaging, London, UK). The control and MT-prepared images from all anatomical runs were averaged to generate the final MT-weighted anatomical reference image. For functional data, time points were censored from further regression analysis whenever the Euclidean norm of the motion derivatives exceeded 0.4 mm or when at least 20% of image voxels were seen as outliers from the trend.

Following motion correction, spatial smoothing was applied to the functional data according to the analysis objectives. For ROI analyses—including both subcortical and cortical regions—all functional data were smoothed with a 1 mm full-width half maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel. This minimal smoothing improved the signal-to-noise ratio while preserving spatial specificity across adjacent small structures (e.g., LC, GMB, caudate, putamen, NAc, and VTA/SN), and ensured consistent preprocessing across subcortical and cortical ROIs for direct comparison. For cortical ROI definition (e.g., prefrontal subregions, large-scale networks), a 2 mm FWHM kernel was applied, and the resulting activation maps were used to constrain the atlas-derived cortical ROIs to voxels showing significant activation during any LCT condition, ensuring functional relevance and spatial continuity. For laminar-specific cortical analyses, no spatial smoothing was applied to avoid blurring across cortical depths.

fMRI GLM analysis

Voxel-wise GLM analyses were performed on preprocessed functional images using AFNI software69 to estimate the mean neural responses for each decision-making condition. A first-level regression model included regressors representing each choice condition (combinations of probability and magnitude level) and for each outcome condition (gain, loss, SH gain, and SH loss). All regressors were convolved with a gamma-variate hemodynamic response function. Time-series data for each voxel were normalized by the voxel’s mean signal over the scan duration, converting regression beta weights into per cent signal changes. This allowed for clear interpretation of the neural responses associated with each condition.

To convert atlas-based parcellation, including the PALS and Yeo 7-network atlases, into individual-specific regions, atlas labels defined in the Freesurfer’s fsaverage space were first registered to each participant’s cortical surface reconstructed from anatomical MT-weighted EPI images and then projected into the corresponding native EPI volume space. This procedure ensured that all subsequent analyses were performed in each individual’s native fMRI space.

To ensure functional relevance, cortical ROIs derived from atlas-based definitions were further constrained by activation maps combining all LCT probability and magnitude condition levels, with spatial smoothing using a 2 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel to maintain ROI continuity. Only voxels showing significant (puncorrected < 0.05) positive activation during any LCT condition were included. For the DMN, an additional ROI was defined to include voxels showing significant negative activation during any task condition.

For each ROI analysis, mean per cent signal change values were extracted across all task conditions for subsequent statistical analyses. As a control, the primary visual cortex (V1) was included, defined using the retinotopy-based probabilistic atlas78.

Laminar analysis

Cortical depth was calculated individually for each participant using anatomical MT-weighted EPI images. WM and gray matter (GM) segmentation, along with cortical surface reconstruction, were performed using the FreeSurfer software package76. Based on the reconstructed cortical surfaces, cortical depths were computed utilizing the equi-volume approach implemented in the LAYNII software79. The cortex was divided into 18 equi-volume layers to improve visualization of laminar profiles and to minimize partial volume effects across voxels65,79. If a lower number of layers was applied, multiple voxels with centroids across a wider range of cortical depths would have been binned into the same layer, resulting in a loss of depth-specific resolution. Within the context of this study, the terms laminar or layer refer specifically to measurements along the cortical depth axis, rather than to cytoarchitectonic cortical layers.

Laminar analyses focused on the vPFC, dlPFC, and prefrontal limbic ROIs defined above, to explore depth-dependent activation profiles during the choice and outcome phases of decision-making. For each ROI, laminar profiles of per cent signal change were first extracted separately for superhigh-stake and regular-stake conditions across all layers. An effect size profile was then computed by dividing the superhigh-stake profile by the regular-stake profile at each cortical depth, thereby correcting for superficial bias in the BOLD response and highlighting stake-related modulation80. This process was performed separately for the choice phase and the outcome phase. Effect size profiles were plotted as a function of cortical depth for each phase, and mean effect sizes were calculated for superficial (depth 1–9) and deep (depth 10–18) cortical layer compartments. Finally, superficial and deep layer effect sizes were statistically compared between the choice and outcome phases.

Statistical analysis

For behavioral data, AR were analyzed in two steps. First, we tested for a systematic effect of probability level (PL: low, PM: medium, PH: high) using a linear mixed-effects model, with probability entered as an ordinal predictor. Second, AR across different magnitudes (ML: low, MM: medium, MSH: superhigh) were compared using a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA within the medium-probability condition. Additionally, two-tailed paired t-tests were conducted to compare AR between low and medium magnitudes under either low- or high-probability conditions, with p-values corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. For RT, two-tailed paired t-tests were used to compare: (1) medium vs. low probability at both low and medium magnitudes, (2) medium vs. high probability at both low and medium magnitudes, and (3) low/medium vs. SH magnitudes under the medium-probability condition. FDR correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at an alpha level of 0.05.

For ROI-based fMRI analyses, average percent signal changes were extracted and compared across experimental conditions using two-tailed paired t-tests, with FDR correction applied to account for multiple comparisons across ROIs. The primary contrasts included: (1) superhigh-stake (SH magnitude) vs. regular-stake (combined low and medium magnitudes); (2) short vs. long RT within SH trials; (3) outcome gain vs. loss within SH trials; (4) accepted SH choices vs. rejected SH choices.

For laminar-specific analyses, a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted on effect size, with factors of decision-making phases (choice, outcome) and cortical layers (superficial: layers 1–9; deep: layers 10–18). Post-hoc paired t-tests were conducted to compare mean effect sizes between superficial and deep cortical layers within each decision-making phase. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the FDR procedure.
