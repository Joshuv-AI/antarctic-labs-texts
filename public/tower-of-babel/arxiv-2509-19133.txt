On the Fe xxii Emission in the X-ray spectrum of NGC 1068 Facilities: Chandra X-ray Observatory , Swift -BAT
M. Z. Buhariwalla, J. M. Miller, L. C. Gallo, J. Mao, J. Raymond, T. Kallman

Abstract
The Fe xxii doublet has been previously used to determine the density of collisionally ionized emission from magnetic cataclysmic variable stars. We test how this diagnostic doublet behaves for a photoionized plasma with an active galactic nucleus (AGN) spectral energy distribution (SED). We use the photoionized plasma code pion and ∼ 440 \sim 440 ks of archival Chandra HETG for the well-known Seyfert 2 galaxy NGC 1068 to test the behaviour of the Fe xxii doublet in the context of an AGN. This marks the first time these data have been examined with pion . We find that in a photoionized plasma, the Fe xxii doublet is dependent on the density, ionization state, and SED used. Thus, this density diagnostic remains model-dependent. In the context of NGC 1068 the doublet predicts an emission region ∼ 100 ​ r g \sim 100\,r_{g} from the central black hole. This would require a direct line of sight to the central engine, which is at odds with the Seyfert 2 nature of this source. In practice, these results highlight the complexities and challenges of applying photoionized models. With these data, we cannot exclude the possibility of a direct line of sight to the central engine of NGC 1068, but we cannot confirm it. Future observations with instruments such as Athena are needed to explore the Fe xxii doublet further.

I
Introduction

The effects of ionized plasma are commonly seen in the soft X-ray emission of Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN). These effects appear either as photoionized emission lines
(
Kinkhabwala et al. 2002
;
Kallman et al. 2014
;
Grafton-Waters et al. 2021
)
or absorption from ionized plasmas called warm absorbers
(
Blustin et al. 2005
;
Tombesi et al. 2013
;
Gallo et al. 2023
, WA,)
. More than half of all unobscured (
{\rm N}_{\rm H}<2\times 10^{22}\,{\rm cm}^{-2}
) Seyfert galaxies are expected to host warm absorbers
(
Laha et al. 2014
)
.
The properties of these warm absorbers and emitters can be inferred using line diagnostics ratios. In emission, the most commonly used are the G and R ratios of He-like ions
(
Porquet & Dubau 2000
;
Kinkhabwala et al. 2002
, e.g.)
, while a variety of diagnostic ratios have been explored in absorption
(
Mao et al. 2017
, see)
.

There is the potential for a new diagnostic ratio utilizing doublet emission from Fe
xxii
to probe the density of the emitting plasma. These lines originate from highly ionized B-like Fe, when an electron transitions from
2s^{2}3d^{1}\,{}^{2}\mathrm{D}_{3/2}\rightarrow 2s^{2}2p^{1}\,{}^{2}\mathrm{P}_{1/2}
producing an emission line at
11.77\,
Å, a second emission line from Fe
xxii
found at
11.92\,
Å (
2s^{2}3d^{1}\,{}^{2}\mathrm{D}_{5/2}\rightarrow 2s^{2}2p^{1}\,{}^{2}\mathrm{P}_{3/2}
) accompanies the emission. Together, the ratio of these lines forms a density diagnostic sensitive between
n\sim 10^{12}-10^{16}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}

(
Mauche et al. 2003
;
Mauche et al. 2004
;
Liang & Zhao 2008
)
.

The original Fe
xxii
diagnostic curves were used to explore the density of magnetic cataclysmic variables, where the emission region is assumed to be either a completely collisionally ionized plasma or a combination of collisionally and photoionized plasmas
(
Mauche et al. 2003
;
Mauche et al. 2004
;
Chen et al. 2004
;
Liang & Zhao 2008
, see)
. Photoionized absorption from Fe
xxii
has been studied for AGN and X-ray binaries
(
King et al. 2012
;
Miller et al. 2014
;
Mao et al. 2017
;
Ogorzalek et al. 2022
;
Tomaru et al. 2023
, see)
. Thus far, this diagnostic has not been applied to a photoionized emission spectrum of an AGN.

NGC 1068 is a Seyfert 2 galaxy located at a redshift
z=0.00379

(
Huchra et al. 1999
)
and observed through a Galactic column density of
{\rm N}_{\rm H}=3.22\times 10^{20}\,{\rm cm}^{-2}

(
Willingale et al. 2013
)
. This galaxy has been extensively observed using the High Energy Transmission Grating
(
Canizares et al. 2000
, HETG,)
aboard
Chandra X-ray Observatory

(
Weisskopf et al. 2000
)
.
Ogle et al. 2003
reported the first observation from 2000, where strong evidence of photoionized emission in the form of He-like triplets and radiative recombination continuum (RRC) was identified
(
Porquet & Dubau 2000
;
Kinkhabwala et al. 2002
, e.g.)
.
Evidence of outflowing winds was reported based on this first observation. Subsequent observations of NGC 1068 were obtained and modelled with six outflowing winds
(
Kallman et al. 2014
)
. The ionization of these winds was fixed to be log
(\xi/\hbox{${\rm erg\,cm\,s}^{-1}\,$})=1,\,1.8,\,2.6
where
\xi=L/r^{2}n
(
L
is the ionizing luminosity between
1-1000
Ry,
r
is the distance between the ionizing source and the emission region, and
n
is the density of the emission region,
Tarter et al. 1969
). These winds outflow at
\sim-450\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
, and they account for most of the emission lines observed in NGC 1068. The photoionized wind model found by
Kallman et al. 2014
does not account for the emission line features seen at
\sim 11.5-12\,
Å. Thus, we conclude that not all emission components have been accounted for by these models.
The Fe
xxii
line at
11.77\,
Å was identified in NGC 1068 in the HETG
(
Ogle et al. 2003
)
, and RGS
(
Grafton-Waters et al. 2021
)
data. This makes NGC 1068 a strong candidate to test the Fe
xxii
diagnostic as a tool to find the density of emission regions in AGN.

In this work, we explore the Fe
xxii
density diagnostic and apply it to the HETG spectrum of NGC 1068. The Chapter is organized as follows: Section
II
outlines the data used in this work; Section
III
applies the literature line diagnostic; Section
IV
contains the spectral fitting results, and we explore the photoionized line diagnostic in Section
V
. We discuss the implications of our results in Section
VI
, and summarize our conclusions in Section
VII
.

The diagnostic region of interest for the Fe

The lines are outflowing at

I(11.92\,

(11.92 Å)/

(11.77 Å)
(

;

;

. This indicates that we can only derive an upper limit for the density of the region emitting the Fe

. We note here again that this limit was calculated for a plasma in collisionally ionized equilibrium, or a blended plasma.

(
Kallman et al. 2014
, 600
{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
,)
. This could be due to an intrinsic difference in the emitting material, or it could be the manifestation of the blended lines. If we add a Gaussian at 11.74 Å with the same strength as 11.77 Å to account for emission from Fe

(

;
Liang & Zhao 2008
)
, the turbulent velocity becomes
\sigma=630\pm 180
and agrees with
Kallman et al. 2014
. More complex modelling is required to further constrain the density of the material responsible for the Fe
xxii
.

IV
Spectral Fitting

The underlying assumption of the Fe
xxii
diagnostic in the literature is that the material is collisionally ionized. This is a fair assumption for a cataclysmic variable star. However, we wish to explore how these diagnostic lines behave in a plasma photoionized by an AGN. To achieve this, we utilize the photoionization code
pion

(
Miller et al. 2015
;
Mehdipour et al. 2016
;
Di Gesu et al. 2017
)
and explore a narrow range surrounding the Fe
xxii
doublet,
11-13
Å. While the RGS data for this source has been fit with
pion

(
Grafton-Waters et al. 2021
)
, we note that these HETG data have not.

pion
requires an input SED to calculate the photoionized emission spectra. We build an SED for the central engine of NGC 1068 to model the ionizing continuum, using
xspec
. We implemented it in
spex
as a file model. It comprised two components: an accretion disk and an X-ray corona.
The file model was scaled such that the ionizing luminosity between
1-1000
Ry (
0.0136-13.6
keV,
Tarter et al. 1969
) was
L_{ion}=10^{44}\,\hbox{${\rm erg\,s}^{-1}\,$}
. This is the same ionizing luminosity used by
Kallman et al. 2014
.
Grafton-Waters et al. 2021
used a similar value of
L_{ion}=1.5\times 10^{44}\,\hbox{${\rm erg\,s}^{-1}\,$}
in their analysis of the RGS spectrum.

For the accretion disc, we used
NTsed
3
3

3

https://github.com/adamgonzalez/NTsed
(Gonzalez, in prep.), which models the UV/soft X-ray spectrum originating from an accretion disk, as described by
Novikov & Thorne 1973
. This is the same disc as used by
AGNSED

(
Kubota & Done 2018
)
and the input parameters are black hole mass (M
SMBH
), Eddington accretion rate (
{\rm\dot{M}_{Edd}}
), black hole spin (
a

r_{in}
,
r_{out}
), inclination, colour temperature correction factor (
f_{col}
), and co-moving distance to the source (
D
). We assumed M
{}_{\rm SMBH}=10^{7}\hbox{$\rm\thinspace M_{\odot}$}

(
Greenhill & Gwinn 1997
;
Lodato & Bertin 2003
;
Gallimore & Impellizzeri 2023
)
,
{\rm\dot{M}_{Edd}}=0.1
,
a=0.998
,
r_{in}
to be at the innermost stable circular orbit (
r_{in}=r_{isco}=1.2r_{g}
; where
r_{g}
is the gravitational radius
4
4

4

The gravitational radius is defined as
r_{g}=GM/c^{2}
, where

is the speed of light.
) outer disc radius fixed at
r_{out}=10^{5}\,r_{g}
, a colour temperature correction of
f_{col}=1.7

(
Done et al. 2012
)
and
D=14.4\,{\rm Mpc}

(
Bland-Hawthorn et al. 1997

). This results in a

L_{0.01-2\,{\rm keV}}=3.1\times 10^{43}\,{\rm erg\,s}^{-1}

The power law component of the SED is modelled using
nthcomp

Our SED is defined between

We fit the data using
spex v3.08.01

(
Kaastra et al. 2024
)
. The data were optimally binned in
spex

(
Kaastra & Bleeker 2016
)
, and the fit statistics were measured using C-stat
(
Kaastra 2017
)
. The Bayesian Information Criterion
(
Schwarz 1978
, BIC,)
will be used to compare models. It is given by:

\textrm{BIC}=k{\rm ln}(n)+\textrm{C-stat}\,.

(1)

Here,
k
is the number of fitted parameters, and
n
is the number of data points fitted in the spectra. We use
6\leq\Delta\textrm{BIC}\leq 10
to indicate strong evidence and
\Delta\textrm{BIC}\geq 10
to indicate decisive evidence one model is favoured over another
(
Kass & Raftery 1995
;
Pouliasis et al. 2020
, see)
.

Following the iterative method of
Grafton-Waters et al. 2021
, the data are first fit with a fiducial continuum model. A photoionized emission component (
pion
) is then added, and a new best fit is found. The parameters are frozen, and another pion component is added. This continues until there is no fit improvement for an additional
pion
component. We do this in an effort not to over-fit the data with endless additions of
pion
.

The powerlaw continuum is here to describe the general shape of all the weak and unresolved lines originating from plasma components that we do not directly attempt to model. It is true that there are degeneracies between
\Gamma
and other model parameters, such as powerlaw normalization. Fixing the photon index to
\Gamma=1
will reduce the statistical errors when calculating uncertainties; the effect is minor for all parameters except the powerlaw normalization. However, this condition is too strict during initial testing, and when exploring the
\Omega-{\rm N}_{\rm H}
degeneracy (Section
VI
). Thus, for the sake of consistency, and the lack of a physically justifiable reason, we leave
\Gamma
free.

When initially fitting with
pion
, we keep the absorbing covering fraction (
F_{cov}
) fixed at zero, density fixed at
n=10^{4}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}
matching
Kallman et al. 2014
. We leave the emitting covering fraction (
CF=\Omega/4\pi
) fixed at
\Omega=1
due to the degeneracies between
\Omega
and column density
(
Di Gesu et al. 2017
, e.g.)
. The validity of this is explored later in Section
VI
. We keep the turbulent velocity of all components fixed to
v_{turb}=600\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
. For each of the
pion
components, we left the column density (
{\rm N}_{\rm H}
), outflowing velocity (
v
), and ionization parameter (log
\xi
) free to vary.

Fitting the data with a powerlaw (two fit parameters) gives
{\rm C}=2096
for 197 degrees of freedom (
{\rm dof}
),
{\rm BIC}=2106.8

Adding one
pion
component improves the fit (
{\rm C/{\rm dof}}=768/194
,
{\rm BIC}=794.1
) significantly (
\Delta{\rm BIC}=1312.7
). The ionization of this component is
{\rm log}\,(\xi/\hbox{${\rm erg\,cm\,s}^{-1}\,$})=2.3
. This turns out to be the lowest ionization component we find; thus, it is named Low. We now freeze the parameters of the Low ionization component and add a second
pion
component. The second
pion
reduces the
{\rm BIC}=580.4
, again improving the fit significantly (
\Delta{\rm BIC}=268.7
). The ionization of this component is
{\rm log}\,(\xi/\hbox{${\rm erg\,cm\,s}^{-1}\,$})=3.7
, and this is the middle ionization (Mid) component.

We then added a third
pion
component, and again, it improved the fit significantly (
{\rm BIC}=558.3
;
\Delta{\rm BIC}=17.7
). This component had the highest ionization,
{\rm log}\,(\xi/\hbox{${\rm erg\,cm\,s}^{-1}\,$})=3.9
, and is named High. We tested the need for a fourth
pion
component. However, this had no improvement in the C-stat for three additional fit parameters. Thus, it produced a significantly worse fit (
{\rm BIC}=574.2;\,\Delta{\rm BIC}=-15.9
, where the negative indicates the previous model was superior).

Allowing parameters for all three
pion
components free to vary produced the best fit thus far with
{\rm C/{\rm dof}}=419/188
(
{\rm BIC}=477.5
). The main physical difference from the previous model is that the ionization of all three components is lower (
\Delta{\rm BIC}=80.8
).

Next, we probe the density of each photoionized component to compare with the upper limit determined by the Fe
xxii
doublet line ratio (Section
III
). We test the density parameter space by stepping through the density of each ionization component from
n=10^{2}\rightarrow 10^{14}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}
with 20 logarithmic steps. The results of this search in parameter space can be seen in Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
Change in fit statistic versus density for each photoionized component. The C-stat is measured relative to the best-fit pion model.

As the density increases, the fit statistic improves for the High ionization component and, to a lesser extent, for the Mid ionization component. The opposite is true for the Low-ionization components, as the fit statistic degrades with increasing density. A density
n<10^{10}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}
is preferred for the Low-ionization component.
The highest densities
>10^{13}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}
are disfavoured by all three components, but especially for the Low- and Mid ionization components. This agrees with our line diagnostic analysis, which excluded densities above
3\times 10^{13}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}
.

Densities around
\sim 10^{12}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}
are preferred for the High ionization component. This also appears to be the case for the Mid ionization components. However, this might be an artifact of the complex model fitting when varying only the density of one component at a time and fixing the others. That is, when the High ionization component is fixed at a low density, the Mid ionization component finds a local minimum at higher densities. However, when both components are free to vary, the density of the Mid ionization component becomes very small while that of the High ionization component remains high.

For the photoionized plasmas we explore in this work, the first excited state is populated entirely by radiative processes in the low-density limit. As the density of the plasma increases, collisional excitations are necessary for the population of the first excited state to surpass the ground state, thus making the 11.92 Å emission line strong. The combination of collisional and radiative processes populating the first excited state results in a lower critical density in the presence of an AGN radiation field. We discuss this in more detail in Appendix
A

VI
Discussion

We first wish to identify the emission radius of the ionized material. We do this by utilizing the definition of ionization,

\xi=\frac{L_{ion}}{n\,r^{2}}\,,

(3)

where
r
is the distance between the ionization source and the emitting material, we can calculate the distance from where the High ionization component is emitted. The density of the High ionization component is
n=8.7\times 10^{11}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}
, and the ionization is
\xi=10^{3.69}{\rm\thinspace erg}\,{\rm cm\,s}^{-1}
. The ionizing radius is
\sim 1.5\times 10^{14}\,{\rm cm}
. Assuming the black hole mass to be
\sim 10^{7}\hbox{$\rm\thinspace M_{\odot}$}

(
Greenhill & Gwinn 1997
;
Lodato & Bertin 2003
;
Gallimore & Impellizzeri 2023
)
, the emitting region is at
\sim 100\,r_{g}
away from the ionizing source. An emission line at this radius would be expected to have a velocity broadening due to Keplerian rotation of
\sim 30000\,{\rm km\,s}^{-1}
. The observed broadening is
\sim 2
% of this.

In an attempt to quantify uncertainty on this measurement, we assume that the errors are Gaussian in nature. This is a crude assumption as the error bars derived in Table
2
are often uneven. However, the Gaussian assumption should be sufficient for an order-of-magnitude approximation of uncertainty. We find the uncertainty in this calculation to be of order
\sim 80\,r_{g}
.

To achieve a true statistical error bar for this measurement, the probability distribution function must be known. This is typically achieved with the use of tools such as MCMC
(see
Robert & Casella 2008
, and references therein)
or Nautilus
(
Lange 2023
)
. Unfortunately, these methods are extremely computationally expensive and not built-in methods within the
spex
framework, requiring implementation with
pyspex
5
5

5

https://spex-xray.github.io/spex-help/pyspex.html
. We do not report the uncertainty on any other calculated value, due to the limitations discussed above. This is not expected to significantly impact the major results of this work.

The column density and density of the High ionization component allow us to calculate the size of the emission region. By assuming
d_{H}={\rm N_{H}}/n
, where
d_{H}
is the diameter, we find for
{\rm N_{H}}=1.2\times 10^{21}\,{\rm cm}^{-2}
,
d_{H}\sim 10^{9}\,{\rm cm}
(
\sim 10^{-3}\,r_{g}
). This is a rather thin shell of material that would surround the central region uniformly.

If we instead assume that the column density is
N_{H}=5\times 10^{23}\,{\rm cm}^{-2}
, which is comparable to values of column density in the literature
(
Kallman et al. 2014
;
Grafton-Waters et al. 2021
)
, the emission covering fraction,
\Omega
, becomes small. This scenario is consistent with a small observing window in clumpy obscuring material. The value of
\Omega
in this scenario would be
\Omega\simeq 0.03
and produces a fit statistic of
{\rm C/{\rm dof}}=409/187
(
{\rm BIC}=472.8
). This is greater than the thin shell scenario (
N_{H}
free and
\Omega
fixed) with
\Delta{\rm BIC}=7.8
. However, there is insufficient evidence to decisively exclude this scenario. This column density would have a cloud diameter of
\sim 0.4\,r_{g}
.

At low values of
{\rm N}_{\rm H}
, the effects on the line ratios are limited. We note again that only a limited study of the effects of
{\rm N}_{\rm H}
on the line ratios has been performed here. However, it is not until
{\rm N}_{\rm H}>10^{22}\,{\rm cm}^{-2}
that changes to the diagnostic ratio are observable. The changes are most substantial at higher densities. Below this threshold, the column density acts like a sort of normalization factor, having much the same effect as
\Omega
. When we attempted to fit the spectra with
\Omega
and
{\rm N}_{\rm H}
as free parameters, the data preferred
\Omega>1
, which is extremely challenging to explain physically. Thus, we fixed
\Omega=1
, and accepted the lower as perhaps a product of the limited band pass we are fitting over.

An emission radius of
\sim 100\,r_{g}
is challenging to explain given the Seyfert 2 nature of this source. This would place the emission region in the accretion disc and would require a direct line of sight to the central engine. This would be very atypical behaviour for a Seyfert 2 as they are thought to be absorbed completely by an obscuring torus
(
Urry & Padovani 1995
)
. We now explore alternative scenarios to reconcile the diagnostic results with the expected behaviour of this object.

VI.1
Effects of SED

The properties of a photoionized spectrum are deeply dependent on the ionizing continuum that is used
(
Mehdipour et al. 2015
)
. Thus, we explored the effects of four different ionizing continua on the density and ionization measured. We assign the base ionizing continuum constructed in Section
IV
and Figure
2
as SED1. It is shown in the left panel of Figure
7
with the blue dot-dashed line. The three remaining ionizing continua are variations on SED1.

The first modification we make reduces the strength and shape of the UV component of the ionizing continuum. This was done by adjusting the inclination of the
NTsed
to
85^{\rm o}
. We label this SED2, which is shown in the left panel of Figure
7
with the black dotted line.

For SED3, we reduce the strength of the primary X-ray continuum to increase the relative strength of the UV component. The luminosity of the
nthcomp

\sim 50
percent to
L_{2-10{\rm\thinspace keV}}=10^{43}\,\hbox{${\rm erg\,s}^{-1}\,$}
. The new optical to X-ray slope is
\alpha_{\rm OX}=-1.04

7
with the red dashed line.

To simulate the effects of absorption between the central engine and the emitting cloud, SED4 was constructed. It consists of the base ionizing continuum modified by four warm absorbers. The ionization and column density of each absorber were consistent with those found in NGC 1068 by
Grafton-Waters et al. 2021
; however, the covering fraction of each was increased to
CF=0.8
to produce a stronger absorption feature. We used the
xspec

zxipcf
to apply this absorption to the base model, despite
Grafton-Waters et al. 2021
using
pion

7

\Delta{\rm BIC}=15.2
. The separate SEDs were applied to all three emission components, so this change in the fit statistic could be due, in part, to improvements in the fit from the other two components.

VI.2
Indirect Methods to Probe the Location of the Fe
xxii
Emission

Here, we probe two methods external to the ionization and density that could provide an indication of the location of the Fe
xxii
emission. The first is an exploration of any variability from the High ionization component. The second is emission in higher energy bands from the High ionization component.

VI.2.1
Variability Analysis

The central engine of an AGN is often very variable
(
Leighly 1999a
;
Leighly 1999b
;
Grupe et al. 2001
;
Parker et al. 2013
;
Wilkins et al. 2014
;
Wilkins & Gallo 2015
;
Buhariwalla et al. 2024
, e.g.)
; however, the obscuring torus of a Seyfert 2 galaxy blocks this variability from our sight.
Bauer et al. 2015
concluded that there was no detectable broadband variability from the central engine in NGC 1068. In this section, we attempt to determine if we are able to detect variability in the Fe
xxii
doublet if any were present.

In order to accomplish this, we must first attempt to quantify the level of variability from the central engine that is present in NGC 1068. To avoid the obscuration that affects the soft band, we examine the hard X-ray lightcurve from the BAT-157 month catalogue
(
Oh et al. 2018
;
Lien et al. 2023
)
. The
14-195
keV band should not be subject to the same level of absorption as the soft band; thus, variation in the central engine should be more easily detected. Figure
8
shows the monthly BAT light curves of NGC 1068. The red data in this figure indicate months when the source is background-dominated; these data are not used in the analysis but are included here for completeness.

When compared to the mean count rate, the light curve has a
\chi_{\nu}^{2}=1.1
for 153 degrees of freedom. This indicates a relatively constant count rate. Using the
Edelson et al. 2002
method to calculate the fractional variability on this light curve results in a null value due to poor statistics. We bin the data into six mission-month bins to improve the statistics. These data are shown in blue in Figure
8
. This results in a long-term fractional variability of
27\pm 7
% and a
\chi_{\nu}^{2}=2.3
when the data is compared to a constant line. This variability provides us with a useful metric to work from.

To test whether we expect to see any variability in these spectral features with current or future instrumentation, we first assume that the central engine varies by 25 percent on years-long time scales. This is a generous assumption with limited evidence; however, such an assumption must be made. We also assume that all variations in the central engine correspond directly to a change in the luminosity of the emission line component. These assumptions are not exactly based on reality, but instead give us a framework from which we can determine if we could detect variability in the soft band of this source.

In practice, we adjust the strength of the High ionization component such that its
1-1000
Ry luminosity increased by 25 percent. We then simulated 100 ks of data for both the base model and the 25 percent model using
spex simulate
command. We did this for
Chandra
HETG,
XRISM
Resolve (assuming gate value open), and
Athena
X-IFU. For all data, we bin by three channels per bin. The results of these simulations are shown in Figure
9
.

VI.2.2
High Energy Emission

Initial indications were that the High ionization component would produce negligible emission compared to the other emission components in the hard energy band. However, this may not be the case. During the modelling of this source, we did not apply any absorption to our ionization model. This is consistent with the previous techniques used to model this source (e.g.
Kallman et al. 2014
;
Grafton-Waters et al. 2021
) and is common practice for modelling the emission line spectrum of Seyfert 2 galaxies. The fundamental assumption is that the continuum has been completely absorbed and is being re-emitted in an emission line spectrum,
Kinkhabwala et al. 2002
used NGC 1068 to demonstrate this.

While we do not fit the data using any absorption models, we can assume that if Fe
xxii
emission originates from near the inner region, it must be absorbed. To explore this, we applied a fiducial absorber with a column density of
\sim 10^{23}\,{\rm cm}^{-2}
and a covering fraction of
99\%
to mimic a possible clumpy torus obscuring the line of sight to the High ionization
pion
component. For the emission to be visible with this level of obscuration, it must be intrinsically brighter. We estimate that if this component is intrinsically brighter than the emission from it would be visible in the Fe K
\alpha
emission region, and therefore might be visible with the
XRISM
observations. Emission lines from Fe have been detected in this region with the HETG and PN instruments
(
Kallman et al. 2014
;
Grafton-Waters et al. 2021
)
. However, the detection capabilities of these instruments are limited. HETG lacks the collecting area, and PN lacks the energy resolution to disentangle to complex emission regions that are present in the Fe K
\alpha

(
XRISM Collaboration 2024
, e.g. NGC 4151)
. To fully constrain the presence of a plasma this close to the SMBH, broadband modelling that utilizes high-resolution spectroscopy would need to be implemented. This would allow for the simultaneous detection of the
11.77-11.92
Å Fe
xxii
lines with any accompanying emission from the Fe K
\alpha

VI.3
Other Possible Origins

The spectrum of NGC 1068 is rich and complex in all energy bands, not just in the X-ray regime. The radio jets in NGC 1068 are pushing dense molecular gas (
n_{{\rm H}_{2}}\sim 10^{6}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}

(
García-Burillo et al. 2014

. Interactions between the jet and the ISM have resulted in a burst of star formation
(

;
Bruhweiler et al. 1991

(

and in other sources (e.g. NGC 4388

Grafton-Waters et al. 2021
finds evidence of collisionally ionized emission in the RGS data of this source. The regions of star formation and/or where the jet has interacted with the ISM could be sites of this collisionally ionized emission. The original work by

VI.4
Systematic Uncertainty

The errors calculated in this work have been strictly statistical. They have all been calculated at a 90% confidence interval. As with any work, some baseline assumptions must be made. Here, we discuss the effects some of these assumptions may have had on our results.

We expect our photoionization code to be incomplete. When
Hitomi

(
Takahashi et al. 2016
)
observed the Perseus cluster, one of the major conclusions was that our photoionized and collisional ionized codes were incomplete
(
Hitomi Collaboration et al. 2018
)
. In the intervening years, attempts have been made to quantify any systematic errors associated with the atomic data used by line emission codes.

Gu et al. 2022
showed that the line flux uncertainties in a collisional plasma could be of order 10%. While,
Ballhausen et al. 2023
, argue that the majority of systematic effects result from the inadequate model selection in photoionized plasmas and not inadequacies in the atomic data.

We first assume that our model selection is correct and consider whether uncertainties in our atomic data could reconcile the putative small size (
\sim 100\,r_{g}
) of the Fe
xxii
emitting region. If the measured line value falls below the low-density limit of a diagnostic curve, then we can only determine an upper limit on the density of the emitting material. Thus, the measurement of
100\,r_{g}
becomes a lower limit.

The measured line ratio was
R_{Fe}=0.41^{+0.17}_{-0.15}
, yielding a 90% confidence interval of (0.26, 0.58). The line diagnostic in Figure
6
states the lowest value of
I(11.92
Å
)/I(11.77
Å
)
is
\sim 0.17
. Our spectral data (Figure
1
) agree with this line ratio within a 99.5% confidence interval. If we consider the possible flux uncertainty between the emission at 11.77 Å and 11.92 Å to be at 10 %
(
Gu et al. 2022
)
, then the low-density limit could be as high as
\sim 0.20
. Our measured ratio agrees with this low-density limit within a 98 % confidence interval. This is still greater than a
2\sigma
result, but it demonstrates how uncertainties in the atomic data may affect the calculated line diagnostic curves.

Our model assumptions are likely to have an effect as photoionization models are highly dependent on their SED
(
Mehdipour et al. 2015
)
. We saw this in Figure
7
, where we presented four different SEDs that could conceivably be created for NGC 1068. Each one produced a fit that was comparable or superior to our base model. However, the inferred ionization values spanned a full order of magnitude, and the allowed densities ranged from
10^{11}
to
10^{13}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}
. Notably, none of these four SEDs match those used in previous works.
Kallman et al. 2014
used a power law with
\Gamma=2
, while
Grafton-Waters et al. 2021
used a power law, reflection and soft excess components to form their SED. The power law and reflection components were fit to the PN spectrum simultaneously with their pion components.

The common assumption between previous work and our base SED is that all emission regions see the same ionizing continuum as the others. This was, in part, because none of them had any intervening absorbers between the central engine and the emission-line gas. This seems unlikely in such a complex source as NGC 1068. Having layered absorption is possible with
spex
and can have significant effects on the resultant spectrum (e.g.,
Xu et al. 2025
), but it is very computationally expensive. Furthermore, for a Seyfert 2 galaxy, it would be impossible to ascertain the exact order and properties of the intervening absorbers from the emission line spectra. It is hard enough to identify the base continuum from the central engine in NGC 1068.

Despite this, we attempted to explore absorption with SED4, where we apply absorption between the ionizing continuum and the emission region. As stated before, this SED produced the best fit to our data between 11 and 13 Å. The
\Delta{\rm BIC}
suggests that this model is preferred over the base model with 99.95% confidence. Furthermore, the ratio of our diagnostic lines in the low-density limit is
I(11.92
Å
)/I(11.77
Å
)=0.25
. This is consistent with the 90% confidence interval of the measured Fe doublet. The reason density is constrained above the low-density limit may be due to contributions to the Fe
xxii
line from the Mid ionization component.

We caution against assuming this is the most accurate SED to describe the ionizing continuum that the emission clouds in NGC 1068 see. This model only fits over
11-13
Å and the warm absorbers are only somewhat grounded in reality. These factors highlight the effects of simply selecting a different SED and do not even consider having different model parameters free (e.g.,
\Omega
vs.
{\rm N}_{\rm H}
) or the number of model components.

In summary, the results of the line diagnostic are very model-dependent. Seemingly small changes in the SED result in systematically different diagnostic curves. Furthermore, the ionization state of the plasma has a profound effect on the critical density of the diagnostic curves. As we transition into a high-resolution regime with instruments like
XRISM
and
Athena
, care must be taken in constructing the most realistic SED possible for the photoionized plasma. As well as exercising caution when confronted with exciting or novel parameters.

VII
Conclusions

This work explored the density-sensitive Fe
xxii
diagnostic, which has been used to explore the density of emitting plasma surrounding magnetic cataclysmic variable stars. We showed that the diagnostic behaves differently in a photoionized plasma, and calculated the first diagnostic curves for an emission spectrum under an AGN SED.

We find that the Fe
xxii
doublet is dependent on the ionizing continuum, the ionization state of the plasma, and the density of the material. Thus, it may be difficult to ascertain the density of the emitting plasma if the SED or ionization is unknown. We cannot expect to use the same diagnostic ratio for every AGN and every emitting plasma. Thus, these results remain model-dependent. These results also highlight the complexity of photoionized emitting plasmas, and NGC 1068 in particular. We demonstrate that care must be exercised when interpreting results from spectral models.

Ultimately, it is unlikely that the emitting region for the Fe
xxii
doublet in NGC 1068 originates from material
\sim 100\,r_{g}
from the central engine. However, this is the result that our photoionized modelling determined under four different SEDs tested. Our spectral fitting demands this result, and we can not exclude this possibility with our variability analysis. Future deep observation NGC 1068 with
XRISM
(GVO) and
Athena
are needed to probe the Fe
xxii
lines more fully.

Fe
xxii
Excitation and Emission Processes in an AGN Radiation Field

As discussed in the text, the Fe
xxii
density diagnostic has been commonly used to determine the density of material surrounding magnetic cataclysmic variable stars under collisionally ionized equilibrium (CIE) conditions
(
Mauche et al. 2003
;
Mauche et al. 2004
;
Chen et al. 2004
;
Liang & Zhao 2008
, e.g.)
. The density diagnostic is dependant on the population of the first excited state (
2s^{2}2p^{1}\,{}^{2}
P
3/2
) compared to the ground state (
2s^{2}2p^{1}\,{}^{2}
P
1/2
). The transitions that form our diagnostic line are
2s^{2}3d^{1}\,{}^{2}{\rm D}_{3/2}\,\rightarrow\,2s^{2}2p^{1}\,{}^{2}{\rm P}_{1/2}
and
2s^{2}3d^{1}\,{}^{2}{\rm D}_{5/2}\,\rightarrow\,2s^{2}2p^{1}\,{}^{2}{\rm P}_{3/2}
. These are bound-bound transitions between the nineteenth excited state and the ground state, and between the twenty-first excited state and the first excited state, respectively. For the rest of this discussion, we will refer to the ground state as level 1, the first excited state as level 2, the nineteenth excited state as level 20 and the twenty-first excited state as level 22. These are the labels used by
spex
. Levels three to ten are also important for this discussion, as they form the
2s^{1}2p^{2}
manifold, and they will be labelled as levels
3-10
. Figure
10
shows the energy level diagram with the electron configuration, term symbols and labels for the levels of interest.

Figure 10:
Energy level diagram for the Fe
xxii
doublet in photoionized equilibrium under best fit conditions. Each level is labelled with its electron configuration, term symbol, and energy level (e.g., the ground state is level 1, the first excited state is level 2, and so on). The

Red dot dashed lines indicate the collisional excitation, and the red dashed line shows collisional de-excitation. The green dashed line is the combination of radiative recombination and cascades.
The percentages listed provide information about the location and method by which an electron will transition from each level. The excitation energy between level 1 and level 2 is
14.7
eV (845.55 Å). The excitation energies from level 1 to levels
3-10

\sim 50-125

\sim 250-100

The diagnostic curves in the literature assume that the predominant excitation mechanism is collisional. Ions in the ground state or the first excited state will collisionally excite into either level 2 (ground state only), levels
3-10
, level 20 or level 22. Most collisional excitations will place the electrons in levels
3-10
, which will quickly decay back to either level 1 or level 2.

To produce the observed emission lines, we require radiative decays from levels 20 and 22.
If an electron is excited to level 20, it will most likely decay to the ground state, producing the 11.77 Å emission line. It can also decay to level 2 about
15\,
percent of the time, producing the 11.93 Å emission line.
If the electron is excited to level 22, it will decay to level 2, producing the 11.92 Å emission line. No radiative transition can occur between level 22 and the ground state.

An electron in level 2 is more likely to excite to level 22 than to level 20. An electron in the ground state is more likely to excite to level 20 than to level 22. Thus, the ratio of the emission from the 11.77 Å and 11.92 Å gives an indication of the respective populations of the ground and first excited state.

The lifetime of the level
2\rightarrow 1
transition is
\sim 0.7
s
(
Kramida et al. 2024
)
. In the low-density limit, excited ions with electrons in level 2 will have sufficient time to decay before a second interaction can take place to excite to levels 20 or 22. In this limit, the population of electrons in level 2 will remain small, and the 11.92 Å line will be weak. As the density increases, more electrons are collisionally excited to all levels. The majority of electrons that are excited to levels
3-10
will decay to level 2, significantly populating this level. Thus, the population of level 2 electrons will build and excitation to level 22 will exceed excitation to level 20. Thus, the 11.92 Å line will be strong.

In a radiation field, under photoionized equilibrium (PIE), the fundamental mechanism of the diagnostic ratio is the same. The population of levels 20 and 22 depend on the populations of the ground and first excited states. Figure
10
shows an energy level diagram for the Fe
xxii
density diagnostic for the best-fit High ionization component.
Absorption is shown with blue dotted lines, and emission is shown with blue solid lines. Collisional excitations are shown with red dot-dashed lines, while collisional de-excitation is shown with a red dashed line. The green, long-dashed arrows represent both radiative recombination and cascades from higher energy levels. The number of electrons flowing (via collisional or radiative processes) relative to the total number of electrons leaving a given level is given as percentages in Figure

. The information has been extracted using the
spex
commands

and
ascdump pop
.

While collisional excitations between level 1, level 2 and levels
3-10
are not negligible, they are, however, less important than the radiative transitions. Perhaps most important are the numerous radiative transitions that promote electrons to levels
3-10
. In the low-density limit, the electrons in level 1 are promoted to levels
3-10
by radiative, and to a lesser extent, collisional transitions. Electrons in level 2 almost exclusively decay to level 1 before any secondary excitation can occur. This results in a very limited population in level 22, as this cannot be radiatively populated from ions in the ground state. Therefore, in the low-density limit, the only method by which electrons can reach level 22 is through radiative recombination and cascades from higher energy levels. In this case, the 11.92 Å line is weak and is overshadowed by the 11.93 Å line originating from the
20\,\rightarrow\,2
transition

In Figure
10
, where the density is reaching critical, we can see that the population of level 2 is beginning to build up. The primary method for electrons leaving this level is still radiative decay to level 1. However, a significant fraction of electrons are excited to levels
3-10
, level 20 and level 22. This allows the population of level 22 to increase and allows for direct radiative transition to level 22. The 11.92 Å emission line gains strength.

In the high-density case, there are very limited radiative transitions between level 1 and level 2. Electron exchanges between these levels are more likely to be collisional in nature. The fraction of radiative transitions between level 2 and level 22 increases, further strengthening the 11.92 Å emission line.

As noted in the text, the critical density of the diagnostic curves produced with PIE plasmas is significantly smaller (up to three orders of magnitude) than that seen in the literature. We theorize that this effect results from the large number of continuum photons that are absorbed from levels 1 and 2, into levels
3-10
. The ground-state excitation energy for levels
3-10
ranges from
\sim 50-125\,
eV, and the AGN continuum can readily supply these photons. Once ions are excited to levels
3-10
, they will preferentially decay to level 2. This process, in addition to the collisional excitations that occur as the density increases, will populate level 2. With the radiative transitions aiding the collisional excitations, the population of level 2 will increase to the point where excitations between level 2 and level 22 are common at a lower critical density.
