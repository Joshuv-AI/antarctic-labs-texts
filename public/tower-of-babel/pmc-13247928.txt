3-hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA hydrolase deficiency: a case description
Authors: Lu Jiang, Meixian Wu, Wenhua Qin, Luoxiao Qin, Guangming Cheng, Hui Qin, Jieru Wei, Zhongjun Liao, Xingqin Wei, Liuyan Yang, Hao Wei, Xiaoxin Qin
Published: 2026

Introduction

3-hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA hydrolase deficiency (HIBCH) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by pathogenic variants in the HIBCH gene. First described by Brown et al. in 1982 (1), it remains a rare condition globally. The disease is characterized by a disruption in the valine catabolic pathway, resulting in the accumulation of toxic intermediates such as methacrylyl-CoA. This leads to widespread tissue damage, particularly affecting the nervous system. The core clinical manifestation is progressive neurodegeneration. Symptom onset typically occurs in infancy, with initial features including delayed or regressive psychomotor development, hypotonia, and feeding difficulties. Disease progression may lead to dystonia, epileptic seizures, and ataxia. Neuroimaging, especially brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), plays a key role in diagnostic suspicion. Characteristic MRI findings include bilateral symmetrical signal abnormalities in the basal ganglia, most frequently involving the globus pallidus, which demonstrates hyperintensity on T2-weighted imaging (T2WI). This article reviews the clinical presentation, neuroimaging features, and genetic findings in a pediatric case of HIBCH deficiency, with an emphasis on its radiological characteristics to improve diagnostic recognition among radiologists.

Case presentation

A 4-month-old male infant was admitted to our hospital due to developmental delay and regression for over one month. He was born full-term via cesarean section. His mother received tocolytic therapy at 23 weeks of gestation due to cervical shortening. There was no history of birth asphyxia or resuscitation. After birth, he was hospitalized in the neonatal unit for 5 days due to the presence of meconium-stained amniotic fluid. He experienced recurrent intestinal colic in the second month. Developmentally, he was able to visually track light and objects and exhibited spontaneous limb movements. However, he began to show significant developmental regression three months later, including poor head control, poor neck muscle tone, diminished smiling response, markedly reduced limb movements (including absence of kicking at blankets), sluggish visual tracking, weak sucking force, and decreased milk intake. His parents are non-consanguineous and healthy, with no family history of inherited diseases. Physical examination on admission: vital signs were stable. The patient was alert but appeared thin with delayed growth. Head circumference measured at 40 cm, and the anterior fontanelle was 2 cm × 2 cm, flat and soft. Neurological examination: the infant was lethargic with diminished social responsiveness, including weak smile response and poor visual tracking. Cranial nerve examination was unremarkable: pupils were equal and round with normal light reflex, extraocular movements were intact without nystagmus, and facial symmetry was preserved. Motor examination revealed poor head control and markedly reduced spontaneous limb movements. Muscle tone was normal, although bilateral knee reflexes were hyperactive. Pathological reflexes (Babinski, Chaddock, Oppenheim, Gordon) were negative, and ankle clonus was absent. No meningeal signs were elicited. Sensory examination could not be reliably assessed due to poor cooperation.

Ancillary investigations: initial cranial MRI performed at 4 months of age revealed symmetric patchy abnormal signals confined to the caudate nuclei and putamina, appearing hypointense on T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) and hyperintense on T2WI (Figures 1,2). Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) of the basal ganglia lesions demonstrated an inverted lactate (Lac) doublet, reduced N-acetylaspartate (NAA) peak, and elevated choline (Cho) peak (Figure 3). Follow-up cranial MRI at 9 months of age showed interval evolution of disease. The abnormal signals in the bilateral basal ganglia had reduced in extent; however, new symmetric abnormal signals emerged in the bilateral cerebral peduncles, tegmentum of pons, and cerebellar dentate nuclei, cerebellar white matter, bilateral middle cerebellar peduncles, and basis pontis, appearing hyperintense on T2WI and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences (Figures 4-6). Additionally, progressive cerebral atrophy and ventricular dilatation were observed (Figure 7). In summary, serial imaging demonstrated disease progression characterized by partial regression of initial basal ganglia lesions, emergence of new lesions in the brainstem and cerebellar structures, and development of progressive cerebral atrophy.

Genetic analysis (Figure 8) identified two pathogenic variants in the HIBCH gene: c.640A>G (p.Thr214Ala) in exon 8 and c.451_452del (p.Val152Profs*25) in exon 7 of transcript NM_014362.4. The HIBCH gene (NM_014362.4), which comprises 23 exons, encodes a hydrolase involved in the metabolism of 3-hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA and β-hydroxypropionyl-CoA. Pathogenic variants in this gene are associated with 3-hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA hydrolase deficiency. The two variants detected in this patient were inherited from the father and mother, respectively, consistent with an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern.

Discussion

HIBCH deficiency is an inborn error of metabolism resulting from pathogenic variants in the HIBCH gene. Its pathophysiology originates from a deficiency of the HIBCH enzyme within the valine catabolic pathway. This impairs the conversion of 3-hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA to 3-hydroxyisobutyric acid, leading to the accumulation of toxic metabolites such as methacrylyl-CoA. Subsequent biochemical disturbances result in progressive neurological impairment and other clinical manifestations (1,2).

The clinical phenotype of HIBCH deficiency is heterogeneous, with core features including significant psychomotor delay or regression, neurodegenerative progression, elevated Lac levels, and characteristic basal ganglia lesions. Multisystem involvement is common and may include the following: neurological: seizures, myoclonus, dystonia, ataxia, head tremor, nystagmus. Craniofacial: dysmorphic features, strabismus, epicanthal folds. Cardiovascular: tetralogy of Fallot (in a subset of patients). Gastrointestinal: feeding difficulties, persistent vomiting. Symptom onset typically occurs in infancy, with considerable interindividual variability in presentation and severity. Neuroimaging findings typically reveal bilateral basal ganglia involvement on brain MRI. MRS often demonstrates an inverted doublet Lac peak, a pattern similar to that observed in Leigh syndrome (LS). The MRS findings in this case—specifically the inverted Lac doublet, reduced NAA peak, and elevated Cho peak—carry important diagnostic implications. The presence of a Lac peak indicates underlying impaired oxidative metabolism and mitochondrial dysfunction, a common feature in both HIBCH deficiency and various mitochondrial disorders, particularly LS. However, the combination of Lac elevation with reduced NAA (reflecting neuronal loss) and elevated Cho (suggesting membrane turnover or gliosis) is consistent with active neurodegeneration. Although these MRS abnormalities are not pathognomonic for HIBCH deficiency, they provide supportive evidence of metabolic brain injury. The key differential diagnostic value lies in integrating MRS findings with the characteristic topographic distribution on conventional MRI—bilateral symmetrical basal ganglia involvement with predilection for the globus pallidus—and with metabolic markers such as elevated plasma hydroxyl-C4-carnitine (C4-OH). This integrated approach helps differentiate HIBCH deficiency from LS and other organic acidemias, which may exhibit overlapping MRS features but distinct lesional patterns and metabolic profiles. In progressive stages, the disease may extend symmetrically to the bilateral dorsal thalami, cerebral peduncles, brainstem, and cerebellar dentate nuclei, appearing as hyperintense signals on T2WI and FLAIR sequences. This is frequently accompanied by cerebral atrophy and ventriculomegaly. HIBCH deficiency severely impacts patient survival and quality of life. Current management centers on nutritional intervention, primarily through a valine-restricted diet—limiting intake of poultry, meat, eggs, and fish—to maintain plasma valine within an optimal range. This strategy aims to reduce toxic metabolite accumulation and is supplemented with micronutrients and carnitine. It has shown benefit in metabolic stabilization, slowing disease progression, and improving quality of life (2,3). Additionally, cocktail therapy—typically comprising various vitamins and cofactors—may offer symptomatic improvement (4). In the present case, the patient received metabolic cocktail therapy immediately following diagnostic confirmation at 4 months of age, comprising oral idebenone, coenzyme Q10 capsules, and levocarnitine oral solution, in combination with vitamin B1, vitamin B2, and folic acid tablets. This combination regimen aims to improve mitochondrial function and enhance residual metabolic pathway activity. Follow-up brain MRI performed after five months of continuous therapy (at 9 months of age) demonstrated a reduction in the extent of abnormal signal in the bilateral basal ganglia; however, new lesions emerged in the brainstem and cerebellar structures, accompanied by progressive cerebral atrophy. The timing of imaging relative to treatment initiation suggests that while cocktail therapy may have contributed to partial regression of basal ganglia lesions, it did not prevent disease progression to other central nervous system (CNS) regions (5-8).

In this case, the infant was in overall good condition at birth, with normal early visual tracking and spontaneous limb movements noted. At two months of age, the infant presented with recurrent episodes of colic. By three months, manifestations included weak sucking, reduced milk intake, and feeding difficulties. At four months, developmental delay accompanied by regression was observed. Brain MRI performed at four months of age revealed lesions predominantly localized to the bilateral basal ganglia. MRS demonstrated an inverted doublet Lac peak. A follow-up MRI at nine months showed disease progression with involvement extending to the bilateral cerebral peduncles, pons, and cerebellar dentate nuclei, alongside progressive cerebral atrophy and ventriculomegaly. Considering the patient’s age of onset, clinical presentation, and imaging characteristics—all aligning with typical features of HIBCH deficiency—along with confirmatory genetic testing results, the diagnosis of HIBCH deficiency was strongly supported. Although the initial bilateral basal ganglia involvement in this patient aligns with previously reported HIBCH deficiency cases, serial neuroimaging in this instance offers unique longitudinal insights into disease evolution. Notably, sequential MRI revealed a distinct spatiotemporal progression pattern, with lesions extending from the basal ganglia to the cerebral peduncles, pons, and cerebellar dentate nuclei—a rostrocaudal dissemination infrequently illustrated radiologically. Furthermore, follow-up imaging demonstrated a dissociated response to metabolic intervention: partial regression of the initial basal ganglia lesions occurred concurrently with the emergence of new lesions in the brainstem and cerebellar structures, accompanied by progressive cerebral atrophy and ventriculomegaly. This observation suggests that radiological improvement in one region does not preclude disease progression elsewhere, underscoring the importance of surveillance neuroimaging. Additionally, this case provides a more comprehensive depiction of the cranial MRI phenotypic spectrum in HIBCH deficiency, confirming that the pathological process is not confined to the basal ganglia but can extensively and symmetrically involve the brainstem and dentate nuclei over time. Collectively, these longitudinal imaging findings enhance diagnostic recognition of this rare disorder and highlight the value of serial MRI in capturing its full phenotypic evolution.

Patients with HIBCH deficiency and LS share similar clinical presentations and cranial MRI findings, yet their etiologies are distinct. LS represents a heterogeneous group of mitochondrial disorders most frequently observed in infancy and childhood, resulting from mutations in either mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) or nuclear DNA (nDNA) (5). In contrast, HIBCH deficiency is caused by a specific enzymatic defect in the valine catabolic pathway due to mutations in the HIBCH gene, and is categorized as an organic acidemia. A distinguishing biochemical feature of HIBCH deficiency is the characteristic elevation of plasma C4-OH, whereas LS lacks such a specific metabolic marker but may be associated with increased levels of pyruvate and alanine (6). The difference in metabolic markers between HIBCH deficiency and LS holds significant value for differential diagnosis. On cranial MRI, both conditions typically demonstrate bilateral symmetrical T2/FLAIR hyperintense lesions predominantly involving the basal ganglia—especially the globus pallidus and putamen—and brainstem structures such as the midbrain and pons (7-9). However, HIBCH deficiency exhibits a distinct regional predilection, with lesions particularly concentrated in the globus pallidus, most notably its posterior portion. As the disease advances, this area often progresses to characteristic cystic necrosis (10), serving as an important imaging clue to differentiate it from other etiologies of LS. In comparison, classic LS usually presents with more widespread lesion distribution, commonly involving the globus pallidus, caudate nucleus, periventricular gray matter areas, and brainstem regions. During the active phase of LS, lesions often display edematous changes, whereas chronic stages may show atrophy or encephalomalacia without cystic necrosis formation. HIBCH deficiency must also be differentiated from methylmalonic acidemia (MMA), another rare multisystem organic acidemia (11). MMA can be classified as isolated or combined based on the presence of hyperhomocysteinemia. Both disorders belong to the organic acidemias and may present with developmental regression, dystonia, and acute encephalopathy triggered by infections. However, MMA is more frequently associated with metabolic acidosis. The key to differentiation lies in metabolic markers: MMA is characterized by significantly elevated urinary methylmalonic acid and methylcitric acid, whereas HIBCH deficiency shows increased plasma C4-OH with normal methylmalonic acid levels. Neuroimaging further aids in the distinction. HIBCH deficiency typically manifests as symmetrical lesions in the globus pallidus (particularly posteriorly) with progression to cystic necrosis. In contrast, MMA imaging often highlights acute bilateral damage to the globus pallidus and putamen, frequently accompanied by extensive white matter abnormalities. The presence of restricted diffusion within lesions during the acute phase is a key distinguishing feature of MMA. These imaging differences provide crucial evidence for initial clinical differentiation between the two disorders. Regarding management, MMA treatment involves protein restriction and vitamin B12 supplementation, whereas HIBCH deficiency management centers on valine restriction and supportive metabolic therapy.

Conclusions

HIBCH deficiency is a rare disorder characterized by progressive neurological deterioration. Diagnostic indicators are relatively specific: when an infant presents with feeding difficulties, developmental delay or regression, and abnormal muscle tone—particularly when plasma acylcarnitine analysis reveals elevated C4-OH—clinical suspicion for this condition should be heightened (12,13). Neuroimaging, especially cranial MRI, provides important supporting evidence at this stage, typically demonstrating bilateral basal ganglia involvement along with a characteristic inverted doublet Lac peak on MRS. In progressive stages, involvement may extend symmetrically to the dorsomedial thalami, cerebral peduncles, brainstem, and cerebellar dentate nuclei. This is often accompanied by cerebral atrophy and ventriculomegaly. Definitive diagnosis relies on confirmatory genetic testing.
