Metabolomics at the cutting edge of risk prediction of MASLD
Authors: En Ying Tan, Mark D. Muthiah, Arun J. Sanyal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a major public health threat globally. Management of patients afflicted with MASLD and research in this domain are limited by the lack of robust well-established non-invasive biomarkers for diagnosis, prognostication, and monitoring. The circulating metabolome reflects both the systemic metabo-inflammatory milieu and changes in the liver in affected individuals. In this review we summarize the available literature on changes in the different components of the metabolome in MASLD with a focus on changes that are linked to the presence of underlying steatohepatitis, severity of disease activity, and fibrosis stage. We further summarize the existing literature around biomarker panels that are derived from interrogation of the metabolome. Their relevance to disease biology and utility in practice are also discussed. We further highlight potential direction for future studies particularly to ensure they are fit for purpose and suitable for widespread use.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a heterogeneous disease, encompassing simple steatosis, steatohepatitis (also referred to as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis [MASH]), and increasing degrees of hepatic fibrosis culminating in cirrhosis as well as hepatocellular carcinoma. MASLD is highly prevalent with a global prevalence of close to 30%, and its prevalence continues to increase in tandem with that of metabolic syndrome and obesity.1,2,3 Combined with its association with disabling and life-limiting hepatic and extrahepatic events such as cancer4 and cardiovascular disease,5,6 MASLD is rapidly becoming a global public health threat.

A global consensus was reached in 2023 to replace the term non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with metabolic dysfunction-associated liver disease (MASLD), and the term non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) with MASH. New diagnostic criteria were also defined for the condition.7 Studies have since demonstrated a near-complete overlap in the populations defined by NAFLD and MASLD.8 For clarity and consistency, we will therefore refer to the disease as MASLD and MASH in this manuscript, even if underlying populations of the referenced studies were recruited as patients with NAFLD or NASH. We will only retain the terms NAFLD or NASH if used within proprietary test names, or when referring to the NAFLD activity score (NAS).

Unmet needs in MASLD

Management of MASLD begins with diagnosis of the disease. MASLD afflicts large swathes of the population, but only some patients are at risk of developing adverse outcomes such as the aforementioned hepatic and extrahepatic events. It is thus paramount to identify these at-risk patients, so finite resources may be appropriately allocated to those who will benefit significantly from surveillance, specialist hepatology care, or treatment. The ability to do so is especially relevant, with multiple liver-specific therapeutics on the horizon. Further, there is a need for robust and readily available means to monitor disease over time, assess treatment response, as well as serve as “companion diagnostics” in MASLD to predict response to specific therapeutics.

The current reference standard for the diagnosis and risk stratification of MASLD and MASH is histological assessment of liver biopsies, but this entails risks including mortality, has sampling error, has poor physician and patient acceptance, is subject to intra- and interobserver variability, and is not readily accessible.9 Yet, robust, readily accessible and non-invasive biomarkers do not exist for each of these contexts of use.

Metabolomics and biomarkers

While “older” omics such as genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics have significantly advanced our understanding of biological systems, these approaches are limited in providing insight into phenotype and biochemical activity. Metabolomics assesses intermediates and end products of various metabolic pathways, therefore providing direct phenotypic characterization and tissue- and temporally specific information. Metabolomic analysis is therefore positioned to be valuable in a disease such as MASLD, which involves a complex interplay between metabolic syndrome, liver disease, and other end organs, with polygenic inheritance and host-environment interaction. The advantages of metabolomics make it a well-positioned science for risk prediction in MASLD and for the study of mechanisms of disease in MASLD.

There are certain challenges involved in considering metabolomic alterations in MASLD. Firstly, there is a significant overlap between the metabolic syndrome and MASLD population, making it difficult to differentiate between metabolomic changes due to metabolic syndrome versus MASLD; only some studies have tried to control for the presence of obesity or type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Secondly, most studies compared controls with histologically proven MASH, while less studies compared steatosis and MASH, thus understanding the role of metabolomics in risk stratifying MASLD patients requires further study.

Nevertheless, distinct metabolomic signatures have been found for each of the various phenotypes of MASLD from simple steatosis to advanced liver disease. This review will discuss these signatures and the potential of metabolomic-based biomarkers for diagnosis, assessment of severity, prognostication, disease monitoring, and assessing treatment response in MASLD. Most studies measure the plasma metabolome in MASLD, and, unless otherwise stated, this review will discuss alterations in the plasma metabolome.

In biomarker development, context of use is paramount; it must be ensured that the biomarker in question is developed and tested within a specific context of use. For instance, a biomarker’s utility in diagnosis is necessarily distinct from its utility in prognosis. As part of deciding whether a test is clinically useful, one must not only consider the performance of a biomarker through statistical testing but also evaluate the pre-analytical (such as sample collection, storage and processing methods, and their effects on measurement) and analytical aspects (such as reproducibility as well as variance in measurement between different instruments or laboratories) of the test. Specific to metabolomics, these analytic considerations are significant. Analytical methods are complex and varied as molecules have vastly different physical properties, from soluble amino acids to very polar lipids. Rapid developments in instrumentation, software, and databases have allowed frequent refinement of processes and methods. In reviewing specific metabolomic panels for various contexts of use, independent validation of these analytical standards is imperative. Further, metabolite coverage may not overlap between laboratories. Various initiatives worldwide aim to mitigate these issues, such as the Metabolomics Standards Initiative, MetaboLights (EU), and Metabolomics Workbench (NIH).

The utility of a novel biomarker for a given context of use, metabolomic-based or otherwise, should be evaluated through direct comparison with the most robust, established, and often more cost-effective and widely available biomarkers such as Fibrosis-4 (FIB4), Enhanced Liver Fibrosis (ELF), or vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE). For context, the recent NIMBLE phase 1 study demonstrated that FIB4 had area under receiver operator curve values (AUCs) of 0.704 for at-risk MASH, 0.798 for ≥F2 fibrosis and 0.789 for ≥F3 fibrosis. ELF test identified ≥F2 fibrosis with AUC of 0.828 and ≥F3 fibrosis with AUC of 0.835. VCTE identified ≥F2 fibrosis with AUC of 0.841 and ≥F3 fibrosis with AUC of 0.858. The plasma-based NIS4 panel identified at-risk MASH with an AUC of 0.815.

The lipidome in MASLD

MASLD is defined by hepatic steatosis. This arises from hepatic de novo lipogenesis (DNL) and fatty acid uptake exceeding the liver’s ability for fatty acid oxidation and generation of very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) particles that transport triglycerides (TAGs) out of the liver.10 While TAG accumulation in hepatocytes is considered a defining feature of MASLD, other compounds such as free fatty acids (FFAs), diacylglycerols (DAGs), cholesterols, ceramides, and phospholipids also accumulate and are regarded as significant contributors to hepatic lipotoxicity. DAGs, sphingolipids, cholesterols, and bile acids (BAs) have myriad signaling functions that are likely to contribute to pathogenesis of MASLD.11 Notably, DAGs regulate transcription and play a Fatty acids

Fatty acids either exist free (FFAs) or are incorporated into lipid species such as TAGs and DAGs. In plasma, MASLD is associated with increased total FFAs,13,14 and in particular saturated (SFAs) and monounsaturated (MUFAs) fatty acids, with no concomitant increase in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs).14 A higher PUFA to total FFA ratio has been found to be strongly and negatively correlated with incident MASLD.15 Additionally, increased MUFAs and decreased total PUFAs with increased n6 to n3 PUFA ratio are observed across all lipid classes in MASLD.14 Certain FFAs such as palmitoleic acid (16:1 n7) and vaccenic acid (18:1 n7) are stepwise increased in MASLD and MASH as well14; these are elongation and desaturation products of palmitic acid (16:0), the product of DNL. Studies on the hepatic lipidome in MASLD are limited, but changes are generally similar to those of plasma lipids. Puri, in a study of 27 patients split equally between healthy controls, MASLD, and MASH, found a progressive decrease in hepatic n6 and n3 PUFAs and increase in n6:n3 PUFA ratio within both FFAs and lipids, from controls to steatosis to MASH.16

These findings are notable; firstly, they support the notion that hepatic DNL is significantly increased in MASLD since FFAs from DNL have a higher composition of SFA/MUFA than adipose tissue.17 It is known that FAs incorporated into liver TAGs primarily originate from lipolysis in adipose tissue (a process increased in adipose tissue IR), but hepatic DNL and dietary fatty acids also contribute to FAs in liver TAG.18 Secondly, it is known that free SFAs and MUFAs are lipotoxic and likely contribute to pathogenesis in MASLD, and these lipid species appear disproportionately increased in MASLD.19 Thirdly, n6 PUFAs are generally pro-inflammatory and vasoconstrictive while n3 PUFAs are anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative and may counter carcinogenesis. An increased n6:n3 ratio has been linked to pathogenesis of many diseases including metabolic and cardiovascular disease. PUFA synthesis involves various elongases and desaturases, and it has been suggested that dysregulation in activity of these enzymes leads to the observed increase in SFAs and n6/n3 PUFA ratio in MASH. Particularly, reduced fatty acid desaturase 1 (FADS1 or Δ5 desaturase) activity leads to accumulation of SFAs and shunts PUFA synthesis toward n6 PUFAs. FADS1 polymorphisms have been observed to affect the hepatic lipidome, and dietary intake leads to epigenetic changes in FADS. These alterations in PUFA synthesis, in addition to increased dietary n6/n3 PUFA intake ratio in the modern western diet, contribute to an imbalance in eicosanoids favoring pro-inflammatory n6 eicosanoids. This imbalance is possibly further exacerbated by increased lipid oxidation and eicosanoid synthesis in the setting of oxidative stress. The role of eicosanoids in MASLD is further discussed in the following. In addition, changes in the composition of PUFAs in membrane phospholipids lead to disrupted hepatocyte cell membranes and extracellular leak of lipids, potentiating lipotoxicity.20,21

Interestingly, Puri identified a pattern of plasma metabolomic alterations that reflected increased Δ 9 stearoyl-coenzyme A (CoA) desaturase (SCD) activity such as reduced stearic acid (18:0); increased downstream product oleic acid (18:1 n9); and decreased elongation products arachidonic (20:0), behenic (22:0), and lignoceric (24:0) acid. It has been suggested that stearic acid is more cytotoxic than palmitic acid while palmitoleic acid counters IR driven by palmitic acid; thus, lower stearic acid and higher palmitoleic acid may represent adaptive responses to fatty acid overload through increased TAG synthesis and altered FFA metabolism.14

FFAs may also undergo oxidation. Alterations in acylcarnitines, which are intermediates in FFA beta-oxidation, have been reported in MASLD. Studies have reported an increase in short- and/or long-chain acylcarnitines.22,23,24 Chang interestingly described elevations in acylcarnitines most prominent in even-numbered long-chain species.25 Additionally, Barr reported a reduction in oxidized fatty acids in MASLD.26 Increased acylcarnitines have also been found in MASH compared to MASLD26,27 Overall, these findings could suggest progressively defective beta-oxidation in MASLD.

DAGs and TAGs

Not surprisingly, many studies also show an increase in serum and hepatic TAGs and DAGs in MASLD.13,14,15,16 Puri reported an increase in total hepatic lipids as well as TAG to DAG ratio.16

Changes in the fatty acid composition of TAGs and DAGs have also been observed. There is a progressive decrease in PUFA and increase in SFA and MUFA within serum and hepatic TAGs (in essence, a lower double bond count) from controls to steatosis to MASH.14,28,29,30,31 Within these glycerolipids, there is a significant reduction in the product/precursor ratio for the n6 pathway (arachidonic 20:4n-6 to linoleic 18:2n-6 acid) and a close to significant reduction in the product/precursor ratio for the n3 pathway (eicosapentaenoic 20:5n-3 to α-linolenic acid 18:3n-3),16 suggesting reduced PUFA synthesis. Fatty acid chains in TAGs also get progressively shorter (in essence, a lower carbon number) from controls to steatosis to MASH.29,31 Similar to FFAs, palmitoleic acid within TAGs and DAGs is increased14 in a stepwise fashion from controls to MASLD to MASH. Otherwise, the abundance of lipid groups, as well as composition of fatty acid chains within lipid groups, did not clearly change in a consistent direction from normal to steatosis to MASH, making their use in discriminating MASH from MASLD difficult. For instance, DAGs and TAGs were lower in MASH than in MASLD, and MASLD had greater reductions in n3 and n6 PUFA within TAGs and DAGs than in MASH.16,29

Eicosanoids

It appears that oxidative stress and inflammation are central features of advanced MASLD. MASH is associated with increases in acylcarnitines, ether phospholipids including plasmalogens, and eicosanoids, respectively suggesting increases in mitochondrial oxidation, peroxisomal oxidation, and other extra-mitochondrial oxidative activity.26 Oxidative stress leads to alterations in nucleic acids, proteins as well as lipids, ultimately inducing production of inflammatory cytokines that stimulate Kupffer and stellate cells, hepatocyte apoptosis, and fibrogenesis.

PUFAs are stored in the cell membrane bound to phospholipids and are liberated by phospholipase A2 forming lysophospholipids in the process. PUFAs may then be oxidized by cyclooxygenases (COXs), lipoxygenases (LOXs), and cytochrome P-450s (CYP450) or through non-enzymatic peroxidation by free radicals to form oxylipins, known in mammals as eicosanoids owing to their 20-carbon backbone. Eicosanoids have diverse and often opposing effects in mediating inflammation. Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentanoic acid (EPA) directly compete with arachidonic acid (AA) for eicosanoid synthesis through the same enzymatic pathways. COX converts the n6 PUFA AA to 2-series prostaglandins, prostacyclins, and thromboxanes, while LOX converts AA to leukotrienes. LOX and CYP450 monooxygenases may also metabolize AA to form hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acids (HETEs) and lipoxins. The n3 PUFA EPA may likewise be oxidized by these same enzymes to form a separate family of 3-series prostaglandins, as well as E-series resolvins. While 2-series prostaglandins are generally pro-inflammatory and mitogenic, 3-series prostaglandins are significantly less inflammatory and may be chemopreventive against neoplasm. Likewise, the n3 PUFA DHA can be oxidized to form docosanoids including D-series resolvins, protectins, and maresins. Lipoxins and docosanoids have anti-inflammatory properties and are referred to as specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs). SPMs enhance self-limitation and resolution of inflammation and fibrosis, including in the liver.32

Hepatic and circulating eicosanoid species are significantly elevated in the setting of steatosis, MASH, and fibrosis. While COX metabolites are not significantly altered in MASLD, LOX metabolites such as leukotriene C5, 8-HETE, and 15-HETE are significantly increased in both serum and the liver.14,16,33 In comparing MASH to steatosis, Puri found that products of COX metabolism did not change significantly but AA-derived LOX products 5/8/15-HETE, non-enzymatic oxidation product 11-HETE, and products of CYP oxidation were elevated, without concomitant increase in anti-inflammatory lipoxin A4 despite it also being an LOX product.14 Barr found that patients with MASH had lower AA and increased eicosanoids compared to MASLD,13 suggesting increased AA utilization or reduced mobilization from phospholipids. Interestingly, Loomba described elevations in AA-derived metabolites from 5-LOX and 12/15-LOX metabolism that were maximal in steatosis, beyond levels in controls and MASH.34

Feldstein found 13-HODE, a non-enzymatically derived eicosanoid, to be associated with histological inflammation and fibrosis regardless of metabolic risk factors. The paper, with a training cohort of 73 and validation cohort of 49, put forth the OxNASH score taking into account 13-HODE, linoleic acid, age, BMI, and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) to predict MASH and demonstrated an AUC of 0.74 in the validation cohort.35 Loomba showed that both 11,12-dihydroxyeicosatrienoic acid (diHETrE) and a combined panel of dihydroketo (dhk) PGD2 and 20-COOH AA had an AUC of 1 for identification of MASH, whereas dhk PGD2L and 20-COOH AA alone also had good performance with AUC of 0.93 and 0.96, respectively34; however, the analysis was performed in a small and single cohort of 10 MASLD and 9 MASH patients.

Caussy showed eicosanoids 11,12-diHETE, tetranor-12-HETE, adrenic acid, and 14,15-diHETE to be associated with fibrosis stage and 7,17-dodecylhydroxydiphosphonic acid (DHDPA), 11,12-DiHETrE, and dhk PGD2 to be associated with advanced fibrosis while 8-HETE, 11-HETE, and 15HETrE were associated with cirrhosis. None of these molecules correlated with lobular inflammation.36 In hepatic lipidomics, Gorden found several eicosanoids and FAs to be elevated and many plasma neutral lipids, phospholipids, and sterols to be decreased in cirrhosis only.29

Cholesterols

Free cholesterol is cytotoxic. Cholesterol esters (CEs) are more inert than cholesterol and are the predominant form of cholesterol transported in plasma. Even so, CEs assume a pro-inflammatory and atherosclerotic role upon oxidation.37 Puri described a stepwise increase in hepatic free cholesterol from normal to MASLD to MASH, but not for CEs.16 Alterations in individual CE species have been noted in MASLD,28 as well as between MASLD and MASH.29

Cholesterol hormones such as sex hormones, glucocorticoids, and mineralocorticoids also serve important signaling functions. Dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) is the most abundant circulating steroid hormone and protects against oxidation, IR, and atherosclerosis. DHEA sulfate is independently and inversely correlated to all-cause and cardiovascular mortality.38 While DHEA appears to be raised or unchanged in early MASLD,39,40 it is reduced in MASH and advanced fibrosis and may play a role in progression of disease.41,42 Sumida evaluated the predictive value of DHEA sulfate in a cohort of 133 Japanese patients with biopsy-proven MASLD and 399 healthy controls and found it to have an AUC of 0.788 for advanced fibrosis in MASLD.43

BAs

Primary BAs are synthesized from cholesterol in the liver through two separate pathways. The classical (CYP7A1) pathway gives rise to cholic (CA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), while the acidic (CYP27A) pathway only gives rise to CDCA. CDCA may also undergo hepatic conversion to the other primary BAs, muricholic (MCA) and hyocholic acid (HCA), which are far less abundant than CA and CDCA. Primary BAs must undergo conjugation to taurine or glycine prior to release into bile. Secondary BAs are formed through bacterial metabolism of these primary conjugated BAs in the distal ileum and colon. BAs are a central aspect of the gut-liver axis and play a significant role in pathogenesis of MASH. Aside from their role in lipid digestion, BAs have signaling functions through activation of liver and intestinal farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and the G protein-coupled receptor TGR5 on intestinal L cells. FXR regulates insulin sensitivity, glucose and lipid metabolism, cell proliferation, gut barrier function, inflammation, and fibrosis. While liver FXR inhibits DNL, modulates lipid transport, promotes fatty acid oxidation, stimulates hydrolysis of TAGs, and upregulates FGF21 leading to an overall protective effect against MASH, the role of intestinal FXR is more controversial as both deleterious and protective effects seem to co-exist.44 Activation of TGR5 on intestinal L cells stimulates their release of GLP-1, thus increasing insulin secretion and sensitivity. Of note, obeticholic acid is a synthetic BA with potent FXR agonist activity, and use in MASH improves histological fibrosis, liver stiffness, and serum alanine transaminase (ALT).45,46 IR and MASLD are both associated with a shift to more trihydroxylic (such as CA and its conjugates) rather than CDCA-derived BA profile.47 CDCA is the most FXR-avid BA while trihydroxylic BA is less potent FXR agonists, leading to worsening IR and further BA profile changes.

BA composition affects microbiome composition and function and vice versa.48 BAs have antimicrobial properties and induce antimicrobial peptide release through FXR signaling. Some microbial taxa are sensitive to antibiotic effects of BAs, while others are dependent on BAs for proliferation. With progression from steatosis to steatohepatitis and with increasing fibrosis, there are progressive changes in the structure and functional profile of the microbiome. Specifically, there is an increase in activity of the bile salt hydrolase and acid inducible operons (BaIs) responsible for deoxycholate synthesis. There is also a reduction in taxa sensitive to the antibiotic properties of BAs whereas there is an increase in bilophilic taxa.48

In comparing steatosis and controls, there is an increase in plasma primary BAs, decrease in secondary Bas, and thus decrease in secondary-to-primary-BA ratio. There is an increased CA-to-CDCA ratio independent of T2DM.23,47 From steatosis to MASH, there is a further increase in plasma primary BAs and decrease in secondary BAs. CA is particularly increased in MASH, thus further increasing the CA-to -CDCA ratio. MASH is also associated with an increase in conjugation of total BAs, CDCA, CA, and ursodeoxycholic acid.23,47 Taurine-conjugated BAs are increased to a greater degree than glycine-conjugated BAs.27,48,49 Taurocholate correlates with steatosis grade on histology.23 Glycocholate, total primary BA, and total secondary BA correlate with lobular inflammation, and taurolithocholate correlates with portal inflammation while taurocholate correlates with ballooning. Conjugated cholate, taurocholate, and taurine-conjugated BAs are positively correlated to NAS while secondary-to-primary-BA ratio is inversely correlated.47,48 A recent study observed that patients with MASH had reduced total HCA and hyodeoxycholic acid (HDCA), and HDCA supplementation in high-fat-diet mice improved MASH through inhibition of intestinal FXR signaling and gut microbiome alterations.50 With regard to hepatic fibrosis, Puri showed that higher circulating glycocholate, taurocholate, and total conjugated cholate and lower secondary-to-primary-BA ratio were seen in F2-4.47 A study of 134 participants split into training and validation cohorts found the ratio of circulating conjugated CDCA to MCAs to rise in a stepwise manner from healthy controls to MASLD to MASH. The ratio predicted MASH and fibrosis with respective AUCs of 0.75 and 0.71.51

Data on hepatic BAs are comparatively sparse, and studies generally included smaller cohorts, necessitating cautious interpretation. Aranha compared MASH to controls in a cohort of 23 patients and observed an increase in total hepatic BAs, as well as increased CA, CDCA, and deoxycholic acid (DCA).52 CA was positively correlated while DCA was negatively correlated with histological inflammation. The ratio of trihydroxylic to dihydroxylic BAs also positively correlated with histological inflammation.52 Lake compared hepatic BAs between steatosis and MASH in 37 patients and found decreased CA and glycodeoxycholic acid, with an increase in taurine-conjugated BAs.53 Chen observed increased conjugated CDCA in both serum and the liver when comparing controls to MASH.51 Lastly, Aranha found hepatic CDCA to be associated with fibrosis.52

Smirnova studied fecal BAs in MASLD in a cohort of 75 patients. In contrast to serum, stool secondary BAs are increased in MASLD. Stool unconjugated BAs are reduced. Some fecal BAs are raised in MASH compared to steatosis, such as 7,12-diketolithocholic acid, glycodeoxycholic acid, and lithocholic acid. Higher NAS was associated with higher fecal oxidized BA epimers derived from deoxycholate, dehydrocholic acid, lithocolic acid, and taurine-conjugated BAs. Glycodeoxycholate and oxidized BA epimers were also increased in fibrosis. It is notable that deoxycholate in serum and stool and its metabolites in stool are raised in MASH with fibrosis or high activity. In contrast to changes in MASLD, the study also found a lower proportion of secondary BAs and increased total unconjugated BAs in F2 and F3 fibrosis. Also in contrast to MASH with high NAS, there was a decrease in fecal taurine-conjugated BAs in F2 and F3 fibrosis. Fecal deoxycholate and its metabolites appear to be good markers of disease severity. Metabolites of DCA 12-dehydrocholate, 7-ketodeoxycholate, dehydrocholate, and glycodeoxycholate are increased in F2 fibrosis. Reduced taurolithocholic acid, increased glycodeoxycholic acid, and oxidized BA epimers derived from deoxycholate were noted in F3 fibrosis.48 This study further demonstrated that changes in deoxycholate and its conjugates correspond with fibrosis regression, fibrosis progression, and decompensation of cirrhosis.48

In summary, MASLD and MASH are associated with increased hepatic and serum BAs, increased serum primary BAs, decreased serum secondary BAs (asides from DCA and ursodeoxycholic acid), a resultant decreased secondary-to-primary-BA ratio, increased hepatic and serum CA and CDCA as well as CA-to-CDCA ratio, and increased serum and stool conjugated BAs. Stool secondary BAs are increased. These changes were more prominent with MASH and are independent of diabetic status.

Lipoproteins

Cardiovascular disease is a major source of morbidity and mortality in MASLD. MASLD is associated with cardiovascular disease independent of obesity, IR, dyslipidemia, and T2DM.5,54 Interestingly, certain genetic polymorphisms such as in PNPLA3 and TM6SF2 favor MASLD development but are associated with a lower-risk lipoprotein profile55; these are discussed later in this review. Historically, cardiovascular risk as per the lipoprotein profile has been assessed through levels of TAG, low-, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C and HDL-C). It is now known that lipoprotein particle size and concentration, apolipoprotein levels, and other indices are also important determinants of cardiovascular risk. MASLD is associated with higher TAG, VLDL particle concentration, LDL-C, LDL particle concentration, concentration and proportion of highly atherogenic small dense LDL (sdLDL), remnant lipoprotein cholesterol (the sum of cholesterol associated with VLDL and IDL), apoB, and ApoB:ApoA1. Degree of steatosis is positively correlated to VLDL concentration, while lobular inflammation positively correlates with LDL, sdLDL, and TAG concentration, while being inversely correlated with apoA1. This increase in atherogenicity of the lipid profile was similar between steatosis and MASH but was reversed in cirrhosis due to failure of lipid synthesis.56,57 Of interest, lipoprotein(a), while a known cardiovascular risk factor, is reduced in advanced MASLD and may underestimate cardiovascular risk in MASLD.58

Lipid and cholesterol concentrations within specific lipoproteins subclasses also differ in patients with MASH. Increased total lipid concentration of VLDL and LDL has been found to correlated with MASH, while total lipid and cholesterol concentrations within VLDL and LDL associate with histological inflammation and fibrosis independent of steatosis and serum total TAGs. Interestingly, total lipid concentrations within VLDL and LDL were highest in F1 fibrosis but were lower in F2-4 fibrosis, suggesting progressive failure of lipid synthesis with increasing severity of hepatic fibrosis. Further, it was noted that these changes reversed after bariatric surgery.59

Fernandez-Ramos observed that MASLD patients have differential VLDL-TAG secretion rates and proposed that there are different metabolic subtypes of MASLD patients with differential VLDL-TAG secretion. Interestingly, a group of MASLD patients had a lipidomic profile similar to MAT1A knockout mice. This group of patients had lower TAG, VLDL-TAG, and VLDL-ApoB, less sdLDL particles, and remnant lipoprotein cholesterol (the sum of cholesterol associated with VLDL and IDL). This group of patients also had relatively lower cardiovascular disease risk, AST, ALT, and gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), as well as lower prevalence of PNPLA3. The authors proposed that S-adenosyl-methionine (SAMe) supplementation may be efficacious in patients who are of this subtype.60

Phospholipids

Phospholipids and sphingolipids are major components of plasma, endosome, and lipoprotein (particularly VLDL) particle membranes. Phospholipids also regulate DNL, are a vital component of mitochondrial membranes, and participate in bile emulsification with enterohepatic circulation. Phospholipids are defined by the presence of a phosphate group and two fatty acid tails attached to a glycerol backbone. The simplest phospholipid is phosphatidic acid. Additional chemical groups may be attached to its phosphate head, to form phosphatidylcholines (PCs), phosphatidylserines (PSs), and phosphatidylinositols (PIs), listed in order of decreasing abundance. PCs followed by PEs are the most abundant phospholipids. Lysophospholipids are formed through removal of one fatty acid tail by phospholipase, and they serve roles in mediating proliferation, cell migration, angiogenesis, and inflammation. Of interest, lysophosphatidylcholine (lysoPC) may play a central role in mediating lipotoxicity in MASLD.61

Though there was no change in total hepatic phospholipids, hepatic PC was decreased in both steatosis and MASH when compared to controls16 and decreased further in MASH compared to steatosis.62 Alterations in individual plasma lysoPC and lysophosphatidylethanolamine (lysoPE) species were noted in MASLD.13,28,29,33 Decreased plasma PUFA-containing phospholipids and ether lipids were found to be associated with more severe steatosis.31 Studies suggest a reduction in total plasma lysoPC and lysoPE in MASH compared to controls or MASLD,23,30 whereas, in hepatic lipidomics, total lysoPC is increased16 while PUFA-containing lysoPEs are reduced in MASH.29 Mazzini developed a panel of 2 PCs that had an AUC of 1 for discrimination of MASH from steatosis, but the analysis was performed on a single cohort of 12 patients with MASLD and 21 patients with MASH.63 These findings are consistent with those of murine studies, in which it has been demonstrated that an altered PC/PE ratio in either direction leads to impaired VLDL metabolism, hepatic steatosis, and failure.64

Cardiolipin is a phospholipid found primarily in the inner mitochondrial membrane and plays a significant role in mitochondrial bioenergetics, oxidative stress, and apoptosis. Cardiolipin is susceptible to oxidation, which leads to destabilization of respiratory chain proteins and apoptosis. Hepatic cardiolipin has been found to be elevated in steatosis or MASH compared to controls.24

Plasmalogens are a group of vinyl ether phospholipids with antioxidant properties. Puri showed reduced plasmalogen levels from normal to MASLD to MASH, particularly dm16:0.14 Barr similarly observed significant changes in PC species, including decreased lysoplasmalogen species in MASH compared to MASLD, though he described an increase in ether phospholipids including plasmalogens in a later paper in 2012.26

Sphingolipids

The basic structure of a sphingolipid comprises a sphingoid base attached to a fatty acid; this simplest form is known as a ceramide. The preferred substrates for the initial step of de novo sphingolipid synthesis are palmitoyl-CoA and serine, which lead to production of the sphingoid base dihydrosphingosine. Acyl chain-specific ceramide synthases (CerSs) then catalyze the addition of fatty acids to sphingoid bases. CerSs exist as subtypes numbered 1–6, each with different affinity for various lengths of fatty acyl-CoAs. Ceramides are of interest given their pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic properties, and known links to cardiovascular disease, oxidative stress, and IR in various end organs including the liver. Fatty acid excess (particularly in SFAs), adiponectin deficiency, and the pro-inflammatory milieu of metabolic syndrome promote ceramide synthesis. Ceramides then inhibit insulin signaling, forming a positive feedback loop.65,66 The ceramides most strongly associated with IR are long-chain species such as C16:0 or C18:0 ceramides, predominantly produced by CerS6. CerS6 expression has also been observed to be upregulated in the setting of obesity.67 Ceramides may also be metabolized to form sphingosine and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) and vice versa in the “salvage pathway.” Interestingly, S1P binds to receptors on myofibroblasts and is known to exert an anti-proliferative effect.65 Also, notably, ceramides appear to decrease in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), possibly contributing to reduced apoptotic activity.68

Sphingomyelins (SMs) are more complex sphingolipids formed by the addition of a phosphocholine or phosphoethanolamine group to a ceramide. These groups are donated by PC or PE, forming DAGs in the process. Conversely, SM can also be hydrolyzed to regenerate ceramides. SMs have been implicated in hepatocyte apoptosis.69 Lastly, glycosphingolipids such as cerebrosides and gangliosides are formed by the addition of sugar moieties to ceramides65 and play an important role in mediating IR.

Luukkonen observed that individuals with high homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) score had significantly more hepatic ceramides than those with low HOMA-IR score. There was also an increase in dihydroceramides formed via the de novo ceramide synthesis pathway, but not lipid species involved in the other pathways of ceramide synthesis (SM hydrolysis and salvage), suggesting the de novo pathway to be largely responsible for the increase in ceramides seen in IR and MASLD. This study also observed an increase in 16:0 and 18:0 dihydroceramides within the insulin resistant liver.66 Total, long- and very-long-chain ceramides are positively correlated with liver fat determined by magnetic resonance imaging proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF).70 In MASH, Gorden observed elevated serum ceramides, dihydroceramides, and 1-deoxy-dihydroceramides and -ceramides, with the greatest differences seen in very-long-chain dihydroceramides and 1-deoxydihydroceramides.29 Apostolopoulou showed no significant difference in total serum ceramides but significantly higher dihydroceramide levels in MASH compared to steatosis. This paper also observed that total hepatic ceramides and dihydroceramides were raised in MASH and correlated with IR; these were also long- and very-long-chain species. Hepatic ceramides also correlated with lipid peroxides, suggesting an association with oxidative stress.71 Lastly, SMs are also altered in MASLD and MASH. MASLD is associated with alterations in individual SM species, while MASH is associated with decreased levels of SMs.13,30

Similar lipidomic changes have been shown to correlate with recurrent MASLD after liver transplantation. These include increased FFAs and TAGs, particularly saturated TAGs. There were also changes in SM, CDCA, CA, PE, and PCs.72

Amino acid metabolomics

Amino acids serve various relevant functions. They are a substrate for protein synthesis and gluconeogenesis. Serine is involved in ceramide and SM synthesis. Glutamate synthesis involves glutamate, cysteine, glycine, serine, and methionine. Glutamine synthesized from glutamate is essential for fibrotic repair (Figure 2)

Glutathione metabolism

Amino acids and peptides involved in glutathione metabolism are altered in MASLD, overall likely a reflection of increased glutathione turnover in the setting of oxidative stress. Studies observe reduced glutathione73 and increased glutamate, pyroglutamate, cysteine, and homocysteine but reduced glycine and serine23,26,73,74,75 in MASLD. MASH was associated with a further increase in glutamate,23,74,76 pyroglutamate,73 and glutamate-to-glutamine ratio.76 Glutamate in particular correlates with liver enzyme elevations and hepatocyte ballooning.74 An increase in the amount of gamma-glutamyl dipeptides has also been observed,23,77 as well as reduced cysteine-glutathione disulfide.23

Gaggini noted that the glutamate-serine-glycine (GSG) index was elevated in MASLD and higher in obese MASLD than in lean MASLD.74 In this study. the index also correlated strongly with liver enzyme elevations, histological inflammation, hepatocyte ballooning, and fibrosis (when comparing F0-2 to F3-4 fibrosis). Ioannou however observed that the index was not useful in discriminating MASLD from MASH, nor degree of fibrosis or steatosis.27

Du found that an increased glutamate/glutamine ratio was associated with fibrosis. In this study, it was also demonstrated that 18F-fluoroglutamine positron emission tomography (PET) imaging in fibrotic mice showed higher hepatic update of the tracer, thus suggesting this imaging study as a possible non-invasive test for fibrogenic activity even in early MASH.76

One-carbon metabolism

One-carbon metabolism (1CM), referring to transmethylation reactions, is likely to be closely linked to hepatic lipid metabolism and homeostasis, and its dysregulation is likely one of the possible mechanisms by which MASLD develops. Methionine, choline, betaine, serine, and glycine are the source of methyl groups in SAMe synthesis, in turn facilitating essential transmethylation reactions (also known as one-carbon metabolism or 1CM) such as DNA methylation and VLDL particle synthesis.60 Deficiency in enzymes such as methionine adenosyltransferase 1a (MAT1A), glycine N-methyltransferase (GNMT), cystathionine beta-synthase (CBS), betaine-homocysteine S-methyltransferase (BHMT), and methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) or an inadequate supply of labile methyl groups from methionine, choline, betaine, serine, and glycine has been demonstrated to lead to reduced SAMe.60 Studies on mice with deficiencies in any of these enzymes show spontaneous development of hepatic steatosis with subsequent progression to MASH, fibrosis, and HCC.78,79,80,81 For instance, MAT1A knockout mice show impaired export of VLDL and accumulation of TAG in the liver, while administration of SAMe to MAT1A knockout mice eliminated MASLD.82 A methionine- and choline-deficient diet leads to MASH in mice despite inducing weight loss, low blood TAG, and glucose.60

It has been observed that global DNA methylation is reduced along with increased serum homocysteine levels (a marker of impaired methionine metabolism) in patients with MASLD compared to overweight controls. Further, the magnitude of these changes was associated with increased histological inflammation and fibrosis grade.83 Since the advent of genome-wide DNA methylation analysis of liver tissue and peripheral blood cells, differences in methylation of MASLD-associated genes have been described in MASLD, including insulin-like growth factor 1, CYP27A1, and dipeptidylpeptidase 4. In more advanced disease, changes in methylation of PNPLA3 and other genes involved in fibrogenesis, inflammation, 1CM, and branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolism have been observed.84

Betaine has been observed to be lower in MASH compared to steatosis and had a discriminatory power of AUC 0.73 in differentiating these two disease states in a study comprising 48 patients in the discovery cohort and 51 patients in the validation cohort. Additionally, betaine was observed to be inversely correlated with degree of steatosis, inflammation, ballooning, fibrosis, and NAS.85 SAMe synthesis is impaired in MASH.86

Branched-chain and aromatic AAs

The BCAAs leucine, isoleucine, and valine are essential amino acids that have regulatory effects.87 They have an overall anabolic effect through stimulation of muscle growth and inhibition of proteolysis. IR reduces BCAA catabolism. In turn, higher BCAA levels drive IR through persistent activation of mTOR complex 1 and are associated with obesity and T2DM. However, as liver disease progresses, BCAAs are reduced due to increased catabolism and alterations in muscle amino acid metabolism induced by hyperammonemia.88 Additionally, BCAA supplementation in cirrhosis is observed to reduce serum glucose rather than potentiate hyperglycemia. Lastly, BCAAs may exert an anti-neoplastic effect.89 Aromatic amino acids (AAAs) tryptophan, phenylalanine, and tyrosine are another group of amino acids associated with IR and MASLD. AAAs are increased in advanced liver disease, possibly due to reduced hepatic clearance. Overall, the Fischer’s ratio (BCAA/AAA ratio) is a marker of hepatic functional reserve and decreases as liver disease progresses.

Higher BCAA and AAA levels as well as other essential amino acids such as lysine are seen in early MASLD.23,33,39,74,75,77,90 These alterations were more profound in obese compared to lean MASLD39,74 and are independent of metabolic comorbidities and IR.90,91,92 These findings suggest that BCAAs and AAAs may serve as reliable biomarkers for MASLD, regardless of the presence or severity of metabolic syndrome, and dietary intake of amino acids. Of note, tryptophan may play a role in hepatic DNA methylation as part of pathogenesis of MASH and may be the only amino acid significantly raised in MASH compared to steatosis.93

Amino acids and the gut-liver axis

Amino acids also play a significant part in the gut-liver axis. Given that gut microbes can synthesize and degrade BCAAs, it is not surprising that BCAA levels and the microbiome have effects on each other, with levels of some specific bacterial species observed to correlate with BCAA levels.94 AAAs are metabolized by gut microbiota, and these metabolites are fed back to the liver.95 Tryptophan is metabolized in the gut to form kynurenines, indoles, and 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), and it is proposed that these metabolites play a crucial role in mediating the brain-gut-liver axis.96,97 Additionally, reduced choline (involved in 1CM) and increased trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) (a metabolite of lysoPC, choline, and carnitine) are observed to be associated with MASLD. TMAO may decrease BA synthesis and disrupt BA metabolism.98 Another study showed red meat intake increases TMAO, showing the complex interplay between diet, the gut microbiome, and disease.99

The metabolome in different MASLD subpopulations

MASLD is a complex and heterogeneous disease with many mechanisms of disease leading to the common end result of hepatic steatosis. Recent metabolomic data have revealed that obese and lean MASLD patients are phenotypically distinct. In addition, patients with polymorphisms in MASLD-related genes also exhibit distinct metabolomic alterations.

Lean and obese phenotypes

Approximately 10%–20% of individuals with MASLD are lean by BMI criteria but are similarly at risk of progression to MASH and cirrhosis and all-cause, liver-related, and cardiovascular mortality. Lean and obese individuals with MASLD share common features; both are defined by lipid accumulation in the liver and resultant lipotoxicity and are likely to have some degree of increased visceral adiposity, sarcopenia, and IR.100 However, genetic predisposition is a significant factor in the pathogenesis of MASLD in lean individuals101; these are discussed in the following section.

Studies comparing metabolomic changes between lean and obese humans with MASLD are few. Liu observed higher AA, C22:5, C22:6, and carnitine only in lean individuals with MASLD, whereas lower benzoic acid, arginine, tyrosine, and phenylalanine were found only in obese individuals with MASLD. The study concluded that, compared to healthy controls, obese MASLD patients had more alterations in the amino acid metabolome while lean MASLD patients had more changes in the fatty acid metabolome and proposed that these were reflections of different mechanisms of development of MASLD.39 Interestingly, these findings are consistent with those of murine studies on lean and obese phenotypes of MASLD.102 Barr observed that only morbidly obese individuals with steatosis had increased serum FFAs,26 though this change reversed in morbidly obese individuals with MASH. Among those with MASH, only obese individuals had increased oxidized fatty acids, while lean individuals exhibited reduced sphingolipids including ceramides and SMs in MASH compared to steatosis. Feldman compared lean healthy controls and lean individuals with MASLD and observed significant reductions in lysoPC C17:0 and PC ae C36:3 but an increase in glutamate. The study found that lysoPC C17:0, SM C24:1, PC ae C30:0, and PC aa C40:3 had a combined AUC of 0.923 for discriminating lean individuals with MASLD from healthy controls. Feldman also compared lean and obese individuals with MASLD and found that lean individuals had significantly higher PC aa C38:1, PC ae C42:3, and lysine. The 3 BCAAs lysine, tyrosine, and valine discriminated between lean and obese individuals with MASLD with an AUC of 0.903, while PC aa C38:1, PC ae C42:3, and PC ae C42:5 had a combined AUC of 0.814. The study performed its analyses on a single cohort comprising 71 lean controls, 55 lean patients with MASLD, and 61 obese patients with MASLD. Additionally the study excluded patients with previously diagnosed T2DM, but both the lean and obese cohorts had similarly impaired glucose tolerance and reduced adiponectin, suggesting these metabolomic changes are independent of IR.101

Genetic subtypes of MASLD

PNPLA3 rs738409 (I148M) is the most robust and well-replicated genetic variant associated with MASLD. Approximately 50% of individuals carry this variant allele. Lean individuals with MASLD are more likely to have the variant allele than lean controls.101 Patients with MASLD and carrying the PNPLA3 risk allele variant of rs738409 are less likely to have IR, T2DM, or cardiovascular disease compared to those with metabolic syndrome-related MASLD, though the high prevalence of both metabolic syndrome and the aforementioned PNPLA3 polymorphism necessarily means there is a significant overlap. Luukkonen showed lower adiponectin levels and higher lipotoxic lipid species such as ceramides and DAGs, as well as SFA- and MUFA-containing TAGs and FFAs in the liver with metabolic syndrome-related MASLD but not with MASLD associated with the PNPLA3 rs738409 risk variant allele.66 In fact, Hyysalo observed lower total serum TAGs in patients carrying the variant allele.103 In addition, Luukkonen also showed that patients with MASLD and carrying the risk allele variant of rs738409 had higher PUFA-containing DAGs and TAGs though there was no change in free FFA composition and proposed that this is consistent with other studies showing gain of function in lysophosphatidic acid acyltransferases (LPAATs) activity, particularly on long unsaturated FAs. LPAAT converts lysophospholipids to phospholipids via incorporation of FFAs. Phospholipids then act as the source of fatty acid chains in TAG and DAG synthesis.104 Luukkonen also showed in a later study that patients homozygous for I148M had decreased PUFA TAGs in serum VLDL particles but increased hepatic PUFA TAGs. At the same time, hepatic PC levels were reduced.105 Further, it was demonstrated that cells with either the I148M mutation or PNPLA3 knockout exhibited increased DAG in lipolytic conditions without evidence of increased DAG synthesis. These studies demonstrate defective hepatic lipolysis in MASLD associated with the rs738409 risk variant allele, specifically a loss of function in release of FFAs from DAGs, leading to reduced substrate for PC synthesis. In summary, MASLD associated with the rs738409 risk variant allele is defined by an increase in liver TAG due to both increased synthesis and reduced catabolism and export.

Metabolomics have also provided insight into the role of other genetic polymorphisms. It has been demonstrated that TM6SF2 E167K carriers have increased PUFA synthesis yet reduced incorporation into various lipids species, including PCs. As PCs are integral to the VLDL outer layer, carriers have impaired VLDL synthesis and reduced hepatic TAG export. MBOAT7 rs641738 C>T carriers have reduced MBOAT7 expression and decreased arachidonoyl-PI to total PI ratio, reflecting MBOAT7’s function as a lysophosphatidylinositol transferase that preferentially incorporates AA into LPI.106 Aside from contributing to increased AA, reduced MBOAT7 expression also alters hepatocyte lipid metabolism and leads to TAG accumulation.107,108 It has been observed that MBOAT expression is downregulated in metabolic syndrome,109 suggesting an epigenetic mechanism for development of MASLD in the setting of metabolic syndrome. HSD17B13 rs72613567 is an allele protective against hepatic fibrosis in MASLD and is associated with increased hepatic phospholipids, pyrimidines, and sex steroids including DHEA.110,111

Fibrosis and at-risk MASH

Patients with increased risk of liver-related adverse outcomes and death such as liver failure and HCC are identified by the presence of “at-risk MASH,” defined as steatohepatitis with an NAS of ≥4 and fibrosis stage of ≥2. Individuals with at-risk MASH remain the target population for most clinical trials for MASLD.112 Accurate identification of individuals with at-risk MASH is of paramount importance in clinical management and drug development. Liver biopsy remains the reference standard for determination of MASH and fibrosis.

The metabolomics-advanced steatohepatitis fibrosis (MASEF) score is the only metabolomics-based non-invasive test that has been developed with identification of at-risk NASH in mind. The international multicenter study involved training and validation cohorts of 790 and 565, respectively. The score comprised 12 lipids (2 TAGs, 5 glyceroPCs, 1 CE, 1 ceramide, and 3 SMs) as well as BMI, AST, and ALT. The MASEF score had an AUC 0.789 in validation cohort compared to 0.736 with FibroScan-AST (FAST). With 2 cutoffs, the score achieved a sensitivity of 89.1% and specificity of 88% in the validation cohort. This was also superior to sensitivity of 85.4% and specificity of 83.4% for the FAST score. When used in a two-step diagnostic test, FIB4+ MASEF had higher but not statistically significant performance than FIB4 + VCTE, though MASEF alone achieved a higher sensitivity than FIB-4 + VCTE. It was proposed that the MASEF score may improve risk prediction of patients with indeterminate results on FIB4 + VCTE. The MASEF score, being a plasma lipidomic-based test, is also more accessible than VCTE.113

Other notable metabolite panels in MASLD and MASH

OWLiver Care is a panel of 10 lipids combined with BMI, with an AUC of 0.88 for discriminating MASLD from healthy individuals determined in an initial study comprising 467 patients in the discovery cohort and 239 patients in the validation cohort. However, its performance was poorer at AUC 0.64 in a subsequent validation in a 220-patients cohort with T2DM. OWLiver includes 21 lipids and BMI and was developed for discrimination of MASH from steatosis. As with the OWLiver Care panel, it performed better in the initial study comprising 377 patients in the discovery cohort and 185 patients in the validation cohort with an AUC of 0.79 but performed poorer in the subsequent validation study in a cohort of individuals with T2DM, with an AUC of 0.69.114,115

Oresic developed a lipid triplet for diagnosis of MASLD in a study including 287 patients in the discovery cohort and 392 patients in the validation cohort that exhibited an AUC of 0.79.31 Flores developed a panel of 10 lipids for diagnosis of MASLD. With training and validation on a single cohort of 198 patients, the panel performed with an AUC of 0.81 when combined with age and sex, and with an AUC of 0.830 when further combined with BMI and PNPLA3 genotype.28 Liu used a panel of 5 lipids to predict MASLD, with a training cohort of 110 and validation cohort of 220, demonstrating AUC 0.808 in the validation cohort.39 NASH ClinLipMet includes 3 clinical indices (PNPLA3 genotype, AST, and fasting insulin), 3 lipids, and 5 amino acids. Other metabolomic changes

Few studies have also analyzed stool and urinary metabolites in MASLD but are comparatively small. Fecal BA changes are discussed in an earlier section. Mazzini showed that stool cysteine and xanthine were increased in MASLD and when combined could discriminate MASH from controls with AUC of 0.81.63 Fecal SCFAs, byproducts of microbial fermentation of dietary fiber, are also altered in MASLD. Rau showed an increase116 while Ganesan showed a stepwise decrease in fecal SCFAs from normal to MASLD to MASH to cirrhosis.97 Fecal metabolites of tryptophan, particularly pro-inflammatory kynurenines, are also raised in MASLD.117

Urinary metabolites may also have predictive value. Dong showed 7-methylxanthine, 2-methylguanosine, gluconic acid, indoxylsulfuric acid were significantly correlated with MASLD, while 3-indoleacetic acid, L-carnitine, pyroglutamic acid, and indolelactic acid may have utility in discriminating between MASLD and MASH. These metabolites were involved in pentose phosphate, carbon, and glutathione metabolism.118 Moolla, by training and validation on a single cohort of 275 patients, demonstrated that a panel of 10 urinary steroid metabolites could discriminate MASH cirrhosis from alcohol-related cirrhosis with an AUC of 0.83,119 as well as another panel comprising 10 urinary steroid metabolites, age, and BMI, which could identify advanced fibrosis with an AUC of 0.92 and F4 fibrosis with an AUC of 0.90.119

The polyamine spermidine was shown to be decreased more than 2-fold in severe MASH and advanced fibrosis.27

Metabolomics in disease monitoring, treatment response, and prediction

There is a paucity of available data for utility of metabolomics in prognostication and disease monitoring. Most available data revolve around early HCC detection, given that MASH-associated HCC is much less frequently associated with alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) elevation than with other etiologies of liver disease.120 Emerging data suggest a role for metabolomics in early HCC detection,121,122,123,124 but more studies in exclusively MASLD cohorts are needed.

Several studies have also evaluated the role of metabolomics in assessing disease response. Caussy demonstrated that a panel of 7 eicosanoids identified at least 1 stage of fibrosis improvement with AUC 0.74 in a phase 2 trial for Selonsertib including 63 patients observed over 24 weeks.36 In a study of pentoxifylline, oxidized lipid products of linoleic acid 9-hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (HODE) and 13-oxooctadecadienoic acid (oxoODE) corresponded with histological improvement in fibrosis while 8-, 9-, 11-, and 15-HETE corresponded with histological improvement in inflammation.125 A study observed reductions in palmitic, stearic, palmitoleic, and oleic acid, with rise in PUFAs including EPA and DHA, corresponding with reductions in hepatic steatosis, AST, ALT, and IR after 6 months of lifestyle intervention.126 Another study demonstrated that high intensity interval training (HIIT) led to improvements in MASLD and alterations in amino acids, BAs, and lipids in adipose tissue, urine, stool, and plasma.127

Lastly, data on the predictive value of metabolomics in MASLD are scarce. In a post hoc analysis of the PIVENS trial data, Cheng demonstrated that higher phenyl-propionic acid and indole-propionic acid and lower γ-carboxyethylhydroxychroman were strongly associated with histological response to vitamin E. Moreover, end-of-treatment levels of gamma-glutamyl leucine and valine were significantly lower in vitamin E responders.128

Conclusions

Presently available non-invasive biomarkers show lackluster performance in risk stratification and other use cases in MASLD. Metabolomics not only provides insight into pathogenesis and potential therapeutic targets but may also serve as robust biomarkers. Data have thus far been encouraging, considering the presence of distinct metabolite alterations identified along the spectrum of disease.

At current, the MASEF score is the metabolomic-based biomarker demonstrating the most clinical utility as it was developed with the aim of identifying at-risk MASH, thus facilitating decision-making regarding whether an individual with MASLD would benefit from pharmacological intervention.

Future studies ought to be conducted with the aim of ensuring that developed biomarkers are fit for purpose and suitable for widespread clinical application. To achieve this, studies should incorporate adequately large cohorts, not only enabling robust validation of biomarkers but also facilitating important subgroup analyses. These analyses may aid in delineating differences between probable disease subtypes, such as individuals with versus without T2DM, or those with genetic risk factors versus purely metabolic risk factors. Lastly, studies developing novel biomarkers should compare their performance to that of existing biomarkers as part of demonstrating clinical relevance.
