Gastric Adenocarcinoma With Enteroblastic Differentiation Presenting as Lung Nodules: A Diagnostic Dilemma
Authors: Jain Evani, Yadav Devvrat, Sainatham Chiranjeevi, Kc Manisha, Jain Anirudh, Jindal Vishal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastric Adenocarcinoma with enteroblastic differentiation (GAED) is a rare subtype of alpha fetoprotein-producing gastric cancers characterized by a tubulo-papillary pattern resembling fetal gastrointestinal epithelium. It expresses at least one of the following enteroblastic markers (EMs): glypican-3 (GPC3), spalt-like transcription factor 4 (SALL4), and α-fetoprotein (AFP).Case reportA 78-year-old man with a history of chronic dry cough was found to have multiple lung nodules on Chest X-ray and CT, suspicious for a malignancy. Therefore, a search for the primary site was initiated. Endoscopy revealed a fungating mass at the gastroesophageal junction while colonoscopy was unrevealing. It is imperative for patients to undergo a thorough workup for a timely diagnosis. IHC plays a vital role in delineating the origin of the malignant cells. High HER2 expression and PDL-1 immunostaining is commonly seen in GAED cases with the most common mutation being p53. Currently, there are no set guidelines for management of GAED. Unresectable, metastatic HER2 positive conventional adenocarcinoma is being managed using platinum-fluoropyrimidine doublet therapy with anti-HER2 monoclonal antibody trastuzumab. This case report calls for aggressive IHC staining for prompt diagnosis where necessary as well as proper guidelines for management.

Introduction

Gastric Adenocarcinoma with enteroblastic differentiation (GAED) is an aggressive and rare subtype of AFP-producing gastric cancers representing less than 1 % of all gastric cancers which is seen predominantly in elderly men.1 GAED lesions are frequently located in the middle to lower third of the stomach. It has been adopted as a new subtype since the 15th edition of the Japanese Classification of Gastric Carcinoma. The 2019 “World Health Organization Classification of Tumors of the Digestive System” has also categorized it as a subtype of gastric adenocarcinoma. It is morphologically characterized by a tubulo-papillary pattern composed of cuboid or columnar cells with clear cytoplasm that resembles fetal gastrointestinal epithelium. It typically expresses at least one of the following enteroblastic markers (EMs): glypican-3 (GPC3), spalt-like transcription factor 4 (SALL4), and α-fetoprotein (AFP).2 GAED is known for lymphovascular invasion and therefore is often diagnosed after distant metastasis has occurred rendering its poor prognosis. The 5-year survival rate is less than 30 % even after radical tumor resection as compared to 70 % in conventional gastric adenocarcinoma (CGA).3,4 Herein, we describe a rare case of metastatic GAED presenting as chronic cough (see Table 1).

Case description

78-year-old Caucasian male with a history of heterozygous hemochromatosis presented with dry cough and recurrent episodes of bronchitis over the past 6 months. His history was positive for asbestos exposure and denied a history of smoking/alcohol intake. Vitals, physical examination and laboratory parameters were unremarkable. Anemia was probably masked by hemochromatosis. Chest X-ray showed multiple scattered nodules with enlarged mediastinal nodes and a follow-up Positron Emission Tomography (PET)-CT showed multiple hyper metabolic lesions in the lung and mediastinal nodes, however, no fluorodeoxyglucose avid tumor was found outside the chest. The patient underwent biopsy of the left lower lung nodule. Hematoxylin and Eosin staining showed a well differentiated adenocarcinoma with mucinous features (see Fig. 1). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining showed positivity only for caudal-type homeobox 2 (CDX2) with a faint nonspecific blush on Thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1). Further staining was negative for CK7, CK20, NAPSINA, SATB2, PAX8, NKX3 and GATA3. Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) was negative while Caris GPSai was inconclusive. Given CDX2 positivity, patient underwent esophagogastroduodenoscopy to identify a gastrointestinal source of malignancy, which revealed a medium-sized non-obstructing, circumferential, fungating mass originating from the Z line on the gastroesophageal junction. The biopsy results showed a well-differentiated adenocarcinoma with gland formation and subnuclear vacuoles without clear intestinal-type features. IHC for HER2 expression returned a score of 3+ while programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression was negative. Additional immunostaining on endoscopically resected specimen showed diffuse SALL-4 positivity, focal glypican-3 positivity (GPC3) as shown in Fig. 2, and negative AFP staining thereby establishing the pathological diagnosis of GAED. Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) level was elevated to 68.4 ng/mL (10–40 ng/mL). The patient received 4 cycles of capecitabine, oxaliplatin, and trastuzumab given HER2 expression. 3 month follow up reported continued activity without physical limitation with subsequent PET-CT scan showing partial response to treatment.

Discussion

AFP-producing gastric cancers include three subtypes: GAED, Hepatoid adenocarcinoma (HAC) and yolk-sac tumor like gastric cancer.2 According to the study by Murakami et al., cases of GAED show 76 % lymphatic, 72 % venous, and 31 % liver metastasis as compared to 41 %, 31 %, and 6 % of the cases in CGA, respectively. These aggressive features contribute to the significantly poorer prognosis of GAED.5 The patient’s initial presentation with a chronic cough and multiple episodes of bronchitis prompted a thorough evaluation to investigate the underlying cause of recurrence. Pulmonary tumours, whether primary or metastatic, are important considerations in the differential for chronic cough. This case highlights the critical role of detailed history-taking and basic diagnostic tools, such as a chest X-ray, which can unveil a wide range of differential diagnoses and facilitate timely intervention. While chronic bronchitis is a clinical diagnosis, it should be supported by an X-ray to rule out any suspicious nodules or masses.

In this case, multiple lung nodules eventually being diagnosed as GAED upon the discovery of a gastric mass emphasizes the challenge to diagnose this cancer early given its tendency to aggressively metastasize. CDX2 is one of the most intestine-specific transcription factors for intestinal differentiation. 6 The initial ambiguity was streamlined by performing a gastrointestinal search, prompted by the CDX2 positivity. Furthermore, the patient’s oncologic profile might have been influenced by his history of heterozygous hemochromatosis since iron excess may have produced a favorable microenvironment for carcinogenesis. A European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition (EPIC) found a significant association between the H63D variant in the HFE gene and the risk of gastric cancer.3

Histologically, the tumor’s well-differentiated nature with mucinous features is consistent with enteroblastic differentiation. In contrast, hepatoid adenocarcinoma shows a trabecular pattern with fibrous bands and sinusoid-like capillaries in majority of the patient population.5 The identification of enteroblastic markers, such as GPC3, SALL4 and AFP is essential in confirming the diagnosis, even though the clinical presentation might initially suggest alternative diagnoses.1 HAC can be differentiated from GAED by its diffuse and strong positivity for EMs and intestinal mucin phenotype.7 On the review of literature, GPC3 expression was observed in majority of the GAED cases and was thereby the most sensitive marker, followed by SALL4 and AFP respectively.1,5 Additionally, there have been cases of gastric cancers with absence of clear cell cytoplasm but presence of EMs expression, being referred to as Gastric Adenocarcinoma (GA) with EMs. Therefore, based on the status of histology and IHC, it can be divided into GAED and GA with EM. Serum AFP levels, particularly with levels exceeding 1000 ng/mL, correlate directly with the strength of immunostaining and are associated with poor prognosis.8 High preoperative serum AFP levels are linked to a higher incidence of liver metastasis, advanced tumor stages, and increased lymphovascular invasion.8 This aggressive behavior is reflected in significantly poorer 5-year overall survival rates and recurrence-free survival rates compared to patients with normal AFP levels.8

In terms of management, GAED requires a multidisciplinary approach including radiologists, pathologists and oncologists, akin to conventional gastric adenocarcinomas. On review of the literature, surgical resection and adjuvant chemotherapy are the mainstay of treatment. Endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) or endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) with curative intent may be considered as a minimally invasive surgical option for early-stage tumors.9 Systemic management is largely driven by IHC staining. High HER2 expression and PDL-1 immunostaining is commonly seen in GAED cases with the most common mutation being p53.10 The first-line treatment for unresectable, metastatic HER2-positive GAED is platinum-fluoropyrimidine doublet with anti-HER2 monoclonal antibody trastuzumab. Our patient was initiated on capecitabine and oxaliplatin (CapOX) with Trastuzumab. Recent trials suggest the use of S-1 in place of capecitabine due to a better efficacy and safety profile. In patients with both PDL-1 and HER2 positivity, an immune checkpoint inhibitor like pembrolizumab can be given upfront in addition to trastuzumab and chemotherapy.9

In conclusion, GAED is a rare histopathological entity that requires prompt recognition by aggressive immunostaining where hematoxylin and eosin staining elude the relevant morphology. Few cases are reported to date; therefore, this case significantly adds to the existing literature. Additionally, with relatively increasing reports over the past decade, it is imperative that specific management guidelines must be laid down.
