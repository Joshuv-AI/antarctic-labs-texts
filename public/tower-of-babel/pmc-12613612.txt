Juvenile dermatomyositis: new insights into pathogenesis and clinical applications
Authors: Pauline PL Chan Ng, Aris E Syntakas, Lucy R Wedderburn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundJuvenile dermatomyositis (JDM), the most common inflammatory myopathy of childhood, is a complex autoimmune condition with significant disease burden and variable treatment responses. While conventional immunosuppression remains standard, advances in immunopathology are revealing new therapeutic and biomarker opportunities.Main bodyThis review provides emerging insights into JDM pathogenesis. Interferon (IFN)-stimulated gene (ISG) expression, and IFN-related emerging biomarkers which may be valuable for monitoring disease activity and treatment response. Mitochondrial dysfunction, involving oxidative stress and oxidized mtDNA, contributes to systemic pathology and offers new therapeutic targets. Other critical pathogenic processes include pyroptosis, neutrophil extracellular traps, and abnormal B-cell activity. Novel treatments targeting IFN pathways, B cells, and mitochondrial oxidation are under investigation. CAR T-cell therapies have shown early promise in refractory cases.ConclusionOngoing research is transforming our understanding of JDM, enabling biomarker-driven precision medicine and targeted therapies. These advances offer the potential to improve outcomes and achieve durable remission, even in refractory or high-risk disease subsets.Keywords: Juvenile Dermatomyositis, Pathogenesis, Biomarkers, Treatment

Background

The juvenile idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (JIIM) remain a challenging and heterogeneous group of conditions to manage and treat, with very significant disease burden for patients and families. In this review we consider novel evidence for aspects of both innate and adaptive immune mechanisms of disease, and potential biomarkers of those disease processes. We consider evidence for new and emerging treatments of JIIM, or parallel clinical studies in adult myositis, and how they can inform our clinical practice.

Pathogenic interferon signaling and its role as a biomarker in JDM

The role of pathogenic interferon (IFN) signaling in juvenile dermatomyositis (JDM) pathogenesis was first suggested over 20 years ago when increased expression of IFN stimulated genes (ISG) was observed in the tissue and peripheral blood of JDM patients [1–3]. Subsequent studies suggested several mechanisms of how type I IFN may drive the pathogenesis of JDM. This included direct effects on muscle cells with upregulation of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) Class I expression, a typical histological finding in muscle biopsies from JDM patients [4], alteration of myofiber physiology [5], with IFN-β thought to have distinct impact on muscle function compared to IFN-α [6], as well as effects on endothelial cells resulting in the pathological vasculopathic changes typically seen in JDM [5, 7]. Type I IFNs have also been shown to drive the inflammatory activity and activate adaptive immune responses in JDM by inducing natural killer and T-cell cytotoxicity, promoting the activation of T-cell survival, supporting plasmacytoid dendritic cell maturation, and inducing dendritic cells and macrophages to produce excess amounts of B-cell activating factor (BAFF), promoting the survival of autoreactive B cells [8, 9].

It is therefore of great interest to assess how interferon activity correlates with disease activity. Current disease activity measures rely on clinical examination, inflammatory markers and muscle enzyme levels. Inflammatory markers and muscle enzymes may sometimes be normal even at diagnosis or in the setting of low disease activity [10] and also correlate poorly with extra-muscular disease activity. Direct type I IFN measurement is challenging and hence, IFN is frequently measured using ISG expression, also known as IFN-signature or score.

It is well established that the expression of ISG is significantly elevated in both dermatomyositis (DM) and JDM [1, 2, 11, 12]. It was recently demonstrated that a 28-gene ISG score correlated with disease activity longitudinally in patients with JDM before and after treatment, with a decrease in ISG scores in parallel with reduction in disease activity [13]. Patients on treatment with high disease activity continued to have raised ISG scores. Furthermore, the normalization of ISG scores preceded that of modified disease activity scores indicating that the ISG score was a responsive marker of treatment [13], highlighting its potential role in monitoring treatment response. Future prospective studies to explore correlation of IFN score with disease activity in different organ systems will be important.

Amongst the IFN-related proteins, measurements of IFN-induced protein 10 (IP-10)/CXC motif chemokine 10 (CXCL-10) and galectin-9 had been shown to correlate better with disease activity than creatine kinase (CK) and may be helpful in predicting disease flares for some patients [14]. Sialic acid-binding Ig-like lectin 1 (Siglec-1), also known as sialoadhesin or CD169, is an IFN-induced protein [11] found on the surface of macrophages and activated monocytes, which is readily detected by flow cytometry [15]. Siglec-1 has been shown to correlate with the IFN signature as well as levels of CXCL-10 and galectin-9 in patients with JDM [11]. Interestingly high Siglec-1 expression was found to be superior to the IFN signature in predicting suboptimal response to standard treatment [11]. This observation may be accounted for by its expression being influenced by broader inflammatory stimuli including both type I and II IFNs and other inflammatory cytokines. A subsequent study demonstrated that Siglec-1 expression on peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) was significantly induced with incubation using plasma from newly diagnosed JDM patients and markedly reduced when PBMC were pre-treated with Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi) or monoclonal antibodies that block the IFN-αβ receptor [16]. These findings provide optimism in possible clinical applications of Siglec-1, a relatively easy and accessible laboratory measure using flow cytometry, as a biomarker for disease activity in JDM and response to JAKi.

When discussing pathogenic IFN signaling, anti-melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5) positive JDM is notable. MDA5 is a cytoplasmic protein that is part of the immune defense against viruses, which is activated when it binds viral double stranded RNA leading to the activation of the type I IFN pathway [17]. Multiple studies have demonstrated high ISG expression in anti-MDA5 positive myositis patients, with levels of type I IFN expression associated with a more severe course of disease[18]. Among the type I IFNs, anti-MDA5 positive myositis patients had significantly higher IFN-a levels [19] measured by the sensitive Simoa assay, when compared to patients with other myositis specific antibodies. However, this study did not measure IFN-β levels. Myobundles treated with IFN-α showed significantly less reduction in contractility compared to those treated with IFN-β [6, 20]. In a study of adult DM where a type I IFN-inducible gene signature correlated with cutaneous disease activity, this correlation was with IFN-β but not IFN-a levels[21]. A recent study comparing serum concentration of several cytokines (measured by the less sensitive cytokine bead array), suggested that IFN-l (a type III IFN) and to a lesser extent IFN-β and IFN-a were raised in anti-MDA5 positive DM compared to other IIM[22]. Further studies are needed to clarify how IFN-a and β correlate with JDM phenotypes and evaluate the efficacy of therapeutic agents that target these IFNs differentially across JDM subtypes.

Although type I IFN appears to dominate in the pathogenesis of JDM, IFN-γ, a type II IFN, has also been shown to be dysregulated in JDM and changes with treatment, including in anti-MDA5 positive disease[23, 24]. IFN-γ stimulates macrophages which in turn produce neopterin, a readily-measured biomarker. Neopterin has long been recognised as a potential biomarker for disease activity in JDM [25, 26]. A recent study suggested that serum neopterin levels reflect disease activity better than routine laboratory markers such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) [27]. Longitudinal measurements showed a decrease in neopterin values following medical treatment which corresponded to increasing muscle strength [27]. There was no difference in neopterin levels amongst the different MSAs [27], in contrast with prior studies suggesting that neopterin levels were significantly higher in anti-MDA5 positive disease, especially in the setting of severe lung involvement [28, 29]. Larger-scale studies are needed to evaluate the potential of neopterin as a biomarker for disease activity in JDM and JDM-associated interstitial lung disease (ILD), as well as explore how disease subtype and ethnicity may influence its interpretation.

Treatment: blocking the interferon pathway

Interferons signal through the Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) pathway and hence JAKi have been used in the treatment of myositis. JAKi have also been demonstrated in vitroto reverse type I IFN-induced muscle dysfunction and damage as well as pathological features, such as MHC Class I upregulation, and IFN-induced endothelial damage [5, 6]. These findings provide proof-of-concept for the use of JAKi in JDM. In the real-world setting, JAKi have been used with success in severe and/or refractory cases of myositis [30–33], albeit still off-label. A randomized, treatment delayed-start trial looking at the efficacy of barictinib in the treatment of adult DM and PM found that 14/15 (93%) patients met the primary outcome of at least minimal clinical response while 9/15 (60%) achieved at least moderate clinical response at 24 weeks post-active treatment [34]. Real world data have suggested that the early use of IFN pathway targets may be beneficial in achieving disease control, including in anti-MDA5 patients with severe course of disease e.g. rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease [35–37]. Current phase III trials assessing the efficacy of JAKi in JDM as first line treatment are therefore ongoing, including the MYOCIT (France) [38] and BAR-JDM (United Kingdom) [39] studies.

Other therapeutic agents targeting the IFN pathway have also been used in myositis. Anifrolumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits the IFN-αβ receptor, has shown promising responses in DM [40, 41] and in case reports of JDM [42–44], particularly for patients with refractory skin disease. Dazukibart, a monoclonal antibody against IFNβ, had been reported to rapidly resolve muscle weakness as well as cutaneous and gastrointestinal ulcers in one 9-year-old patient with refractory JDM [45]. In adults, a phase 2 trial showed that dazukibart was safe and efficacious for DM patients with moderate-severe disease [46]. The primary endpoints for skin disease were met but not for muscle disease though improvements in key muscle outcomes were observed. This may suggest that the pathogenesis for impaired muscle function in myositis patients may extend beyond the IFN pathway.

Exciting areas for further development in the field of pathogenic IFN signaling would include: (1) identifying reliable yet easy-to-measure biomarkers for more accurate disease activity assessment, (2) developing tools for precision medicine to guide selection of the best agent for an individual amongst the different anti-IFN therapies available; (3) ascertaining the optimum time to start anti-IFN therapies in the hope of altering the disease course given that IFN dysregulation drives muscle and endothelial damage in JDM.

Vasculopathy and markers of endothelial damage

Vasculopathy is a key feature of JDM with histological evidence of vascular damage on muscle biopsy, abnormal nailfold and gingival capillaries in patients with active disease and may contribute to mechanisms of skin ulceration, calcinosis, gastrointestinal disease, ILD and cardiac involvement notable in severe disease. Vasculopathy may involve two stages: an initial stage marked by immune complex-driven inflammatory small vessel vasculitis, followed by a later stage characterized by non-inflammatory vasculopathy. The latter involves endothelial cell edema and necrosis, leading to luminal narrowing and occlusion of capillaries and arterioles [5, 47].. In view of its role in disease pathogenesis, contribution to ongoing subclinical disease leading to poor outcomes and morbidity, monitoring vasculopathy activity may be valuable in disease management. Several biomarkers have been identified to correlate with vasculopathy in JDM including circulating endothelial cells (CECs), endothelium-derived soluble adhesion molecules and coagulation factors.

CECs are markers of vascular injury and endothelial damage; they represent endothelial cells that have detached from the vessel wall during endothelial damage. Elevated CEC levels have been observed in patients with JDM compared to healthy controls, with significantly higher counts in those with active versus inactive disease [7, 48]. Additionally, patients with abnormal nailfold capillaries exhibited higher CEC levels than those without such abnormalities, although no significant difference was found between patients with and without calcinosis [7]. A strong correlation was identified between CEC levels and galectin-9 [7], further supporting the link between pathogenic IFN signaling and vasculopathy in JDM. CECs have also been found to correlate with pulmonary activity in patients with JDM[48], an important finding given the paucity of reliable measures of extramuscular disease.

Endothelium-derived soluble adhesion molecules facilitate the adhesion of inflammatory cells to the endothelial surface and are thought to play an important role in vasculopathy. In muscle biopsies of patients with JDM, intercellular adhesion molecule‐1 (ICAM-1) expression was significantly increased on capillaries, perimysial and endomysial large vessels when compared to controls, with a predominant distribution over capillaries and perimyseal large vessels [49]. There was however no significant increase in vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) expression in muscle biopsies of JDM patients, although an increased pattern of expression was noted over lumen of large vessels with increased inflammatory infiltrate [49]. Soluble ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 are elevated in serum of DM patients, especially those with anti-MDA5 positive disease [50]. ICAM-1 was also found to be significantly higher in DM patients who had ILD and may be a marker for ILD severity [50].

In JDM, ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 levels were significantly raised in serum compared to controls, however these values did not differentiate between active and inactive disease [7]. In contrast, a conflicting study suggested that lower ICAM-1 levels were associated with more severe vasculopathy. In this study, the number of end row capillary loops (ERL) on nailfold capillaroscopy was taken as a surrogate for severity of vasculopathy; ICAM-1 levels were lower in patients with ERL<4 compared to those with ERL³4. ICAM-1 has been shown to be higher in early disease [50] when the initial vasculitis process may be more active and hence, may not be an accurate measure in later stages of disease. Selectins, another family of soluble adhesion molecules, have shown conflicting results in both DM [51–54] and JDM [7, 48]. Further studies to evaluate the utility of adhesion molecules, particularly ICAM-1, in assessing early vasculopathy and ILD in JDM are needed.

Von Willebrand factor (vWF) is synthesised by endothelial cells and plays a crucial role in blood clotting by facilitating platelet adhesion and aggregation at sites of vascular injury. It is released during endothelial damage and hence, its levels are thought to be a measure of vascular injury. In JDM, serum vWF antigen (vWFAg) were present in higher amounts when compared with controls, while vWF activity was not significantly different [48]. In a cohort of 305 JDM patients, 25% of untreated patients had elevated vWFAg. Elevated vWFAg levels were associated with higher total disease activity scores, difficulty swallowing, more generalized skin involvement, eyelid peripheral blood vessel dilation, more muscle weakness, and higher muscle enzymes, neopterin and ESR as compared to patients with normal vWFAg levels [55]. Longitudinal studies to assess if changes in vWFAg levels correlate with extent of vasculopathy and disease activity will be helpful.

Mitochondrial dysfunction in JDM

Recent transcriptomic, proteomic, and functional studies have highlighted a central role for mitochondrial dysfunction in the pathogenesis of JDM. Using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) of muscle biopsy microarray gene expression data, mitochondrial dysregulation was identified as a key feature distinguishing JDM patients from healthy controls. Functional enrichment of differentially expressed gene modules revealed downregulation of genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation, including components of NADH dehydrogenase, cytochrome c oxidase, and ATP synthase. These mitochondrial genes are integral to energy production and muscle function, and their suppression suggested impaired metabolism and reduced oxidative capacity in JDM muscle tissue [56].

Wilkinson et al.demonstrated that mitochondrial dysfunction in JDM extends beyond muscle tissue to involve peripheral immune cells, particularly CD14⁺ monocytes [57]. In this study, CD14⁺ monocytes from treatment-naïve JDM patients exhibited significant dysregulation of mitochondrial-associated gene expression, which remained abnormal even after clinical treatment. This gene signature was associated with elevated ISG expression in new onset patients but persisted even when IFN-signature had fallen. Mitochondrial morphology in JDM monocytes was altered, characterised by significant mitochondrial fragmentation and formation of abnormally enlarged structures (megamitochondria). These structural changes were accompanied by reduced glycolytic capacity and transcriptional downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation. Importantly, the antioxidant enzyme SOD1, essential for mitochondrial superoxide detoxification, was decreased, leading to increased mitochondrial superoxide production and accumulation of oxidised mitochondrial DNA (oxmtDNA) within monocytes. Elevated levels of circulating cell-free mtDNA were also observed in JDM plasma, which positively correlated with ISG expression, supporting the systemic relevance of mitochondrial-derived danger signals in driving disease. This study further demonstrated that oxmtDNA itself is a potent inducer of ISG expression, capable of activating interferon responses in vitro through both endosomal TLR9 and the cytosolic cGAS–STING pathway [57], opening up novel therapeutic possibilities.

Subsequent work by Duvvuri et al. provided further insights by demonstrating direct evidence of mitochondrial calcification within JDM skeletal muscle. Using electron microscopy, this study identified dense calcium crystals within the mitochondria of degenerating muscle fibres. In parallel, elevated levels of extracellular mitochondrial markers were observed, including circulating mitochondrial DNA and mitochondrial MT-ND6 protein, both correlating with increased CK levels and the presence of calcinosis. Plasma from JDM patients also exhibited elevated levels of novel anti-mitochondrial autoantibodies (AMAs), particularly in patients who developed calcinosis. In vitro modelling using human skeletal muscle cells exposed to calcium ions demonstrated mitochondrial accumulation of hydroxyapatite crystals, resulting in impaired mitochondrial respiration, increased mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), and activation of IFN responses via cGAS–STING [58].

Recent work using non-invasive tape stripping techniques has demonstrated that mitochondrial dysregulation is also evident in the skin of JDM patients. Samples from both lesional and non-lesional skin exhibited substantial transcriptional abnormalities compared to healthy controls, including dysregulation of mitochondrial and oxidative phosphorylation related genes. Importantly, a distinct subgroup of patients with treatment-refractory disease displayed more pronounced mitochondrial defects, characterised by an increase of mitochondrial stress and dysfunction signatures relative to controls. These findings suggest that mitochondrial dysfunction in JDM is not confined to muscle or the immune system but is a systemic feature detectable even in clinically unaffected tissues. Skin, even outside of overt lesions, may therefore serve as a sensitive tissue to monitor mitochondrial pathology and to identify biologically distinct patient subgroups [59].

In more recent work, we investigated muscle tissue using spatial transcriptomics to characterise mitochondrial abnormalities within distinct regions of JDM muscle. We found a marked downregulation of mitochondrial-related genes, particularly those involved in oxidative phosphorylation, ATP synthesis, and respiratory chain function. This suppression was evident both in isolated muscle fibres and in regions enriched with immune cell infiltration and was consistently observed across all patients analysed. Greater suppression of mitochondrial genes was associated with downregulation of key metabolic pathways and pathways linked to peroxisomal function, an organelle essential for ROS metabolism. We validated these findings in an independent RNA-seq dataset. Interestingly, we observed that the degree of mitochondrial deficiency within muscle tissue did not correlate with interferon activity or clinical severity, suggesting that mitochondrial dysfunction in muscle may represent a pathological process parallel to, but which may be independent of, IFN-driven inflammation [60].

Treatments of the future: targeting mitochondrial dysfunction

Given accumulating evidence implicating mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in JDM pathogenesis, there is growing interest in developing targeted therapies that modulate these pathways. One has been on N-acetylcysteine (NAC), a potent antioxidant. In vitro work has demonstrated that NAC dampens oxmtDNA-driven responses in JDM PBMC, reducing ISG expression [57]. While the precise mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated, NAC is known to scavenge ROS, and inhibit complex I activity [57]. Further supporting the therapeutic potential of NAC, a study in an experimental autoimmune myositis mouse model showed that NAC reduced muscle ROS levels, prevented muscle weakness, and normalised ISG and mitochondrial transcription [61].

Targeting nucleic acid sensing pathways is another promising strategy. Both TLR9 antagonism and cGAS inhibition have been shown to reduce oxmtDNA-induced ISG expression in PBMC from treatment- naive JDM patients, indicating that both endosomal and cytosolic DNA sensing pathways may contribute to interferon activation in JDM. Therapeutic modulation of these pathways may offer additional avenues to target mitochondrial-driven immune activation [57].

Collectively, these findings highlight the central role of ROS and oxidative stress in driving mitochondrial dysfunction, interferon activation, and muscle damage in JDM, and highlight multiple therapeutic avenues, including antioxidant strategies, cytokine blockade, and nucleic acid sensing inhibition, with potential translational relevance.

Pyroptosis bridging the innate and adaptive immune system

Pyroptosis, a process of cell death, is characterised by pore formation on cell membranes followed by cell lysis and excretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18, both of which are inflammatory mediators identified in myositis [62–64]. Pyroptosis has been proposed as a mechanism that bridges the innate and adaptive immune responses. In the process of pyroptosis, intracellular components are exposed in a milieu of pro-inflammatory cytokines that assist T-cell differentiation to induce autoimmunity [65]. This may explain why the targets of myositis-specific autoantibodies (MSA) are frequently cytoplasmic or nucleolar in origin. In adult DM, Gasdermin E (GSDME), a protein that can convert mitochondrial apoptosis to pyroptosis, was found to be highly expressed along with IL-1α in muscle fibres from areas with perifascular atrophy. Activated caspase-3 which cleaves GSDME to generate GSDME-N fragment, an important effector for pyroptosis and IL-1α secretion, was also highly expressed in muscle fibres. Furthermore, histological features of mitochondrial dysfunction was detected around these areas of perifascicular atrophy, supporting the concept of intersection of pyroptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction [66]. A study in adult DM and polymyositis, found that pathological muscle lesions had upregulated glycolysis, which in turn activates the NLRP3 inflammasome that drives pyroptosis and consequent muscle damage [67]. An increased expression of pyroptotic related genes has been reported in JDM [68], though further mechanistic studies are needed to explore its exact role in the pathogenesis of JDM.

Elevated neutrophil extracellular traps in JDM

Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are net-like structures composed of DNA, histones, and granule proteins released by activated neutrophils to trap pathogens but also implicated in autoimmune tissue injury. In JDM, NETs were significantly elevated compared to controls and correlated with muscle, lung, and vascular disease activity. Low-density granulocyte (LDG) levels were increased and associated with more severe skin and muscle disease and vasculopathy [69]. Elevated NETs were most strongly associated with anti-MDA5 and anti-TIF1γ autoantibodies in a combined juvenile and adult myositis cohort [69]. In parallel, transcriptomic and proteomic profiling of JDM peripheral blood revealed an upregulated NET-associated signature, reflecting active neutrophil degranulation and extracellular trap formation [70]. Neutrophils were found to infiltrate calcified JDM muscle tissue, where they phagocytose calcium crystals and undergo NETosis, including the release of both nuclear and mitochondrial DNA. Circulating NET levels were particularly elevated in patients with calcinosis, and NET degradation was impaired in patients with anti-MDA5 and anti-TIF1γ autoantibodies [71].

The role of B cells in JDM

The pathogenesis of JDM has been thought to involve the adaptive immune system and autoreactive B cells in view of the associations with particular MSAs and distinct clinical phenotypes of JDM [72]. We previously reported that total B cells are expanded in JDM patients, with notable alterations in B cell subsets, including abnormal proportions of immature transitional, mature and memory B cell populations. The expansion of immature transitional B cells was strongly correlated with disease activity and normalized with treatment. B cells exhibited heightened proliferation and were linked to strong IFNα and TLR7 pathway signatures. Further, stimulation of these pathways selectively promoted IL-6 production over IL-10 in JDM B cells, a skewing not observed in healthy controls, suggesting a pro-inflammatory bias [73].

Recent work confirmed that this expansion of B cells is associated with increased disease activity in early untreated JDM. Patients with higher B cell counts exhibited worse clinical and laboratory markers, including higher muscle and overall disease activity scores, increased neopterin levels, and more severe nailfold capillary changes. Additionally, BAFF, a cytokine known to increase proliferation of B cells, was elevated in JDM patients and correlated with the lymphocyte count, supporting further exploration of B-cell-targeted therapies, such as BAFF inhibition, in JDM [74].

A recent study identified the expansion of a distinct subset of memory B cells lacking the follicular homing receptor CXCR5 (CXCR5low/−CM B cells) in JDM patients. The frequency and absolute numbers of these cells correlated with muscle disease activity and aldolase levels. This expansion was correlated with increased ISG expression. In parallel, CXCR5− CD4+ Th2 memory cells were expanded, particularly early in disease, and their frequency was correlated with the expansion of the B cell subset, highlighting a B–T cell axis contributing to JDM pathogenesis [75].

Building on these findings, Rabadam et al. used single-cell CITEseq profiling to show that treatment-naive JDM is characterized by marked expansion of several naïve B cell populations. An immature naïve subset (CD38⁺CD24⁺CD10⁺) expressed genes essential for plasma cell differentiation, including MZB1 and CD38. This immature skewing was associated with clinical disease activity and paralleled persistent Th2-skewed CD4⁺ T cell programs, supporting a model of extrafollicular cell interactions in JDM. B cell gene programs enriched for mRNA processing, chromatin remodeling, and cytoskeletal reorganization remained upregulated even in patients in remission off therapy, suggesting that transcriptional dysregulation in the B cell compartment may underlie chronic immune activation. These findings further support the hypothesis that JDM pathogenesis involves extrafollicular immune interactions, with implications for therapies targeting these cell states [76].

Role of myositis-specific antibodies

MSAs have been an important part of JDM diagnosis, allowing clinicians to better estimate risks of specific clinical features. MSAs frequently target proteins that are either within the cytoplasm (MDA5 and Jo-1) or nuclei (Mi2, NXP-2, and TIF1γ) of cells. Their role in JDM pathogenesis has been unclear since it had been classically thought that autoantibodies could only act on cells extracellularly and were not able to reach their intracellular targets. A recent study has suggested that MSAs may be internalised intracellularly to act on their cytoplasmic or nucleic protein targets [77]. Pinal-Fernandez et al.observed that muscle tissue from adult DM patients with anti-Mi2 antibodies were found to have antibodies deposited within the nuclei of the muscle fibres, corresponding to the location of the Mi2 protein [78]. Transcriptomic analysis showed that these muscle fibres overexpressed genes normally repressed by the Mi2-containing nucleosome remodelling and deacetylation complex and overexpression of these genes could be reproduced when cultured muscle cells internalized purified antibodies from anti-Mi2 positive patients. Similarly in anti-PM/Scl-positive patients, immunoglobulin deposition was seen predominantly in the nuclear and nucleolar regions. These antibodies recognize components of the nuclear RNA exosome complex which function to degrade RNA. Muscle fibres of these patients overexpressed long noncoding RNAs and other transcripts, suggesting dysfunction of the nuclear RNA exosome complex in anti- PM/Scl-positive patients. This was also observed in human myoblasts after internalization of purified anti-PM/Scl antibodies. Likewise, confocal immunofluorescence localised immunoglobulins to the cytoplasm in muscle cells from anti-MDA5 patients and electroporation of cultured myoblasts with anti-MDA5 Ab led to robust overexpression of IFNβ and IFNβ1-inducible genes, suggesting that anti-MDA5 antibodies may bind and activate cytoplasmic MDA5 directly as part of DM pathogenesis [77]. Further work and replication of these results will be crucial to enhance our understanding of how MSAs affect target protein function, impact downstream signaling and alter cellular biology to allow personalised treatment targets and create treatment options for synthetic small molecule treatment in JDM.

Treatment: B cell targets

The Rituximab in Myositis (RIM) prospective randomized trial [79] assessed the efficacy of rituximab in refractory JDM and DM patients. Although this did not meet its primary endpoint, sub analysis did suggest benefit in JDM patients, with glucocorticoid-sparing effect. Obinutuzumab and ofatumumab fully humanized or human anti-CD20 agents enable more effective B-cell depletion than rituximab. Ofatumumab was reported to be beneficial in 2 adult patients with anti-SRP positive immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) [80, 81]. Obinutuzumab was successfully used in an adult with anti-Jo1 syndrome following non-response to rituximab, resulting in marked improvement in muscle strength and reduction in muscle enzymes [82], suggesting that this may be an alternative for patients who are intolerant to rituximab or where rituximab has been ineffective.

Daratumumab, an anti-CD38 which depletes plasma cells, had been reported to be beneficial in patients with anti-MDA5 antibody positive DM and rapidly progressive ILD (RP-ILD) [83–85] and in one patient with refractory anti-SRP positive IMNM[86]. Following treatment with daratumumab, clinical improvement along with decreased or undetectable autoantibody titers were observed [83–86]. A case series of 6 DM and 7 JDM patients treated with belimumab (anti-BAFF) had improvements in muscle pain, fatigue, muscle enzymes and skin disease [87], although changes in validated outcome measures MMT, CMAS and CAT scores were not reported. Efgartigimod is a human IgG1 antibody fragment that binds to FcRn, preventing IgG recycling and reducing circulating levels of autoantibody. In a mouse model with IMNM, efgartigimod prevented further muscle necrosis and allowed for muscle regeneration [88]. Seven patients with refractory IMNM were treated with efgartigimod and 4 (57%) achieved clinical response [89]. These discoveries offer hope for treatment options beyond rituximab in patients with inflammatory myositis, making this an area of active interest and anticipation.

Treatment: CAR T-cell therapy

An exciting recent development in the field of rheumatology is chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy which involves engineering autologous T cells from the patient to target specific cellular proteins. This therapy is unique to each patient and allows for targeting of the desired protein in tandem with cytotoxic destruction of the target cell, and better tissue penetration of the therapeutic agent. CD19 CAR T-cell therapy has been used in refractory autoimmune diseases including IIM with encouraging results [90]. The first CD19 CAR T-cell therapy in JDM was performed in a 12-year-old boy with antibody negative myositis refractory to multiple therapies including rituximab and cyclophosphamide [91]. Following CD19 CAR T-cell therapy, the patient achieved sustained B-cell depletion with clinical and radiological improvement off medications 8 months post-infusion. Type I IFN scores along with circulating CXCL9 and CXCL10 normalized by week 24 [91], indicating beneficial effects of CAR T-cell therapy on pathogenic IFN signaling in JDM. A 10-year-old patient with anti-MDA5 positive JDM with macrophage activation syndrome and RP-ILD requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation achieved treatment free remission 11 months following CD19 CAR T-cell therapy [92].

In contrast to the experience of CAR T-cell therapy in haematological malignancies, patients with autoimmune diseases receiving CAR T-cell therapy appear to develop fewer side effects, with less cytokine release syndrome and no cases of immune-effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) reported [93]. Instead, a phenomenon known as local immune effector cell-associated toxicity syndrome (LICATS) was observed in patients with SLE, systemic sclerosis and IIM receiving CD19 CAR T-cell therapy [94]. Following CAR T-cell therapy, 77% of patients developed impairment in disease-specific end organs which were affected by primary disease prior to infusion. Most patients who developed LICATS had onset within a month of infusion coinciding with the period of B-cell aplasia. The authors inferred that this may be a result of B-cell cleansing effects by effector cells within the organs that were affected by the patient’s primary autoimmune disease [94]. The majority of these reactions were mild lasting a median of 11 days, resolving spontaneously or with a short course of glucocorticoids.

Multiple clinical trials to assess the efficacy and safety of CAR T-cell therapy against CD19, CD20, CD22, B-Cell Maturation Antigen and CD7 (autoreactive T cell target) are currently ongoing for IIM and other autoimmune diseases in both adults and children: this is an exciting space to watch.

Conclusions

Modern studies across both paediatric and adult myositis are revealing many new treatment targets and potentially useful biomarkers, as well as linking the contribution of several aspects of the immune system and cellular processes to the pathophysiology of IIM. Together these insights provide new hope for a more complete understanding of the key processes which drive myositis pathology and therefore enable more targeted treatments, to realise our common goal of true biological remission for patients.
