Multilingual LLM Prompting Strategies for Medical English–Vietnamese Machine Translation
Nhu Vo 1,3 , Nu-Uyen-Phuong Le 2 , Dung D. Le 1 , Massimo Piccardi 3 , Wray Buntine 1 1 College of Engineering and Computer Science,VinUniversity,Vietnam 2 University of Queensland, Australia 3 University of Technology Sydney, Australia {nhu.vd,dung.ld,wray.b}@vinuni.edu.vn nuuyenphuong.le@student.uq.edu.au, massimo.piccardi@uts.edu.au

Abstract
Medical English–Vietnamese machine translation (En–Vi MT) is essential for healthcare access and communication in Vietnam, yet Vietnamese remains a low-resource and under-studied language. We systematically evaluate prompting strategies for six multilingual LLMs (0.5B–9B parameters) on the MedEV dataset, comparing zero-shot, few-shot, and dictionary-augmented prompting with Meddict, an English–Vietnamese medical lexicon. Results show that model scale is the primary driver of performance: larger LLMs achieve strong zero-shot results, while few-shot prompting yields only marginal improvements. In contrast, terminology-aware cues and embedding-based example retrieval consistently improve domain-specific translation. These findings underscore both the promise and the current limitations of multilingual LLMs for medical En–Vi MT.



1
Introduction

Accurate machine translation (MT) of medical texts is crucial for improving healthcare access in low-resource languages such as Vietnamese. However, medical translation poses particular challenges: domain-specific terminology, abbreviations, and clinical expressions are often mistranslated by general-purpose LLMs
(
Ganesh et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Nazi and Peng 2024
)
. Errors in terminology can have serious consequences in downstream healthcare communication.

While recent work has explored prompting strategies for general-domain multilingual translation
(
Verma et al. 2022
;
Gao et al. 2024
)
, there has been no controlled comparison of prompting techniques for medical En-Vi MT, particularly in handling of medical terminology. Prior studies have introduced approaches such as dictionary-enhanced prompting and semantically guided example selection
(
Zheng et al. 2024
)
, but their combined effect has not been systematically examined in this setting.
Semenov et al. 2023
highlighted that even state-of-the-art MT systems frequently fail to enforce correct terminology, underscoring the need for methods that explicitly incorporate domain-specific lexicons in medical MT.
Furthermore, existing evaluations of small-sized and medium-sized multilingual LLMs for medical MT remain fragmented and lack standardized protocols
(
Bedi et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2025
)
.

In this work, we first present a systematic evaluation of prompting strategies for medical En-Vi MT across a diverse set of multilingual LLMs, including Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
, Sailor2-1B-SFT
(
Dou et al. 2025
)
, Sailor2-8B-SFT
(
Dou et al. 2025
)
, Llama-SEA-LION-v3-8B-IT
(
Ng et al. 2025
)
, and Gemma-SEA-LION-v3-9B-IT
(
Ng et al. 2025
)
. We evaluate multilingual LLMs on medical En-Vi MT task under multiple prompting strategies: (i) zero-shot prompting, (ii)
n
-shot prompting with randomly sampled examples (
n\in{1,8,16,32}
), (iii) dictionary-augmented prompting using a curated medical lexicon (Meddict-VinUni Medical Dictionary
1
1

1

http://meddict-vinuni.com/
) with terminology-aware prompting. For terminology-aware prompting, we test both zero-shot hints injected into the input and 1-shot settings where the example is either randomly chosen or retrieved via embeddings.
To provide a comprehensive picture, we combine automatic evaluation metrics included: (i) SacreBLEU
(
Post 2018
)
, (ii) CometKiwi
(
Rei et al. 2022
)
, (iii) XCOMET
(
Guerreiro et al. 2024
)
with qualitative error analysis focusing on terminology fidelity.

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic comparison of prompting strategies for medical En-Vi MT, and our findings highlight key trade-offs between prompting methods, demonstrate the advantages of dictionary integration, and reveal persistent challenges in ensuring reliable translation of medical terminology.

2
Dataset and Medical dictionary

For our experiments, we use the MedEV dataset
2
2

2

https://huggingface.co/datasets/nhuvo/MedEV

(
Vo et al. 2024
)
, a recently released English–Vietnamese medical parallel corpus. MedEV and Meddict provide complementary resources: MedEV supplies real-world sentence-level translation data, while Meddict offers high-quality terminology mappings that enable terminology-aware prompting strategies.

MedEV consists of de-identified medical texts, ensuring that personally identifiable information is removed, with 360K parallel sentence pairs. Each validation and test set contains over 8,000 bilingual sentence pairs
(
Vo et al. 2024
)
. The dataset is distributed under an open license for research purposes and has been widely adopted as a benchmark for evaluating medical machine translation systems in low-resource settings.

To support terminology fidelity, we further incorporate Meddict, an English–Vietnamese medical dictionary developed at VinUniversity with over 64K entries. Meddict provides curated translations of specialized medical terminology and has been officially recognized under Intellectual Property Rights Certificate No. 3365/2024/QTG. The dictionary is distributed for academic and healthcare applications under its institutional license.

3
Experimental Details

3.1
Models

We conduct experiments on six publicly available multilingual LLMs covering small-scale to medium-scale sizes: Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Sailor2-1B-SFT, Sailor2-8B-SFT, Llama-SEA-LION-v3-8B-IT, and Gemma-SEA-LION-v3-9B-IT. These models were selected to represent a range of parameter scales (0.5B–9B), instruction-tuning regimes (base vs. SFT vs. instruct-tuned), and multilingual coverage.

Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct
, released under Apache-2.0, is a 0.5B-parameter instruction-tuned model with 24 transformer layers and 14 attention heads. It supports a context length of 32,768 tokens and can generate up to 8,192 tokens
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
. The model covers 29 languages, including Vietnamese.

Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
, also under Apache-2.0, is a 7.6B-parameter instruction-tuned model with 28 layers and 28 attention heads. It extends the context length to 131,072 tokens, with a generation capacity of up to 8,192 tokens
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
. Like the 0.5B variant, it supports 29 languages, including Vietnamese.

Sailor2-1B-SFT
, developed by the SAIL team and released under Apache-2.0, is a 1B-parameter model based on the Llama2 backbone and fine-tuned with supervised instruction data and expanded from the Qwen2.5 base models of 0.5B
(
Dou et al. 2025
)
. It is designed for Southeast Asian languages, with explicit support for Vietnamese.

Sailor2-8B-SFT
, an 8B-parameter model and expanded from the Qwen2.5 base models of 7B, is also instruction-tuned (SFT) and released under Apache-2.0
(
Dou et al. 2025
)
. Compared to the 1B version, it provides stronger performance while maintaining coverage of Southeast Asian languages, including Vietnamese.

Llama-SEA-LION-v3-8B-IT
, developed by AI Singapore, is an 8B-parameter multilingual decoder model built on the Llama-3.1 architecture and released under the Llama 3.1 Community License
(
Ng et al. 2025
)
. It has been further pre-trained on approximately 200B tokens across 11 Southeast Asian languages, including Vietnamese, and employs the default Llama-3.1 tokenizer.

Gemma-SEA-LION-v3-9B-IT
, also developed by AI Singapore, is a 9B-parameter decoder model instruction-tuned on English and ASEAN languages (including Vietnamese) using the Gemma2 architecture
(
Ng et al. 2025
)
. It uses the default Gemma-2 tokenizer, supports a context length of 8,192 tokens, and is distributed under the Gemma Community License for non-commercial research.

All models are tested in the same inference environment. We set a maximum output length (
\operatorname{max\_length}=512
) to avoid truncation. Experiments are executed on 6 NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPUs to ensure consistent hardware conditions.

3.2
Prompting methods

We conduct a comprehensive set of prompting experiments to evaluate model performance under diverse conditions. Specifically, our study examines three main prompting strategies:

Zero-shot prompting:
the model generates translations without access to any examples
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
. See
A.1
for details of the implementation.

N-shot prompting:
the model is provided with
n
randomly sampled examples from the training set, where
n\in\{1,8,16,32\}

(
Brown et al. 2020
)
. See
A.2
for details of the implementation.

Dictionary-augmented prompting:
a curated medical dictionary is used to guide English-to-Vietnamese translation, providing terminology-aware cues that supplement the input
(
Ghazvininejad et al. 2023
)
. Since Meddict is compiled with English medical terms and their definitions or Vietnamese phrases, our experiments focus on the English-to-Vietnamese translation direction. We examine two prompting strategies that incorporate terminology hints:

•

Zero-shot with terminology hints:
terminology cues are directly injected into the input sentence, enabling the model to attend to domain-specific vocabulary without reference examples. See
A.3
for details of the implementation.

•

1-shot with terminology hints:
the model is provided with one illustrative example alongside the input sentence, where both contain explicit terminology cues. Unlike N-shot prompting, the example is drawn from a subset of the training data. This subset is restricted to sentences that include the relevant medical terms, as defined in Meddict. This ensures that the in-context example directly reinforces the use of terminology hints. See
A.4
for details of the implementation.

3.3
Evaluation

To assess translation quality, we employ three automatic metrics: (i)
SacreBLEU

Post 2018
for surface-level correspondence, (ii)
CometKiwi-XL

Rei et al. 2022
for reference-free adequacy and fluency, and (iii)
XCOMET-XL

Guerreiro et al. 2024
for advanced reference-based evaluation.

Few shot promptings:
Figure
1
shows that model scale is the dominant factor affecting the MT performance: small models such as Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct achieve only 11–14 BLEU, whereas Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct exceeds 31 BLEU on English-to-Vietnamese and 29 BLEU on Vietnamese-to-English. Among all models, Gemma-SEA-LION-v3-9B-IT sets the strongest baseline, consistently surpassing 36 BLEU on English-to-Vietnamese and 26–28 BLEU on Vietnamese-to-English. Instruction-tuned models yield stronger performance than SFT-only variants across all sizes. In addition, when comparing models of similar scale, the Gemma and Llama series outperform the Sailor and Qwen models. Interestingly, few-shot prompting provides only marginal gains: across models, moving from 0-shot to 32-shot typically changes BLEU by less than one point, suggesting that large multilingual LLMs generalize well in zero-shot clinical MT. Sentence length sensitivity:
Regarding BLEU scores by sentence length, Figure
2
shows that translation quality peaks on medium-length sentences (20–50 tokens) and drops for very short (less than 10 tokens) and very long (greater or equal 50 tokens) inputs. This could be due to the lack of context in shorter sentences and the structural complexity of longer ones
(
Wan et al. 2022
;
Pouget-Abadie et al. 2014
)
. Besides, larger models (Sailor2-8B-SFT,Llama-SEA-LION-v3-8B-IT, Gemma-SEA-LION-v3-9B-IT) are more robust across lengths, maintaining strong performance on long sentences, while smaller ones degrade sharply.

Prompting strategies:
Table
1
shows that adding medical terminology hints improves performance across all models, indicating that terminology-aware prompting helps models align better with domain-specific vocabulary. Meanwhile, combining hints with a random in-context example yields only marginal gains. By contrast, pairing hints with examples retrieved via embeddings provides the largest and most consistent gains. These results highlight that example relevance matters more than quantity. For smaller models such as Qwen2.5-0.5B, however, CometKiwi scores were unstable, often producing incomplete or off-topic outputs despite clear prompting. Nevertheless, the relative improvements from hints and embedding-based retrieval remain consistent across BLEU, CometKiwi, and XCOMET, suggesting that these strategies enhance not only surface accuracy but also semantic adequacy and terminology fidelity.

5
Conclusion

In this paper we have presented three prompting strategies on six LLMs (0.5B–9B parameters) on medical En-Vi MT. Our results show that model scale is the primary factor affecting the translation quality, given that performance improves steadily as the model’s size increases. While few-shot prompting provides only marginal gains, incorporating terminology-aware cues and embedding-based example retrieval yields consistent improvements, particularly in handling domain-specific vocabulary. These findings highlight both the robustness and the limitations of current multilingual LLMs for medical En-Vi MT.

Limitations and Future Work

Our research have several standing limitations. Firstly, our experiments are limited to models up to 9B parameters. While we observe clear gains with scale, we have not examined very large LLMs (>9B), which may reveal different behaviors in few-shot prompting and terminology handling. Secondly, our evaluation in this research uses automatic metrics (BLEU, CometKiwi, XCOMET), with only qualitative error analysis. Human evaluation—particularly on terminology fidelity and clinical adequacy—would provide stronger evidence. Moreover, evaluating on terminology-specific subsets could more clearly isolate the impact of dictionary integration. Finally, we focus on zero-shot, few-shot, and dictionary-augmented prompting. Other techniques such as instruction tuning with domain-specific corpora or reinforcement-based approaches (e.g., DPO) were not explored. Thus, our findings only reflect the relative performance of prompting-based methods without broader coverage of adaptation strategies. Therefore, in future work, we will apply fine-tuning techniques such as Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to investigate how domain-specific data and medical dictionaries can further improve medical En-Vi MT.

Ethical Considerations

The MedEV dataset is derived exclusively from publicly available sources and does not contain any private or identifiable patient information. Additionally, the content is not intended for public or commercial use
(
Vo et al. 2024
)
.
