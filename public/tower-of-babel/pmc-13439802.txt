Exploring nursing students’ perceptions of the clinical learning environment: a sequential mixed-methods study at two Moroccan institutes
Authors: El hassan Roussi, Fatima Barich, Kawtar Chafik, Milouda Chebabe, Amina Barkat, Fatima Zahra Laamiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinical experiential learning is a vital component of nursing education, ensuring that students integrate theoretical knowledge into practice and professional development. Evaluating clinical placements is essential to ensure effective learning. This study aims to evaluate nursing students’ perceptions of the clinical learning environment and identify factors associated with students’ satisfaction at two public nursing institutes.MethodsThis study used an explanatory sequential mixed-method design in which A cross-sectional research used the Clinical Learning Environment, Supervision and Nurse Teacher (CLES + T) scale among 486 students, followed by a qualitative phase using semi-structured interviews with 26 students. Qualitative and quantitative findings were integrated using a joint display.ResultsStudents were generally dissatisfied with their placements, with a low overall CLES + T score (2.51 ± 0.32). Subscale scores ranged from 2.38 ± 0.63 for the ward manager’s leadership style to 2.62 ± 0.56 for the role of clinical nurse teacher. Students reported being more satisfied with their experiences in hospital settings than in primary healthcare centers (p < 0.001). The qualitative results support and provide in-depth explanations for the CLES + T domains. The unsuitable learning environment, the theory-practice gap, combined with supervision shortcomings and the ineffective role of the nurse teacher, represent the most recurrent challenges faced by students during internships.ConclusionsStudents at both institutes rated their clinical placements as below average. Despite various reforms implemented to promote clinical training at the national level, several structural, organizational, and supervisory shortcomings persist at the institutes studied. It is recommended to enhance the role of clinical nurse instructors and strengthen partnerships between institutions and clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09578-w.

Introduction

In a constantly changing context where the population’s health needs are becoming complex and diverse, and safety and quality requirements are growing, it is essential that nurses acquire the necessary skills [1]. Acquiring these skills requires training that aligns with standards [2]. Nursing education combines theoretical courses and clinical learning. Clinical learning is a vital component of health education, representing 40%-50% of the education program [3].

Clinical training is based on Kolb’s experiential learning model [4]. This approach is based on two principles: contextualization and mentoring. Immersing students in real clinical learning environments (CLE), like hospitals, allows them to confront authentic situations to develop professional skills [5]. However, CLEs are often complex and challenging [3], they often present structural, organizational, and socio-relational constraints that can hinder student learning [6, 7]. The pedagogical atmosphere, the relationship with supervisors, the institutional culture and the work organization in the clinical setting profoundly influence the internships experiences. A supportive CLE enables the development of technical and non-technical skills, contributes to the construction of students’ professional identity, and increases their commitment and satisfaction [8, 9]. While unsuitable CLE can negatively affect the learning process, leading to frustration and demotivation among students [10], it can also fuel a negative attitude toward the nursing profession [11]. Previous studies have reported students dropping out of training and the profession due to unpleasant conditions and unsupportive pedagogical atmosphere within clinical settings [3, 12].

Within CLEs, students learn under the supervision of an expert. Kolb (2015) [4] emphasizes the vital role of supervision in experiential learning. This component in health education is based on principles including scaffolding, modeling, coaching/supervision, articulation, reflection, and exploration [13]. The effectiveness of supervision depends on several factors, including the supervision model (one-on-one preceptorship or group-based approach) [14] and the nature of the supervisor/student relationship [8].

Several previous studies have reported challenges related to group-based supervision models [10, 15] in which students highlighted the inefficient clinical mentoring due to the large number of trainees mentored by the same supervisor. The group-based approach does not allow for free discussion (about difficulties and needs) and does not guarantee confidentiality in the relationship [14]. Other studies have emphasized the importance of the role of the clinical nurse teacher in clinical supervision [8, 15, 16]. Students highlighted a lack of involvement, low level of pedagogical, managerial skills, and expertise among clinical nurse teachers, which hinders their role [8, 10].

Nursing education in Morocco underwent major reforms starting in 2019, with the training program being revised to align with global standards. Then, in 2020, the first cohort of clinical nurse teachers with a first Master’s degree (as part of the Bachelor’s-Master’s-Doctorate system) joined the academic staff of nursing teachers to strengthen clinical supervision within HINPHT. However, in 2022, the number of students increased exponentially, particularly after the adoption of the new framework law 09–21 on social security coverage [17]. This increase in the number of trainees has created a major problem of overcrowding in clinical settings and has had a negative impact on the quality of clinical supervision. These constraints, combined with the reduction in the amount of time devoted to internships in the new programs [18], limit the effectiveness of experiential learning for students in clinical settings. Insufficient practice would lead to weak preparation of the new graduates, which threatens the safety of the care provided [8]. Therefore, it is essential to regularly evaluate training conditions in clinical settings to ensure effective learning and thus adequate preparation for future nurses [18].

Research in this area is still underdeveloped, particularly after the COVID-19 crisis, which shook healthcare systems. To our knowledge, the only study that evaluated students’ experiences in clinical placements was conducted in 2019, well before the reforms were implemented. This study aims to evaluate students’ perceptions of the clinical learning environment using a validated scale and explain associated factors with the students’ satisfaction.

Methods

Study design

An explanatory sequential mixed design was used to evaluate students’ satisfaction with the clinical learning environment using a valid scale in the first quantitative phase, and to examine the perspectives and underlying factors in depth through semi-structured interviews. This design allows for a thorough understanding of students’ perceptions [19, 20].

Mixed methods integration

The research objective guided the connection between the two phases. First, the results of the quantitative phase were used to select participants for the qualitative phase using the extreme scoring method. In the second step, the results from both components were integrated into the interpretation phase. The results from the qualitative and quantitative components were integrated using a joint display board. Data were compared side by side to gain a deeper understanding of CLE at the institutes studied. The joint display provides a visual means of the qualitative and quantitative results. The side-by-side presentation of the two types of results is part of a complementary triangulation approach that provides explanations for the quantitative scores observed.

Study setting

This study was conducted from September to November 2025 at two Moroccan public nursing institutes: (1) The Higher Institute of Health Sciences (HIHS) is the only higher education institution affiliated with the Hassan 1st University, and (2) The Higher Institute of Nursing Professions and Health Techniques (HINPHT) is a higher education institution under the departments of the Moroccan Ministry of Health. Both institutes offer a three-year program. In clinical placement students are supervised by both clinical staff and nurse teachers who are affiliated to the institute.

Sample size calculation

The estimated minimum sample size (486 students) for this study was calculated using the Cochran formula for finite population [22, 23] with a 95% confidence level (5% margin of error), and an expected mean score and standard deviation of 3.56 ± 0.70 as documented in previous literature in a similar Arabic country [24]. The data collection team visited the students in their classrooms after their last clinical placement and verbally informed them about the purpose of the study. The students then signed a written consent form before completing the survey.

Sampling strategy for the qualitative phase

Participants in this phase were selected using purposive and convenience sampling. The nested purposive sampling method is consistent with the proposed explanatory strategy [25]. Participants were recruited from the sample that had participated in the quantitative phase. Following a descriptive analysis of the CLES + T scores, students with extreme overall CLES + T total scores in the lowest quartile (CLES + T total score ≤ 2.30) and highest quartile (CLES + T total score ≥ 2.72) were selected to examine contrasting perceptions of the clinical learning environment. A total of 26 participants were recruited (13 students from each group of extreme scores). To ensure heterogeneity of profiles and data richness, the selection also took into account gender, level of study, and clinical placement site. Eligible students were contacted and invited to voluntarily participate in semi-structured interviews. The objectives of the qualitative component were explained to them, and their informed consent was obtained prior to conducting the interviews. Participant recruitment continued until data saturation was reached.,

Data collection

For quantitative data, we used the Arabic version of the Clinical Learning Environment, Supervision and Nurse Teacher Scale (CLES + T) to evaluate the students’ satisfaction with the clinical learning environment [26]. This tool scale comprises 34 items grouped into five dimensions: “Pedagogical atmosphere in the ward”, “Leadership style of the ward manager”, “Premises of care in the ward”, “the supervisory relationship”, and “the role of the nurse teacher.” The questions were rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). In the present study the CLES + T scale had a good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.93). The five dimensions show strong consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha ranging between 0.678 and 0.836 (the pedagogical atmosphere on the ward (0.696), the leadership style of the unit/ward manager (0.767), the premises of care on the ward (0.678); The supervisory relationship (0.836) and the role of the (clinical) nurse teacher (0.835). Students also answered questions related to sociodemographic information, including gender, academic year, internship duration, clinical setting, and type of supervision.

For qualitative data: Following the guidelines of the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [27]. Interviews were conducted by the first author, who is a nurse educator at HINPHT and an expert in qualitative methodology. The interviews were based on a topic guide comprising open-ended questions based on the five domains of the CLES + T scale like “How would you describe the general atmosphere within the clinical setting in which you completed your internship?” “How do you perceive the role and style of the department heads/managers?” Quantitative item-by-item analysis identified items with high or low scores, which were the focus of some questions in the interviews to examine the underlying explanatory factors. Follow-up questions were used to deepen the understanding of the students’ views (Additional File1). The face-to-face interviews were conducted at the institute and lasted approximately 60 min. The discussions were recorded. None of the interviews were repeated. The interviews continued until data saturation was reached, defined as the point at which additional interviews no longer yielded new insights or significant findings. Saturation was observed starting with the 23rd interview; however, three additional interviews were conducted to confirm this saturation, bringing the total number of interviews to 26.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were presented using descriptive statistics such as means with standard deviation, frequencies, and proportions to describe the students’ sociodemographic and academic data. For the inferential analysis, the normality of the CLES + T scale score distribution was first tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (D = 0.027; p = 0.2). The student’s t-test was used to compare the means of the CLES + T subscales in the different groups (Gender, academic year categories, and clinical site). Multiple linear regression was used for the multivariate analysis of factors associated with the total CLES + T score. Statistical analysis were conducted using the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 25.

The qualitative data from the interviews were analyzed using the directed deductive content analysis framework [28]. As a first step, recordings were transcribed after each interview. Transcripts were then reviewed and compared with the original recordings to verify their accuracy, completeness, and fidelity to the participants’ statements. Any inconsistencies or omissions were corrected prior to the coding stage. A member checking process with some participants to verify the accuracy of the interpretations derived from the interviews. The analysis process then began in a structured manner; tow researchers developed a categorization matrix/coding grid based on the five predefined dimensions of the CLES + T scale (Additional file 2). This coding framework thus included hierarchical categories (themes, subthemes, and codes) that allowed for a systematic analysis consistent with the study’s objectives. The transcripts were then read repeatedly to identify relevant units of meaning, which were assigned to the corresponding categories in the grid. When some excerpts did not fully correspond to the predefined codes but provided important explanatory elements, new codes were created and discussed among the researchers to refine the analytical matrix. The coding grid thus served as an analytical framework for organizing the data, comparing participants’ perceptions, and linking the qualitative results with the quantitative results. Quotations were used for illustration. We used NVivo 12 software to organize and manage the data, particularly for importing transcripts and coding by classifying significant units of meaning into defined themes.

Trustworthiness

To ensure rigor, the following criteria [29] were respected.

Credibility

The profile of the researchers, who are both professors and clinical instructors with in-depth knowledge of the clinical environment. The qualitative analysis was conducted collaboratively among the researchers throughout the process to discuss disagreements (transcription, development of the coding grid, identification of units of meaning). In addition transcripts were returned to the participants for verification to ensure the credibility.

Transferability

To promote the transferability of the results, a detailed description of the study context and participant characteristics was provided; this will allow for a cautious interpretation of the results and an assessment of their relevance in similar contexts.

Reliability

Was ensured using the valid CLES + T scale to guide the interview guide questions and the directed content analysis method, which enabled us to define a coding grid that guaranteed rigorous categorization.

Confirmability

Was ensured by using participants’ quotes for illustration and by verification carried out by a third author, a nursing professor with over 10 years’ experience in clinical supervision.

Quantitative results

486 students completed the questionnaire, 80.7% of whom were female with an average age of 20.4 ± 1.3. Second-year students accounted for 63.4% of the sample. More than half (58.2%) had completed more than 8 weeks of clinical training before responding to the questionnaire, indicating that the students had been sufficiently immersed in clinical learning environments to ensure a reliable assessment of environmental conditions (Table 1).

As shown in Table 1, clinical supervision of students was mainly provided by clinical staff, particularly nurses (48.1%) and head nurses (45.7%). On the other hand, faculty members were very little involved in supervising nursing trainees (4.5%). Most students (70.2%) reported that their supervisors change from one internship site to another, while 13.8% said they do not have a designated supervisor. Most students (77.7%) highlighted that supervisors were responsible for supporting several students at the same time.

Students’ satisfaction with the clinical learning environment

Table 2 revealed an average CLES + T total score of 2.51 ± 0.32 on the CLES + T scale. Within the scale’s dimensions, the role of the nurse teacher received the highest score (2.62 ± 0.56), followed by the premises of care on the ward (2.54 ± 0.71), the pedagogical atmosphere on the ward (2.53 ± 0.46), and the supervisory relationship (2.46 ± 0.69). The leadership style of the ward manager dimension was the least rated, with a mean score of (2.38 ± 0.63).

Students who completed their internships in hospitals had a significantly higher average CLES + T score (2.56 ± 0.28) than students who completed their internships in primary health care centers (2.44 ± 0.35) (p < 0.001). The comparative analysis by academic year of study revealed that third-year students were significantly more satisfied than their second-year peers with regard to the pedagogical atmosphere (p < 0.001), the premises of care in the ward (p < 0.001), the leadership style of the ward manager (p < 0.001), and the supervisory relationship (p < 0.001), suggesting a difference in perception depending on the level of study.

As stated in Table 3, the results showed that students who completed their internships at hospitals reported significantly higher levels of satisfaction than participants who had experiences at primary health care centers in terms of the pedagogical atmosphere (p = 0.009), the leadership style of the ward manager (p < 0.001), the supervisory relationship (p = 0.034), and the role of the clinical nurse teacher (p = 0.031).

Multivariate analysis of factors associated with the CLES + T total score

Independent variables were selected based on both theoretical relevance and bivariate associations with the total CLES + T score. As stated in Table 4, the multiple linear regression model was statistically significant (F (3.482) = 93.157, p < 0.001) indicating that the set of predictors significantly explained variations in the total CLES + T score. The model explained 36.7% of the variance in the total CLES + T score, with an adjusted R² of 0.363. Gender does not significantly predict the total CLES + T score (β = -0.004; 95% CI [-0.067; 0.058]; p = 0.895). Academic year is also not significantly associated with the total CLES + T score (β = 0.044; 95% CI [-0.009; 0.096]; p = 0.081); although the association appears positive, it does not reach the threshold for statistical significance. Clinical site is the only factor significantly associated with the CLES + T total score (β = 0.376; 95% CI [0.327; 0.425]; p = 0.007). This indicates that clinical site is an important predictor of the CLES + T total score after adjusting for gender and academic year.

Discussion

Nursing education in Morocco has undergone numerous reforms, particularly following the COVID-19 pandemic crisis, which shook the healthcare system and exposed its fragility. Regular evaluation of the actions taken is a key component of quality assurance procedures. This study aims to evaluate the clinical learning environment, which is a vital component of clinical training for nurses.

This study revealed an alarming level of dissatisfaction among students with the clinical learning environment, as demonstrated by an average CLES + T total score of less than 3 out of 5. None of the five subscales received a score greater than or equal to 3. This reflects an isolated and exceptional situation compared to the results documented in similar Arab countries [30, 31] and similar African countries [32], which reported moderate to high students’ satisfaction levels.

This study identified that the pedagogical atmosphere dimension received a low score, which reflects a result that is exceptionally different from those described in the literature [33]. The interviews confirmed this dissatisfaction and revealed the relational constraints and lack of support encountered by students in the ward. These findings are consistent with similar challenges reported in previous studies [34, 35]. The lack of cooperation and indifference of the healthcare professionals toward students’ learning activities negatively undermines student motivation and their commitment to the training activities [36].

This study showed that Satisfaction with pedagogical atmosphere increases as students progress through their academic years. This can be explained by familiarity with the environment; students who are further along in their training become more familiar with clinical settings as they gain experience. An Egyptian study [37] reported that higher satisfaction among third-year students than among those in the early years of the training program is mainly associated with the maturity and autonomy acquired through years of study and internship experience. Students who had accumulated multiple internship experiences developed a confident professional attitude and interpersonal and communication skills, along with stress management strategies, which enabled them to immerse themselves smoothly and engage easily in learning activities within complex clinical contexts.

The data showed that students prefer hospital-based internships to those at primary health care centers. This can be explained by the differences in organizational conditions and human resources between clinical settings [37, 38]. In Morocco, the number of clinical staff in the primary care centers is limited, which can reduce the time devoted to student learning activities, unlike hospitals, which have vast and diverse human resources. Furthermore, hospitals offer a wide variety of clinical cases for learning, unlike primary care centers, which provide routine and limited services, thereby limiting the opportunity to learn nursing practices, particularly in emergency care and resuscitation [39].

Concerning the leadership style of the ward manager, quantitative and qualitative results converge toward dissatisfaction among most students in this regard. A previous study reported the lack of involvement of head nurses in training activities [40]. The workload and administrative responsibilities of head nurses (management, supplies, scheduling) prevent them from fully engaging in trainee training activities [30].

This study showed that students did not appreciate the premises of care in the ward. The low score assigned to this factor does not reflect an isolated situation but rather aligns with the findings of previous studies in which this dimension was generally rated as low to moderate [3, 41]. Qualitative reports explain the main reasons of the students’ dissatisfaction, the lack of clarity regarding the philosophy of care and poor practices’ organization were the main concerns raised by the interviewees. The holistic care models and standards taught to students in academic institutions clash with the reality of poorly organized routine practices in the ward [42]. The gap between theory and practice hinders the learning process; when care practices are unstructured, learning will be as well [43]. This situation stems from organizational rather than pedagogical issues, requiring close collaboration between academic and clinical staff to guide students toward the proper application of theory in practice and the development of reflective practices [44].

The supervisory relationship is a key component of nursing education [10]. This study revealed significant dissatisfaction among students in this regard. Several concerns which explain the students’ dissatisfaction emerged during the interviews such as the ineffective supervision models, the lack of respect, and mistrust. In Morocco, the student-to-supervisor ratio generally exceeds 1:8 which makes individualized supervision impossible. Students prefer one-on-one supervision, which allows for more private time to discuss their individual weaknesses and the prospects for their progress [33]. Furthermore, the supervisor’s profile and attitude profoundly influence the educational relationship [10]. Negative attitudes on the part of supervisors are a source of stress and frustration, particularly for students at the beginning of their studies who need a great deal of support and guidance [45].

The clinical nurse teacher affiliated with the institute plays a vital role in the clinical setting (coordinating between the institute and clinical environments, supporting students, and bridging the theory-practice gap) [10]. This study showed that even though this dimension had the highest score among the five subscales, it remains lower than what is documented in the literature [46, 47], where this factor was generally the highest rated. The interviews revealed several causes of student dissatisfaction, including low engagement and a lack of skills. These problems are similar to those reported in international studies [6, 48, 49]. When clinical teachers lack pedagogical skills and practical expertise, students perceive that these limitations hinder knowledge transfer and make their learning ineffective [50]. Given the pivotal role of nurse teachers in the clinical training process, a core of competencies (pedagogical, clinical, research, assessment, ethical, and relational competencies) has been defined in global standards to ensure effective intervention of the nurse teacher [51, 52]. Despite the recruitment of several cohorts of clinical nurse teachers in Moroccan institutes, their role remains insufficient in clinical settings. This can be explained by the high number of students, which increases the workload for these clinical teachers and reduces the quality of supervision.

Limitations

We acknowledge that some limitations may affect the robustness of this study. Firstly, First, the cross-sectional design of the quantitative phase does not allow for the establishment of causal relationships between the variables studied, but only for the identification of associations. Regarding the participant recruitment strategy, potential selection bias cannot be ruled out, as the recruited participants may not be fully representative of the entire population of students concerned. Non-response bias is also possible, as students who refused to participate may have different perceptions from those of the participants included in the study. The assessment of satisfaction may also be influenced by students’ recent internship experiences, also since the data are based in part on students’ subjective reports, recall bias may have influenced some of the responses reported The fact that the interviewer is a teacher at the institute may have introduced a social desirability bias, as students may be inclined to tone down their comments or avoid expressing criticism. Finally, The generalizability of the results remains limited to the institutional and clinical contexts studied, which calls for a cautious interpretation of the results in other training environments.

Conclusions

Nursing students’ satisfaction with the clinical learning environment is lower than expected. This reflects major dysfunctions and challenges that hinder clinical training. The reforms initiated by the Moroccan government to improve training remain insufficient. It is recommended that the role of clinical teachers be strengthened through continuing training. The partnership between academic institutions and clinical settings should be reinforced.

The current study highlights the immediate need for regulatory and normative frameworks to structure clinical education. Clear guidelines defining standards, roles, and responsibilities of the various stakeholders must be put in place.
