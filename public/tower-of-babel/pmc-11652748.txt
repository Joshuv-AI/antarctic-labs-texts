Postherpetic neuralgia mimicking lumbar radiculopathy☆
Authors: Mara Martinez-Santori, Diana Ekechukwu, Eduardo Bauer, Vishal Bansal, Kemly Philip
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

Low back pain (LBP) is one of the most common causes of pain and disability, with a lifetime prevalence as high as 84 % [1]. When faced with a patient presenting with acute LBP, our initial thoughts often gravitate towards common causes such as muscle strain, herniated discs, or degenerative diseases like spinal stenosis [2]. If these are ruled out, we might consider infections or tumors. However, we would like to spotlight a less common but significant cause in the elderly population: the reactivation of latent varicella-zoster virus.

In our case, a 79-year-old male with a past medical history of hypertension and hypothyroidism presented to our outpatient non-operative interventional spine clinic as a referral from an orthopedic surgery (ORS) spine clinic for lumbar epidural steroid injection (ESI) evaluation.

The patient reported a two-week history of acute LBP radiating to the left hip, lateral thigh crossing anteriorly towards the knee. He denied inciting events, or prior episodes with the same level of acuity and weakness. He felt his pain was limiting his ability to ambulate, and his numbness interfered with his sleep. His pain was aggravated with sitting or lying down for a prolonged period and was only partially alleviated with over-the-counter oral analgesic medications. There was no associated bladder or bowel incontinence, night sweats, unintentional weight loss, balance or coordination deficits.

The patient was evaluated in an urgent care center the day his symptoms began, after which he was prescribed a six-day methylprednisolone dose pack with taper, gabapentin 300mg three times daily and daily meloxicam 15mg, all of which provided minimal improvement.

Nine days after the onset of LBP, the patient was evaluated at the ORS spine clinic, and their physical exam revealed mild greater trochanteric tenderness to palpation, full lumbar range of motion, negative dural tension testing, positive pain provocative maneuvers, and negative skin findings. The patient completed an MRI of the lumbar spine that showed multilevel degenerative disc disease, resulting in moderate spinal canal stenosis at L2-L3 and L4-L5 and bilateral foraminal stenosis at L3-L4 and L4-L5 (Fig. 1). Ten days after the onset of LBP, the patient started over-the-counter acetaminophen, and around the same time, he developed erythematous papules in the L2-L3 dermatome distribution.

The patient visited our clinic 14 days after LBP onset. During a thorough physical examination, the rash was observed as dried-out blisters (Fig. 2), aligning with the distribution of his radiating lower back pain symptoms. Further investigation revealed a history of cervical dermatome herpes zoster infection 15 years prior.Fig. 2Overlapping erythematous papules in different stages of healing across L2-L3 distribution noted in the patient's left low back (A) and left anterior thigh (B).Fig. 2

Due to the medical history and recent findings of skin lesions accompanied by the description of his pain, the patient was diagnosed with acute left herpes zoster infection in the L2-L3 distribution. Also, given the active skin infection, no ESIs at the L2-L3 level or left trochanteric bursa were offered to avoid exacerbating symptoms. The patient was instructed to promptly follow up with his primary care physician to begin antiviral medication and to continue using gabapentin for symptom management. It was also recommended that he follow up in the interventional spine clinic if LBP symptoms persisted after viral treatment. However, the patient has not required follow-up after a year of diagnosis at either our clinic or ORS spine clinic.

Herpes zoster is the reactivation of the latent varicella-zoster (VZV) virus in the dorsal root ganglia of the spinal cord. The general population has an annual incidence rate of about 3.6–5.2 cases in 1000 individuals [3]. Additionally, incidences are higher in adults over 55, with 9.5–11 cases in 1000 patients 70–80 years old, respectively [3,4]. Typically, shingles present with a rash often found in the thoracic region with accompanying neuropathic pain that may persist even after the rash has cleared, also known as postherpetic neuralgia. To our knowledge, this is the fifth documented case of herpes zoster infection with neuropathic pain localized to the lumbosacral region.

There have been two reported cases of patients who present with neuropathic pain in a dermatomal distribution with the emergence of skin lesions after failing conservative management and undergoing ESI [5,6]. These two cases are included within the four documented cases of pain localized to the lumbosacral dermatomes, mimicking symptoms of lumbar radicular pain [[7], [8], [9]].

Given the clinical distribution of pain, the initial differential diagnosis in our case was left L2-L3 radiculopathy. Other factors included MRI findings of lumbar spinal canal stenosis, multilevel degenerative disc disease, and facet arthropathy. However, this case highlights the critical importance of thorough patient interviews and physical examinations to identify less common etiologies of LBP [[1], [10]]. The shift in medical practice during the post-pandemic era has significantly reduced physical examinations, raising concerns about the loss of essential diagnostic and therapeutic interactions between patients and healthcare providers [[11], [12]]. In this instance, the detailed physical evaluation and comprehensive patient interview at our clinic were pivotal in identifying the dried-out blisters and correlating them with the patient's radiating lower back pain symptoms and previous history, ultimately leading to the accurate diagnosis of herpes zoster neuralgia.
