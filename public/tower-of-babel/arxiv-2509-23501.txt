The Impact of Role Design in In-Context Learning for Large Language Models
Hamidreza Rouzegar, Masoud Makrehchi

Abstract
In-context learning (ICL) enables Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate predictions based on prompts without additional fine-tuning. While prompt engineering has been widely studied, the impact of role design within prompts remains underexplored. This study examines the influence of role configurations in zero-shot and few-shot learning scenarios using GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o from OpenAI and Llama2-7b and Llama2-13b from Meta. We evaluate the models’ performance across datasets, focusing on tasks like sentiment analysis, text classification, question answering, and math reasoning. Our findings suggest the potential of role-based prompt structuring to enhance LLM performance.

Introduction

In recent years, the field of Large Language Models (LLMs) has seen remarkable advancements. Models such as GPT-3
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
and Llama
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
have showcased impressive capabilities in various natural language tasks, including question answering
(
Rouzegar and Makrehchi 2024a
)
, sentiment analysis
(
Zhang et al. 2023b
)
, and text classification
(
Abburi et al. 2023
)
.

A new paradigm known as In-Context Learning (ICL) has emerged in natural language processing (NLP)
(
Dong et al. 2022
)
. In ICL, LLMs generate predictions based on provided prompts, which usually include a few training examples. This capability is particularly valuable as it allows models to adapt dynamically to diverse tasks without the computational cost of retraining.

While previous research has extensively studied prompt optimization techniques, much of the focus has been on content selection and structure rather than the explicit assignment of conversational roles.
(
Juneja et al. 2024
)
The underlying mechanisms through which role-based prompting affects model behavior remain underexplored. Role design involves structuring prompts with distinct roles such as system, user, and assistant. Understanding how these roles influence model performance can provide valuable insights for optimizing prompt engineering strategies.

In this paper, we examine the impact of role design in zero-shot and few-shot experiments using two prominent instruction-tuned LLMs
(
Zhang et al. 2023a
)
, namely GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o from OpenAI, as well as Llama2-7b and Llama2-13b from Meta. By selecting a diverse set of LLMs, our study provides a broad perspective on the effectiveness of role-based prompting. We systematically evaluate the models’ performance across a range of natural language tasks, including sentiment analysis, text classification, question answering, and mathematical reasoning. Furthermore, our introduction of Structural Accuracy as a secondary metric offers a new lens to assess model compliance with intended prompt structures, contributing a novel evaluation dimension to prompt engineering research.

This study examines the impact of role-based prompt design across various datasets and LLMs, including tasks that involve complex reasoning. Our findings offer insights into optimal prompt design strategies, potentially improving LLM performance across various natural language processing and reasoning tasks. We observed that incorporating pre-defined roles without altering the main prompts often led to better overall performance. For more complex reasoning tasks, we implemented additional steps in our prompt design to address the increased complexity. The source code, implementation details, and all datasets used in our experiments are available in a GitHub repository to ensure the reproducibility of our results.

Related Works

Significant research has been conducted in the field of prompt engineering and in-context learning. ICL refers to the ability of LLMs to generate predictions based on their input without additional fine-tuning.
(
Dong et al. 2022
)
While some research has focused on training and fine-tuning to improve ICL
(
Gu et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Shi et al. 2023
)
, it generally operates at the inference level of LLMs, relying on prompt engineering rather than model modification.
(
Bhattamishra et al. 2023
)

ICL typically involves providing few-shot examples, and several aspects can be considered in this process: selection, formatting, and instruction. Selection methods focus on choosing optimal examples from the training data for few-shot learning.
(
Dong et al. 2022
)
These can include techniques based on k-nearest neighbors (KNN)
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
, using mutual information
(
Sorensen et al. 2022
)
, perplexity estimation
(
Gonen et al. 2022
)
, or reinforcement learning (RL)
(
Zhang et al. 2022a
)
, for example selection.

Instruction engineering is another crucial aspect of ICL. Instruction engineering in ICL has seen various approaches: Instruction Induction generates instructions from input-output pairs
(
Honovich et al. 2022
)
, APE optimizes instructions by treating them as programs
(
Zhou et al. 2022b
)
, and SELF-INSTRUCT improves instruction-following by using self-generated examples
(
Wang et al. 2022b
)
. These methods aim to enhance LLMs’ ability to understand and follow complex directions.

Formatting plays a significant role in both ICL and broader prompt engineering techniques. Methods like Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
provide reasoning demonstrations, allowing models to develop reasoning abilities naturally when given sufficiently complex tasks. Variants such as complex CoT
(
Fu et al. 2022
)
and Auto-CoT
(
Zhang et al. 2022b
)
further refine these demonstrations under different conditions.

The Self-Ask method
(
Press et al. 2022
)
improves performance by decomposing complex questions into simpler sub-questions, which the model answers sequentially. The Memory-of-Thought (MoT)
(
Li and Qiu 2023
)
approach involves pre-thinking on an unlabeled dataset and storing high-confidence thoughts as external memory, which the model can recall during testing to aid reasoning.

Super In-Context Learning (SuperICL)
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
combines large language models with locally fine-tuned smaller models to improve performance on supervised tasks. These smaller models act as plug-ins, providing specific task knowledge, while the larger models focus on general language understanding.

Iterative Context-Aware Prompter (iCAP)
(
Wang et al. 2022a
)
explores an iterative prompting framework that progressively elicits relevant knowledge from LLMs for multi-step inference. Similarly, the LEAST-TO-MOST Prompting method breaks down complex problems into a series of simpler subproblems and solves them in sequence
(
Zhou et al. 2022a
)
. The Task-Agnostic Prefix Prompt (TAPP)
(
Ye et al. 2024
)
is a fixed prompt anchored to the beginning of every input, regardless of the target task, which facilitates zero-shot generalization.

Lastly, while recent work has explored role-play prompting to enhance LLMs’ reasoning abilities
(
Kong et al. 2023
)
, these studies have primarily focused on applying pre-defined personas in zero-shot settings without systematically comparing their impact across different learning paradigms.

Previous research has largely evaluated zero-shot and few-shot prompting independently, often assuming that few-shot learning consistently outperforms zero-shot. However, studies have shown that few-shot prompting does not always yield better results, depending on the task and example selection. In contrast to prior work, our study not only examines the effect of role-based prompt structuring but also introduces multiple few-shot configurations beyond the standard approach of providing three examples. By comparing these variations systematically, we demonstrate that role design plays a critical role in optimizing few-shot learning performance, providing new insights into effective prompt engineering strategies.

4
Experimental Setup

4.1
Data Preparation and Model Configuration

For each dataset, we selected a balanced subset to ensure a comprehensive evaluation. For classification tasks (such as genre classification and sentiment analysis), we ensured a balanced distribution of categories and labels. For the question-answering tasks and math word problems, we maintained an equal distribution of answers across the multiple-choice options. For the few-shot scenario, we randomly selected three samples from each dataset, ensuring that these examples were not included in the test set.

All GPT and Llama models were configured with a temperature of 0.0. This setting minimizes the randomness in the model’s output, ensuring more deterministic responses. Other parameters, such as maximum sequence length and batch size, were optimized for each task to ensure optimal performance.

4.2
Prompt Construction

As demonstrated in Figure 1, we maintained a consistent format for prompt design in all five data sets, with minor configurations for each specific task. The core structure of each prompt consisted of two main parts:

1.

High-level instruction: This part provided the general task description and output format. For example, for the movie genre classification task, it read: "Determine the genre of the movie based on the provided plot. For the plot provided, classify its genre as a single word (without other marks or words like ’genre:’), either "comedy", "action", "drama", or "horror"." This instruction was modified for each dataset with simple word changes, such as "Determine the answer of the example based on the provided question" for question-answering tasks, with the output format adjusted accordingly (e.g., "a single small letter (without other marks or words like ’answer:’), either "a", "b", "c", "d", or "e".").

2.

Task-specific input: This part contained the actual content to be analyzed, such as the movie plot, review text, or question, always provided in the user prompt or at the end of the user prompt in few-shot experiments.

4.3
Adaptive Prompt Design

In addition to our standard prompt designs, we implemented three more steps of prompt engineering for the MATH dataset, which represents a more complex reasoning task compared to the other question-answering datasets. The progression of these steps was as follows:

1.

Basic Math Prompt: This step used a prompt similar to our other datasets, with minor adjustments for math questions. The system instruction was: "Determine the answer of the example based on the provided question. For the question provided, classify its answer as a single small letter (without other marks or words like ’answer:’), either "a", "b", "c", "d", or "e"."

2.

Specialized Math Prompt: We refined the system instructions to more explicitly match the math problem-solving process. The new instruction was: "Solve the following math question and determine the correct answer. Provide the answer as a single small letter (without any additional marks or words like ’answer:’), choosing from: "a", "b", "c", "d", or "e"." This change aimed to better align the prompt with the mathematical nature of the task.

3.

Explanation Request: In this step, we added a request for the model to explain its rationale after providing the answer. The instruction was extended to: "After providing the letter answer, explain your rationale and how you arrived at the solution." In the few-shot examples for this configuration, we used the format of an answer followed by an explanation.

4.

Reasoning-First Approach: In the final configuration, we modified the order of operations, asking the model to explain its reasoning process before providing the final answer. The instruction was changed to: "Solve the following math question and determine the correct answer. Explain your rationale and how you came to the solution. After explaining how you reached the answer, provide the answer as a single small letter (without any additional marks), choosing from: "a", "b", "c", "d", or "e"."

By implementing these progressive refinements in prompt design for math reasoning tasks, we created a spectrum of instruction complexities. This ranged from basic answer selection to detailed reasoning processes. This approach enabled us to assess how different cognitive demands embedded within the prompts influence the model performance in complex problem-solving scenarios. In addition, it provided information on how models adapt to increasingly sophisticated instruction sets while maintaining consistent role-based structures.

4.4
Evaluation Metrics

The primary evaluation metric was the F1 score, which checks if the output of the prompt matches the class for sentiment analysis and movie genres or the correct answer on QA tasks. The second metric is structural accuracy, which measures how closely the output matches the requested structure in the prompt. For example, the movie genre should be a single word among the specified genres, the sentiment should be a single word, either "positive or "negative," and the QA answers should be a single capital letter among the choices (A, B, C, or D).

We examined structural accuracy regardless of the correctness of the answer. For instance, if the output was a single letter (A to D) for ai2_arc, it was considered structured. However, outputs like "the answer to this question is D: state park" or simply "D: state park" were considered unstructured because they did not match the desired format. Additionally, for measuring the F1 score, we used post-processing to extract the label from the output and determine if it matched the true label of the dataset.

Results

5.1
General NLP Task Performance

For the general NLP tasks (Table
3
), in most cases, the few-shot system, user, and assistant prompt (FewSUA) configuration exhibited a superior F1 score across the experiments, indicating that incorporating clear role distinctions and examples significantly enhances model performance.

For Llama models, the use of few-shot user (FewU) and few-shot system and user (FewSU) commands led to a noticeable decrease in F1 scores. This suggests that embedding few-shot examples within user prompts may cause these models to produce more incorrect answers and hallucinations.

The structural accuracy of GPT models was generally high, with both models adhering to the desired output structure in most cases. However, the Llama models frequently generated more than one word, failing to follow the instructions, which is understandable given their smaller size. A standout finding is that the FewSUA prompt configuration helped the Llama models generate outputs in the desired structure, making it potentially useful for applications like chatbots where maintaining a specific structure is crucial. In addition, Llama2-13b outperformed Llama2-7b in structural adherence.

Furthermore, in tasks such as movie genre classification and sentiment analysis, the Llama2-13b model showed results comparable to GPT models, demonstrating its capability in certain NLP tasks despite its smaller size. However, in question-answering tasks, the performance of the GPT models was significantly better and not comparable to that of the Llama models.

The results from these general NLP tasks highlighted the effectiveness of role-based prompting, particularly the FewSUA configuration. However, they also revealed some limitations, especially for smaller models like Llama. These findings prompted us to explore more refined prompt engineering techniques for more complex reasoning tasks, specifically in the domain of mathematical problem-solving. The following subsection details our approach and findings in this more challenging domain.

5.2
Mathematical Reasoning Task Performance

The results of our experiments on the MATH dataset, as presented in Table
4
, reveal intriguing and sometimes unexpected patterns in the performance of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o across different prompt designs.

In the Basic Math prompt, we observed an unusual trend with the superiority of the ZeroSU configuration, particularly for GPT-3.5 (F1 score of 0.38). The overall performance of other approaches was not as strong as anticipated. This led us to refine our prompt by making minor adjustments to better suit mathematical questions, resulting in the Specialized Math prompt.

The Specialized Math prompt showed a similar trend, with ZeroSU maintaining its edge (F1 score of 0.53 for GPT-3.5). Interestingly, we noticed that configurations with lower structural accuracy often yielded better performance in providing correct answers. Upon analyzing the model responses, we found that better-performing answers often included explanations and reasoning rather than just a single-letter response.

This observation prompted us to test a new hypothesis by incorporating reasoning and rationale into our prompts, leading to the Explanation Request design. In this configuration, we altered the few-shot examples to include explanations for each answer. The results showed that FewSU achieved better F1 scores, again with low structural accuracy. We observed that in some cases, the model made mistakes in attempting to maintain the structure, such as providing an initial answer, followed by an explanation, and then a different final answer (e.g., "a, [explanation], the answer is b").

To address these issues, we further evolved the prompt in the Reasoning-First approach, asking the model to explain its reasoning before providing the final answer. This design aimed to avoid the errors observed in the previous configuration. As a result, we did not evaluate structural accuracy for this prompt (marked as N/A in the table).

For GPT-4o, we observed similar trends, although the results across different configurations were more consistent, which aligns with its status as a more advanced LLM.

Regarding the Llama models, we generally observed poor performance across the board, which is understandable given the complex nature of the mathematical reasoning tasks. However, an interesting exception was observed for Llama2-13b in the FewSUA configuration, which achieved a 31% F1 score, significantly outperforming other configurations for this model.

These findings highlight the complex interplay between prompt design, model capabilities, and task complexity in mathematical reasoning. They also underscore the importance of allowing models to provide explanations and reasoning, which often leads to more accurate responses, even if at the cost of strict adherence to a prescribed output structure.

In general, our experiments reveal that the effectiveness of prompt design varies significantly between task types and model architectures. Although FewSUA configuration excelled in standard NLP tasks, mathematical reasoning required more nuanced approaches, often benefiting from explanatory prompts. Larger models consistently outperformed smaller ones, although Llama2-13b showed promise in certain NLP tasks. In particular, strict structural adherence did not always correlate with better performance, especially in complex reasoning tasks. These findings highlight the need for task-specific and model-specific prompt engineering strategies.

6
Limitations and Future Works

Our study has limitations that present opportunities for future research. We focused on a limited range of tasks and datasets. Future work should expand to include a broader array of NLP tasks, such as summarization and translation, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the effectiveness of prompt design across different language processing challenges.

While we utilized pre-defined roles for instruction-tuned LLMs, future studies could explore creating new, task-specific roles to further optimize prompt designs. Additionally, investigating how these role-based strategies translate to non-instruction-tuned language models such as GPT-3 and Llama Base models could yield valuable insights into the broader applicability of this approach.

Due to resource limitations, we were unable to experiment with larger Llama models, which may show comparable results to the experimented GPT models in certain tasks. Future research with access to more computational resources could explore the performance of these larger models in role-based prompt designs, potentially revealing new insights into the scalability of this approach across different model sizes and architectures.

Lastly, future research could delve deeper into the relationship between structural accuracy and task performance, potentially leading to more optimized prompt engineering strategies. Exploring adaptive prompt designs that dynamically adjust based on the specific task and model could also prove fruitful.

Conclusion

This study examines the impact of role-based prompt design and task-specific prompt engineering on Large Language Models (LLMs) across various NLP tasks and mathematical reasoning problems. Our experiments with GPT-3.5, GPT-4o, Llama2-7b, and Llama2-13b revealed that the few-shot system, user, and assistant (FewSUA) prompt configuration generally performed best in NLP tasks. However, in mathematical reasoning, we found that configurations allowing for explanations often outperformed those strictly adhering to structural accuracy.

Significant performance differences were observed between model sizes and architectures, with larger models generally performing better. Interestingly, in some simpler NLP tasks, Llama2-13b achieved results comparable to GPT models.

Our findings suggest that optimal prompt design varies not only between task types but also between models of different sizes and architectures. The success of the Reasoning-First approach in mathematical tasks indicates that encouraging models to articulate their thought processes can lead to more accurate outputs. These insights contribute to the growing understanding of effective prompt engineering for LLMs, offering valuable guidance for researchers and practitioners in the field of natural language processing.
