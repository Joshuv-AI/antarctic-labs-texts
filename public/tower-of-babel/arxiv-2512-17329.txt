How back reaction and hydrogen transport control the performance of hydrogen release from liquid organic carriers
T. Nizkaia, T. Solymosi, P. Malgaretti, P. Wasserscheid, J. Harting

Abstract
Hydrogen, as a clean energy carrier, is a promising option for sustainable energy storage and utilization, yet its storage and transportation remain challenging. Liquid Organic Hydrogen Carriers (LOHCs) provide a potential solution by enabling the reversible chemical binding and release of hydrogen. However, recent experimental studies have revealed a puzzling inhibition of catalytic activity during LOHC dehydrogenation, associated with the absence of hydrogen bubble formation, reduced hydrogen production rates and significant variability across experiments. In this work, we derive a model to elucidate the mechanisms underlying this inhibition, taking into account both the reversible nature of the hydrogenation–dehydrogenation reaction and the role of transport phenomena. Our results demonstrate that efficient transport of hydrogen away from the catalytic pellet is essential to suppress back-reactions and thereby maximize the performance of porous catalysts. In particular, we demonstrate that two distinct kinetic regimes — with high or strongly inhibited hydrogen production — can arise depending on whether bubble nucleation is enabled or suppressed. Beyond LOHC systems, our findings are applicable to a broader class of reversible reactions, particularly those involving volatile products that can leave the liquid reaction medium in form of bubbles.



I
Introduction

Hydrogen has emerged as a promising candidate for clean energy storage and transport, playing a key role in the transition towards a sustainable energy future
Staffell et al. 2019
;
Singla et al. 2021
;
Ivanova et al. 2023
. However, the practical challenges in hydrogen storage and transportation remain significant: hydrogen being a highly flammable gas, its storage in large quantities requires expert knowledge and dedicated infrastructures. A potential solution lies in the use of Liquid Organic Hydrogen Carriers (LOHCs): compounds that can chemically bind and release hydrogen in a reversible way
Taube et al. 1983
;
Newson et al. 1998
;
Teichmann et al. 2011
;
Heublein et al. 2020
;
Rao and Yoon 2020
;
Sekine and Higo 2021
;
Jo et al. 2022
. By this method, hydrogen is chemically bonded to the LOHC molecules during the hydrogenation reaction, allowing for safe and efficient storage at ambient conditions. When needed, hydrogen can be released from the LOHC molecules using the dehydrogenation reaction and utilized for various applications.

Both hydrogenation and dehydrogenation of LOHC compounds require the use of a catalyst, usually in the form of metallic nano-particles embedded in a porous support (catalytic pellet)
Salman et al. 2022
;
Kwak et al. 2021
.

The overall performance of a catalytic pellet is determined by the interplay of the intrinsic kinetics at the catalyst active sites and the transport of reactants and products. Transport phenomena are particularly important for easily reversible reactions, for which accumulation of products shifts the chemical equilibrium locally and can slow down the reaction or stop it completely
Kao and Satterfield 1968
;
Rios et al. 2023
. This is indeed the case for hydrogenation/dehydrogenation of LOHC compounds: at the same temperature, both hydrogenation and dehydrogenation may occur depending on its current degree of hydrogenation of the LOHC mixture and on the partial pressure of hydrogen in contact with the liquid
Dürr et al. 2021
;
Jorschick et al. 2017
. Therefore, we can expect the local concentration of dissolved hydrogen to have a significant impact on the efficiency of hydrogen production.

In recent years, significant advances have been made in elucidating the mechanisms of catalytic hydrogenation/dehydrogenation
Taube et al. 1983
;
Newson et al. 1998
;
Teichmann et al. 2011
;
Heublein et al. 2020
;
Rao and Yoon 2020
;
Sekine and Higo 2021
;
Jo et al. 2022
;
Rakić et al. 2023
and in modeling these processes on the reactor scale
Van Hoecke et al. 2024
. However, while the importance of back reaction and its dependence on the operational pressure has been recognized
Geißelbrecht et al. 2024
;
Fan et al. 2025
;
Kadar et al. 2024
;
Gambini et al. 2024
, the influence of hydrogen transport has not been considered so far: it is generally assumed that the concentration of dissolved hydrogen in the LOHC mixture is in equilibrium with the gas phase.

Here, we propose a simple reaction-diffusion model that allows us to calculate hydrogen production in a porous pellet for reversible hydrogenation/dehydrogenation reactions with a non-linear dependency of the hydrogenation rate on the local hydrogen concentration. We explore the dependency of hydrogen production on transport conditions inside and outside the pellet and show that the efficient evacuation of produced hydrogen is crucial for the pellet performance.

In particular, we show that hydrogen transport plays a crucial role in deactivation of catalytic pellets, observed for some LOHC systems
Solymosi et al. 2022
. It has been observed that at the same thermodynamic conditions catalytic pellets used for LOHC hydrogenation can be either in active state (intense bubble formation and high productivity) or in the inhibited state (few or no bubbles, low productivity), and the transition between the states is associated with bubble nucleation in the pores of the pellet
Solymosi et al. 2022
. Moreover, the overall performance of pellets depends on the external transport of reactants and products: while in batch experiments the inhibited pellets produce 50 times less hydrogen than active ones
Solymosi et al. 2022
, in a flow-through reactor inhibited pellets perform surprisingly well, with productivity reduction of just
10-20\%

Uhrig et al. 2024
.
This highlights the sensitivity of the pellets to the
transport conditions both inside and outside the catalytic pellets.

Our results show that the different reduction in performance for nucleation-inhibited pellets observed in flow-through
Uhrig et al. 2024
or batch
Solymosi et al. 2022
experiments on H18-DBT (perhydro-dibenzyltoluene) dehydrogenation is due to two typically overlooked phenomena: the back reaction and the transport of hydrogen away from the pellet.
Accordingly, parameters that have been typically disregarded, such as the distance between the pellets and the liquid-gas interface in batch experiments, are indeed crucial in determining the performance of the pellets.
Finally, we use our model to discuss the dependence of the performance of the pellets on their inner structure.

II
Reaction-diffusion model in the pellet

To estimate the hydrogen production of a catalytic pellet in the absence of bubbling, we need to model the reaction and transport of hydrogen and LOHC compounds inside the pellet. Dehydrogenation of most LOHC compounds proceeds in several steps, producing a number of long-living partially dehydrogenated intermediaries LOHC-X, where X denotes the number of hydrogen atoms bound to LOHC
Sekine and Higo 2021
.
The local composition of the mixture of LOHC compounds is defined by a system of reaction-diffusion equations as
Wakao and Smith 1964
;
Huang and Kuo 1965

\displaystyle\frac{\partial C_{X}}{\partial t}=\nabla\left[D^{\mathrm{eff}}_{X}\nabla C_{X}\right]+S_{X}\cdot\phi(\mathbf{x}),

(1)

where
C_{X}(\mathbf{x})
are molar concentrations of partially hydrogenated LOHC compounds,
D^{\mathrm{eff}}_{X}
are effective diffusion coefficients of LOHC compounds in a porous medium, the source terms
S_{X}
are the local rates of production/consumption of partially dehydrogenated LOHC compounds due to hydrogenation/dehydrogenation and
\phi(\mathbf{x})
is a non-dimensional function describing the spatial distribution of the available catalyst surface in the pellet.
The source of hydrogen is then defined as

S_{H_{2}}=-\dfrac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{X}XS_{X}.

(2)

Tracking the reaction intermediates LOHC-X is important for understanding the hydrogenation and dehydrogenation pathways
Do et al. 2016
;
Shi et al. 2023
. However, these intermediates are difficult to measure in situ, and the detailed composition of the mixture is typically not considered in the literature. Instead, the composition of the mixture of LOHC compounds is characterised by a degree of hydrogenation (DoH)
Aslam et al. 2018
;
Berger Bioucas et al. 2020
;
Dürr et al. 2021
;
Park et al. 2021

\mathrm{DoH}=\dfrac{C_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{bound}}}{n\bar{C}},

(3)

which is the ratio of the number of
H_{2}
molecules bound to LOHC molecules (per unit volume)

\displaystyle C_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{bound}}=\sum\limits_{X}\dfrac{X}{2}\cdot C_{X}

(4)

to the loading capacity of the LOHC system:

\displaystyle n\bar{C}=n\sum\limits_{X}C_{X},

(5)

where
\bar{C}
is the molar density of the LOHC mixture
and
n
is the maximum number of
H_{2}
molecules that can be chemically incorporated into an LOHC molecule.

At working temperatures (
500-600K
), the differences in molar densities and binary diffusion coefficients of typical LOHC compounds in hydrogenated and dehydrogenated forms do not exceed
10\%

Heller et al. 2016
;
Kerscher et al. 2020
. We can therefore assume that the molar density of the LOHC mixture does not depend on its composition (
\bar{C}=const
), and the
diffusion coefficients of all partially dehydrogenated LOHC compounds are equal and also do not depend on the mixture composition:

D_{X}=D_{+}=const\,

(6)

This allows us to write a reaction-diffusion model for an LOHC mixture with varying degree of hydrogenation without resolving for intermediaries. Indeed, we can multiply the reaction-diffusion equations Eqs. (
1
) by
X/2
(the number of
H_{2}
molecules, ’stored’ in one LOHC-X molecule), sum them up and obtain a reaction-diffusion equation for bound hydrogen

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial C_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{bound}}}{\partial t}=D_{+}^{\mathrm{eff}}\nabla^{2}C_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{bound}}-S_{H_{2}}\,,

(7)

which is complemented by a reaction-diffusion equation for the concentration
C_{H_{2}}(\mathbf{x})
of dissolved hydrogen:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial C_{H_{2}}}{\partial t}=D_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{eff}}\nabla^{2}C_{H_{2}}+S_{H_{2}}

(8)

The source term
S_{H_{2}}
describes the reversible chemical binding of
H_{2}
to the mixture of LOHC molecules. We represent the reaction as a combination of forward (dehydrogenation) and back (hydrogenation) reactions happening simultaneously at different rates (see Fig.
1
(b)):

\displaystyle S_{H_{2}}=\left[k_{\uparrow}C_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{bound}}-k_{\downarrow}(n\bar{C}-C_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{bound}})\right]

(9)

Here,
k_{\uparrow},k_{\downarrow}
are the effective binding/unbinding rates and (
n\bar{C}-C_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{bound}}
) is the number of free “binding slots” in the mixture of LOHC compounds.
Unlike dehydrogenation, the hydrogenation reaction involves dissolved hydrogen and therefore its rate depends on the
H_{2}
concentration near catalytic sites.

Using the data in Ref.
Dürr et al. 2021
, we approximate the empirical dependency of the hydrogenation rate on hydrogen concentration with a power law (see Appendix
A
) as

\displaystyle k_{\uparrow}=const,\hskip 28.45274ptk_{\downarrow}=k_{\uparrow}\cdot\gamma\cdot\left(\dfrac{C_{H_{2}}}{C_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{sat}}}\right)^{m},

(10)

where
m
is the effective reaction order with respect to hydrogen,
C_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{sat}}
is the saturation concentration of
H_{2}
in the LOHC mixture and
\gamma
is the equilibrium constant of hydrogenation (both taken at atmospheric pressure) given by

\displaystyle\gamma=\dfrac{\mathrm{DoH}^{\mathrm{eq}}(P_{a})}{1-\mathrm{DoH}^{\mathrm{eq}}(P_{a})},

(11)

where
\mathrm{DoH}^{\mathrm{eq}}(P_{a})
is the equilibrium degree of hydrogenation at fixed working temperature and atmospheric pressure
P_{a}
.
In principle, the rate constants
k_{\uparrow,\downarrow}
depend in a real system on the local degree of hydrogenation. However, in this simplified setup, we consider effective average rates independent of the mixture composition. We recall that since hydrogenation is in reality a multistep reaction, its effective reaction order can be different from unity.

We consider a spherical pellet of radius
R
and assume that all distributions are spherically symmetric, i.e. depend only on the spherical coordinate
\bar{r}
.
The hydrogen production rate of the pellet can be determined by integrating the flux of LOHC-bound hydrogen entering the pellet over its surface:

Q_{H_{2}}=4\pi R^{2}D_{+}^{\mathrm{eff}}\left.\frac{dC_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{bound}}}{d\bar{r}}\right|_{\bar{r}=R}.

(12)

Note that this definition of
Q_{H_{2}}
is valid for both the nucleation-inhibited (non-bubbling) state (when it is equal to the diffusive flow of produced
H_{2}
) and the active state (when hydrogen leaves the system in the form of bubbles and the diffusive flux of
H_{2}
is undefined).

Taking the saturation concentration as a reference value for the amount of dissolved hydrogen, we define the following non-dimensional variables:

\displaystyle r=\dfrac{\bar{r}}{R},\;c_{+}=\dfrac{C_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{bound}}}{n\bar{C}},\;c_{H_{2}}=\dfrac{C_{H_{2}}}{C_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{sat}}},\;s_{H_{2}}=\dfrac{S_{H_{2}}}{k_{\uparrow}n\bar{C}}

(13)

Substituting them into Eqns. (
7
),(
8
), gives

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}c_{+}-\mathrm{Da}_{+}s_{H_{2}}(c_{+},c_{H_{2}})\phi(r)=0,

(14)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}c_{H_{2}}+\dfrac{\bar{C}}{C_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{sat}}}\mathrm{Da}_{H_{2}}s_{H_{2}}(c_{+},c_{H_{2}})\phi(r)=0,

(15)

where

s_{H_{2}}(c_{+},c_{H_{2}})=c_{+}-\gamma\cdot\left(c_{H_{2}}\right)^{m}\cdot(1-c_{+})

(16)

is the non-dimensional reaction term obtained using Eq. (
10
) and

\mathrm{Da}_{+}=\dfrac{k_{\uparrow}R^{2}}{D_{+}^{\mathrm{eff}}},\;\mathrm{Da}_{H_{2}}=\dfrac{n\cdot k_{\uparrow}R^{2}}{D_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{eff}}}

(17)

are the Damköhler numbers for the LOHC compounds and hydrogen, respectively. They are defined as the ratio of reaction to diffusion rates. We recall that the factor
n
in Eq. (
17
) accounts for the fact that full dehydrogenation of one LOHC molecule produces
n
molecules of
H_{2}
, and the ratio
\dfrac{\bar{C}}{C_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{sat}}}
appears because we use different reference concentrations for the LOHC mixture and
H_{2}
in Eqs. (
13
).
Finally, Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
) are complemented with no-flux boundary conditions at the center of the pellet
and continuity conditions at the boundary of the pellet:

\displaystyle c_{+}(r=1)=c_{+}^{\mathrm{ex}},\quad c_{H_{2}}(r=1)=c_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{ex}}.

(18)

Here,
c_{+}^{\mathrm{ex}}
and
c_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{ex}}
are the local degree hydrogenation and the hydrogen oversaturation at the external surface of the pellet.

Then, the non-dimensional hydrogen flux in the inhibited state (”inh”) can be introduced as follows:

\displaystyle q_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{inh}}(c_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{ex}},c_{+}^{\mathrm{ex}})=\dfrac{Q_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{inh}}}{4\pi RD_{+}^{\mathrm{eff}}n\bar{C}}=\left.\dfrac{\partial c^{\mathrm{inh}}_{+}}{\partial r}\right|_{r=1}

(19)

Here,
c^{\mathrm{inh}}_{+}
is the solution of Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
) with boundary conditions given by Eq. (
18
).

In the active state (“act”), when hydrogen is taken away by bubbles very efficiently, we can assume
C_{H_{2}}=C_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{sat}}
(no oversaturation) throughout the pellet. The flux of hydrogen in the active state then reads:

\displaystyle q_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{act}}(c_{+}^{\mathrm{ex}})=\dfrac{Q_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{act}}}{4\pi RD_{+}^{\mathrm{eff}}n\bar{C}}=\left.\dfrac{\partial c^{\mathrm{act}}_{+}}{\partial r}\right|_{r=1},

(20)

where
c^{\mathrm{act}}_{+}
is the solution of Eq. (
14
) with
c_{H_{2}}\equiv 1
and the boundary condition given by Eq. (
18
)(left).
The degree of inhibition is defined by the ratio of hydrogen production in the active and passive states. As we shall see later, this ratio can vary tremendously depending on the conditions of hydrogen transport outside the pellet.

III
Results and discussion

Hydrogen production in nucleation-inhibited pellets depends on a complex interplay of reaction kinetics, transport properties, and external conditions. Accordingly, here we restrict ourselves to the case of a spherical catalytic pellet
with radius
R
, where the active catalytic sites are uniformly distributed in an outer shell of thickness
H

1

14

15

\phi(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&r<1-h\\

In order to compare against experimental data, it is useful to introduce here a new non-dimensional parameter

\epsilon=\dfrac{D_{+}C_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{sat}}}{D_{H_{2}}n\bar{C}},

(22)

which allows to rewrite Eq. (
15

\nabla^{2}c_{H_{2}}+\epsilon^{-1}\mathrm{Da}_{+}S_{H_{2}}(c_{+},c_{H_{2}})\phi(r)=0

Unless stated otherwise, we use the following set of non-dimensional parameters, calculated using for H18-DBT at T=573 K using the data from the literature

;

;
Dürr et al. 2021

(see Appendix

\gamma=0.087,\;m=1.6,\;\epsilon=0.0125,\;h=0.1.

(24)

The Dammköhler number

III.1
Hydrogen production depending on external conditions:
c_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{ex}}
and
c_{+}^{\mathrm{ex}}

From experimental data, we know that, unlike active pellets, nucleation-inhibited pellets show a strongly varying productivity under different external conditions, ranging from

Such a variation in the performance of inhibited pellets upon changing from batch to flow-through conditions is quite surprising, since these experiments have been performed at very similar thermodynamic conditions. Such a sensitivity to batch and flow-through conditions may be due to very different concentrations of hydrogen and LOHC compounds at the surface of the pellets.

III.2
Hydrogen production and oversaturation in batch experiments

So far, we have shown that the hydrogen production within the pellets is very sensitive to both
c_{+}^{\mathrm{ex}}
and
c_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{ex}}
. However, these parameters cannot be directly controlled in real experimental scenarios. For example, in the batch experiment used to study inhibition, the pellets were placed at the bottom of a test tube filled with hydrogen-rich LOHC compound H18-DBT

III.3
Inhibited pellet in a flow-through reactor

As we discussed so far, the dynamics inside the pellet are very sensitive to the supersaturation of hydrogen,
c^{\mathrm{ex}}_{H_{2}}
, on its surface. However, in batch experiments, this magnitude cannot be directly controlled, and it depends on both the dynamics inside and outside the pellets. This makes the analysis of the dependence of the performance of the pellets on their inner structure complicated since changing the properties of the pellets, such as their porosity (and hence
k
) or the thickness of the catalytic layer,
h
, will also affect the value of
c^{\mathrm{ex}}_{H_{2}}
. However, contrary to batch experiments, in flow-through reactors the first layers of pellets are exposed to “fresh” LOHC mixture, which does not have any excess dissolved hydrogen, i.e.,
c^{\mathrm{ex}}_{H_{2}}=1
(see Fig.
1
(d)).
In this case, the performance of the pellet is limited by hydrogen accumulation inside the pellet itself. Therefore, this is a very interesting setup to characterize the dependence of the hydrogen production on the inner properties of the pellets. Indeed, Fig.
7
shows that the density of
H_{2}
inside the pellet is sensitive to
\mathrm{Da}_{+}
, hence raising the issue of the dependence of the performance of the pellet on the inner distribution of hydrogen.

At first, we estimate the degree of inhibition in the flow-through scenario with
c_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{ex}}=1
. We plot in Fig.
8
(a) the ratio of hydrogen fluxes in the inhibited and active states (inhibition factor) as a function of
\mathrm{Da}_{+}
for different
c_{+}^{\mathrm{ex}}
. It can be seen that the inhibition is stronger for less hydrogenated LOHC mixtures. This is in agreement with experimental observations
Uhrig et al. 2024
and is due to the dependence of the hydrogenation rate on the DoH in Eq. (
16
): partially dehydrogenated LOHC mixture has more available “sites” for hydrogen to attach. We can also see that at small
\mathrm{Da}_{+}
the productivity in active and inhibited states is almost the same, while at large
\mathrm{Da}_{+}
the ratio of the fluxes decreases. However, the reduction is not as drastic as in the batch case (compare the red curve in Fig.
8
(a) and Fig.
5
(a)).

Indeed, we can see in Fig.
8
(b) that, unlike in the batch scenario, the flux in the inhibited state (solid curve) does not saturate at large
\mathrm{Da}_{+}
, but keeps growing at a sub-linear rate. The flux in the active state (dashed curves) behaves in a similar manner, but the transition to the sub-linear regime occurs at larger
\mathrm{Da}_{+}
, so that eventually the ratio of the fluxes becomes constant.

This behavior can be captured by a simple model that takes into account the hydrogen distribution in the pellet. This model can be derived and solved exactly for the case
m=1
(hydrogenation as a 1st order reaction with respect to hydrogen) and in the limit
\epsilon\ll 1
,
h\ll 1
(see Appendix
C
). In this case, an analytical solution for the hydrogen flux can be obtained as

q_{H_{2}}=-s_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{ex}}\lambda^{-1}\tanh(h/\lambda),

(30)

where

s_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{ex}}=c_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{ex}}-c_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(c_{+}^{\mathrm{ex}}),\;c_{H_{2}}^{\mathrm{eq}}=\dfrac{c_{+}^{\mathrm{ex}}}{\gamma(1-c_{+}^{\mathrm{ex}})}

(31)

and

\lambda=[\gamma(1-c_{+}^{\mathrm{ex}})\epsilon^{-1}\mathrm{Da}_{+}]^{-1/2}

(32)

is a non-dimensional length at which chemical equilibrium is attained. The transition from the linear to the sub-linear regime occurs at
\lambda\sim h
, and the transition point depends both on
c_{+}^{\mathrm{ex}}
and
h
. Increasing
c_{+}^{\mathrm{ex}}
shifts the transition to the sub-linear regime (and hence inhibition) to higher values of
\mathrm{Da}_{+}
, while increasing
h
shifts the transition to smaller values of
\mathrm{Da}_{+}
.

While this analytical solution is not directly applicable for
m\neq 1
, the simple model correctly reproduces the scaling of the inhibited flux in the Damköhler number
\mathrm{Da}_{+}
: it is linear at small
\mathrm{Da}_{+}
and proportional to
\sqrt{\mathrm{Da}_{+}}
at large
\mathrm{Da}_{+}
. It also predicts the direction in which the transition point shifts with the change of
c_{+}^{\mathrm{ex}}
. Indeed, we can see in Fig.
8
(b) that the inhibition begins at a smaller
\mathrm{Da}_{+}
for LOHC mixtures with a lower degree of hydrogenation
c_{+}^{\mathrm{ex}}
.

This model also has important implications for the choice of the optimal thickness of the catalytic layer. Eq. (
30
) suggests that at
h\gg\lambda
the flux becomes insensitive to
h
: increasing the thickness of the catalytic layer does not improve the productivity. In Fig.
8
(c), we plot the dependence of the hydrogen flux on the thickness of the catalytic layer
h
for different values of
\mathrm{Da}_{+}
. We can see that, while at small values of
h
the flux grows linearly, the curves indeed saturate after some critical thickness. This thickness decreases with the growth of
\mathrm{Da}_{+}
. Pellets with a thin catalytic layer show almost the same productivity in the inhibited state (solid curves in Fig.
8
(c)) and in the active one (dashed curves in Fig.
8
(c)).

We can see that the productivity of inhibited catalytic pellets in ideal flow-through conditions can be comparable to that in the active (bubbling) state. As shown in Fig.
5
(c), unlike the batch scenario in flow-through situations, the productivity of the pellet is sensitive to the catalyst distribution in the pellet and to the inner properties of the pellet encoded in the Damköhler number
\mathrm{Da}_{+}
. The inhibition of hydrogen production is stronger for pellets with a thick catalytic layer and in LOHC mixtures with a lower degree of hydrogenation
c_{+}^{\mathrm{ex}}
. For
\mathrm{Da}_{+}=10\ldots 50
, typical for dehydrogenation of H18-DBT, we expect the flux in the inhibited state to be
70-90\%
of the active one (see Fig.
8
(a)), in line with what was observed in flow-through experiments
Uhrig et al. 2024
.

IV
Conclusion

We derived a simple model for the reversible hydrogenation/dehydrogenation and the transport of LOHC compounds and hydrogen in nucleation-inhibited (non-bubbling) catalyst pellets.
Our analysis shows that hydrogen production in the inhibited state depends strongly on the transport of dissolved hydrogen outside the pellet as well as the back reactions. Thus, in a small batch experiment, when transport is dominated by diffusion, hydrogen production in the absence of bubbling can be 50-100 times smaller than in the active state, which is in good agreement with experimental observations
Solymosi et al. 2022
.
In contrast, in a flow-though scenario, when the hydrogen concentration near the pellet is small, the difference between the nucleation-inhibited (non-bubbling) and the active (bubbling) state is much smaller and decreases with the degree of hydrogenation of incoming LOHC mixture, which is also consistent with experimental observations
Uhrig et al. 2024
.

All in all, our model shows that the inhibition of dehydrogenation in the presence of dissolved hydrogen (back hydrogenation reaction) is the key mechanism in pellet deactivation. Indeed, in the absence of bubbles, the transport of hydrogen inside the pellet is dominated by diffusion, which leads to the build-up of produced hydrogen inside and around the catalytic pellet.
A high local concentration of hydrogen promotes the back hydrogenation reaction and thus reduces the overall dehydrogenation rate. This effect can also be relevant for the active (bubbling) state. In this paper, we used a very simple model of the active state, assuming that bubbles grow at atmospheric pressure. However, in reality, the pressure in growing bubbles can be higher
Wang et al. 2021
, leading to the buildup of hydrogen concentration at catalytic sites. Moreover, this pressure will likely depend on the typical size of macropores of the pellet. Indeed, it has been recently shown that the productivity of catalytic pellets for LOHC hydrogenation can be significantly enhanced by tuning the structure of the porous support
Auer et al. 2025
.

Here, we focused our attention on the case of LOHC hydrogenation/dehydrogenation due to its relevance in the energy transition. However, the impact of back reactions and transport of volatile products on the performance of catalytic pellets is quite general, and we expect it to be observable also for other chemical reactions.
