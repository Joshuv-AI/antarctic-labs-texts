Prefrontal dysfunction and impulsive decision-making in female methamphetamine use disorder: a fNIRS study
Authors: Hao Zhang, ShiNian Ou, Yousong Hu, Cunfeng Yuan, Qianli Zou, Huoyin Zhang, Peng Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeightened preference for immediate over delayed rewards is a well-established feature of substance use disorders, yet the neural basis of this tendency in women with methamphetamine use disorder (MUD) remains insufficiently characterized. Delay discounting provides a useful behavioral index of impulsive decision-making, and convergent evidence implicates prefrontal valuation and control systems in addiction-related choice abnormalities.MethodsTwenty-five women with MUD and 25 healthy women completed an intertemporal decision-making task under gain and loss frames. Delay discounting rates were estimated using the hyperbolic discounting parameter (k). Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) was used to assess oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) responses in predefined prefrontal regions of interest, with a focus on the medial orbitofrontal cortex (mOFC) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). Trait impulsivity/reinforcement sensitivity was assessed using the BIS/BAS scales. Behavioral discounting was analyzed using a linear mixed model, and neural indices were analyzed using repeated-measures ANCOVAs with age as a covariate and FDR correction across ROIs.ResultsCompared with healthy controls, women with MUD showed significantly steeper delay discounting, indicating a stronger preference for immediate outcomes. They also exhibited lower HbO responses in the right mOFC and left DLPFC during task performance, as well as reduced task-related HbO change in the right DLPFC. Lower right mOFC mean task-window HbO was associated with higher BIS/BAS composite scores. No robust Group × Frame interaction was observed for behavioral discounting.ConclusionsWomen with MUD showed altered intertemporal choice and reduced prefrontal hemodynamic responses in regions implicated in value evaluation and cognitive control. These findings support the relevance of prefrontal dysfunction to impulsive decision-making in female MUD while also indicating that framing-related effects should be interpreted cautiously. The study extends the limited literature on women with MUD and supports the use of fNIRS to examine prefrontal mechanisms of addiction-related decision-making.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08434-x.

Introduction

Delay discounting as a behavioral index of impulsive decision-making in addiction

Impulsivity is a multidimensional construct encompassing distinct but interrelated components—including motor impulsivity, attentional impulsivity, and reward-related impulsive choice—that are linked to addictive behavior through partially separable mechanisms [1], and its relationship to addiction is best interpreted with conceptual precision. In the addiction literature, a particularly relevant aspect is impulsive decision-making, which refers to a tendency to favor smaller immediate outcomes over larger delayed ones. One of the most widely used behavioral measures of this tendency is delay discounting, the process by which the subjective value of an outcome decreases as the delay to its receipt increases [2, 3]. Delay discounting is therefore better understood not as a synonym for all impulsivity, but as a specific behavioral manifestation of impulsive choice within intertemporal decision-making.

A large body of work suggests that steeper delay discounting is associated with substance-related problems across multiple drug classes. From a behavioral economic perspective, addiction has been conceptualized as a disorder characterized by excessive preference for immediate reinforcement despite long-term adverse consequences, with delay discounting serving as one candidate marker of this imbalance [3]. More broadly, addiction may arise and be maintained through multiple overlapping mechanisms, of which impulsive decision-making represents one important but not exhaustive pathway [4]. Related neuroeconomic models similarly propose that addiction reflects disrupted valuation of delayed outcomes and diminished ability to sustain choices aligned with longer-term goals [5]. These frameworks converge in suggesting that addiction-related decision-making is shaped by both altered reward valuation and insufficient self-regulatory control.

Recent literature continues to support the clinical relevance of impulsive choice in substance use disorders. Delay discounting has been linked not only to addictive behavior itself but also to broader clinically relevant outcomes, including craving, treatment engagement, and recovery-related functioning [6]. In methamphetamine-related conditions specifically, available behavioral evidence indicates that individuals with methamphetamine use disorder (MUD) show elevated discounting relative to healthy controls, supporting the view that impulsive intertemporal choice is relevant to the disorder rather than incidental to it. At the same time, current evidence also suggests that impulsive traits and impulsive choice should not be collapsed into a single construct, because self-report impulsivity and task-based discounting may show overlapping but non-identical patterns [7].

Accordingly, the present study focuses on delay discounting as a theoretically grounded and behaviorally quantifiable index of impulsive decision-making in women with MUD. On this basis, we hypothesized that women with MUD would show steeper delay discounting than healthy controls.

Why focus on women with methamphetamine use disorder

Although the role of impulsive decision-making in addiction has received considerable attention, the evidence base remains disproportionately derived from male or mixed-sex samples, with women often underrepresented or insufficiently analyzed as a distinct group. This imbalance is problematic because women with substance use disorders can differ from men in clinically meaningful ways, including accelerated progression from initial use to dependence (i.e., the telescoping effect; [8]), greater relapse vulnerability, cue responsivity, emotional burden, and distinct treatment needs [9]. In methamphetamine-related disorders, sex-specific differences have also been reported in metabolic and treatment-related profiles, further indicating that direct extrapolation from predominantly male samples is inadequate [10, 11].

For women with MUD in particular, emerging evidence points to a clinically important intersection of impulsivity, psychiatric symptoms, and social vulnerability. In a sample of women with MUD, impulsivity and lower perceived social support predicted more severe psychiatric symptoms, suggesting that difficulties in self-regulation may be embedded within a broader psychosocial and affective burden rather than occurring in isolation [12]. Qualitative and treatment-oriented studies likewise suggest that female methamphetamine users may show distinctive use motivations, recovery challenges, and psychosocial contexts, reinforcing the need for women-specific investigation rather than simple post hoc sex adjustment.

Recent work in methamphetamine abstainers reported sex differences in prefrontal activation during craving-related task switching, with women showing stronger activation in dorsolateral and anterior prefrontal regions under some conditions, suggesting that the neural organization of cognitive control under drug-related challenge may differ by sex [13]. In addition, preclinical work has shown that methamphetamine self-administration can induce sex-specific medial prefrontal neuronal dysfunction that varies across withdrawal stages, further supporting the idea that sex is not a peripheral demographic variable but a biologically relevant factor in methamphetamine-related neuroadaptation [14]. Broader human addiction neuroimaging evidence also indicates that sex- and hormone-related differences in prefrontal cue reactivity and regulation may shape vulnerability and craving-related processes [15].

Taken together, these findings justify a specific focus on women with MUD. Rather than assuming that female patterns mirror male patterns, the present study treats women with MUD as an empirically important population in whom impulsive decision-making and its neural correlates require direct characterization.

Prefrontal mechanisms of intertemporal decision-making: valuation and control

Intertemporal decision-making, a key component of value-based decision-making [16], depends on coordinated processing across prefrontal systems involved in valuation, goal maintenance, and behavioral control. Two regions are especially relevant to the present study: the medial orbitofrontal cortex (mOFC) and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). Existing work suggests that these regions contribute different but complementary functions during choice.

The orbitofrontal cortex has long been implicated in flexible, value-based behavior. Contemporary accounts emphasize that OFC-related regions integrate sensory, affective, and contextual information to support valuation and adaptive choice, with abnormalities in OFC function linked to impaired reward processing, impulsivity, and addiction-related behavioral dysregulation [17]. More focused experimental work further indicates that medial orbitofrontal-striatal circuits support dissociable components of risk/reward computation, consistent with the idea that the mOFC contributes to evaluating delayed versus immediate reward contingencies [18]. In this sense, reduced mOFC recruitment during delay discounting may reflect impoverished representation of delayed reward value rather than mere global task under-engagement.

The DLPFC, by contrast, is more consistently linked to executive and control-related operations relevant to intertemporal choice, including maintaining long-term goals, suppressing prepotent responses, and implementing top-down regulation over immediate reward bias. More recent evidence suggests that individual differences in delay discounting are associated not only with self-control measures but also with structural and functional properties of dorsolateral-medial prefrontal systems [19]. In addiction, convergent evidence implicates prefrontal control abnormalities as a central feature of the disorder, with reduced or inefficient engagement of DLPFC-related systems contributing to impaired inhibitory control, craving regulation, and maladaptive decision-making [9, 20–22].

This valuation-control distinction is also consistent with broader addiction models. Neurocircuitry accounts emphasize that addiction involves dysregulation in interacting reward and control systems rather than dysfunction in a single isolated process [23]. In practical terms, this framework predicts that individuals with MUD may show reduced prefrontal responses in regions supporting delayed reward valuation and behavioral control during intertemporal choice. On that basis, the present study focused on the mOFC and DLPFC as a priori regions of interest and hypothesized that women with MUD would show lower prefrontal hemodynamic responses than healthy controls during delay discounting.

Why use fNIRS

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) is well suited for the present research question because it enables noninvasive, relatively portable measurement of task-related hemodynamic changes in cortical regions, particularly the prefrontal cortex. Compared with more resource-intensive neuroimaging approaches, fNIRS offers practical advantages for studying addiction-related cognition in clinical or quasi-clinical settings while preserving reasonable ecological flexibility during behavioral task performance [24, 25]. These features are especially relevant when examining decision-making in participants for whom scanner-based paradigms may be less feasible.

Importantly, fNIRS has already been used in addiction-related research to examine prefrontal function during cognitive and affective tasks. In a study of internet addiction, fNIRS captured altered OFC and left DLPFC activation during intertemporal decision-making, demonstrating the feasibility of using prefrontal hemodynamic signals to study delayed choice behavior in an addiction-related population [26]. Additional fNIRS studies in addiction-like or addiction-related conditions have also implicated DLPFC and OFC systems in inhibitory control and reward-related processing [27, 28]. While these studies are not direct substitutes for MUD-specific evidence, they provide a useful methodological precedent for examining prefrontal decision-related function with fNIRS.

At the same time, the current literature remains limited. Existing fNIRS work in addiction is still sparse, and directly comparable studies focusing on women with MUD during delay discounting are lacking. That gap is methodologically important: it means that fNIRS is justified here not because the exact paradigm has already been established, but because the method is suitable for capturing prefrontal activity in addiction-related decision/control tasks and because the specific application to women with MUD remains underexplored.

Framing context and study aims

Another issue relevant to intertemporal decision-making is whether the behavioral and neural expression of impulsive choice varies across gain and loss contexts. Existing theory suggests that gains and losses are not evaluated symmetrically, and more recent work indicates that individual differences in impulsivity under loss-related contexts may involve partially distinct computational and neural substrates [29]. However, for addiction—especially female MUD—direct evidence on gain/loss framing effects in delay discounting remains limited. Therefore, gain/loss framing was included in the present study as a theoretically motivated contextual manipulation, but not as a domain in which strong prior evidence already exists for women with MUD.

Against this background, the present study examined delay discounting and prefrontal hemodynamic responses during intertemporal decision-making in women with MUD using fNIRS. Specifically, we aimed to determine whether women with MUD would show (a) steeper delay discounting than healthy controls, (b) reduced prefrontal activation in regions implicated in valuation and control, especially the mOFC and DLPFC, and (c) associations between altered prefrontal responses and trait impulsivity. We also explored whether these patterns differed across gain and loss contexts, while interpreting any framing-related effects cautiously given the current state of the literature.

Methods

Study design and participants

Study design

This study used a cross-sectional case-control design to examine intertemporal decision-making and prefrontal hemodynamic responses in women with methamphetamine use disorder (MUD) and healthy control women. Because the present study focused on biological sex rather than gender identity, we use the terms sex and women consistently throughout the manuscript. Sex was recorded as female for all participants, whereas gender identity was not assessed separately.

Sample size determination

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 for a 2 (Group: MUD vs. healthy control) × 2 (Frame: gain vs. loss) mixed design. Because directly comparable fNIRS studies in women with MUD performing delay discounting tasks were not available at the time of study planning, we used a conventional medium effect size (f = 0.25) as a feasibility-informed planning value rather than as a precision estimate for neuroimaging effects. With α = 0.05 and 1 − β = 0.80, the minimum required sample size was estimated at 34 participants. We therefore aimed to recruit a larger sample to offset potential attrition and data loss due to poor signal quality or participant discomfort. The final analyzed sample comprised 50 participants, exceeding the minimum target while still being interpreted as modest for brain-behavior inference.

Participant recruitment and diagnostic assessment

We initially recruited 30 women with MUD from drug rehabilitation centers in Sichuan Province, China. All participants in the clinical group met DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for methamphetamine use disorder [30]. Inclusion criteria for the MUD group were: (a) primary diagnosis of MUD; (b) methamphetamine use history of at least 1 year; and (c) reported methamphetamine intake exceeding 0.1 g per day during the 6 months prior to rehabilitation.

Participants in the MUD group had been abstinent for a mean duration of 14.70 months (SD = 4.51, range = 3–24 months) at the time of testing. The diagnostic interview also assessed other current and past substance use disorders (e.g., alcohol, opioid, cannabis). While general alcohol consumption frequency was recorded, individuals meeting the diagnostic criteria for any current comorbid substance use disorder (including alcohol use disorder) were excluded. Tobacco use and non-disordered alcohol use were assessed but not treated as exclusion criteria.

The healthy control group consisted of 25 women recruited from the local community. Exclusion criteria for both groups were: (a) history of neurological disease or major medical illness affecting cognition; (b) history of head injury involving loss of consciousness; and (c) use of psychoactive medication during the 3 months preceding assessment. For the healthy control group, any lifetime diagnosis of substance use disorder or other major psychiatric disorder was additionally excluded. Five women in the MUD group discontinued participation because of physical discomfort during data collection, resulting in a final sample of 25 women with MUD and 25 healthy control women.

Age characteristics

The healthy control group (M = 20.60, SD = 1.35) was significantly younger than the MUD group (M = 29.64, SD = 4.42), 95% CI for the mean difference [7.18, 10.90], t(48) = 9.77, p < 0.001. Because age may influence both delay discounting and prefrontal hemodynamic responses, age was modeled as a covariate in group comparisons. To avoid inappropriate covariate adjustment, the homogeneity-of-regression assumption was explicitly evaluated by testing the Group × Age interaction before interpretation of ANCOVA results (see Statistical Analysis).

Ethical approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation and were informed that they could withdraw at any time without penalty. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Sichuan Psychological Society (Approval No. 2022100). Because data collection was conducted across multiple decentralized drug rehabilitation centers in Sichuan Province rather than within a single institutional facility, ethical oversight was coordinated centrally by the Sichuan Psychological Society, which serves as the regional coordinating body for behavioral and psychological research ethics in non-hospital rehabilitation settings across this province.

Incentive structure

To improve task engagement and ecological validity, participant compensation was partially choice-contingent. At the end of the task, one trial was randomly selected for payment implementation. If an immediate option had been chosen on that trial, the participant received the corresponding amount immediately; if a delayed option had been chosen, payment was delivered after the specified delay interval. For loss-framed trials, participants were endowed with a fixed amount from which the selected loss was deducted. All task tokens were converted into Chinese Yuan (RMB) using a predetermined exchange rate. The average final compensation was 45.75 RMB (SD = 14.25).

Experimental paradigm and procedure

Delay discounting task

Participants completed a modified delay discounting task adapted for fNIRS recording. Delay discounting tasks are commonly used to quantify impulsive choice by requiring participants to choose between smaller immediate outcomes and larger delayed outcomes [31, 32]. The present task used a 2 (Group: MUD vs. healthy control) × 2 (Frame: gain vs. loss) mixed structure.

A total of 72 trials were administered, including 36 gain-framed trials and 36 loss-framed trials presented in pseudorandom order. In gain-framed trials, participants chose between a smaller immediate gain and a larger delayed gain. In loss-framed trials, participants chose between a smaller immediate loss and a larger delayed loss. To reduce task monotony while preserving feasibility for fNIRS acquisition, the task varied two delay intervals (7 days and 30 days) and three outcome magnitudes (100, 400, and 1000 tokens), with repeated presentations across trials. This abbreviated design was chosen to balance psychometric sensitivity with practical constraints related to participant fatigue, prolonged abstinence-center testing, and motion contamination during fNIRS acquisition. Accordingly, the task was intended to capture relative individual and group differences in discounting tendency within an fNIRS-compatible paradigm rather than to provide an exhaustive parametric characterization of discounting across many delays and magnitudes.

Trial structure

The temporal structure of each trial is illustrated in Fig. 1. Each trial began with a fixation cross presented for 1000 ms, followed by simultaneous presentation of the two choice options for up to 10 s, during which the participant made a response. After the response, a feedback screen was presented during the 15-s inter-trial interval. The feedback screen re-displayed the same two options from the just-completed trial, with the participant’s selected option highlighted in yellow to confirm the response. This feedback screen did not constitute a second decision phase. The 15-s inter-trial interval was used to reduce carryover of hemodynamic responses across successive trials.

Computation of delay discounting

Delay discounting was indexed using the hyperbolic discounting parameter k, based on the standard hyperbolic model:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{V}\hspace{0.17em}=\hspace{0.17em}\mathrm{A}\:/\:(1\hspace{0.17em}+\hspace{0.17em}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{D})$$\end{document}

where V denotes the subjective value of the delayed option, A denotes the nominal amount, and D denotes the delay in days [31]. Because raw k values are typically positively skewed and can show strong non-normality, analyses were performed on the natural log-transformed absolute value, ln|k|. This transformation reduces skewness and stabilizes variance while preserving the ordinal interpretation of discounting steepness. Higher ln|k| values indicate steeper discounting and therefore stronger preference for immediate outcomes. The same transformed variable was used in all inferential behavioral analyses.

Self-report measure

BIS/BAS assessment

Trait motivational sensitivity was assessed using the Behavioral Inhibition System/Behavioral Activation System (BIS/BAS) scales [33]. The Chinese version validated by Li et al., [34] was used in the present study. This instrument is grounded in Gray and McNaughton’s reinforcement sensitivity theory [35], which proposes that the BIS mediates responses to threat, punishment, and novelty, while the BAS mediates appetitive motivation and sensitivity to reward-related cues. The BAS includes the Drive, Fun Seeking, and Reward Responsiveness subscales. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). In the present sample, Cronbach’s α for the total scale was 0.85. Because BIS/BAS scales do not measure delay discounting directly, they were treated as trait-level motivational/impulsivity-related indicators rather than as interchangeable with task-based impulsive choice. Their inclusion allowed us to examine whether individual differences in self-reported behavioral inhibition/activation were associated with task-derived discounting and prefrontal activation patterns.

fNIRS Acquisition, ROIs, and signal processing

fNIRS acquisition

fNIRS data were acquired using the NirSmart II-3000 A system (Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China). The system recorded near-infrared light absorption at 730 and 850 nm at a sampling rate of 11 Hz. A source-detector separation of approximately 3 cm was used, yielding 48 active channels over frontal regions. The optode arrangement is shown in Fig. 2.

Although bilateral ROIs were extracted for completeness, the strongest a priori hypotheses concerned prefrontal valuation and control systems, especially the mOFC and DLPFC. The ROI-based approach was chosen deliberately because the present study did not estimate functional connectivity or network-level dynamics.

Preprocessing strategy

To reduce unnecessary analytic flexibility, one primary preprocessing pathway was used for inferential analyses. Raw fNIRS signals were processed using HOMER2 together with standard task-fNIRS procedures described in prior methodological work [25, 36]. Preprocessing included: (a) visual and automated identification of poor-quality channels; (b) motion artifact correction; (c) temporal filtering (0.01–0.10 Hz) to reduce slow drift and high-frequency physiological noise; and (d) conversion of optical density changes into oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) concentration changes using the modified Beer–Lambert law. Because HbO is generally more sensitive than HbR to task-related cerebral blood flow changes in block-style paradigms, HbO was used as the primary hemodynamic index.

NIRS_KIT was used as a complementary quality-control and visualization tool rather than as an independent inferential pipeline. Representative preprocessing stages are shown in Fig. 4, illustrating: (A) raw signal, (B) detrended signal, (C) motion-corrected signal, and (D) filtered signal.

Definition of baseline, HbO, and ΔHbO

Because fNIRS measures relative hemoglobin concentration changes rather than absolute physiological concentrations, all concentration-based indices in the present study were defined with explicit reference to the preprocessing stream and trial structure.

For concentration-based analyses, the preprocessed fNIRS signals were segmented into epochs from − 1 to 20 s relative to stimulus onset. The pre-stimulus baseline was defined as the mean HbO signal during the 1-s interval immediately preceding option onset (-1 to 0 s). This interval was used as the local pre-decision reference after the inter-trial interval.

Based on these epoched data, two related but distinct concentration-derived indices were extracted for each participant, condition, and ROI:

Mean task-window HbO: the mean HbO signal during the 0–10 s window after stimulus onset, derived from the preprocessed concentration time series. This index reflects the average relative HbO level during the decision period. For brevity, this variable is referred to as “HbO” in the Results section, but it should be understood as a relative concentration measure rather than an absolute physiological concentration.

ΔHbO (baseline-corrected task response): the mean HbO signal during the 0–10 s post-stimulus window after subtraction of the pre-stimulus baseline (-1 to 0 s) for the same trial or condition. Thus, ΔHbO reflects the task-evoked change in HbO relative to the local pre-stimulus baseline.

For both indices, ROI values were obtained by averaging channel-level estimates within each predefined ROI. This distinction between mean task-window HbO and baseline-corrected ΔHbO was maintained consistently throughout the Results and Discussion to avoid ambiguity.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0).

Preliminary checks and assumption testing

Before fitting the main models, we examined descriptive distributions, stem-and-leaf plots, Q–Q plots, and Shapiro–Wilk tests for the behavioral discounting indices and the key ROI variables. Homogeneity of variance for between-group comparisons was assessed using Levene’s test. Because age differed substantially between groups, the homogeneity-of-regression assumption underlying ANCOVA was evaluated by testing the Group × Age interaction for each candidate dependent variable before interpreting covariate-adjusted group effects. As summarized in Supplementary Table S1, the behavioral discounting variables remained non-normal after transformation, and the gain-domain discounting index showed a significant Group × Age interaction, F = 15.623, p <0.001. Accordingly, the behavioral outcome was analyzed using a linear mixed model rather than standard ANCOVA. By contrast, the condition-specific HbO variables in the key hypothesized ROIs showed no significant Group × Age interactions (all ps ≥ 0.324) and acceptable homogeneity of variance (Levene’s ps = 0.062–0.269), supporting covariate-adjusted neural analyses. Because several raw groupwise ROI distributions still showed minor deviations from normality, model residuals and Q–Q plots were inspected in parallel and no severe departures were observed. Detailed assumption-check statistics for the behavioral indices and the key hypothesized neural variables are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Outlier handling

Potentially influential observations in the behavioral models were evaluated using standardized residuals, leverage, and Cook’s distance. For task-fNIRS data, poor-quality signal segments were addressed during preprocessing through bad-channel identification and motion correction, rather than through post hoc deletion of ROI averages solely on the basis of dispersion.

Main behavioral analysis

To test group differences in discounting (ln|k|), a Linear Mixed Model (LMM) was implemented due to the violation of the parallel-slopes assumption required for standard ANCOVA (i.e., a significant Group × Age interaction). The model included Group, Decision Framework (Frame), and Age as fixed main effects, alongside Group × Frame and Group × Age interaction terms. Frame was modeled as a repeated measure within subjects. Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimation was utilized, and significant main effects or interactions were followed by Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons based on estimated marginal means.

Main neural analyses

Because preliminary checks did not indicate violations of the homogeneity-of-regression assumption for the key neural indices, and variance assumptions were acceptable for the hypothesized ROIs, covariate-adjusted repeated-measures ANCOVAs were retained for neural analyses. For each ROI, separate 2 (Group: MUD vs. healthy control) × 2 (Frame: gain vs. loss) repeated-measures ANCOVAs were performed on (a) mean task-window HbO and (b) baseline-corrected ΔHbO, with age included as a covariate. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied across the six ROI-level tests within each outcome family (i.e., one FDR correction family for HbO, one for ΔHbO). Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons were used to decompose significant main effects or interactions.

Brain-behavior associations

To examine whether trait-level impulsivity/reinforcement sensitivity was related to task-derived discounting and prefrontal hemodynamic response, partial correlation analyses controlling for age were conducted between BIS/BAS composite scores and delay discounting rates (ln|k|), as well as between BIS/BAS composite scores and the neural indices in the hypothesized ROIs. Partial correlations were selected for these association tests because the primary aim was to characterize pairwise associations between trait-level and task-derived measures after removing the influence of the age covariate. Given the modest sample size and the exploratory nature of these analyses, more complex model-based approaches—such as linear mixed models or Bayesian regression frameworks—were considered disproportionate to the inferential objectives; partial correlations provided a transparent and appropriately conservative estimate of the target associations. These analyses were treated as brain-behavior association tests rather than as evidence that any single ROI constitutes a definitive biomarker. Where multiple related neural correlations were tested within the same family, FDR correction was applied.

Results

Delay discounting performance

Due to a significant Group × Age interaction violating the parallel-slopes assumption of standard ANCOVA, delay discounting rates (ln|k|) were evaluated using a Linear Mixed Model (LMM). The model included Group, Decision Framework (Frame), and Age as fixed main effects, alongside Group × Frame and Group × Age as interaction terms, with Frame treated as a repeated measure.

The LMM revealed a significant main effect of Group, F(1, 93.98) = 14.04, p < 0.001. Pairwise comparisons based on estimated marginal means (evaluated at the mean covariate age of 25.12 years) showed that the MUD group exhibited significantly higher log-transformed discount rates (M = -0.851, SE = 0.335) than the healthy control group (M = -2.032, SE = 0.136; mean difference = 1.181, p = 0.002). This indicates a stronger preference for immediate rewards among female MUD patients.

Additionally, the model yielded a significant Group × Age interaction, F(1, 93.98) = 14.86, p < 0.001, indicating differing age-related trajectories of impulsive decision-making between the two groups. However, neither the main effect of Decision Framework, F(1, 93.98) = 2.32, p = 0.131, nor the Group × Framework interaction, F(1, 93.98) = 0.003, p = 0.959, reached statistical significance. These findings indicate that the group difference in delay discounting did not differ detectably between gain and loss frames in the present sample (Fig. 5).

Prefrontal hemodynamic responses during intertemporal decision-making

Prior to the primary neuroimaging analyses, assumption checks were conducted for the key ROI variables. Detailed diagnostic statistics are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Briefly, the condition-specific HbO measures in the key hypothesized ROIs showed no significant Group × Age interactions (all ps ≥ 0.324), and Levene’s tests indicated acceptable between-group homogeneity of variance (ps = 0.062–0.269). Although several groupwise Shapiro–Wilk tests for raw ROI distributions were significant, visual inspection of Q–Q plots and model residuals did not indicate severe departures that would invalidate the covariate-adjusted analyses. Accordingly, the neural data were analyzed using repeated-measures ANCOVAs with age as a covariate.

Two related but distinct hemodynamic indices were analyzed separately. First, we examined mean task-window HbO, defined as the mean oxygenated hemoglobin signal during the 0–10 s post-option-onset window. Second, we examined ΔHbO, defined as the baseline-corrected task-evoked change during the same window. This distinction is important because HbO and ΔHbO address related but non-identical aspects of the task response.

Mean task-window HbO

We conducted ROI-wise 2 (Group: MUD vs. healthy control) × 2 (Frame: gain vs. loss) repeated-measures ANCOVAs on mean task-window HbO, with age as a covariate. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied across the six ROI tests for this outcome family.

After FDR correction, significant main effects of Group were observed in two prefrontal regions: the right medial orbitofrontal cortex (right mOFC), F(1, 48) = 10.122, FDR-adjusted p = 0.003, partial η² = 0.174, and the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (left DLPFC), F(1, 48) = 6.304, FDR-adjusted p = 0.015, partial η² = 0.116. Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons showed that, in both regions, the MUD group exhibited lower mean task-window HbO than the healthy control group. In the right mOFC, the healthy control group showed a mean HbO value of 0.004 (SE = 0.014), whereas the MUD group showed a mean value of -0.023 (SE = 0.014). In the left DLPFC, the corresponding means were 0.054 (SE = 0.017) for the healthy control group and − 0.006 (SE = 0.017) for the MUD group.

No significant Group × Frame interactions were observed in any ROI after FDR correction (all adjusted ps > 0.05). Likewise, no significant main effects of Frame were observed after correction. In the remaining four ROIs (left mOFC, left vmPFC, right vmPFC, and right DLPFC), neither the main effects nor the interaction effects reached significance for mean task-window HbO.

Accordingly, the HbO results indicate that women with MUD showed reduced prefrontal hemodynamic responses in the right mOFC and left DLPFC during intertemporal decision-making, but these group differences were not detectably modulated by gain versus loss framing in the present dataset. These results are shown in Fig. 6, with the significant group effects displayed specifically for the right mOFC (Fig. 6B) and left DLPFC (Fig. 6C).

Baseline-corrected ΔHbO

We next examined baseline-corrected task-related hemodynamic change (ΔHbO), defined as the difference between the pre-stimulus baseline and the 0–10 s post-option-onset window. Before the main ROI comparisons, one-sample t tests were conducted to determine whether any ROI showed significant overall task-related activation across participants. After FDR correction, only the left mOFC showed significant overall task-related activation, t = 6.128, FDR-adjusted p < 0.001. We then conducted ROI-wise 2 (Group: MUD vs. healthy control) × 2 (Frame: gain vs. loss) repeated-measures ANCOVAs on ΔHbO, again with age as a covariate and FDR correction applied across the six ROI tests.

After correction, a significant main effect of Group emerged only in the right DLPFC, F(1, 48) = 7.426, FDR-adjusted p = 0.009, partial η² = 0.134. Bonferroni-adjusted comparisons showed that women with MUD had lower ΔHbO in the right DLPFC (M = -0.012, SE = 0.012) than healthy control women (M = 0.034, SE = 0.012).

No significant main effects of Frame or Group × Frame interactions were observed for ΔHbO in any ROI after FDR correction (all adjusted ps > 0.05). Thus, the baseline-corrected analysis converged with the HbO findings in indicating reduced prefrontal recruitment in the MUD group, while again providing no evidence for a robust framing-related modulation at the group level. Brain-behavior associations

To examine the relationship between trait-level impulsivity and task-derived impulsive choice, partial correlation analyses controlling for age were conducted between BIS/BAS composite scores and delay discounting rates (ln|k|). The results revealed no significant correlations in either the gain condition (r = -.090, p = 0.541) or the loss condition (r = -.250, p = 0.083).

Next, we evaluated associations between BIS/BAS composite scores and the a priori prefrontal neural indices. Following FDR correction for multiple comparisons, mean task-window HbO in the right mOFC showed a significant negative correlation with BIS/BAS composite scores (r = -.373, p = 0.008, FDR-corrected). This finding suggests that lower right mOFC recruitment was associated with greater impulsivity-related trait tendency in the current sample.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study examined intertemporal decision-making and prefrontal hemodynamic responses in women with methamphetamine use disorder (MUD) using a delay discounting task combined with functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS). Three main findings emerged. First, compared with healthy control women, women with MUD showed steeper delay discounting, indicating a stronger preference for immediate outcomes. Second, at the neural level, the MUD group showed reduced prefrontal hemodynamic responses in regions implicated in value evaluation and cognitive control, specifically lower mean task-window HbO in the right medial orbitofrontal cortex (mOFC) and left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), as well as reduced baseline-corrected ΔHbO in the right DLPFC. Third, lower right mOFC activation was associated with higher BIS/BAS composite scores, suggesting a link between reduced prefrontal recruitment and impulsivity-related trait measures. Because the primary analyses did not show a robust Group × Frame interaction, framing-related effects should be interpreted cautiously.

Taken together, these findings support the view that women with MUD show altered intertemporal decision-making and reduced recruitment of prefrontal systems relevant to valuation and control. At the same time, the findings do not justify strong causal claims about mechanism, nor do they establish a uniquely female neural phenotype. Rather, the present results add female-specific data to a literature that has historically relied heavily on male or mixed-sex samples.

Delay discounting in women with MUD

The behavioral results showed that women with MUD discounted delayed outcomes more steeply than healthy control women. This pattern is broadly consistent with prior work indicating that delay discounting is elevated across multiple substance use disorders and may serve as a useful behavioral index of impulsive choice in addiction [2, 3]. It is also compatible with more recent evidence indicating that individuals with methamphetamine-related disorders show greater discounting than healthy individuals, although impulsive traits and discounting behavior should not be treated as interchangeable constructs [7].

Importantly, the present findings extend this literature by focusing specifically on women with MUD. The result does not imply that women necessarily show steeper discounting than men with MUD, because no male comparison group was included. Instead, the more defensible conclusion is that within a female sample, MUD was associated with greater impulsive intertemporal choice relative to healthy control women.

From a theoretical perspective, the behavioral finding is consistent with models proposing that addiction involves a shift toward immediate reinforcement at the expense of delayed outcomes. However, the current data do not indicate whether steeper discounting in the MUD group reflects pre-existing vulnerability, consequences of chronic methamphetamine exposure, treatment environment effects, or some combination of these factors. This issue remains unresolved because of the cross-sectional design.

Reduced prefrontal hemodynamic responses during intertemporal decision-making

At the neural level, the MUD group showed lower mean task-window HbO in the right mOFC and left DLPFC, as well as reduced baseline-corrected ΔHbO in the right DLPFC. These findings are broadly consistent with the conceptual distinction proposed in the Introduction, in which orbitofrontal regions contribute to reward/value representation and dorsolateral prefrontal regions contribute to control-related functions during choice. Existing literature suggests that orbitofrontal systems are involved in integrating affective and contextual information during value-based decision-making, whereas dorsolateral prefrontal systems contribute to maintaining goal-relevant information and regulating prepotent responses [9, 18, 19].

In this context, reduced right mOFC HbO in the MUD group is consistent with weaker recruitment of prefrontal valuation-related processes during intertemporal choice. Likewise, lower left DLPFC HbO and reduced right DLPFC ΔHbO are consistent with less efficient engagement of control-related prefrontal systems. Nevertheless, these interpretations should remain functionally modest. The present fNIRS data allow us to infer altered cortical hemodynamic response in these prefrontal ROIs, but they do not allow us to conclude that reward valuation or inhibitory control were directly isolated in a process-pure sense. Nor do they permit network-level or subcortical inferences. Accordingly, the most appropriate interpretation is that women with MUD showed reduced prefrontal hemodynamic recruitment in cortical regions commonly implicated in valuation- and control-related aspects of decision-making.

The baseline-corrected ΔHbO finding in the right DLPFC is especially useful because it complements the mean task-window HbO analysis. The two measures are related but not identical: the HbO analysis reflects mean signal during the task window, whereas ΔHbO indexes the task-evoked change relative to the pre-stimulus baseline. That both analyses pointed to reduced prefrontal recruitment in the MUD group strengthens confidence that the observed group difference was not merely a by-product of one operationalization of the hemodynamic signal.

Framing effects: limited evidence in the present dataset

One motivation for including gain and loss framing was the possibility that intertemporal choice in addiction might vary across motivational contexts. However, the present data did not show a significant Group × Frame interaction for discounting behavior, mean task-window HbO, or baseline-corrected ΔHbO. Therefore, the primary results do not provide robust support for a strong framing-dependent group difference in this sample.

A more appropriate interpretation is that gain/loss framing remained a theoretically motivated exploratory dimension rather than a strongly confirmed mechanism in the present study. The absence of a significant interaction may reflect several possibilities, including limited power to detect modest interaction effects, the abbreviated delay discounting paradigm used for fNIRS compatibility, or the possibility that group-related impulsive choice in this female MUD sample is relatively stable across gain and loss contexts. The current data cannot distinguish these explanations.

Accordingly, the framing results should be discussed in terms of limited or non-robust evidence, not as evidence that gain/loss context definitively modulates female MUD decision-making.

Brain-behavior association

The negative association between right mOFC activation and BIS/BAS composite score suggests that lower prefrontal recruitment in this region was related to greater impulsivity-related trait tendency. This brain-behavior association is broadly consistent with the idea that prefrontal dysfunction may be relevant to self-regulatory difficulties in addiction. However, BIS/BAS captures reinforcement sensitivity rather than delay discounting directly, so this association should be interpreted as linking prefrontal response to a trait-like motivational/impulsivity-related measure rather than to a pure measure of intertemporal choice. Given the cross-sectional design, the present data do not establish the direction of association between group status, prefrontal response, and trait-level impulsivity.

Relevance to female-focused addiction research

One contribution of the present study is that it adds female-specific data to a literature in which women have often been underrepresented. The findings are broadly compatible with emerging work suggesting that sex-related differences in addiction may extend to prefrontal and craving-related mechanisms [13, 15]. They also align with prior evidence indicating that impulsivity and psychosocial burden are clinically relevant in women with MUD [12].

At the same time, it is important not to overstate what this study demonstrates. Because only women were included, the current results do not identify a female-specific effect in the strict comparative sense. Rather, they show that women with MUD differ from healthy control women in behavior and prefrontal hemodynamic response. Demonstrating a true sex difference would require direct comparison with a male MUD group under the same experimental conditions. Thus, the present study should be viewed as helping to address an underrepresentation gap, rather than as establishing a definitive sex-differentiated neural signature.

Clinical and methodological implications

The present findings have cautious but potentially meaningful implications. Behaviorally, they support the relevance of delay discounting as a task-based marker of impulsive choice in women with MUD. Neurally, they suggest that fNIRS can detect group-related differences in prefrontal hemodynamic response during addiction-relevant decision-making. Given its portability and relative practicality, fNIRS may be useful for mechanistic research in treatment or rehabilitation settings where more resource-intensive imaging approaches are difficult to implement.

However, the present data do not justify describing the identified ROIs as established biomarkers, nor do they directly validate fNIRS for clinical prediction. A more appropriate implication is that prefrontal hemodynamic response measured with fNIRS may represent a promising research tool for studying cortical aspects of decision-making in underexamined clinical groups, including women with MUD. Replication in larger and more heterogeneous samples will be needed before stronger translational claims can be made.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample size was relatively small (n = 25 per group). Recent methodological work has demonstrated that small samples in brain-behavior research can yield effect estimates that are unreliable and fail to replicate, even when conventional significance thresholds are nominally satisfied [37]. The present findings—particularly the brain-behavior correlations and any interaction-level effects—should therefore be treated as preliminary estimates rather than established effects, and replication in larger, more heterogeneous samples is needed before stronger inferential claims can be supported. Second, the cross-sectional design does not permit causal inference; therefore, the observed behavioral and neural differences cannot be determined to be consequences of methamphetamine use rather than pre-existing vulnerability factors. Third, the MUD and control groups differed substantially in age. Although age was included as a covariate, residual confounding cannot be excluded because statistical adjustment does not fully replace close matching at recruitment. Fourth, participants with MUD were recruited from rehabilitation settings and were abstinent at the time of assessment. The findings may therefore not generalize to women with active methamphetamine use, community-based samples, or individuals at different stages of recovery. Fifth, the delay discounting task was abbreviated to ensure feasibility during fNIRS acquisition. While this design was sufficient to detect overall group differences in discounting, it may have been less sensitive to more subtle contextual effects, including gain–loss differences. Sixth, fNIRS is limited to cortical hemodynamic signals and cannot capture subcortical regions that are also central to addiction-related neurocircuitry. The present findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting cortical prefrontal correlates rather than the broader reward-control system. Additionally, menstrual cycle phase and sex hormone levels—including estradiol and progesterone—were not assessed in the present study. Accumulating evidence indicates that gonadal hormones can influence prefrontal cortical function and modulate reward-related and cue-related neural processing in women [15]. Uncontrolled variation in hormonal status across participants may therefore have contributed to individual differences in both behavioral discounting and prefrontal hemodynamic outcomes observed here. This represents a meaningful biological variable that was not characterized in the present study and should be explicitly assessed in future research. Finally, although the study focused on women, gender identity was not assessed separately and no male comparison group was included. Accordingly, the findings are best interpreted as characterizing women with MUD in this sample, rather than as establishing sex differences.

Future studies should recruit larger samples and include direct male comparison groups to test whether the present pattern generalizes across sexes or reflects sex-related differentiation. More detailed characterization of abstinence duration, comorbid substance use, menstrual or hormonal status, and treatment stage would also improve interpretability. Methodologically, combining fNIRS with complementary imaging or physiological approaches may help clarify how cortical prefrontal signals relate to broader addiction circuitry. Finally, replication of gain/loss framing effects in better-powered designs will be important before stronger claims can be made about contextual modulation of intertemporal decision-making in women with MUD. Pre-registration of future replication studies would also enhance the reproducibility and credibility of brain-behavior findings in this area. Additionally, application of alternative fNIRS signal processing approaches, such as pre-whitening or pre-weighting methods, may further improve signal quality and reduce preprocessing-related analytic variability in future work.

Conclusion

In conclusion, women with methamphetamine use disorder exhibited steeper delay discounting than healthy controls, together with reduced prefrontal hemodynamic responses during intertemporal decision-making. Group differences were observed in the right medial orbitofrontal cortex and left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex for mean task-window HbO, and in the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex for baseline-corrected ΔHbO. These findings suggest that altered prefrontal function may be involved in the abnormal evaluation of delayed outcomes and reduced cognitive control in women with methamphetamine use disorder. However, these results should be interpreted cautiously. The cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference, no male comparison group was included, and the evidence for framing-related effects was limited. Within these constraints, the present study adds to the limited literature on women with methamphetamine use disorder and supports the feasibility of using fNIRS to examine prefrontal correlates of addiction-related decision-making. Further studies with larger samples, direct sex comparisons, and longitudinal designs are needed to clarify the robustness and clinical relevance of these findings.
