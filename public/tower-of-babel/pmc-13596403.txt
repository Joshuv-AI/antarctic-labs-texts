Is Higher Academic Workload Associated with AI Dependency? Investigating the Roles of Academic Anxiety, Procrastination, and Satisfaction with AI
Authors: Shuyang Jiang, Nuo Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the increasing integration of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies into higher education, concerns have emerged regarding students’ growing dependency on AI tools. AI dependency refers to a maladaptive pattern of reliance on AI characterized by addiction-related symptoms. Grounded in the Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution (I-PACE) model, this study examines the associations between academic workload and AI dependency among undergraduate students by incorporating academic anxiety (affective), academic procrastination (executive), and satisfaction with AI (cognitive) into a moderated mediation framework.MethodsData were collected from 690 Chinese undergraduate students using validated self-report questionnaires with established psychometric properties. Structural equation modeling (SEM) with bootstrapping was used to construct latent variables and test the hypothesized model.ResultsResults showed that academic workload was positively associated with AI dependency (total β = 0.35), with a remaining direct association (β = 0.14) and indirect associations through academic anxiety (β = 0.10), academic procrastination (β = 0.06), and their sequential pathway (β = 0.05). Satisfaction with AI moderated the association between academic procrastination and AI dependency (β = 0.13), with stronger effects at higher levels of satisfaction.DiscussionThese findings extend the application of the I-PACE model by identifying affective and execution-related pathways associated with higher AI dependency. The pathway-specific moderation further suggests that positive appraisal of AI may amplify the link between procrastination and AI dependency. The findings highlight the need for educational strategies that address excessive academic demands, support students in managing anxiety and procrastination, and promote critical, balanced, and self-regulated AI use in higher education.

Introduction

The advent of generative artificial intelligence (AI), particularly large language models such as ChatGPT and DeepSeek, has profoundly reshaped various aspects of human activity, especially in higher education.1 According to the latest report from the China Internet Network Information Center (CNNIC), the number of generative AI users in China reached 515 million by June 2025, with 37.5% holding an associate degree or above. Consistent with this trend, large-scale survey evidence indicates a rapid increase in AI adoption among university students over the past two years,2 suggesting that AI has become deeply embedded in students’ academic routines and learning workflows.3 A growing body of research has demonstrated that students can leverage AI tools to enhance their learning experiences and promote various aspects of academic progress, including information retrieval, text and code generation, assistance in second language learning, assignment completion, and academic research projects.4–7

Despite these benefits, the rising popularity of AI tools has raised concerns about overreliance in academic contexts. Unlike frequent or adaptive AI use, AI dependency (AID) refers to a maladaptive pattern of reliance on AI tools characterized by addiction-related symptoms, such as salience, tolerance, mood modification, withdrawal, conflict, and unsuccessful attempts to reduce use.8,9 Emerging studies have warned that such dependency may undermine basic learning processes.5 Students who rely heavily on AI tend to engage in less independent verification of information and exhibit uncritical acceptance of AI-generated content, thereby increasing the risk of academic misconduct.10 Over time, sustained reliance on AI for idea generation and problem solving interferes with autonomous learning, reduces motivation for independent thinking, and impairs critical thinking and problem-solving abilities.11

Existing research on AI usage has largely focused on the acceptance and adoption of AI technologies,12 while relatively little attention has been paid to AI dependency. Among the emerging studies addressing this issue, AI dependency (AID) has often been examined in relation to general psychological and social factors, such as social anxiety, loneliness and interpersonal experiences.8,13 However, less is known about how academic-contextual factors are associated with students’ dependency on AI. This limitation is particularly significant given that students primarily use AI for academic purposes.14 For instance, Zhang et al9 reported that 83% of university students used AI for academic assistance, whereas only 24% used it for life-related issues. Given that students predominantly use AI for academic purposes, academic workload represents a proximal contextual factor in AID. Examining its role extends existing research beyond general psychosocial correlates to the academic demands surrounding students’ AI use.

To address this gap, the present study draws upon the Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution (I-PACE) model, an integrative framework for understanding the development and maintenance of technology-related problematic behaviors.15,16 Within this framework, problematic technology use, including AID, emerges from the dynamic interplay among situational triggers, individuals’ affective, cognitive, and executive processes. Experiences of gratification or compensation and the resulting behavior-specific expectancies may further reinforce continued engagement in a particular behavior. Applied to the higher education context, academic workload is conceptualized as a salient situational stressor associated with emotional strain and demands on self-regulation.17,18 Specifically, academic anxiety is considered an affective response to academic strain, academic procrastination reflects execution-related regulatory difficulties, and satisfaction with AI represents a behavior-related cognitive appraisal that may condition the association between academic procrastination and AID. By integrating these components within the I-PACE framework, this study examines the indirect and conditional associations linking academic workload with AID and provides a theoretical basis for understanding maladaptive AI reliance in higher education.

Academic Workload and AI Dependency

Academic workload is a well-documented stressor in higher education and is associated with greater psychological strain and poorer student well-being.17,18 Within the I-PACE framework,16 situational triggers interact with individuals’ affective, cognitive, and execution-related processes in shaping problematic technology use. Academic workload is therefore conceptualized as a context-specific trigger that may increase students’ motivation to seek accessible means of coping with demanding academic tasks.

According to stress coping theory,19 individuals experiencing stress are motivated to adopt coping strategies to alleviate discomfort. In academic contexts, AI may serve as an accessible external resource to reduce cognitive demands and expedite task completion.20,21 Supporting this perspective, a three-wave study of university students found that greater academic workload and time pressure were associated with more frequent ChatGPT use.22 Similarly, qualitative evidence from medical educators identified institutional workload as a prominent factor associated with AI dependency, with more than 80% of participants reporting its influence on their reliance on AI for content generation and instructional support.23 Collectively, these findings suggest that excessive workload may be associated with greater reliance on AI to cope with academic responsibilities. Accordingly, we hypothesize: H1: Academic workload is positively associated with AID.

H1: Academic workload is positively associated with AID.

Mediating Role of Academic Anxiety

Academic anxiety refers to feelings of fear, tension, and unease in response to academic demands.24 According to control-value theory,25 increased workload tends to diminish perceived control by reducing students’ self-efficacy, while simultaneously heightening task value due to elevated performance expectations and social comparison pressures.26 This combination creates an environment that is particularly conducive to anxiety. Empirical research has shown that academic demands are positively associated with negative emotions, including anxiety, shame, and helplessness.27,28 Within the I-PACE framework, academic anxiety represents a domain-specific affective response to academic workload that may increase vulnerability to maladaptive coping behaviors.

Students experiencing academic anxiety may be more inclined to seek external support or task-related assistance to cope with perceived academic strain, including the use of digital technologies.29 For example, Wang and Wang30 suggested that interactive AI systems may enhance users’ perceived sense of control through immediate feedback and responsiveness, which may alleviate tension in demanding contexts. Based on these findings, we propose an indirect association between academic workload and AID through academic anxiety. Therefore, we hypothesize: H2: Academic anxiety mediates the association between academic workload and AID.

H2: Academic anxiety mediates the association between academic workload and AID.

Mediating Role of Academic Procrastination

Building on the I-PACE framework, we further conceptualize academic procrastination as a potential mediator linking academic workload to AID, reflecting an execution-related self-regulatory difficulty characterized by the voluntary delay of intended academic tasks despite anticipated negative consequences.31

According to the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory,32,33 individuals are driven to retain and protect valued resources, such as cognitive resources and emotional stability. When these resources are threatened or depleted, individuals are inclined to adopt defensive strategies to avoid further depletion. In academic settings, increased workload demands impose substantial cognitive and emotional strain, leading students to appraise tasks more pessimistically (eg, perceiving excessive difficulty and reduced self-efficacy) and engage in greater procrastination as a passive, short-term strategy for conserving remaining resources. Prior research has shown that under high academic stress, students are more likely to delay tasks despite being aware of long-term negative costs.34,35

As a form of self-regulation failure, procrastination reflects a discrepancy between intended goals and enacted behavior,36 often resulting in last-minute task engagement under heightened time pressure. In such contexts, AI tools may be perceived as convenient shortcuts that lower the immediate effort required to initiate or complete delayed tasks.9,20 Consistent with this view, Urban et al37 found that using ChatGPT was associated with reduced perceived mental effort during task completion. Accordingly, students with higher levels of procrastination may be more likely to rely on AI tools when confronted with accumulated academic pressure. Therefore, we hypothesize: H3: Academic procrastination mediates the association between academic workload and AID.

H3: Academic procrastination mediates the association between academic workload and AID.

A Sequential Mediation Model

Academic anxiety and academic procrastination may also form a sequential indirect pathway in the association between academic workload and AID. Short-Term Mood Regulation theory31 frames procrastination as a failure of emotion-focused self-regulation, whereby individuals prioritize immediate relief from negative affect over long-term goal pursuit. Research has also indicated that highly anxious students are more prone to procrastination as a temporary escape from emotionally aversive academic demands.35 Accordingly, academic anxiety may be associated with greater procrastination, which may, in turn, be associated with higher AID. Therefore, we hypothesize the following: H4: Academic anxiety and academic procrastination sequentially mediate the association between academic workload and AID.

H4: Academic anxiety and academic procrastination sequentially mediate the association between academic workload and AID.

Moderating Role of Satisfaction with AI

In technology-enhanced learning research, satisfaction reflects learners’ evaluations of whether their technology-mediated experiences meet their expectations and is an important factor associated with continued engagement.21,38,39 Satisfaction with AI (SA) therefore represents a behavior-specific appraisal of the usefulness and overall experience of interacting with AI-powered academic tools. Consistent with the I-PACE framework, experienced gratification and behavior-specific expectancies may shape continued engagement in a particular behavior.15,16 Accordingly, SA is examined as a cognitive appraisal that may condition the association between execution-related difficulties and AID.

Academic procrastination is commonly associated with difficulties in initiating and sustaining goal-directed behavior.34,40 Under such conditions, students may turn to AI as an efficiency-oriented strategy to address accumulated academic tasks. When SA is high, students may perceive AI as capable of reducing the effort required to initiate or complete postponed tasks,10 thereby strengthening the association between procrastination and AID. Accordingly, we propose: H5: Satisfaction with AI positively moderates the relationship between academic procrastination and AID, such that the association is stronger at higher levels of satisfaction with AI.

H5: Satisfaction with AI positively moderates the relationship between academic procrastination and AID, such that the association is stronger at higher levels of satisfaction with AI.

The Current Study

Based on the preceding theoretical and empirical review, the present study applies the I-PACE model to examine the association between academic workload and AID among undergraduate students through a moderated mediation framework involving academic anxiety, academic procrastination, and SA. It extends emerging research on AID by situating it within students’ academic context and examining the distinct and sequential affective and execution-related pathways linking academic workload to AID. By testing SA as a pathway-specific moderator, the model further identifies when procrastination-related execution difficulties are more strongly associated with AID. Methods

Data Collection

This study employed a quantitative research design. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling, and data were collected through an online survey administered over three weeks from January to February 2025. The questionnaire was distributed via Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/), a widely used online survey platform in China, and the survey link was distributed through social media platforms, including WeChat and QQ.

Before beginning the survey, participants were informed of the study purpose, the voluntary nature of participation, and the confidentiality of their responses. They were assured that there were no right or wrong answers and were encouraged to respond honestly based on their actual experiences. All participants provided digital informed consent. For participants younger than 18 years, parental informed consent was also obtained. The study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of our university (Approval Number: SUST2024049). The self-report survey took approximately 10–15 minutes to complete.

The target population consisted of undergraduate students from Suzhou, China. Given the study’s focus on AI dependency, eligibility was limited to individuals with prior experience using AI tools (eg, ChatGPT, DeepSeek, Kimi, Doubao, Wenxin Yiyan). Only respondents who answered “Yes” to the screening question were allowed to proceed with the survey. A total of 754 eligible respondents completed the survey. After excluding responses with excessively short completion times or invariant response patterns, 690 valid responses were retained for analysis, yielding a valid-response rate of 91.5%. Measures

All measures were administered in Chinese. English-language measures were translated into Chinese by the research team and reviewed by bilingual researchers to ensure semantic consistency and clarity for Chinese university students, following a collaborative translation approach.41,42

Academic Workload

A four-item scale was used to assess participants’ perceived academic workload. The scale was adapted from Peterson et al43 and validated in previous studies.22 Students rated the items on a 5-point Likert scale (1= strongly disagree, 5= strongly agree). A sample item is “I feel overburdened due to my studies”. In the present study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.93). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) indicated good model fit: χ2/df = 2.19, CFI = 0.996, TLI = 0.993, RMSEA = 0.042.

Academic Anxiety

Academic anxiety was assessed using an 11-item scale developed by Cassady et al24 which has previously been applied to Chinese sample.44 Participants rated their level of agreement with each item on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). A sample item is “I often worry that my best is not as good as expected in school”. The scale demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.88). CFA results showed good model fit: χ2/df = 4.33, CFI = 0.979, TLI = 0.965, RMSEA = 0.070.

Academic Procrastination

The degree of students’ academic procrastination was measured using a three-item scale developed by Wohl et al.45 Responses were provided on a 5-point Likert scale (1= strongly disagree, 5= strongly agree). A sample item is “I put off studying until the last minute”. The scale showed good reliability in the current study (Cronbach’s α = 0.89). CFA confirmed good model fit: χ2/df = 1.73, CFI =0.996, TLI = 0.992, RMSEA = 0.032.

Satisfaction with AI

A five-item scale adapted from Hong et al46 was used to assess satisfaction with AI. The original scale assessed satisfaction in the context of social media and was subsequently adapted to a different educational context by Zhang et al.47 Following a similar approach, the items were reworded to refer specifically to AI use while retaining the conceptual focus and response format of the original scale. Participants rated their level of agreement on a 5-point Likert scale (1= strongly disagree, 5= strongly agree). A sample item is “The materials provided by AI helped me solve learning problems”. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.94). CFA results confirmed good model fit: χ2/df = 2.58, CFI = 0.997, TLI = 0.995, RMSEA = 0.048.

AI Dependency

AI dependency was measured using the AI dependency scale developed by Zhang et al9 which applies a symptom-based framework derived from behavioral addiction research.8,48 All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very rarely, 5 = very often). A sample item is: “I tried to cut down on my use of AI without success”. The scale demonstrated great internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.94). CFA demonstrated good model fit: χ2/df = 4.11, CFI = 0.979, TLI = 0.961, RMSEA = 0.067.

Control Variable

Prior experience with AI has been shown to influence individuals’ attitudes and behavioral intentions toward AI use.49,50 Therefore, prior AI experience was included as a control variable. Consistent with previous research on technology use,51 prior AI experience was operationalized as the duration of AI use and measured using a single item: “How long have you been using AI?” Responses were rated on a four-point scale: (1) less than six months, (2) six months to one year, (3) one to two years, and (4) more than two years.

Data Analysis

Descriptive Analysis

Before testing our hypotheses, we assessed the potential common method variance (CMV) using both Harman’s single-factor test (SPSS 27.0) and the unmeasured latent marker construct (ULMC) approach (Mplus 7.11). Subsequently, descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and Pearson correlation coefficients, were computed to examine the associations among the main study variables. Demographic variables (age, gender and major) and the control variable (prior AI experience) were included as covariates in subsequent analyses.

Research Model Analyses

Mplus 7.11 was used to conduct structural equation modeling (SEM) to test the research hypotheses. We followed the two-step SEM approach proposed by Anderson and Gerbing,52 in which confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was first conducted to examine the fit of the measurement model, followed by SEM analyses to test the hypothesized structural model (see Figure 1).

First, we tested the direct effect of academic workload on AID to establish the basis for subsequent analyses (H1). Second, to test the hypothesized mediation effects (H2-H4), a SEM was specified to examine the mediating roles of academic anxiety and academic procrastination in the relationship between academic workload and AID. Third, to test the hypothesized moderating effect of SA (H5), interaction terms between SA and academic procrastination were included to predict AID.

The goodness-of-fit indicators were reported, including comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). CFI and TLI values greater than 0.90 and RMSEA less than 0.08 were considered to indicate adequate model fit.53 A bootstrap procedure with 5000 resamples was conducted to estimate the 95% confidence intervals for all direct, indirect, and interaction effects. Effects were considered significant when the 95% confidence interval excluded zero.

Results

Common Method Variance and Multicollinearity Analysis

To assess the potential influence of CMV, Harman’s single-factor test and the unmeasured latent marker construct (ULMC) approach were employed.54 First, an unrotated exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed on all measurement items. The results revealed six factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the first factor accounted for 33.64% of the total variance, which was below the 50% threshold. Second, a latent method factor was added to the original measurement model, with each item loading on both its theoretical construct and method factor. The inclusion of a latent method factor resulted in relatively small changes in model fit (ΔCFI = 0.04, ΔTLI = 0.04, ΔRMSEA = 0.02), which did not exceed the recommended thresholds (ΔCFI/ΔTLI > 0.10, ΔRMSEA > 0.05).55 These results indicate that CMV was not a serious threat to the validity of the findings of the present study.

Multicollinearity among the predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance values based on their composite scores. VIF values below 5 and tolerance values above 0.20 were considered indicative of the absence of substantial multicollinearity. The VIF values ranged from 1.015 to 1.536, and the tolerance values ranged from 0.651 to 0.985, indicating no substantial multicollinearity.

Test of the Measurement Model

CFA was conducted to examine the fit of the measurement model with six latent variables: AW, AA, AP, AID, SA and SA × AP. Following the product-indicator approach,56 indicators with comparable factor loadings were paired to create interaction terms. The CFA results revealed that the measurement model had an adequate fit to the data (χ2 /df = 2.48, CFI = 0.960, TLI = 0.953, RMSEA = 0.046). The factor loadings of all indicators ranged from 0.51 to 0.93, indicating that they all significantly contributed to the assessment of the corresponding latent variables.

Mediation Analysis

To examine the mediating roles of AA and AP in the association between AW and AID. First, we conducted the direct effect model by examining the effect of AW on AID, in which AW was entered as an independent variable, AID was entered into the model as a dependent variable. The model showed a good fit to the data (χ2/df = 2.37, CFI = 0.973, TLI = 0.967, RMSEA = 0.045). The results revealed that AW had a positive effect on AID (β =0.34, p < 0.001).

Next, AID was entered into the model as the dependent variable, AW as the independent variable, and AA and AP as mediating variables. The results of the SEM showed an acceptable fit to the data (χ2 /df = 2.55, CFI = 0.945, TLI = 0.938, RMSEA = 0.047). As shown in Figure 2, the results revealed that students who experienced higher levels of AW reported higher levels of AA and AP and were more likely to demonstrate greater AID. Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is one of the few to examine AID within the I-PACE framework while simultaneously modeling affective, executive, and cognitive processes. The results showed that academic workload was positively associated with AID, both directly and indirectly through academic anxiety and academic procrastination, with SA strengthening the link between procrastination and AID. These findings highlight the relevance of contextual stressors and the joint involvement of affective responses, executive self-regulation, and cognitive appraisal in AID. By clarifying these relational patterns, this study provides useful insights for developing targeted strategies to promote responsible AI use in higher education.

Academic Workload Predicting AI Dependency

The findings indicate that academic workload is positively associated with AID, supporting H1 and aligning with recent evidence linking academic stressors to compensatory AI use in educational settings.23,57 When students encounter a high volume and complexity of academic tasks within limited time frames, they may experience intensified performance pressure and seek strategies to help manage task demands more efficiently. From a technology adoption perspective, AI systems offer rapid information processing, automated content generation, and structured outputs that can assist students in navigating heavy academic requirements.9,11 For example, Goldman et al57 found that ChatGPT was used to reduce special education teachers’ workload by automating paperwork and facilitating instructional adaptation. Similarly, Chen et al20 showed that in AI-assisted learning contexts, students streamlined help-seeking processes by skipping evaluation phase and directly asking questions, suggesting that AI may function as a practical resource under academic pressure. Under sustained workload conditions, repeated reliance on AI tools to cope with performance demands may be associated with more habitual usage patterns, which may be linked to higher levels of AID.

Mediating Roles of Academic Anxiety and Procrastination

Consistent with H2 and Control-Value Theory,25 academic anxiety mediated the association between academic workload and AID. Notably, this pathway made the largest relative contribution among the three indirect pathways. Elevated academic demands may undermine students’ perceived control over tasks, which may be accompanied by intensified anxiety. In such contexts, students may turn to AI not only for efficiency but also for its capacity to provide immediate, structured feedback that reduces uncertainty and alleviates performance-related stress.58 Zong and Yang59 showed that AI systems can promote emotional regulation through personalized, real-time support. Importantly, these findings collectively suggest that AI use under academic pressure is not inherently maladaptive. Rather, the indirect association through academic anxiety may reflect reliance on AI for uncertainty reduction or emotional relief, rather than efficiency seeking alone. This interpretation is consistent with longitudinal evidence linking avoidance- and emotion-focused coping to subsequent problematic Internet use.60 Thus, the coping function of AI use may be critical to understanding its association with dependency.

Consistent with H3 and the Conservation of Resources Theory,33 academic procrastination also mediated the association between academic workload and AID. This finding aligns with prior research identifying self-regulation as a pivotal mechanism through which contextual stressors contribute to problematic technology use61 and extends this relationship to AID. Previous studies have commonly interpreted the link between procrastination and excessive technology use as a form of avoidant or escapist coping, whereby individuals turn to gaming or online activities to temporarily alleviate negative affect associated with academic difficulties.62,63 However, the association between procrastination and AID may reflect a performance-oriented, yet regulation-compromised, pattern of technology use. Rather than disengaging from academic tasks, procrastinating students may rely on AI as a low-effort means of completing postponed work. Although academically directed, such reliance may substitute for sustained self-regulatory engagement and thereby become associated with higher levels of AID.

Consistent with H4 and the I-PACE model,16 the sequential indirect association through academic anxiety and procrastination was significant. Beyond their independent associations, anxiety and procrastination were jointly related to AID. By modeling these variables together, the findings extend single-mediator explanations and highlight the co-occurrence of emotional strain and self-regulatory difficulty under academic stress. This pattern is also consistent with the cumulative risk perspective,64 which suggests that co-existing stress and regulatory deficits are more strongly associated with maladaptive behaviors than isolated risk factors. Thus, although the sequential indirect effect was modest in magnitude, it may remain meaningful in recurring academic contexts where emotional strain and self-regulatory difficulties co-occur. Taken together, these results indicate the joint involvement of affective and self-regulatory processes in the association between academic strain and AID.

Satisfaction with AI as a Moderator

Moderation analyses showed that SA significantly strengthened the association between procrastination and AID, supporting H5. Simple slope analyses further revealed that the association was non-significant at low SA levels but became significant at average and high SA levels. This finding extends prior evidence linking procrastination and AI dependency65 by identifying SA as a potential boundary condition.

From the I-PACE perspective, repeated experiences of gratification or compensation may strengthen reward expectancies and coping-related beliefs about a specific behavior.16 Students with higher SA may hold stronger beliefs that AI is useful for reducing effort, managing delayed tasks, and compensating for insufficient self-regulation. According to cognitive offloading theory, individuals tend to offload cognitive work when internal demands rise and external tools are perceived as effective.66 Students who procrastinate often intend to complete tasks but struggle to translate these intentions into goal-directed actions,40 thereby increasing internal regulatory demands. In this case, when AI is viewed as inefficient or difficult to use, heightened internal demands may be less likely to translate into greater reliance. In contrast, when AI is perceived as efficient and capable of supporting academic tasks, it may serve as a compensatory resource that lowers the cognitive and initiation barriers to task engagement.10 Under such conditions, SA strengthens the relationship between procrastination and AID.

Theoretical and Practical Contributions

Theoretically, this study advances research on AID by situating it within an academic context characterized by workload and performance demands. Whereas prior research has primarily examined AI adoption or general psychosocial correlates of AI dependency, comparatively less attention has been paid to its associations with academic stressors. By integrating academic workload, anxiety, and procrastination into a unified framework, the present study conceptualizes academic workload as a context-specific trigger associated with AID through distinct affective and execution-related indirect pathways, as well as their sequential association. This pathway differentiation provides a more specific account of the distinct and joint involvement of academic anxiety and procrastination in the association between workload and maladaptive AI reliance. Moreover, this study extends the application of the I-PACE model16 to the context of AI use in academic settings. Notably, satisfaction with AI strengthened the association between procrastination and AID, indicating that cognitive appraisal may be particularly relevant to the execution-related pathway. This pathway-specific moderation provides a more differentiated application of the I-PACE framework by clarifying how behavior-related evaluative cognitions may condition the association between execution-related self-regulatory difficulty and AID.

Practically, the findings suggest that promoting adaptive AI use in higher education requires attention not only to academic task design but also to students’ emotional and self-regulatory capacities. Accordingly, institutions and instructors should recognize high workload, academic anxiety, and procrastination-related difficulties as potential risk factors for maladaptive AI reliance. Early intervention efforts may benefit from incorporating anxiety management and self-regulation training,20,27,36 which may help students avoid relying on AI primarily as a habitual coping or compensatory strategy.

The interaction between SA and academic procrastination further suggests that positive evaluations of AI may be particularly relevant to reliance tendencies among students with self-regulatory difficulties. Recent research indicates that higher AI literacy and trust can enhance engagement but may also be associated with increased dependency if not accompanied by critical reflection.67 Therefore, AI literacy education should move beyond promoting efficient AI use and prioritize critical reflection and a balanced understanding of AI affordances and limitations.1,67,68 Structured practices such as requiring students to document how AI outputs were modified, verify AI-generated information, identify potential biases, justify final decisions independently, or complete metacognitive checklists after AI use may help prevent the substitution of self-regulatory effort. Such approaches may support the use of AI as a scaffold for cognitive engagement rather than as a shortcut for task completion.

Limitations and Directions for Future Research

This study had several limitations. First, although the cross-sectional design allowed us to examine the associations among key variables, it could not establish their temporal ordering, and reverse or reciprocal associations remain possible. For example, the “illusion of competence” phenomenon69 suggests that AI dependency may weaken students’ metacognitive awareness and cognitive appraisal of task demands, which may be associated with reduced task engagement and higher procrastination. In particular, satisfaction with AI may partly arise from prior AI use or dependency. Although prior AI experience was included as a control variable to partially account for differences in previous AI exposure, it may not fully address this alternative temporal ordering. Future longitudinal or experimental studies should examine temporal precedence and alternative directional relationships.

Second, participants were recruited through convenience sampling from one university in Suzhou, China, and the sample included a relatively high proportion of female participants. These sampling characteristics may limit the representativeness of the sample and the generalizability of the findings to broader student populations. Research suggests that students’ engagement with AI is shaped by educational background, disciplinary context, and broader sociocultural conditions.70 In Chinese higher education context, intense competition and high performance expectations may orient students toward perceiving AI as an efficiency-enhancing resource, thereby promoting a more efficiency-oriented use pattern.71 Students’ SA may also vary across contexts depending on their AI literacy, access to AI tools, and concerns about the accuracy of AI-generated content.72,73 Therefore, the present findings should be generalized with caution, particularly to Western contexts where academic norms and AI-use practices may differ. Future research should adopt comparative designs to examine whether the present model operates similarly across educational backgrounds.

Third, several measurement issues should be noted. Since all key variables were measured using Likert-type self-report questionnaires, subjective response bias may have affected the findings, and common method variance cannot be completely ruled out despite the results of Harman’s single-factor test and the ULMC analysis. Moreover, prior AI experience was measured using a single item assessing only the duration of AI use and therefore did not capture its frequency, intensity, or specific purposes. Future research should use multi-source or objective measures and incorporate more comprehensive indicators of AI engagement, such as usage frequency, tool diversity, task types, and functional impairment, to better distinguish AID from adaptive high-frequency engagement. Qualitative methods, such as open-ended questions or interviews, could also provide a more nuanced understanding of the contexts, motivations, and subjective experiences underlying students’ AI use.

Finally, although academic workload was associated with AID through anxiety and procrastination, its direct effect remained significant after accounting for these mediators. This pattern suggests that additional mechanisms beyond affective and self-regulatory processes may be involved. Factors such as time pressure, cognitive styles, personality traits, or motivational orientations may also contribute to AID.74 Incorporating these variables in future research may provide a more comprehensive account of the additional pathways involved in this association.

Conclusion

The present study contributes to the literature by clarifying the direct and indirect associations between academic workload and AI dependency among undergraduate students, with distinct and sequential indirect associations operating through academic anxiety and academic procrastination. The association between academic procrastination and AI dependency was stronger at higher levels of satisfaction with AI. These findings extend the I-PACE framework by illustrating how affective, executive, and cognitive factors may jointly relate to students’ maladaptive reliance on AI. From a practical perspective, educators and higher education institutions should attend to excessive academic demands, support students in managing academic anxiety and procrastination, and promote critical and self-regulated AI use. Future longitudinal or experimental research is needed to examine the temporal ordering of these associations.
