Pattern formation in a Swift-Hohenberg equation with spatially periodic coefficients
Jolien Kamphuis, Martina Chirilus-Bruckner

Abstract
We study the Swift–Hohenberg equation – a paradigm model for pattern formation – with “large” spatially periodic coefficients and find a Turing bifurcation that generates patterns whose leading‑order form is a Bloch wave modulated by solutions of a Ginzburg–Landau type equation. Since the interplay between forcing wavenumber and intrinsic wavenumber crucially shapes the spectrum and emerging patterns, we distinguish between resonant and non‑resonant regimes. Extending earlier work that assumed asymptotically small coefficients, we tackle the more involved onset analysis produced by 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathcal{O}(1) forcing and work directly in Bloch space, where the richer structure of the bifurcating solutions becomes apparent. This abstract framework is readily transferable to more complex systems, such as reaction–diffusion equations arising as dryland vegetation models, where topography induces spatial heterogeneity.

Main objective.

Main novelty.
Unlike previous studies, we allow forcing of order one,

This regime introduces subtler bifurcation analysis due to the necessity of Bloch analysis at bifurcation onset, but it better reflects many applications. In particular, in the context of vegetation patterns, an application that partially motivated our work,

Remark 1.1

(
Periodic solutions of GL).

(

1.5.1
Bloch transform

The Bloch transform is an adaption of the Fourier transform in the spatially homogeneous case to the spatially periodic case
[
16
]
. It is defined using the Fourier transform and, similarly as to the Fourier transform, multiple definitions can be used. We use the symmetric Fourier transform

\hat{u}(k)=(\mathcal{F}u)(k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u(x)e^{-ikx}\penalty\ dx,

(1.23)

with inverse Fourier transform

u(x)=(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\hat{u})(k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{u}(k)e^{ikx}\penalty\ dk.

(1.24)

The following definition for the Bloch transform for
2\pi/k_{f}
-periodic functions can be derived

\widetilde{u}(\ell,x)=(\mathcal{B}u)(\ell,x)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{u}\left(k_{f}j+\ell\right)e^{ik_{f}jx},

(1.25)

with inverse Bloch transform

u(x)=(\mathcal{B}^{-1}\widetilde{u})(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\frac{k_{f}}{2}}^{\frac{k_{f}}{2}}\widetilde{u}(\ell,x)e^{i\ell x}\penalty\ d\ell.

(1.26)

This Bloch transform satisfies the following properties

\begin{split}\widetilde{u}\left(\ell,x+\frac{2\pi}{k_{f}}\right)&=\widetilde{u}(\ell,x),\\
\widetilde{u}\left(\ell+k_{f},x\right)&=\widetilde{u}(\ell,x)e^{-ik_{f}x}.\end{split}

(1.27)

The multiplication to convolution property becomes

\widetilde{fg}(\ell,x)=\int_{-k_{f}/2}^{k_{f}/2}\widetilde{f}(p)\widetilde{g}(\ell-p)\penalty\ dp=(\widetilde{f}\star\widetilde{g})(\ell,x).

(1.28)

1.5.2
Function space

Following the reasoning in
[
34
]
, we want to allow solutions of the Ginzburg-Landau equation that do not decay for
|x|\rightarrow\infty
and want to be able to use the Fourier transform and the Bloch transform. Hence we consider the Sobolev space
H_{ul}^{\vartheta}
, which is constructed in the following way.

We take a positive weight function
q:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
that is continuous, bounded and satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(x)\penalty\ dx<\infty
. Furthermore, we impose
q\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
and
|q^{\prime}(x)|
,
|q^{\prime\prime}(x)|\leq q(x)
for all
x
. Introduce

\widetilde{L_{ul}^{2}}=\left\{u\in L_{loc}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\penalty\ :\penalty\ \|u\|_{L_{ul}^{2}}<\infty\right\},\quad\text{ where }\quad\|u\|_{L_{ul}^{2}}^{2}=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(y+x)u(x)^{2}\penalty\ dx.

(1.29)

The
L_{ul}^{2}
norm is equivalent to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
defined as

\|u\|_{*}=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{y-1/2}^{y+1/2}|u(x)|^{2}\penalty\ dx\right)^{1/2}.

(1.30)

Introduce the translation operator
T_{y}\penalty\ :\penalty\ \widetilde{L_{ul}^{2}}\rightarrow\widetilde{L_{ul}^{2}}
,
(T_{y}u)(\cdot)\mapsto u(\cdot+y)
. The space of uniformly local
L^{2}
functions is given by

L_{ul}^{2}=\left\{u\in\widetilde{L_{ul}^{2}}\penalty\ :\penalty\ \|T_{y}u-u\|_{L_{ul}^{2}}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }y\rightarrow 0\right\}.

(1.31)

For
\vartheta\in\mathbb{N}
we define the uniformly local Sobolev space
H_{ul}^{\vartheta}
as

H_{ul}^{\vartheta}=\left\{u\in L_{ul}^{2}\penalty\ :\penalty\ D^{m}u\in L_{ul}^{2}\text{ for all }|m|<\vartheta\right\}.

(1.32)

2
Derivation of the Ginzburg-Landau equation

The conventional way of deriving the Ginzburg-Landau equation is based on making an “ansatz” for the solution. While this is sometimes presented as an educated guess based on observations, a dynamical systems viewpoint reveals that it is feasible to craft this ansatz along the spectral properties of the linearized PDE during a bifurcation. This section is, hence, structured as follows.

Plan of the section.
We first briefly discuss the origin of the band structure for periodic problems in a way that will allow to give a numerical procedure to compute it in Section
2.1
. We will complement this by another viewpoint that directly gives a characterization of coefficients that give rise to an ONB, so that (a)-(c) in Assumption
1.1
are fulfilled. Motivated by the knowledge of the shape of solutions for the linearized equation we derive the Bloch transformed, diagonalized system which explicitly features the bands
\lambda_{n}
in Section
2.2
. This then allows to tailor an elaborate ansatz in Bloch space which, in particular, yields the (Bloch transformed) Ginzburg-Landau equation and matches the claimed ansatz in Theorem
1.1
.

2.1
Linear analysis via Floquet-Bloch theory

Consider the eigenvalue problem for the linear operator arising from linearizing the Swift-Hohenberg equation (
1.1
) around
u_{*}=0
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{p}v(x)=\lambda v(x)\,,

(2.1)

with
\mathcal{L}_{p}
as in (
1.5
). It is well-known (see, e.g.
[
35
]
) that this operator is closed and self-adjoint on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and that it only has essential spectrum in the form of a countably-infinite number of closed intervals that may or may not overlap. This structure can be deduced in various ways of which we will now briefly outline two.

By Floquet theory, bounded fundamental solutions of (
2.1
) can be chosen of the form

\displaystyle v_{+}(x,\lambda)=e^{i\ell(\lambda)x}\psi(\ell(\lambda),x)\,,\quad v_{-}(x,\lambda)=e^{-i\ell(\lambda)x}\psi(-\ell(\lambda),x)\,,\quad 0\leq\ell<k_{f}/2\,,

(2.2)

where
\psi(-\ell(\lambda),x)=\overline{\psi(\ell(\lambda),x)}
and
\psi\left(\ell,x+\frac{2\pi}{k_{f}}\right)=\psi(\ell,x),x\in\mathbb{R}
. There are either none, one or two such pairs since (
2.1
) is a fourth order ODE. In other words,
\lambda\in\sigma_{\rm ess}(\mathcal{L}_{p})
if and only if there are purely imaginary Floquet exponents
\widetilde{\ell}(\lambda)=i\ell(\lambda)\in i\mathbb{R}
. Floquet theory gives explicit formulas for the Floquet exponents in terms of the canonical fundamental matrix
\Phi=\Phi(\lambda)
. Consequently, if
\Phi
is known explicitly, so is the band structure
(\ell_{m(\lambda)}(\lambda),\lambda)
with
m(\lambda)\in\{0,2,4\}
, that is, for any fixed
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
there are either
0,2
or
4
purely imaginary Floquet exponents. Numerical implementation of these relations allow to explore the possible band structure, whose infinitely many bands can be deduced from the Floquet discriminant (see, e.g.
[
35
]
).

The origin of the band structure sketched in Figure
2
can be predicted analytically by adopting the following viewpoint. Using (
2.2
) as coordinate transformation for the eigenvalue problem (
2.1
) gives

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\psi=\lambda\psi\,,\quad\psi\left(x+\frac{2\pi}{k_{f}}\right)=\psi(x)\,,

(2.3)

for

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p}:=-[(\partial_{x}+il)^{2}+k_{0}^{2}]^{2}+p\,.

(2.4)

For
p(x)=\varepsilon^{2}r,r\in\mathbb{R},
we have
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{0}:=r-[(\partial_{x}+il)^{2}+k_{0}^{2}]^{2}
for which
\psi_{n}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{k_{f}}{2\pi}}e^{\pm ik_{f}nx},n\in\mathbb{N},
and
\lambda_{n}(\ell)=-[k_{0}^{2}-(\pm k_{f}n+\ell)^{2}]^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}r
(see blue curves in Figure
5
). Noting that
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p}
can be viewed as a perturbation of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
directly gives the following (see, e.g.
[
24
]
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
p
be a continuous periodic function on
[0,2\pi/k_{f}]
. Then for any fixed
\ell
we have that
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p}
from (

) is a closed, self-adjoint linear operator on

and corresponding eigenfunctions

that form an orthonormal basis of

\lambda(-\ell)=\lambda(\ell)
and
\psi(-\ell,\cdot)=\overline{\psi(\ell,\cdot)}
. In particular, a periodic extension of
p
fulfills (a)-(c) in Assumption
1.1
.

2.1.2
Resonance phenomena

The interplay between the intrinsic wavenumber
k_{0}
and the forcing wavenumber
k_{f}
can lead to resonance phenomena. These phenomena are studied in case of small forcing in
[
7
,
10
,
8
,
11
,
13
,
9
,
12
,
5
]
. The specific choice of
k_{f}
relative to
k_{0}
can lead to different behaviors in the system. The distinction between resonant and non-resonant cases becomes evident when studying the spectrum of the linear operator. We now give several cases of resonances, although we will focus for our analytical results on the case when the forcing is sufficiently large to have led to a spectral gap between the first two spectral curves.

Range of
\bm{\gamma}
.
From numerical observations, we know that for every value of
k_{f}
, there exists a
\gamma^{\prime}\gg 1
such that for any
(\mu_{*},\gamma_{*})
with
\gamma_{*}\geq\gamma^{\prime}
the first spectral curve
\lambda_{1}(\ell)
has deformed such that the maxima are located at
\pm\ell_{0}=\pm\frac{k_{f}}{2}
.
Due to the periodicity of the spectrum, this means that
\ell_{0}=3\ell_{0}\pmod{k_{f}}
. Hence in the presence of for sufficiently large forcing, any wavenumber
k_{f}
can be resonant.

No spectral gap.
In case of small forcing,
\gamma=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
, there are ranges of wavenumbers
k_{f}
for which there is no spectral gap at onset between
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
. In this case, an elaborate examination of the maximum of
\lambda_{2}
is required. Denote the location of the maximum of
\lambda_{2}(\ell)
by
\ell_{1}
. If
\lambda_{2}(\ell_{1})=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
, both
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
can cross the zero axis after bifurcation onset, yielding two instabilities directly after each other. The ansatz would have to also account for this second instability. On the other hand, if
\lambda_{2}(\ell_{1})=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
, only
\lambda_{1}
crosses zero when increasing
\mu
to
\mu_{*}+r\varepsilon^{2}
, hence an approximation can be derived using the analogous ansatz. However, in addition to the violation of Assumption
1.1
(f), Assumption
1.1
(g) might not be satisfied in this case. We leave it to later work to explore the nonlinear stability for these cases.

Large wavenumber forcing.
Even though a priori we did not put assumptions on the magnitude of
k_{f}
, we do exclude the cases
k_{f}\gg 1
. From a spectral point of view,
k_{f}\gg 1
means that the period of the spectrum becomes large, meaning that the intersections of the first two spectral curves occur at very low values of
\lambda_{1}(\ell)
. As a consequence, an extremely large value of
\gamma
is needed to see changes in the spectrum and to destabilize the background state. The case
k_{f}\gg 1
is therefore part of future research.

Resonant wavenumbers.
In case of small forcing
\gamma=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
, resonance phenomena can occur for specific values of
k_{f}

[
7
,
10
,
8
,
11
,
13
,
9
,
12
,
5
]
. These resonant wavenumbers are
k_{f}\approx\frac{2k_{0}}{N}
for
N>0

[
5
]
. In the spectral picture, this means that, due to the
k_{f}
-periodicity, the artificial curves for
\gamma=0
intersect at the maxima. For
N
even, the first spectral curve has its maximum at
\ell_{0}=0
, while for
N
odd, the spectral pictures show that
\ell_{0}=\frac{k_{f}}{2}
.
In case of small forcing, there is no spectral gap between
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
.
For
\gamma=\mathcal{O}(1)
, the curves have deformed in such a way that there is a spectral gap, but
\ell_{0}
does not need to change. Thus
k_{f}=2k_{0}/N
can still be a resonant case (Definition
1.1
) for large forcing.
A Ginzburg-Landau equation has been derived using the classical ansatz (
1.4
) for the case that
k_{f}=k_{0}
and
\gamma=\varepsilon
in
[
9
]
. For general systems, Ginzburg-Landau equations are derived for
\gamma=\mathcal{\varepsilon}
for both
N
even and odd in
[
5
]
.

2.2
Formal derivation of the GL equation in Bloch space

As alluded to, the classical ansatz as used in most works, including
[
38
,
34
,
7
]
, does not work due to the size of the forcing. Instead, we use the Bloch transform to derive an ansatz. For the remainder of this section we will assume that Assumption
1.1
holds true. Applying the Bloch transform (
1.25
) to the Swift-Hohenberg system gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}(\ell,x,t)=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\widetilde{u}(\ell,x,t)-\rho(x)(\widetilde{u}(\ell,x,t))^{\star^{3}}\,,

(2.6)

where the operation
\star
is the Bloch convolution and
(.)^{\star^{m}}
denotes the
(m-1)
-times iteration of the convolution. Using the representation

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}(\ell,x,t)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\widetilde{A}_{n}(\ell,t)\psi_{n}(\ell,x)\,,

(2.7)

the linear part of (
2.6
) further simplifies (while the nonlinear terms become more intricate) to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widetilde{A}_{n}(\ell,t)=\lambda_{n}(\ell)\widetilde{A}_{n}(\ell,t)-\left\langle\rho(x)\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\widetilde{A}_{j}\psi_{j}\right)^{\star^{3}}(\ell,t,\cdot),\penalty\ \psi_{n}(\ell,\cdot)\right\rangle\,,

(2.8)

which we abstractly write as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(\ell,t)=\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}(\ell)\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(\ell,t)+\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}(\ell)(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}(\ell,t))

(2.9)

for
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}=\left(\widetilde{A}_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. In the following we will craft an ansatz that solves this system “well enough” to allow a leading order error bound on long time scales. As a measure for this we introduce the residual

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{Res}}\{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\}:=-\partial_{t}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}+\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}(\ell)\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}+\widetilde{\mathbf{N}}(\ell)(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}})\,.

(2.10)

The overall structure of the ansatz is

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{ansatz}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\widetilde{a}_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}\widetilde{B}_{1}\\
\left(\varepsilon^{2}\widetilde{B}_{n}\right)_{n>1}\end{array}\right)\,,

where
\widetilde{a}_{1}
is the “core ansatz” and
\widetilde{B}_{n}
serve to improve the accuracy beyond this “core ansatz” accounting for nonlinear interactions between different branches of the band structure. The choice of
\varepsilon
-scaling will become clear from the analysis of the nonlinear terms. In order to further refine the ansatz we recall that the Assumptions
1.1
demand that
\lambda_{n}(\ell)<0
for
n>1
and
\lambda_{1}(\pm\ell_{0})=0,\penalty\ \partial_{\ell}\lambda_{1}(\pm\ell_{0})=0
, so Taylor expanding
\lambda_{1}
around
\pm\ell_{0}
results in

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\ell)=\varepsilon^{2}\left[r+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\ell}^{2}\lambda_{1}(\pm\ell_{0})L_{\pm}^{2}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})\,,

(2.13)

with
L_{\pm}=\frac{\ell-(\pm\ell_{0})}{\varepsilon}
. Hence, in order to capture the dynamics of linearly unstable wavenumbers beyond onset, we set

\displaystyle\widetilde{a}_{1}(\ell,t)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{A}_{1,+}\left(\frac{\ell-\ell_{0}}{\varepsilon},\penalty\ \varepsilon^{2}t\right)+\widetilde{A}_{1,-}\left(\frac{\ell+\ell_{0}}{\varepsilon},\penalty\ \varepsilon^{2}t\right)\,,

(2.14)

for which we note that
\widetilde{A}_{1,+}
is centered around
\ell_{0}
, while
\widetilde{A}_{1,-}
is centered around
-\ell_{0}
(see Figure

3
Justification of the modulation equations

To justify the Ginzburg-Landau approximation, it is required to prove error estimate (
1.10
). To that end, we use the function space
H_{ul}^{\vartheta}
as introduced in Section
1.5.2
. In this function space, we give a rigorous estimate of the magnitude of the residual. We show that the semigroup generated by the linear operator is strongly continuous, such that the variation of constant formula can be applied. The remaining terms in the error equation have to be bounded, such that applying Grönwalls theorem gives the desired estimate. Throughout this section many different constants that are independent of
\varepsilon
are denoted by C.

To give an estimate on how close the approximation
u_{\rm Ans}
is to the real solution, we have to estimate the error

\varepsilon^{\beta}S(x,t)=u(x,t)-u_{\rm Ans}(x,t).

(3.1)

The error
S
satisfies the PDE

\begin{split}\partial_{t}S=&-(k_{0}^{2}+\partial_{x}^{2})^{2}S+(p_{*}+\varepsilon^{2}r)S-\varepsilon^{2\beta}\rho\penalty\ S^{3}\\
&-3\varepsilon^{\beta}\rho S^{2}u_{\rm Ans}-3\rho Su_{\rm Ans}^{2}-\varepsilon^{-\beta}\text{Res}(u_{\rm Ans}).\end{split}

(3.2)

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
, we want to bound all the terms in this equation. Introduce

F(S):=\varepsilon^{2}rS-\varepsilon^{2\beta}\rho S^{3}-3\varepsilon^{\beta}\rho S^{2}u_{\rm Ans}-3\rho Su_{\rm Ans}^{2}-\varepsilon^{-\beta}\penalty\ \text{Res}(u_{\rm Ans}).

(3.3)

Then the error equation is given by

\partial_{t}S=\mathcal{L}_{p_{*}}S+F(S).

(3.4)

By standard arguments it can be shown that
\mathcal{L}_{p_{*}}
generates a strongly continuous semigroup of uniformly bounded linear operators. We can split
\mathcal{L}_{p_{*}}
into
\mathcal{L}_{p_{*}}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm SH}+B
with
\mathcal{L}_{\rm SH}=-(k_{0}^{2}+\partial_{x}^{2})^{2}
and
B=p_{*}(x)
. Then we know that
\mathcal{L}_{\rm SH}
generates a strongly continuous semigroup. As
B
is a bounded perturbation, it follows from
[
41
, Theorem 1.3]
that
\mathcal{L}_{p_{*}}
generates a strongly continuous semigroup.

In order to give a semigroup bound we consider the semigroup in Bloch space, generated by
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p_{*}}
, and examine its spectrum. Assumption
1.1
(f) allows a separation of the diffusive mode from the exponentially damped modes. Taking the Bloch transform of equation (
3.4
) yields

\partial_{t}\widetilde{S}=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p_{*}}\widetilde{S}+\widetilde{F}(\widetilde{S}).

(3.5)

Similarly to
[
42
]
, as
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p_{*}}
is self-adjoint, we define the orthogonal projection on the diffusive and the exponentially damped modes by

\widetilde{P}_{c}(\ell)\widetilde{S}(\ell)=\left\langle\psi_{1}(\ell),\widetilde{S}(\ell)\right\rangle\psi_{1}(\ell),

(3.6)

\widetilde{P}_{s}(\ell)\widetilde{S}(\ell)=\widetilde{S}(\ell)-\widetilde{P}_{c}(\ell)\widetilde{S}(\ell).

(3.7)

This separates (
3.5
) into

\partial_{t}\widetilde{S}_{q}(\ell,x,t)=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p_{*},q}(\ell)\widetilde{S}_{q}(\ell,x,t)+\widetilde{P}_{q}(\ell)\widetilde{F}(\widetilde{S})(\ell,x,t),\quad\text{ for }q=c,s,

(3.8)

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p_{*},q}(\ell)=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p_{*}}(\ell)\widetilde{P}_{q}(\ell)
. As the Bloch transform is an isomorphism between
H_{ul}^{\vartheta}
and

L^{2}_{ul}([-\pi/\alpha,\pi/\alpha),H^{\vartheta}_{ul}((0,\alpha],\mathbb{C}))
, we consider the space
L^{2}_{ul}([-\pi/\alpha,\pi/\alpha),H^{\vartheta}_{ul}((0,\alpha],\mathbb{C}))
. Note that the operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p_{*},s}(\ell)
is sectorial and has spectrum in the left half plane, bounded away from the imaginary axis. Hence the following estimate follows
[
43
, Theorem 1.5.3]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

(Estimate of the sectorial part)

For the analytic semigroup generated by
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p_{*},s}
we have

\|e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p_{*},s}t}\|_{L_{ul}^{2}}\leq Ce^{\sigma t/2}\leq C,

(3.9)

for some
\sigma>0
,
C>0
and all
t\geq 0
.

For the diffusive mode we find the following estimate.

Lemma 3.2
.

(Estimate of them diffusive part)

For the semigroup generated by
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p_{*},c}
we have

\sup_{t\in[0,\penalty\ T_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}]}\|e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p_{*},c}t}\|_{L_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq C,

(3.10)

for some
C>0
and all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.
Note that

\|e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p_{*},c}t}\widetilde{S}_{c}\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}=\|e^{\lambda_{1}(\ell)t}\widetilde{S}_{c}\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq\|e^{\lambda_{1}(\ell)t}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\widetilde{S}_{c}\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}.

(3.11)

Furthermore, note that
\lambda_{1}(\ell)<-\sigma_{0}
for some
\sigma_{0}>0
and
\ell\notin I^{-}\cup I^{+}
. For
\ell\in I^{-}\cup I^{+}
we can use
\lambda_{1}(\ell)\leq-C(\ell\pm\ell_{0})^{2}=-C(\varepsilon L_{\pm})^{2}
for some
C>0
(Assumption
1.1
(e)).
Since
\lambda_{1}(\ell)\approx-C(\varepsilon L)^{2}
for small
L
, the term
e^{\lambda_{1}(\ell)t}
behaves as
e^{-L^{2}T}
with
T=\varepsilon^{2}t
so its
C_{b}^{2}
-norm is uniformly bounded for
t
on an
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon^{2})
timescale. Thus for
t\geq 0
we find

\sup_{t\in[0,\penalty\ T_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}]}\|e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p_{*},c}t}\widetilde{S}_{c}\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq\sup_{t\in[0,\penalty\ T_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}]}\|e^{\lambda_{1}(\ell)t}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\widetilde{S}_{c}\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq C\|\widetilde{S}_{c}\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}.

(3.12)

∎

Together, this yields the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

(Semigroup estimate)

For every
\vartheta>1/2
there exists a
C>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
the semigroup
(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{p_{*}}})_{t\geq 0}
generated by
\mathcal{L}_{p_{*}}
satisfies

\sup_{t\in[0,\penalty\ T_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}]}\|e^{t\mathcal{L}_{p_{*}}}\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}\rightarrow H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq C.

(3.13)

Proof.

Let
Z(t):H^{\vartheta}_{ul}\rightarrow H^{\vartheta}_{ul}
be the semigroup generated by
\mathcal{L}_{p_{*}}
. This semigroup is defined by
Z(t)=\mathcal{B}\widetilde{Z}(t)\mathcal{B}^{-1}
, where
\widetilde{Z}(t)=e^{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p_{*}}t}
for
t\geq 0
.

Applying Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
yields the estimate

\begin{split}\sup_{t\in[0,\penalty\ T_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}]}\|Z(t)u\|_{H^{\vartheta}_{ul}}\leq&\sup_{t\in[0,\penalty\ T_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}]}C\|\widetilde{Z}(t)\widetilde{u}\|_{L^{2}_{ul}}\\
\leq&\sup_{t\in[0,\penalty\ T_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}]}C\|\widetilde{u}\|_{L_{ul}^{2}}\leq\sup_{t\in[0,\penalty\ T_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}]}C\|u\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}.\end{split}

(3.14)

∎

Hence the variations of constant formula is applicable
[
44
]
and we find

S(t)=e^{t\mathcal{L}_{p_{*}}}S(0)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-\tau)\mathcal{L}_{p_{*}}}F(S)(\tau)\penalty\ d\tau.

(3.15)

To bound
S(t)
, we bound the different terms in
F
. For that we in particular need an estimate on the residual. We will formulate our results in the following for
u_{\rm Ans}
built on
u_{\rm GL}
, but the analogous holds true for
u_{\rm AC}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

(Residual estimate)

Let
\vartheta\geq 1
and let
A\in C([0,T_{0}],H_{ul}^{\vartheta_{A}})
with
\vartheta_{A}=3+\vartheta
be a solution of the Ginzburg-Landau equation (
1.12
) for
k_{f}
non-resonant, or of (
1.15
) for
k_{f}
resonant. Then for all
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1]
, there exists a
C>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
we have

\sup_{t\in[0,\penalty\ T_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}]}\|{\rm Res}(u_{\rm Ans}(\cdot,t))\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq C\varepsilon^{4},

(3.16)

and

\sup_{t\in[0,\penalty\ T_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}]}\|u_{\rm Ans}(\cdot,t)-u_{\rm GL}(\cdot,t)\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}.

(3.17)

Proof.

Following
[
39
]
, Lemma 3.3 and Lemma 4.3 allows to bound the residual (
2.10
). Furthermore, tracking the estimates for the various transformations using the reasoning in
[
40
, Section 5]
allows to port the estimate to the residual of the original equation (
3.2
). In particular, note that the Bloch transform is an isomorphism between
H_{ul}^{\vartheta}
and
L^{2}_{ul}([-k_{f}/2,k_{f}/2),H^{\vartheta}_{ul}((0,2\pi/k_{f}],\mathbb{C}))

[
45
,
46
]
and that the diagonalization operator formalizing the expansion w.r.t. the ONB
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded
[
39
, Lemma 4.3]
. Finally, the estimate between
u_{\rm Ans}
and
u_{\rm GL}
can be deduced from the expansion of the eigenfunction
\psi_{1}
, as given in (
2.23
).
∎

The remaining terms in (
3.3
) can be estimated as follows.

Lemma 3.5
.

(Estimate of
\bm{F}
)

Let
\vartheta\geq 1
,
\vartheta_{A}=\vartheta+3
and
A\in C([0,T_{0}],H_{ul}^{\vartheta_{A}})
be a solution of the Ginzburg-Landau equation (
1.12
) for
k_{f}
non-resonant, or of (
1.15
) for
k_{f}
resonant. Then there is a
C>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
we have

\|F\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq C(\varepsilon^{2}\|S\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}+\varepsilon^{2\beta}\|S\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}^{3}+\varepsilon^{\beta+1}\|S\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}).

(3.18)

Proof.
First note that we can bound

\|\rho S\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq C\|\rho\|_{C_{b}^{\vartheta}}\|S\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq C\|S\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}},

(3.19)

since
\rho
is continuous and periodic. The different terms in
F
can be bounded by

\begin{split}\|\rho S^{3}\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq&\penalty\ C\|S\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}^{3},\\
\|\rho\penalty\ u_{\rm Ans}S^{2}\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq&\penalty\ C\|u_{\rm Ans}\|_{C_{b}^{\vartheta}}\|S\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}^{2}\\
\|\rho\penalty\ u_{\rm Ans}^{2}S\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq&\penalty\ C\|u_{\rm Ans}\|_{C_{b}^{\vartheta}}^{2}\|S\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}},\\
\|\varepsilon^{-\beta}\text{Res}(u_{\rm Ans})\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq&\penalty\ C\varepsilon^{4-\beta}\leq C\varepsilon^{2},\text{ for $\beta$ chosen such that $\beta\leq 2$}.\end{split}

(3.20)

The last estimate follows from Lemma
3.4
. We moreover have

\|u_{\rm Ans}\|_{C_{b}^{\vartheta}}=\|x\mapsto\varepsilon(A_{1}(\varepsilon x)\psi_{1}(\ell_{0},x)e^{i\ell_{0}x}+\rm{c.c.}+\rm{h.o.t.})\|_{C_{b}^{\vartheta}}\leq C\varepsilon\|A_{1}\|_{C_{b}^{\vartheta}}.

(3.21)

From Sobolev’s embedding theorem we have

\|A\|_{C_{b}^{\vartheta}}\leq C\|A\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta+1}},

(3.22)

which concludes the proof.∎

We now have all the ingredients to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.
We will bound the mild solutions of the equations for the error
S
in (
3.15
). Local existence and uniqueness of solutions in
H_{ul}^{\vartheta}
for
\vartheta>\frac{1}{2}
now follows from Lemma
3.3
together with
[
34
, Theorem 5.2.22]
. We have to ensure the norm of the solution stays finite for
t\in[0,\penalty\ T_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}]
. Using the estimates from Lemma
3.3
and
3.5
together with equation (
3.15
) we obtain

\begin{split}\|S(t)\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}&\leq\|e^{t\mathcal{L}_{p_{*}}}S(0)\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}+\\
&C\varepsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{(t-\tau)\mathcal{L}_{p_{*}}}\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\left(\|S(\tau)\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}+\varepsilon^{2\beta-2}\|S(\tau)\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}^{3}+\varepsilon^{\beta-1}\|S(\tau)\|^{2}_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}+1\right)\penalty\ d\tau\\
\leq&C\varepsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\|S(\tau)\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}+\varepsilon^{2\beta-2}\|S(\tau)\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}^{3}+\varepsilon^{\beta-1}\|S(\tau)\|^{2}_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}+1\right)\penalty\ d\tau.\end{split}

(3.23)

Imposing

\varepsilon^{2\beta-2}\|S(\tau)\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}^{3}+\varepsilon^{\beta-1}\|S(\tau)\|^{2}_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq 1,

(3.24)

yields

\|S(t)\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{t}(\|S(\tau)\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}+2)\penalty\ d\tau\leq 2CT_{0}+C\varepsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|S(\tau)\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\penalty\ d\tau.

(3.25)

Applying Gronwall’s inequality for integrals gives

\|S(t)\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq 2CT_{0}e^{C\varepsilon^{2}t}\leq 2CT_{0}e^{CT_{0}}=:M

(3.26)

for all
t\in[0,\penalty\ T_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}]
. In order to fulfill requirement (
3.24
), we choose
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
\varepsilon_{0}^{2\beta-2}M^{3}+\varepsilon_{0}^{\beta-1}M^{2}\leq 1
. As we want
\beta
to be as large as possible, but
\beta\leq 2
(Lemma
3.5
), we take
\beta=2
. This gives the estimate

\sup_{t\in[0,\penalty\ T_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}]}\|u(t)-u_{\rm Ans}(t)\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}.

(3.27)

The triangle inequality now gives

\begin{split}\sup_{t\in[0,\penalty\ T_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}]}&\|u(t)-u_{\rm GL}(t)\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\\
&\leq\sup_{t\in[0,\penalty\ T_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}]}\|u(t)-u_{\rm Ans}(t)\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}+\sup_{t\in[0,\penalty\ T_{0}/\varepsilon^{2}]}\|u_{\rm Ans}(t)-u_{\rm GL}(t)\|_{H_{ul}^{\vartheta}}\\
&\leq C\varepsilon^{2}+C\varepsilon^{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\end{split}

(3.28)

This proves the approximation theorem (Theorem
1.1
) with function space
Y=H_{ul}^{\vartheta}
and
A_{1}\in C([0,T_{0}],H_{ul}^{\vartheta_{A}})
,
\vartheta_{A}=\vartheta+3
and
\vartheta\geq 1
. ∎

4
Related work

There is a wealth of literature on the derivation of modulation equations for many different systems. While formal derivations have a long tradition in the physics and beyond, a rigorous treatment adding validity proofs with error estimates started in the early 1990s (e.g.
[
28
]
).

We refrain from citing all different systems for which the Ginzburg-Landau equation arises (see, e.g.
[
34
]
for a thorough treatment of the subject). Staying in the context of Swift-Hohenberg equations, we direct attention to the following: In addition to modifying the linear part of the equation by applying a forcing term, non-local effects can be incorporated into other parts of the equation, leading to different alterations in the derived amplitude equation. An amplitude equation for the Swift-Hohenberg system with fractional Laplacian has been derived and justified in
[
47
]

[

1.4

In addition to the Ginzburg-Landau equation, other types of modulation equations can be derived for e.g. dispersive systems with periodic coefficients. The Nonlinear Schrödinger Equation has been derived and justified for a
\phi^{4}
-model with spatially periodic coefficients in
[
39

. Some of the ideas from that work are applied here.
Heterogeneous media can also influence pulse propagation, depending on the size of the period of the heterogeneity, as shown in
[
49
]
. Modulation equations for spatially periodic wavetrains in nonlinear partial differential equations are derived and analysed in
[
50
]
.

This work is partially motivated by the study of dryland ecosystems, where vegetation patterns arise due to scarcity of water. In many dryland ecosystem models, such as the Klausmeier model
[
51
]
, it is assumed that the underlying terrain is flat. However, aerial images show that stripe patterns are prevalent on slopes, while hexagonal pattern structures are most commonly found on flat ground. Furthermore, numerical studies show differences in the movement of vegetation patches on slopes compared to flat terrain. These observations underpin the importance of topography to the possible vegetation patterns. Incorporating topography into a vegetation model results in a system with spatially varying coefficients. Keeping this application in mind, one can think of the periodic forcing in (
1.1
) as a periodic topography.

4.1
Modulation equations for small forcing

As alluded to, the Ginzburg-Landau equation and variants thereof have already been derived for systems with spatially periodic forcing. However, to the best of our knowledge, these derivations have been limited to systems with small forcing strength. Furthermore, the validity of the derived Ginzburg-Landau equation in systems with spatial forcing has not been established for many of such systems. Ginzburg-Landau equations are derived (but not validated) for (

5
Discussion and Outlook

We have proven that, even in the presence of large forcing, solutions of a periodically forced Swift-Hohenberg system can be approximated on long finite timescale by solutions of a Ginzburg-Landau equation. Compared to the constant coefficient or small forcing case, the multiple scaling ansatz must be adjusted to a Bloch wave structure. At leading order, this approximation depends on the first Bloch eigenfunction, and the coefficients of the Ginzburg-Landau equation therein depend on the shape of the first spectral curve. We anticipate many different extensions of the present work.

5.1
Resonances without spectral gap

Throughout this work, we assume that the spectrum satisfies Assumption
1.1
. As explained Section
2.1.2
, further research is required to derive and justify the Ginzburg-Landau equations in case there is no spectral gap between the first two spectral curves (Assumption
1.1
(f)), which often occurs for resonant wavenumbers.
Other potential extensions of this work are considering
k_{f}\gg 1
, or using
\widetilde{p}
to create the bifurcation (see Remark
1.3
). While the latter primarily increases the complexity of the derivation, we do not expect it to introduce new phenomena.

5.5
Pattern formation in reaction-diffusion systems

We have studied the Swift-Hohenberg equation as a paradigm model for pattern formation. Since the applicability of the obtained results to real-world systems is an important consideration, ongoing research addresses this by applying the techniques in this paper to vegetation models with a spatially periodic terrain. To this end, we use the one-dimensional extended Klausmeier model
[
57
]
, given by

\begin{split}\partial_{t}U(x,t)&=D\partial_{x}^{2}U(x,t)+h^{\prime}(x)\partial_{x}U(x,t)+h^{\prime\prime}(x)U(x,t)+a-U(x,t)-U(x,t)V(x,t)^{2},\\
\partial_{t}V(x,t)&=\partial_{x}^{2}V(x,t)-mV(x,t)+U(x,t)V(x,t)^{2},\end{split}
