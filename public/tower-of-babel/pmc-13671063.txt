Hepatic steatosis and postprandial hyperglucagonemia in early type 2 diabetes: Revisiting the liver–α‐cell axis
Authors: Yu‐Ning Kuo, Jun‐Sing Wang
Published: 2026

Glucagon dysregulation is a well‐recognized feature of type 2 diabetes, contributing to both fasting and postprandial hyperglycemia. However, its relationship with hepatic steatosis, particularly during the early stages of type 2 diabetes, remains incompletely understood. Given the role of glucagon in systemic and hepatic energy metabolism and the emergence of glucagon‐based pharmacotherapies, increasing attention has focused on the interplay between glucagon regulation and hepatic lipid metabolism in type 2 diabetes.

The study “Increased Early Postprandial Glucagon Concentrations in Humans with Newly Diagnosed Type 2 Diabetes and Steatotic Liver Disease” 1 examined the association between postprandial glucagon and hepatic lipid content (HLC) and whether amino acids or nonesterified fatty acids (NEFAs) contribute to this relationship. Compared with individuals with normal glucose tolerance (NGT), those with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes had ~65% higher HLC, ~30% higher fasting glucagon, and ~ 75% higher postprandial glucagon levels. Higher fasting glucagon was associated with metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) rather than type 2 diabetes itself. More intriguingly, higher HLC was associated with greater early postprandial glucagon responses in individuals with type 2 diabetes, whereas no corresponding relationship was observed in those with NGT. This association remained evident after accounting for insulin sensitivity and visceral adipose tissue volume and could not be explained by amino acids or NEFAs. Thus, hepatic lipid accumulation appears to identify a distinct phenotype of postprandial glucagon dysregulation already present at the onset of type 2 diabetes. 1

A major strength of this study is its comprehensive deep phenotyping using gold‐standard methodology, including a mixed‐meal tolerance test (MMTT), hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp with stable isotope dilution, magnetic resonance spectroscopy/imaging, and indirect calorimetry. This integrated approach enabled detailed assessment of the relationships among postprandial glucagon responses, HLC, visceral adiposity, insulin sensitivity, substrate oxidation, and hepatic energy metabolism. Such deep phenotyping is particularly important because the relationship between glucagon and hepatic metabolism cannot be adequately characterized by fasting measurements alone. Assessment of dynamic glucagon responses following a mixed meal provides a physiologically relevant perspective on the liver–α‐cell axis.

Notably, the study extended the glucagon–alanine index, previously proposed as a surrogate marker of hepatic glucagon resistance, to the postprandial state. 2 Although the fasting glucagon–alanine index was increased in newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes and positively related to HLC, the postprandial glucagon–alanine index was not associated with HLC. 1 This discrepancy suggests that the mechanisms underlying fasting and postprandial glucagon dysregulation may not be identical. The marked early postprandial hyperglucagonemia observed in newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes also extends previous evidence of glucagon dysregulation during MMTT in long‐standing type 2 diabetes and during oral glucose tolerance testing in recent‐onset diabetes or prediabetes. 3

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain glucagon dysregulation, including impaired intraislet signaling, reduced insulin‐mediated suppression of α‐cells, and increased α‐cell responsiveness to glucagonotropic metabolites. The present findings, however, challenge a simple metabolite‐mediated explanation. Neither amino acids nor NEFAs adequately explained the specific association between HLC and postprandial hyperglucagonemia, and mediation analysis did not support alanine as the causal link between hepatic steatosis and hyperglucagonemia. Medication exposure should also be considered because insulin, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonists, sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, and dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors can influence circulating glucagon concentrations and hepatic lipid metabolism. Although some participants with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes had already received glucose‐lowering treatment, sensitivity analyses excluding treated participants yielded consistent findings, making medication exposure an unlikely explanation for the principal observations.

The paradoxical coexistence of hepatic steatosis and hyperglucagonemia raises the possibility of altered hepatic glucagon action. Hepatic glucagon resistance has been proposed to impair amino acid clearance and lipid oxidation, leading to hyperaminoacidemia and compensatory α‐cell stimulation, while glucagon‐stimulated hepatic glucose production may remain relatively preserved. Such selective glucagon resistance could potentially aggravate both hyperglucagonemia and hyperglycemia. Nevertheless, the present findings do not establish hepatic glucagon resistance. The lack of an association between the postprandial glucagon–alanine index and HLC, together with the absence of mediation by amino acids, suggests that the conventional alanine‐mediated liver–α‐cell axis is insufficient to explain early postprandial glucagon dysregulation.

This interpretation is reinforced by another apparent paradox: Glucagon is expected to promote hepatic lipid oxidation and reduce lipid accumulation, yet greater HLC coexisted with higher postprandial glucagon concentrations in early type 2 diabetes. Rather than simply indicating global glucagon resistance, this discordance may reflect selective alterations in hepatic glucagon signaling, broader hepatic metabolic dysfunction, or other signals linking the steatotic liver to α‐cell function. The observation that MASLD, rather than type 2 diabetes itself, was associated with fasting hyperglucagonemia further implicates hepatic steatosis in disturbed liver–α‐cell communication.

These observations have potentially important clinical implications. Hepatic steatosis accompanied by exaggerated postprandial glucagon secretion may represent a metabolically distinct phenotype of early type 2 diabetes in which abnormalities in liver–α‐cell communication are already present near diagnosis. Characterizing this phenotype may ultimately improve metabolic risk stratification beyond conventional measures of glycemia and insulin resistance. Moreover, altered hepatic glucagon signaling may influence responses to emerging glucagon‐based therapies. This is particularly relevant to glucagon receptor agonism and GLP‐1–glucagon multiagonists, in which glucagon activity is leveraged to increase energy expenditure and hepatic lipid oxidation. Whether individuals with greater hepatic steatosis or postprandial hyperglucagonemia exhibit differential metabolic or hepatic responses to these therapies remains unknown.

Several directions for future research emerge from these findings. First, the cross‐sectional observational design precludes determination of causality or temporal direction. It remains unclear whether hepatic steatosis drives hyperglucagonemia, glucagon dysregulation contributes to hepatic metabolic abnormalities, or both reflect a shared underlying mechanism. Longitudinal studies are therefore needed to clarify the directionality of this interaction and determine whether postprandial hyperglucagonemia predicts MASLD progression, glycemic deterioration, or response to glucagon‐based therapies. The absence of mediation by amino acids or NEFAs also raises the possibility that unidentified liver‐derived metabolites or signaling pathways link hepatic steatosis to α‐cell dysfunction. Multiomics and mechanistic studies may help identify these signals and redefine the molecular basis of the liver–α‐cell axis. Second, differences in MMTT composition and participant adiposity across previous studies may partly contribute to inconsistent findings. 4 , 5 Standardization of mixed‐meal protocols and careful consideration of body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, and body fat distribution should therefore be incorporated into future studies. Third, the highly selected and predominantly European German Diabetes Study cohort may limit generalizability across the heterogeneous spectrum of MASLD. Future studies should determine whether similar glucagon–liver relationships are present in individuals with long‐standing type 2 diabetes, advanced MASLD or substantial hepatic fibrosis, and ethnically diverse populations, particularly Asian populations. Country‐specific prescription patterns may also have contributed to the relatively early initiation of insulin therapy after type 2 diabetes diagnosis in this cohort. Studies across different health care settings and treatment paradigms will therefore be important to determine whether glucose‐lowering therapies modify the observed glucagon–MASLD phenotype.

Ultimately, the central message extends beyond whether hepatic glucagon resistance is present. The study reveals that liver–α‐cell communication is already disturbed at the earliest stages of type 2 diabetes, while the conventional alanine‐mediated paradigm does not fully account for this disturbance. Whether this phenotype reflects selective impairment of hepatic glucagon action, broader hepatic metabolic dysfunction, or previously unrecognized liver‐derived signals remains to be determined. Resolving this paradox may not only refine our understanding of the liver–α‐cell axis in early type 2 diabetes and MASLD but also help identify patients most likely to benefit from emerging glucagon‐based therapies (Figure 1).

In individuals with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes, greater hepatic lipid content (HLC) is associated with increased early postprandial glucagon secretion, suggesting an altered axis between the liver and pancreatic α‐cells. This association was not explained by amino acids, including alanine, or nonesterified fatty acids (NEFAs), raising the possibility that unidentified liver‐derived metabolites or signaling pathways contribute to α‐cell dysregulation. Further mechanistic and longitudinal studies are needed to identify these signals and clarify the directionality of the liver–α‐cell interaction.
