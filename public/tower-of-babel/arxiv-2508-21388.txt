Out-of-time ordered correlation functions for the localized f f electrons in the Falicov-Kimball model
A. M. Shvaika, J. K. Freericks

Abstract
We provide an exact evaluation of the out-of-time correlation (OTOC) functions for the localized f f -particle states in the Falicov-Kimball model within dynamical mean-field theory. Different regimes of quantum chaos and quantum scrambling are distinguished by the winding numbers of the block Toeplitz matrices used in the calculation. The similarities of these fermionic OTOCs and their Lyapunov exponents for time evolution with the OTOCs for quantum spin models with disorder are also discussed.



I
Introduction

Long-time coherent quantum many-body dynamics is important for condensed matter physics, quantum information science, optical physics, and high-energy physics
Xu and Swingle 2024
;
García-Mata et al. 2023
. First, it is connected with the spreading of quantum information, its detection, and decoding. These dynamics can be examined in a system of qubits whose Hamiltonian governs unitary dynamics where the initial state of one qubit becomes nonlocal and is distributed across a system by the unitary dynamics—the so-called quantum information scrambling phenomenon. The rate with which information becomes nonlocal is usually quantified by the out-of-time-ordered correlators
Hayden and Preskill 2007
;
Roberts and Stanford 2015

F(r,t)=\left\langle[W_{0}(t),V_{r}(0)]^{\dagger}[W_{0}(t),V_{r}(0)]\right\rangle,

(1)

where
W_{0}
and
V_{r}
are local operators at positions 0 and
r
.

Out-of-time-ordered correlators (OTOC) were proposed by Larkin and Ovchinnikov
Larkin and Ovchinnikov 1968
;
*larkin_1969:1200
as a measure of importance of quantum motion with respect to the quasiclassical approximation in superconductivity. Later, OTOCs were used to describe the evolution of the overlap of perturbed and unperturbed states as indicators of the quantum motion stability in chaotic and regular systems
Peres 1984
.

For chaotic systems, the initially local operator
W_{0}(t)
expands, under Heisenberg dynamics, with a speed called the butterfly velocity and overlaps later with the operator
V_{r}
resulting from the growth of the OTOC function inside the emergent light-cone in space-time. The growth rate of the OTOC is influenced by the interplay between quantum fluctuations and local scrambling, and depends on the size of the system and on its temperature. For thermal quantum systems with a large number of degrees of freedom, the exponential growth rate is characterized by the Lyapunov exponent bounded by the temperature
\lambda_{L}\leq 2\pi k_{B}T/\hbar
—this bound is a bound on the growth rate of chaos
Maldacena et al. 2016
;
Xu and Swingle 2019
.

In order to estimate the growth rate of an OTOC, in particular, for exponential growth, it is convenient to consider the logarithmic derivative of the OTOC
Lin and Motrunich 2018
;
Riddell and Sørensen 2019
;
Xu and Swingle 2020
;
Buividovich 2022

L(r,t)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln F(r,t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{1}{F(r,t)}\frac{\mathrm{d}F(r,t)}{\mathrm{d}t},

(2)

which is equal to the Lyapunov exponent (obeying the chaos bound for “fast scramblers”) and can provide detailed behavior of the OTOC at its wavefront. Relaxation dynamics in many-body systems can be characterized by different time scales
\tau
, each with their own temperature and wave-vector dependencies, which affect the scrambling, and result in an anomalous behavior for the Lyapunov exponents
Kim et al. 2020
.

For quantum information scrambling, one is interested in the OTOCs of the local two-state operators—that is, for the qubits. As a rule, spin models with a direct interaction between the spins are used to describe their unitary dynamics, see e.g.
Xu and Swingle 2024
and references therein. On the other hand, in real systems, the qubits are strongly localized and their dynamics, in many cases, are governed by the indirect interaction through other degrees of freedom, which often become retarded and long range.

One of the simplest models with two-level states interacting through (nonlocal) band electrons is the Falicov-Kimball model (FKM)
Falicov and Kimball 1969
, which considers localized
f
-states which locally interact with itinerant
d
-electrons. It was shown by Brandt and Schmidt
Brandt and Schmidt 1986
and Kennedy and Lieb
Kennedy and Lieb 1986
that the ground state phase diagram of the FKM can be obtained from the Ising-like (lattice gas) model with an effective interaction between classical spins (occupation of
f
states) after integrating out the fast
d
-electrons, and the cases of 1D and 2D lattices have been examined in detail
Kennedy 1994
;
Gruber et al. 1997
;
Datta et al. 1999
;
Wojtkiewicz and Lemański 2001
.

In addition, the FKM possesses exact solutions for infinite dimensional lattices
Brandt and Mielsch 1989
;
Freericks and Zlatić 2003
within the dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT)
Metzner and Vollhardt 1989
;
Metzner 1991
;
Georges et al. 1996
. Despite the
f
-electron occupation number being a conserved quantity (an integral of motion), the
f
-electron spectral function is a complicated object which displays nontrivial features
Brandt and Urbanek 1992
;
Freericks et al. 2005
;
Shvaika and Freericks 2008
. The local
d
-electron density-density OTOC for the FKM was calculated and its enhancement near the metal-insulator transition point was obtained
Tsuji et al. 2017
. But, since the
f
-electron density-density OTOC for the FKM is exactly equal to zero, one must look at OTOCs constructed from
f
-electron creation and annihilation operators, which describe transitions between local
f
-states, similar to the spin-flip operators for spin systems.

In this article, we investigate OTOC functions constructed from
f
-electron creation and annihilation operators (state-flip operators for the two-level system) for the FKM on an infinite dimension hypercubic lattice. First, we introduce the model and recall the derivation of the
f
-electron DOS. Next, we consider the lattice gas model and obtain analytic results for the single-particle DOS and OTOCs for any lattice, and then examine the infinite dimensional limit. Finally, we calculate OTOCs for
f
-electrons for the FKM and provide numerical results.

II
Model Hamiltonian and single-particle propagators

The Falicov-Kimball model (FKM)
Falicov and Kimball 1969
is the simplest model for strongly correlated electron systems and describes a system of the itinerant
d
-electrons which interact with localized particles. Its Hamiltonian

H=-\sum_{ij}t_{ij}d_{i}^{\dagger}d_{j}+\sum_{i}H_{i}

(3)

contains a hopping contribution from the itinerant electrons (kinetic energy) and a local part

H_{i}=Un_{id}w_{i1}-\mu n_{id}+(E_{f}-\mu)w_{i1}

(4)

which includes the interaction of strength
U
between the itinerant and localized particles, Here,
n_{id}=d_{i}^{\dagger}d_{i}
is the local density of
d
electrons and
w_{i1}
is the occupation of site
i
by the localized particles, which can take two values, 0 or 1, and describes a two-level state subsystem without specifying its internal dynamics. As a rule, it is associated with the localized
f
-electron state, i.e.,
it is the expectation value of the localized electron density operator

w_{i1}=n_{if}=f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{i}

(5)

and its internal dynamics is determined by the
f
-electron creation
f_{i}^{\dagger}
and annihilation
f_{i}
operators. These local
f
-electron densities are conserved in the model, as they commute with the Hamiltonian.

The FKM possesses an exact solution in the limit of infinite dimensions
D\to\infty
, after scaling the nearest-neighbor hopping by
t_{ij}=t^{*}/2\sqrt{D}
, and can be solved exactly with DMFT
Georges et al. 1996
;
Freericks and Zlatić 2003
. Below, we shall put
t^{*}=1
as the energy scale.

In DMFT, the self-energy is local
Metzner and Vollhardt 1989
;
Metzner 1991
, and the lattice model is mapped onto an effective impurity problem with the same self-energy and some auxiliary self-consistent
\lambda
-field
Brandt and Mielsch 1989
. Both the self-energy
\Sigma(\omega)
and
\lambda
-field
\lambda(\omega)
are found from solutions of the self-consistent set of equations

\displaystyle G_{d}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega+\mu-\Sigma(\omega)-\lambda(\omega)}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}\varepsilon\frac{\rho(\varepsilon)}{\omega+\mu-\Sigma(\omega)-\varepsilon},

(6)

where
G_{d}(\omega)
is the
d
-electron Green’s function for the impurity problem and for the FKM it becomes

\displaystyle G_{d}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{0}}{\omega+\mu-\lambda(\omega)}+\frac{w_{1}}{\omega+\mu-U-\lambda(\omega)}

\displaystyle=w_{0}\mathcal{G}_{0}(\omega)+w_{1}\mathcal{G}_{1}(\omega).

(7)

Here,
w_{1}=n_{f}=\langle f^{\dagger}f\rangle
and
w_{0}=1-n_{f}=\langle ff^{\dagger}\rangle
are average occupations of the localized
f
-states on the lattice and
\rho(\omega)
is the unperturbed density of states, which for the infinite-dimensional hypercubic lattice is a Gaussian

\rho(\omega)=\frac{1}{t^{*}\sqrt{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-(\omega/t^{*})^{2}}.

(8)

Finally, for the
d
-electron propagator we find

G_{d,\mathbf{k}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\omega+\mu-\Sigma(\omega)-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}},

(9)

where
\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}=-\frac{t^{*}}{\sqrt{D}}\sum_{\alpha}\cos k_{\alpha}a
is the unperturbed band energy.

Finding the solution for the
f
-electron propagator is more challenging
Brandt and Urbanek 1992
;
Zlatić et al. 2001
;
Freericks and Zlatić 2003
. We follow the derivation in
Shvaika and Freericks 2008
.
The time evolution of the
f
-operators is given by

\displaystyle f_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{i}(t,0)f_{i}(0),

(10)

\displaystyle f_{i}^{\dagger}(t)

\displaystyle=f_{i}^{\dagger}(0)\mathcal{U}_{i}^{\dagger}(t,0)=\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{i}(t,0)f_{i}^{\dagger}(0),

(11)

where the evolution operators are

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{i}(t,0)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}\!\exp\left\{-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\left[Un_{id}(t^{\prime})+E_{f}-\mu\right]\right\},

(12)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{i}(t,0)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}\!\exp\left\{\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\left[Un_{id}(t^{\prime})+E_{f}-\mu\right]\right\}.

(13)

Based on this, the contour-ordered single-particle propagator is defined to be

G_{ijf}^{c}(t,t^{\prime})=-\mathrm{i}\left\langle\mathcal{T}_{c}f_{i}(t)f_{j}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\delta_{ij}G_{f}^{c}(t,t^{\prime}),

(14)

where the time ordering is performed on the Keldysh contour and the Coulomb interaction
U
acts only on the upper (forward) branch of contour. It is a local quantity because there is no hopping for the
f
electrons. In particular, the greater
G_{f}^{>}(t)
and lesser
G_{f}^{<}(t)
Green’s functions can be expressed in terms of functional determinants of Toeplitz matrices calculated on the time interval
[0,t]
with
t>0
(for details, see
Shvaika and Freericks 2008
)

\displaystyle G_{f}^{>}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\left\langle\mathcal{U}_{i}(t,0)f(0)f^{\dagger}(0)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}w_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(E_{f}-\mu)t}

\displaystyle\times\det\nolimits_{[0,t]}\left\|\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})-\mathcal{G}_{0}(t_{1}-t_{2})U\right\|

(15)

and [
G_{f}^{<}(t)=-\{G_{f}^{<}(-t)\}^{*}
]

\displaystyle G_{f}^{<}(-t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}w_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(E_{f}-\mu)t}

\displaystyle\times\det\nolimits_{[0,t]}\left\|\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})+\mathcal{G}_{1}(t_{1}-t_{2})U\right\|.

(16)

Here

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{0}(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})}\left[f(\omega)-\Theta(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\times\operatorname{Im}\frac{1}{\omega+\mu-\lambda(\omega)}

(17)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{1}(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})}\left[f(\omega)-\Theta(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\times\operatorname{Im}\frac{1}{\omega+\mu-U-\lambda(\omega)}

(18)

are the time ordered (causal) impurity Green’s functions for the empty and filled
f
states, respectively, using the boundary condition for the step function, given by
\Theta(0)=0

Pakhira et al. 2019
.
The retarded Green’s functions is defined in the usual way via

\displaystyle G_{f}^{r}(t)

\displaystyle=\Theta(t)\left\{G_{f}^{>}(t)-G_{f}^{<}(t)\right\}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\Theta(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(E_{f}-\mu)t}\bigl\{w_{0}\det\nolimits_{[0,t]}\left\|\mathbf{I}-\mathcal{G}_{0}U\right\|

\displaystyle+w_{1}\left(\det\nolimits_{[0,t]}\left\|\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{G}_{1}U\right\|\right)^{*}\bigr\}

\displaystyle=G_{0}^{f}(t)+G_{1}^{f}(t)

(19)

with a Fourier transform

\displaystyle G_{f}^{r}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\omega+\mu-E_{f})t}\bigl\{w_{0}\det\nolimits_{[0,t]}\left\|\mathbf{I}-\mathcal{G}_{0}U\right\|

\displaystyle+w_{1}\left(\det\nolimits_{[0,t]}\left\|\mathbf{I}+\mathcal{G}_{1}U\right\|\right)^{*}\bigr\},

(20)

which defines the single-particle DOS as

A_{f}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\operatorname{Im}G_{f}^{r}(\omega).

(21)

The long time asymptotic of the Toeplitz determinants in (
19
) can be obtained by using Szegő’s theorem and the Wiener-Hopf approach, see
Shvaika and Freericks 2008
and references therein. It is determined by the winding number (index) of the characteristic functions
C_{0}^{f}(\omega)
and
C_{1}^{f}(\omega)
, which are given by

\displaystyle C_{0,1}^{f}(\omega)=1\mp U\mathcal{G}_{0,1}^{t}(\omega)

\displaystyle=1\mp U\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{G}_{0,1}^{r}(\omega)\mp\mathrm{i}\,U\tanh\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{G}_{0,1}^{r}(\omega),

(22)

respectively.
Here,
\mathcal{G}_{0,1}^{t}(\omega)
is the Fourier transform of the time-ordered impurity Green’s function in (
17
) and (
18
). We choose both time indices
t^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime\prime}
to be on the top branch of the Keldysh contour, and
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}^{r}(\omega)
is the retarded Green’s function. According to Szegő’s theorem, the long time asymptotic is determined by the logarithm of this function, which is not an analytic function. The imaginary part of
C_{\alpha}^{f}(\omega)
crosses the zero line only at
\omega=0
and the winding number is determined by the sign of the real part at this point. Note that we can not calculate the winding number via its strict mathematical definition; instead we use Nyquist (Cole-Cole) type plots that illustrate their topology along with the changes of the sign of the imaginary part for negative values of the real one.

At half filling and for

, when both

, the winding number is equal to zero (see Appendix

T=0
only). This behavior can be seen in the change of the asymptotics of the logarithmic derivative from a constant to a
c/t
dependence (Fig.

). The corresponding DOS displays a Lorentzian peak for high temperatures, which is replaced by a power law singularity in the limit as

.

On the other hand, for
U>0.866
, when both
1\mp U\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{G}_{0,1}^{t}(\omega=0)<0
, the logarithm of the characteristic function is multivalued with a branch cut, and the winding number is equal to
-1
(see Appendix

). In this case, the characteristic function (
22
) is replaced by

\check{C}_{\alpha}^{f}(\omega)=-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega W}C_{\alpha}^{f}(\omega),

(23)

where
W=\Delta t\to 0
is the time discretization, and the long time asymptotic becomes a more complicated nonexponential one with oscillations of the logarithmic derivatives (Fig.

f(\omega)
becomes step-like and one must employ results for singular integral equations

. Thus, at
T=0
, the long time asymptotic becomes a power law in the metallic phase for

U>U_{c}
).

III
Lattice gas

As mentioned above, the FKM can be projected onto a classical lattice gas (Ising) model with effective interactions. So, we first consider the simple lattice gas model

H=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}V_{ij}n_{i}n_{j}-\mu\sum_{i}n_{i},

(24)

where
n_{i}=c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i}
is the occupation of site
i
by a spinless Fermi-type particle, with values
n_{i}=1
or
0
. This model corresponds to a classical Ising model with
S_{i}^{z}=n_{i}-\frac{1}{2}=\pm\frac{1}{2}
.

Despite the occupation operator
n_{i}

being a conserved quantity,
[n_{i},H]=0
, one can still examine nontrivial behavior for the dynamics and for the density of states for such fermions. From the equations of motion, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}c_{i}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\Bigl[\sum_{j}V_{ij}n_{j}(t)-\mu\Bigr]c_{i}(t),

(25)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}c_{i}^{\dagger}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\Bigl[\sum_{j}V_{ij}n_{j}(t)-\mu\Bigr]c_{i}^{\dagger}(t).

(26)

This then yields the time evolution of the annihilation and creation operators, respectively,

\displaystyle c_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=U_{i}(t,0)c_{i}(0)=c_{i}(0)\bar{U}_{i}^{\dagger}(t,0),

(27)

\displaystyle c_{i}^{\dagger}(t)

\displaystyle=\bar{U}_{i}(t,0)c_{i}^{\dagger}(0)=c_{i}^{\dagger}(0)U_{i}^{\dagger}(t,0),

(28)

where the evolution operators are given by

\displaystyle U_{i}(t,0)

\displaystyle=T\exp\biggl\{-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\Bigl[\sum_{j}V_{ij}n_{j}(t^{\prime})-\mu\Bigr]\biggr\},

(29)

\displaystyle\bar{U}_{i}(t,0)

\displaystyle=T\exp\biggl\{\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\Bigl[\sum_{j}V_{ij}n_{j}(t^{\prime})-\mu\Bigr]\biggr\}.

(30)

Now, for the single-particle Green’s function we find [
G_{ij}(t,t^{\prime})=\delta_{ij}G_{i}(t,t^{\prime})
]

\displaystyle G_{i}^{>}(t,0)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\left\langle U_{i}(t,0)c_{i}(0)c_{i}^{\dagger}(0)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\left\langle\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}[\sum_{j}V_{ij}n_{j}-\mu]t}(1-n_{i})\right\rangle,

(31)

\displaystyle G_{i}^{<}(t,0)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\left\langle\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}[\sum_{j}V_{ij}n_{j}-\mu]t}n_{i}\right\rangle,

(32)

\displaystyle G_{i}^{r}(t,0)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\Theta(t)\left\langle\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}[\sum_{j}V_{ij}n_{j}-\mu]t}\right\rangle,

(33)

where we have used the fact that
n_{i}(t)=n_{i}
is a conserved quantity, which allows us to replace the time-ordered exponents by ordinary ones.

In the case of a nearest-neighbor interaction, one has

V_{ij}=\frac{V^{*}}{Z},

(34)

where
Z
is the coordination number (number of nearest neighbors for site
i
). Calculating the local density of states, we obtain

\displaystyle A_{i}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\Bigl\langle\delta\Bigl(\omega+\mu-\sum_{j}V_{ij}n_{j}\Bigr)\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{Z}w_{i}(s)\delta\Bigl(\omega+\mu-V^{*}\frac{s}{Z}\Bigr).

(35)

Here
w_{i}(s)
are statistical weights of the configurations with
s
occupied neighbors of site
i
, and

\sum_{s=0}^{Z}w_{i}(s)=1.

(36)

Then, for the homogeneous phase, we have

\sum_{s=0}^{Z}w_{i}(s)\frac{s}{Z}=\langle n_{j}\rangle=\langle n\rangle.

(37)

Now, we examine the OTOCs for the lattice gas model. For the square modulus of the anticommutator, we have

\displaystyle\Bigl\langle\bigl|\bigl[c_{i}(t),c_{j}^{\dagger}(0)\bigr]_{+}\bigr|^{2}\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle c_{j}(0)c_{i}^{\dagger}(t)c_{i}(t)c_{j}^{\dagger}(0)\bigr\rangle+\bigl\langle c_{i}^{\dagger}(t)c_{j}(0)c_{j}^{\dagger}(0)c_{i}(t)\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle+\bigl\langle c_{j}(0)c_{i}^{\dagger}(t)c_{j}^{\dagger}(0)c_{i}(t)\bigr\rangle+\bigl\langle c_{i}^{\dagger}(t)c_{j}(0)c_{i}(t)c_{j}^{\dagger}(0)\bigr\rangle.

(38)

Using
c_{i}^{\dagger}(t)c_{i}(t)=n_{i}
,
c_{j}(0)c_{j}^{\dagger}(0)=1-n_{j}
, and

\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}[\sum_{l}V_{il}n_{l}-\mu]t}c_{j}^{\dagger}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}[\sum_{l}V_{il}n_{l}-\mu]t}=\mathrm{e}^{iV_{ij}t}c_{j}^{\dagger},

(39)

we find both local and nonlocal terms

\displaystyle\Bigl\langle\bigl|\bigl[c_{i}(t),c_{j}^{\dagger}(0)\bigr]_{+}\bigr|^{2}\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}+(1-\delta_{ij})\,\langle n_{i}(1-n_{j})\rangle\,4\sin^{2}\frac{V_{ij}t}{2}.

(40)

Similarly, another OTOC gives us

\displaystyle\bigl\langle\bigl[c_{i}^{\dagger}(t),c_{j}^{\dagger}(0)\bigr]_{+}\bigl[c_{i}(t),c_{j}(0)\bigr]_{+}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-\delta_{ij})\,\langle n_{i}n_{j}\rangle\,4\sin^{2}\frac{V_{ij}t}{2},

(41)

\displaystyle\bigl\langle\bigl[c_{i}(t),c_{j}(0)\bigr]_{+}\bigl[c_{i}^{\dagger}(t),c_{j}^{\dagger}(0)\bigr]_{+}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-\delta_{ij})\,\langle(1-n_{i})(1-n_{j})\rangle\,4\sin^{2}\frac{V_{ij}t}{2},

(42)

which are also nonlocal. One can see, that these OTOCs expand only with the distance of the interaction range
V_{ij}
and represent a direct instantaneous interaction. In the limit
Z\to\infty
for the nearest-neighbor interactions (
34
), the nonlocal contributions vanish as
1/Z
.

On the other hand, for the square of the anticommutator, we have only local contributions given by

\Bigl\langle\bigl[c_{i}(t),c_{j}^{\dagger}(0)\bigr]_{+}^{2}\Bigr\rangle=\delta_{ij}\left\langle\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}[\sum_{l}V_{il}n_{l}-\mu]t}\right\rangle,

(43)

while for nearest-neighbor interactions (
34
), we find

\Bigl\langle\bigl[c_{i}(t),c_{j}^{\dagger}(0)\bigr]_{+}^{2}\Bigr\rangle=\delta_{ij}\sum_{s=0}^{Z}w_{s}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}[V^{*}\frac{s}{Z}-\mu]t}.

(44)

One can see, that the expression (
43
) for the OTOC constructed by the square of the anticommutators is similar to the one for the retarded Green’s function (
33
), both having the square of the exponent (evolution operator). It can potentially be considered as a higher moment of a (quasi)probability.

IV
OTOCs for the Falicov-Kimball model

Similar to the lattice gas model, we can examine different OTOCs for the
f
electrons of the FKM.

IV.1
Anticommutator squared magnitude

The first one is the squared magnitude of the anticommutator of the creation and annihilation operators at different times
[f_{i}(t),f_{j}^{\dagger}(0)]_{+}
, which satisfy

\displaystyle F_{ij}^{\textrm{mod}}(t)=\left\langle\left|[f_{i}(t),f_{j}^{\dagger}(0)]_{+}\right|^{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle[f_{i}(t),f_{j}^{\dagger}(0)]_{+}[f_{i}(t),f_{j}^{\dagger}(0)]_{+}^{\dagger}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle f_{i}(t)f_{j}^{\dagger}(0)f_{j}(0)f_{i}^{\dagger}(t)\right\rangle+\left\langle f_{j}^{\dagger}(0)f_{i}(t)f_{i}^{\dagger}(t)f_{j}(0)\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\left\langle f_{i}(t)f_{j}^{\dagger}(0)f_{i}^{\dagger}(t)f_{j}(0)\right\rangle+\left\langle f_{j}^{\dagger}(0)f_{i}(t)f_{j}(0)f_{i}^{\dagger}(t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle f_{i}f_{j}^{\dagger}f_{j}f_{i}^{\dagger}\right\rangle+\left\langle f_{j}^{\dagger}f_{i}f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{j}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+2\operatorname{Re}\left\langle f_{i}(t)f_{j}^{\dagger}(0)f_{i}^{\dagger}(t)f_{j}(0)\right\rangle.

(45)

For the FKM, the product of the creation and annihilation operators at the same times in the first two terms give either
P_{i}^{+}=f_{i}^{\dagger}(t)f_{i}(t)
or
P_{i}^{-}=f_{i}(t)f_{i}^{\dagger}(t)
, which are conserved quantities, and do not depend on time. These terms yield static correlation functions.
But, the last two terms of the time evolution of the operators are determined by (
10
) and (
11

It should be noted, that the Szegő-Widom theorem for block Toeplitz matrices
Widom 1976
is applicable for smooth functions. For this case in the fermionic FKM, the functions are continuous at high temperatures. At low temperatures, both the matrix elements in (
V
) and characteristic functions (
70
) develop step-like features from the Fermi factors and the OTOC’s long time behavior is now governed by localization singularities given by the Anderson orthogonality catastrophe
Anderson 1967
;
Nozières and De Dominicis 1969
;
Pakhira et al. 2019
. These cases of three different winding numbers for the characteristic function are also distinguished by the behavior of the Lyapunov exponent at low temperatures (see Appendix
C
, Fig.
A.6
).

At high temperatures, the oscillatory behavior of the Lyapunov exponent is similar to the one seen in
Lin and Motrunich 2018
;
Riddell and Sørensen 2019
for quantum spin models behind the light cone after the wave front passes. This analogy is plausible, because we consider a local OTOC function, which is always behind the wave front and at high temperature the effects of the Fermi statistics become negligible and the electrons behave like Pauli particles. Nevertheless, the localized
f
particles act as impurity scattering centers for the itinerant
d
electrons and the destruction of the oscillations of Lyapunov exponent for large Coulomb interaction
U
is similar to the one observed in
Riddell and Sørensen 2019
with the an increase of disorder, when one observes the post wavefront ripples damped by disorder. This static disorder can protect the fast scrambling, which is manifested as a breaking of the bound on chaos, when at long times we have
L_{0}(t)>2\pi k_{B}T/\hbar
, which, is also manifested by the negative OTOC “density-of-states”
A(\omega)
, see Fig.
6
. According to Yunger Halpern et al.
Yunger Halpern et al. 2018
, OTOCs can be considered as moments of the Kirkwood-Dirac quasiprobabilities which implies that negative values of the “density-of-states”
A(\omega)
are signatures of quantum information scrambling.

In cases where the calculated OTOC function decays with time, the question arises “where has the corresponding quantum information gone?” For lattice models with direct interactions between spins, it spreads to the nearby sites due to quantum information scrambling. For the FKM, there is no direct interaction between
f
particles, only an effective retarded one mediated through the itinerant
d
electrons, and in addition to quantum scrambling, quantum information can be transferred to the
d
electrons. Exactly how this occurs is a project for future investigations.

VII
Conclusions

We have examined how an indirect retarded interaction between qubits can affect the scrambling of quantum information. We considered the dynamics of the localized two-level states (
f
-electrons) interacting with the itinerant
d
-electrons in the Falicov-Kimball model and found exact results for the OTOCs constructed via state-flip operators (
f
-electron creation and annihilation operators) within dynamical mean field theory. Despite the classical (Ising) nature of our two-level states (the occupation
n_{f}
is a conserved quantity), their dynamics is nontrivial and is governed by the winding of the characteristic functions for the corresponding Toeplitz matrices. With an increase of the local interaction potential
U
between
f
and
d
electrons, the Lyapunov exponent (logarithmic derivative) of the OTOCs start to display oscillations at high temperatures similar to those seen in quantum spin models. On the other hand, at low temperatures Fermi statistics and screening effects related to the orthogonality catastrophe become important, which strongly affects the OTOC’s behavior. With a further increase of the interaction
U
, we find a fast damping of these oscillations due to the effects of disorder and, on the other hand, makes the OTOC’s “density-of-states” (moments of the Kirkwood-Dirac quasiprobabilities) become negative, which is another manifestation of the quantum information scrambling.
