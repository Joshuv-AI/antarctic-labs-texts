Axiomatic Choice and the Decision-Evaluation Paradox
Ben Abramowitz, Nicholas Mattei

Abstract
We introduce a framework for modeling decisions with axioms that are statements about decisions, e.g., ethical constraints. Using our framework we define a taxonomy of decision axioms based on their structural properties and demonstrate a tension between the use of axioms to make decisions and the use of axioms to evaluate decisions which we call the Decision-Evaluation Paradox. We argue that the Decision-Evaluation Paradox arises with realistic axiom structures, and the paradox illuminates why one must be exceptionally careful when training models on decision data or applying axioms to make and evaluate decisions.

We develop a highly generic framework of decisions, as a layer of abstraction above traditional Social Choice, to model both individual and collection decisions by any kind of person or agent and the axioms the decision obeys (or violates). The framework also allows us to formally define common subjective concepts like
transparency
and
deception
, which involve the availability and accuracy of statements of facts and counterfactuals.

Axioms are often defined to be properties of functions or
rules
used to make decisions. Classic axioms include a range of efficiency and fairness criteria. These sorts of axioms can therefore be used to characterized rules and select rules for making decisions. It has also been proposed that axioms should be used also to explain or justify decisions

;
boixel2022calculus
. However, if we want to give complete explanations or justifications for decisions made by agents, then we need to be able to capture all of the relevant normative statements that can be made about decisions and which might be required to describe their normative positions. We therefore need to broaden the definition of an axiom to be a property of a decision rather than a property of a rule. Such axioms capture a broader range of ethical principles and normative positions.

Using our framework, we examine how axioms can be used to make and evaluate individual and collective decisions, and reveal a tension between these two tasks that we call the Decision-Evaluation Paradox. In short, the paradox says that for certain poorly-behaved axioms, using the axiom to drive decisions will ultimately lead to decisions that violate the axiom. This paradox does not arise for axioms that are simply properties of rules, but it matters greatly when more complex axioms are needed. It creates a tension between forward and backward AI alignment
ji2023ai
and reveals an obstacle to bottom-up approaches to AI Alignment
tolmeijer2020implementations
.

Toward revealing our paradox, we (1) develop our framework by defining decisions and axioms as properties of decisions, (2) create a taxonomy of axioms based on their structural properties, and (3) define properties and operations for comparing and relating axioms. Our framework reveals that certain axioms cannot be defined in functional terms of input-output pairs, which means models trained by supervised learning using only such pairs (i.e., state-action pairs) have insufficient information to distinguish between these axioms. Similarly, treating models as blackboxes and evaluating them only by their inputs and outputs is inhibited from differentiating between these axioms. This indistinguishability, which we refer to as
extensional equivalence
, is central to developing our paradox.

The treatment of axioms closest to ours is perhaps that of
schmidtlein2023voting
.
schmidtlein2023voting
introduce a method of using axioms (properties of rules) to make decisions and define the concept of
forcing
which allows for determinate decision-making. We expand their definition of forcing to apply to all axioms (properties of decisions) and use it to develop our paradox. The decision-evaluation paradox in return inhibits the use and explainability of their proposed method of decision-making.

Rules vs. Profile-Outcome Pairs

It is informative to begin by illustrating the difference between two simple axioms.
Consider a countable set

The set

we can find a corresponding rule

, and that for any rule

we can create the corresponding set

Now compare the following two axioms. Define axiom
A_{1}
such that
A_{1}(x,f,y)=1
if and only if

, and define axiom
A_{2}
such that
A_{2}(x,f,y)=1
if and only if
f=f_{L}
.
Clearly, if we use
f_{L}
to make any decision
(x,f_{L},f_{L}(x))
then this decision must obey both
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
. However,
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are not the same axiom!
Let
(x,g,y)
be a decision such that
(x,y)\in L
but
g\neq f_{L}
. The decision
(x,g,y)
will obey
A_{1}
because
(x,y)\in L
, but it will violate
A_{2}
because
g\neq f_{L}
.

A_{1}
and
A_{2}
induce different partitions of the set of decisions
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{Y}
. The set of decisions that obey
A_{2}
is a subset of those that obey
A_{1}
. To determine whether a decision
(x,\underline{\hskip 11.38109pt},y)
obeys axiom

, we only need to know the profile
x
and outcome
y
for this single decision. But to determine whether a decision
(x,g,y)
obeys
A_{2}
, we need to know the rule
g
.

Consider for example the difference between the following two axioms: “The rule must be Pareto-efficient” and “The outcome must be Pareto-optimal.” Many rules may select Pareto-efficient outcomes for some profiles, but not all, so when these axioms are used to evaluate decisions they can arrive at different results. However, when used to make decisions, both axioms enforce a Pareto-optimal outcome for every profile. Our paradox will examine the case where the opposite occurs – where making a decision using an axiom leads to a decision that violates the same axiom upon evaluation.

We can further divide trivial axioms into positively trivial ones that are obeyed by every decision, and negative trivial axioms that are violated by every decision. The axiom of “Universal Domain” in Arrow’s Impossibility Theorem is a prime example of a positively trivial axiom. Universal domain says that

Structural, procedural, and consequentialist axioms are the three most basic types of non-trivial axioms. Each axiom partitions

Structural axioms characterize properties of profiles. For example, a quorum requirement for passing legislation is a structural axiom. Other common structural axioms include single-peaked and single-crossing profiles. The term “structural” is taken from

Procedural axioms characterize properties of rules, e.g., whether the rule is ex-ante fair, Pareto-efficient, strategyproof, or can be computed efficiently.
Consequentialist axioms characterize properties of outcomes without respect to the profile or rule, e.g., whether a winning candidate belongs to a particular political party. A person whose decision-making preferences are characterized by a consequentialist axiom cares only about the outcome and not how the decision gets made.

Blackbox and caudal axioms partition
\mathcal{D}
with respect to two components of the decision.
Blackbox axioms can be expressed as sets of profile-outcome

For example, the statement “The outcome must be Pareto-optimal” is a blackbox axiom, which differs from the procedural axiom, “The rule must be Pareto-efficient.”
Structural and consequentialist axioms are edge cases of blackbox axioms.
The set

x

When it comes to notions of fairness and efficiency, ex-ante notions (e.g., anonymity) are typically procedural and ex-post notions (e.g., equity or envy-freeness) are typically blackbox.
A caudal axiom depends on the rule and/or outcome but not directly on the profile, e.g., “We should use Plurality rule to vote on the best paper at the conference, unless my paper wins, in which case the rule doesn’t matter.”

The last combination of variables from which to construct an axiom class would be the profile and rule together. In other words
A(\textbf{x},\textbf{f},y)=A(\textbf{x},\textbf{f},y^{\prime})

Axiomatic approaches to AI Alignment and Social Choice focus largely on procedural and blackbox axioms.
Note that we do not make any judgments here about what axioms are good or desirable or useful, but argue only that normative positions corresponding to each of our axiom classes exist in the real world.

Rather than defining axiom classes according to the structure of the partitions they induce on a decision domain, we could classify axioms by what information about decisions is sufficient to evaluate whether the decisions obey the axioms.
This leads to the same taxonomy, but different intuition.

and whether

5
Axiom Properties

We now move to defining the various properties of axioms that we need to develop the Decision-Evaluation Paradox. First, we will look at the nature of impossibility results. Our model allows us to differentiate between Arrovian impossibility theorems and a stronger form of impossibility we call an
impasse
. Where only procedural axioms are considered this distinction does not appear, but it is revealed by our expanded definition of axioms. Second, we will look at how axioms relate to each other; by reduction, procedural extension, and extensional equivalence. Finally, we define forcing axioms which require a unique outcome for every profile. These properties will be necessary to understand the decision-evaluation paradox in Section
6
.

5.1
Impossibilities

We now examine impossibility theorems as they are generally presented in Social Choice, but generalized to our generic setting with no assumptions about the nature of profiles or outcomes.

Definition 1

(Arrovian Impossibility Theorem)
.

Axiom
A
is an Arrovian impossibility theorem if
\nexists f\in\mathcal{F}
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
A(x,f,f(x))=1
.

Definition
1
says an axiom defines an Arrovian impossibility theorem if there does not exist any rule that one can implement for all profiles such that the resulting decision will always obey the axiom. Classic examples include
arrow1950difficulty

(
arrow1950difficulty
)
, and

(

We define a stronger form of impossibility that we call an

where no decision can be made that obeys the axiom for at least one profile. An axiom is said to be

if it does not allow an impasse.

Definition 2

(Impasse)
.

Given axiom
A
, an impasse is a profile
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that
A(x,f,f(x))=0
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
. We say that an axiom
A
allows an impasse if
\exists x\in\mathcal{X}
such that
A(x,f,f(x))=0
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
, and that it is implementable otherwise.

Theorem 1
.

If an axiom allows an impasse, it must be an Arrovian impossibility theorem.

Proof.

If there exists a profile
x\in X
such that
A(x,f,f(x))=0
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
, then clearly there is no rule
f
such that
A(x,f,f(x))=1
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
∎

Allowing an impasse implies the presence of an impossibility theorem, but not vice versa. An axiom being an Arrovian impossibility theorem does not imply the existence of an impasse.

Theorem 2
.

An axiom that is an Arrovian impossibility theorem does not necessarily allow an impasse, i.e., it may be implementable.

Proof.

Consider the following (admittedly contrived) example using an exigent axiom. Suppose that for every profile

there is a rule

such that
A(x,f_{x},(f_{x}(x))=1
but
A(x^{\prime},f_{x},f_{x}(x^{\prime}))=0
for all

.

is implementable because it does not allow an impasse. We can define the rule

such that

for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. However, this rule

may not yield a decision that obeys the axiom on any profile if
f\neq f_{x}
for all of the functions
f_{x}
.

5.2
Reductions and Extensionality

Here we introduce tools for comparing and relating axioms.
Any axiom can be characterized by a potentially infinite list of decisions
L\subseteq\mathcal{D}
. Given the list of decisions
L
characterizing any axiom, we can derive a blackbox axiom characterized by a list
B
of profile-outcome pairs. The process of reducing an axiom to a blackbox axiom (when
\mathcal{X}
is discrete and finite) is given by Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Axiom Reduction to Blackbox

0:

Input
L\subseteq\mathcal{D}

B\leftarrow\emptyset

for

(x,f,y)\in L

do

B\leftarrow B\cup\{(x,y)\}

end

for

return

B

Observation 1
.

Every axiom has a unique reduction to a single blackbox axiom.

Definition 3

(Extensional Equivalence)
.

Two axioms are said to be extensionally equivalent if they reduce to the same blackbox axiom.

If two axioms are extensionally equivalent, then for every profile
x
, obeying either axiom when making a decision implies the same set of possible outcomes, and they allow the same set of impasses. If two axioms are extensionally equivalent, then either they are both implementable or neither is implementable. However, as we saw in Section
3
, extensionally equivalent axioms may still partition the space of decisions differently and so they evaluate decisions differently. Extensionally equivalent axioms are not the same axiom.

Observation 2
.

Extensional equivalence is transitive. If
A_{1}
is extensionally equivalent to
A_{2}
and
A_{2}
is extensionally equivalent to
A_{3}
, then
A_{1}
is extensionally equivalent to
A_{3}
.

Observation
2
follows from that fact that every axiom has a unique reduction to a blackbox axiom.

Observation 3
.

A model trained by supervised learning only on
(x,y)
pairs can never learn to distinguish between extensionally equivalent axioms, even if trained on all of
\mathcal{X}
.

Extension to Rules

Given any implementable blackbox axiom
A_{B}
characterized by the list
B\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, we can define exactly the set of rules such that a decision made by any of those rules is guaranteed to obey the blackbox axiom for every profile:
F=\{f\in\mathcal{F}:\forall x\in\mathcal{X},(x,f(x))\in B\}
. The procedural axiom characterized by
F
is extensionally equivalent to
A_{B}
and extensionally equivalent to every axiom that reduces to
A_{B}
. Naturally, this also means that the procedural axiom characterized by
F
reduces to
A_{B}
.

Definition 4

(Procedural Extension)
.

Let
A
be any axiom that reduces to the blackbox axiom
A_{B}
characterized by
B\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, and let
F=\{f\in\mathcal{F}:\forall x\in\mathcal{X},(x,f(x))\in B\}
. We call the procedural axiom characterized by
F
the procedural extension of
A
.

Proposition 2
.

Every blackbox axiom has a procedural extension. If a blackbox axiom is not implementable (i.e., allows an impasse) then its procedural extension is negatively trivial.

Proof.

If an axiom is allows an impasse then there is no procedural axiom that is extensionally equivalent to it. This follows from Corollary
1
which says that a procedural axiom allows an impasse if and only if the axiom is trivial.
For any implementable axiom, we simple reduce it to a blackbox axiom first, which must also be implementable by construction, and then we create the procedural extension of that blackbox axiom. Since the blackbox axiom is implementable, the list
B\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
that characterizes it must contain at least one outcome for every profile, and so there is at least one rule that can make decisions which always obey the axiom.
∎

Proposition 3
.

No two implementable blackbox axioms have the same procedural extension.

Proof.

Suppose we have two distinct, implementable blackbox axioms characterized by lists
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
. Since the axioms are distinct, there must be at least one profile-outcome pair
(x,y)
such that
(x,y)\in B_{1}
but
(x,y)\not\in B_{2}
(w.l.o.g). Now the set of rules
F_{1}
that characterizes the procedural extension of
B_{1}
must contain at least one rule
f
such that
f(x)=y
, but we cannot have
f\in F_{2}
where
F_{2}
characterizes the procedural extension of
B_{2}
.
∎

Proposition 4
.

The procedural extension of any implementable axiom is extensionally equivalent to the original axiom.

Proof.

The proof follows from the fact that if we take the procedural extension of a blackbox axiom, and then reduce that procedural extension back to a blackbox axiom, we necessary end up with the original blackbox axiom.
∎

Forcing Axioms

Adapting a definition from

x
if there exists a single outcome
\textbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
such that
A(x,f,y)=1
if and only if
y=\textbf{y}
. We will further say that the axiom itself is
forcing
if it is forcing with respect to every possible profile
x\in\mathcal{X}
, so a forcing axiom must be implementable.

Definition 5

(Forcing Axioms)
.

Axiom
A
is forcing if
A
is implementable and for all profiles
x\in\mathcal{X}
there is a single outcome
\textbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
such that
A(x,f,y)=1
only if
y=\textbf{y}
.

The only difference between our definition and the original definition of forcing given by
schmidtlein2023voting
is that their definition only applied to procedural axioms while we extend it to all axioms. Consequentialist axioms, blackbox axioms, procedural axioms, caudal axioms, and exigent axioms can all be forcing.

Observation 4
.

We observe the following requirements for axioms to be forcing:

•

A structural axiom can never be forcing

•

A consequentialist axiom is forcing if and only if it is characterized by a single outcome;
Y=\{y\}

•

A procedural axiom is forcing if and only if it is characterized by a single rule;
F=\{f\}

•

A blackbox axiom is forcing if and only if it is characterized by a list
B
of
(x,y)
pairs with a single entry for every profile
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

For caudal and exigent axioms, we do not have concise necessary and sufficient condition for them to be forcing.

Theorem 4
.

Every forcing axiom reduces to a forcing blackbox axiom.

Proof.

Let
L\subseteq\mathcal{D}
be the list of decisions that obey forcing axiom
A
. Since
A
is forcing it must be that for all
\textbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, for all
(\textbf{x},f,y),(\textbf{x},f^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in L
,
y=y^{\prime}
. Therefore, for each profile there is at most one profile-outcome pair in
L
. Moreover, since
A
is implementable, we add at least one profile-outcome pair for every profile in
\mathcal{X}
. Hence,
L
characterizes a forcing blackbox axiom by Observation
4
.
∎

Theorem 5
.

The procedural extension of a blackbox axiom is characterized by a single rule if and only if the blackbox axiom is forcing.

Proof.

If a blackbox axiom is forcing, then we simply construct
f
such that
f(x)=y
for all
(x,y)\in L
. If a blackbox axiom is not forcing then either it allows an impasse or there is a profile
x
and outcomes
y_{1}\neq y_{2}
where
(x,y_{1})\in L
and
(x,y_{2})\in L
. If the blackbox axiom allows an impasse then no procedural axiom is extensionally equivalent to it. If the blackbox axiom is obeyed by two decisions on the same profile with different outcomes, then its procedural extension would need to include at least two rules because if
f(x)=y_{1}
then
f(x)\neq y_{2}
.
∎

Definition 6

(Implied Rule)
.

We say that axiom
A
implies rule
f
if
A
is extensionally equivalent to the procedural axiom characterized by
F=\{f\}
.

Proposition 5
.

Two forcing axioms imply the same rule if and only if they are extensionally equivalent.

Proof.

Extensional equivalence is transitive. If two axioms are both extensionally equivalent to a procedural axiom characterized by a single rule then they must also be extensionally equivalent to each other.
∎

Corollary 2
.

Every forcing axiom implies a rule.

Proof.

Every forcing axiom reduces to a single forcing blackbox axiom per Theorem
4
, and every forcing blackbox axiom extends to a unique procedural axiom with a single rule per Theorem
5
.
∎

6
The Decision-Evaluation Paradox

If we have a forcing axiom, then we can commit to obeying that axiom when making a decision before ever observing a profile, e.g., democratic principles behind election laws. The process of making the decision guided by the forcing axiom implements the axiom’s implied rule. Counterintuitively, if we take the rule implied by a forcing axiom and use it to make decisions, we are NOT guaranteed that those decisions will obey the axiom. Even though the outcomes of our decision-making are dictated by the forcing axiom, the decision itself which implements the implied rule does not obey the axiom. We call this the decision-evaluation paradox.

Theorem 6
.

Making decisions using the implied rule of a forcing blackbox axiom or forcing procedural axiom guarantees that the decision obeys the axiom.

Proof.

Forcing procedural axioms are characterized by a single rule which is also its implied rule. Every forcing blackbox axiom
A_{B}
has a unique procedural extension which is a single rule that is its implied rule
f_{B}
, and therefore
f_{B}(x)=y\Rightarrow A_{B}(x,f_{B},y)=1
.
∎

For example, if an axiom requires that a rule be used which is Pareto-efficient, then any decision made by a Pareto-efficient rule will obey the axiom. Similarly, if an axiom requires that the outcome be Pareto-optimal, then any decision made with a Pareto-optimal outcome will obey the axiom. Blackbox and procedural axiom are generally easier to define and formalize than caudal and exigent axioms, and so they are more widely investigated. However, while the paradox does not appear with blackbox or procedural axioms, that does not mean we can ignore it. For example, if we care about a
combination
of ex-ante and ex-post fairness criteria, then this combination may be exigent. Below is a minimal example proving the existence of the paradox.

Theorem 7
.

Making a decision using the implied rule of a forcing axiom does not guarantee that the decision obeys the axiom.

Example 1

(Decision-Evaluation Paradox)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
,
\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
, and
\mathcal{F}
be defined as follows:

\mathcal{F}=\begin{cases}f_{1}:=f_{1}(x_{1})=y_{1},&f_{1}(x_{2})=y_{1}\\
f_{2}:=f_{2}(x_{1})=y_{1},&f_{2}(x_{2})=y_{2}\\
f_{3}:=f_{3}(x_{1})=y_{2},&f_{3}(x_{2})=y_{1}\\
f_{4}:=f_{4}(x_{1})=y_{2},&f_{4}(x_{2})=y_{2}\\
\end{cases}

Suppose
A
is a forcing exigent axiom characterized by the list of decisions

L=\begin{cases}(x_{1},f_{1},y_{1})\\
(x_{2},f_{4},y_{2})\\
\end{cases}

The reduction of
A
to a forcing blackbox axiom yields the list
B=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\}
. We can see that
A
implies the rule
f_{2}
because
f_{2}(x_{1})=y_{1}
and
f_{2}(x_{2})=y_{2}
. However, no decision made using
f_{2}
will ever obey
A
.

Example
1
shows how the paradox arises when an axiom is forcing but is still an Arrovian impossibility theorem because there is no single rule
f
such that
A(x,f,f(x))=1
for all profiles
x
. This can only happen for caudal and exigent axioms, due to Theorem
3
and Corollary
1
. The paradox can be generalized to all cases where the axiom is implementable and an Arrovian impossibility theorem but not necessarily forcing; where none of the rules that characterize its procedural extension can make decisions that obey the axiom for all profiles.

As a concrete example, consider the following axiom whose procedural extension is Black’s Rule: If a Condorcet-winner exists then use the Copeland Rule and otherwise use the Borda Rule. This is an exigent forcing axiom. If we implement this axiom, then we are never truly using the Borda Rule or the Copeland Rule; we are using an implied rule that selects the Copeland winner on a subset of profiles and the Borda winner on others. Now suppose there is a participant involved in the decision who is adamant that the decision should be made by the Borda Rule (i.e., their normative position is a procedural axiom). For a given decision in which the winner is the Borda winner and there is no Condorcet-winner, will that participant be satisfied? If you explain to them that they should be satisfied because the “Borda Rule” was used to select the winner, is that true or are you misleading them by implying false counterfactuals?

6.1
Transparency and Deception

We can easily define general concepts of transparency of decisions and deception of explanations or justifications.

Definition 7

(Transparency)
.

A decision is transparent to an agent whose normative position is described by axiom
A
if the agent has access to sufficient information about the decision to determine if it obeys or violates
A
.

When decisions lack transparency, this creates room for deception.

Definition 8

(Deception)
.

A statement about a decision
(x,f,y)
deceives an agent whose normative position is axiom
A
if the statement contains information implying that the decision is
(x^{\prime},f^{\prime},y^{\prime})
where
A(x,f,y)=0
but
A(x^{\prime},f^{\prime},y^{\prime})=1
.

We now present how a subtle form of deception can arise which is not a paradox
per se
but is closely related to our Decision-Evaluation Paradox. This form of deception involves the implication of false counterfactuals for a decision, even when the facts
(x,y)
are observable.

When multiple agents make a collective decision, e.g., by voting, they may each hold different beliefs about what axioms their collective decision should obey, and use different axioms to evaluate the decision after the fact. It is therefore tempting to offer them each individualized explanations demonstrating that a decision obeys all of their personal axioms. Let
A^{AND}
be taken over the personal axioms of all agents in a population. When
A^{AND}
yields an Arrovian impossibility theorem, we know that there is no rule we can propose that will make decisions guaranteed to obey everyone’s personal axioms. However, once a decision is made, if only
(x,y)
is observable but not
f
, this creates the possibility of finding post-hoc explanations that appear to obey agents’ personal axioms, but rely on rules
f^{\prime}
that were not actually implemented but for which
f^{\prime}(x)=y
. Here we take an example from
abramowitz2021deciding
based on Arrow’s Impossibility Theorem (refer there for axiom definitions and proof).

Example 2

(Post-Hoc Deception)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be the set of all possible profiles of weak orders over a set of candidates, let
\mathcal{Y}
be the set of all possible weak orders over the candidates (i.e., “social orders”), let
\mathcal{F}
be the set of all rules, and let
\{A_{P},A_{I},A_{D}\}
be the axioms Weak Pareto, Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives, and Non-Dictatoriality, respectively. Let
A_{1}=A^{AND}(A^{P},A^{I})
,
A_{2}=A^{AND}(A^{I},A^{D})
,
A_{3}=A^{AND}(A^{D},A^{P})
, and
A^{*}=A^{AND}(A_{1},A_{2},A_{3})
. Arrow’s Theorem tells us that
A^{*}
is an Arrovian Impossibility theorem, but we know
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
, and
A_{3}
are not. Surprisingly, for all profiles
x\in\mathcal{X}
there exists at least one outcome
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and three rules
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}\in\mathcal{F}
such that
f_{1}(x)=f_{2}(x)=f_{3}(x)=y
and
A_{1}(x,f_{1},y)=A_{2}(x,f_{2},y)=A_{3}(x,f_{3},y)=1
.

Thus, if three agents disagree on which two axioms to prioritize, for every profile we can find three decisions with three different rules that all yield the same outcome where one satisfies each agent. The trick underneath is that none of the rules
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
is actually being implemented to determine the outcome
y
based on
x
, and different rules will be generated for the “explanations” for different profiles
x
. The rule actually being implemented will necessarily satisfy
A_{P}
because
A_{P}
is a blackbox axiom, but cannot satisfy both
A_{I}
and
A_{D}
because Arrow’s Impossibility Theorem still applies. When the rule is not observable, these degenerate explanations may give the appearance of overlapping consensus
rawls2017theory
. The ability of an AI agent to generate such deceptive post-hoc explanations creates a dangerous capability to falsely appear aligned, which may only be revealed by subsequent decisions for which the explanation changes.

7
Conclusion

Axioms are normally narrowly construed as properties of decision rules. By taking axioms to be properties of decisions themselves we can capture a broader range of normative positions and ethical constraints. But this broader view also reveals the tension between making and evaluating decisions. The tension poses a challenge for AI Alignment, particularly for approaches that rely on decision data or limited forms of human feedback.

The decision-evaluation paradox arises when an Arrovian impossibility theorem is implementable, and is seen most clearly when the axiom of interest is forcing. But why does it arise at all? The answer lies in the fact that the definition of a rule has counterfactual information baked in – it specifies what the outcome will be for every possible profile, not just the profile we observe. If we make decisions by operationalizing the axiom in Theorem
7
, then we are essentially trying to choose the rule based on the profile. But if the rule differs based on the profile, then we are never
really
implementing that rule. Instead we are implementing the implied rule given by the axiom’s procedural extension.

While using implied rules does not guarantee we obey the original forcing axiom for all axioms, we do get this guarantee for blackbox and procedural axioms. This is because forcing blackbox axioms and forcing procedural axioms cannot be Arrovian impossibility theorems. Blackbox and procedural axioms cover most of the formal and easy-to-explain axioms in the literature, but real-world preferences over decisions can certainly be caudal or exigent and may not be reducible.

A core concern of AI Alignment, and decision-making more broadly, is whether a decision or decision-making process is sufficiently transparent to the relevant stakeholders. Our axiomatic approach suggests means of formalization of what makes a decision truly transparent, including the relevant counterfactuals, and how deception arises when decisions lack transparency.

Future Work

The main limitation of our present axiomatization is that it is deterministic. Many desirable normative properties are non-deterministic in nature, e.g., statistical notions of fairness. Randomization also impacts causal reasoning. Extending our definitions to model settings with uncertainty is an immediate challenge.

Finally, our axioms are categorical in nature. It would be worth considering axioms that order decisions, or assign them cardinal values.
