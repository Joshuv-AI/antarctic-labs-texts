On set-theoretic solutions of pentagon equation and positive basis Hopf algebras
Ilaria Colazzo, Geoffrey Janssens

Abstract
. We investigate the connection between bijective, not necessarily finite, set-theoretic solutions of the pentagon equation and Hopf algebras. Firstly, we prove that finite solutions correspond to Hopf algebras with the positive basis property. As a corollary we generalise Lu-Yan-Zhu classification to arbitrary characteristic 0 0 fields k k . Secondly, we study the general problem of when a Hopf algebra has a basis yielding a set-theoretic solution. Finally, we classify all (co)commutative bijective solutions. This result requires to obtain a description of all bases of a group algebra k ⁡ [ G ] k[G] yielding a set-theoretic solution. We namely show that such bases correspond, through a Fourier transform, to splittings A ⋊ N A\rtimes N of G G with A A a finite abelian group.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

A linear map
f\in\rm End_{k}(V\otimes V)
on a
k
-vector space
V
is a solution of the pentagon equation if it satisfies the equation
f_{23}f_{13}f_{12}=f_{12}f_{23}
in
V^{\otimes 3}
.
The pentagon equation appears naturally across several areas ranging from theory of quantum groups to mathematical physics.
In integrable systems, the pentagon equation can act as a building block for higher-dimensional analogues of the Yang–Baxter equation: Maillet constructs spectral-parameter solutions of the tetrahedron equation from solutions of the pentagon equation
[
18
]
.

In operator algebra, unitary solutions to the pentagon equation are known as multiplicative unitary operators and play a central role in the theory of (locally compact) quantum groups. Baaj and Skandalis showed that, in finite dimension, a multiplicative unitary operator yields a Hopf
C^{*}
-algebra which is in fact a finite-dimensional Kac algebra
[
1
, theorem 4.10]
. Since multiplicative unitary operators have been an important tool in the theory of quantum groups, e.g. see
[
24
,
14
]
.

Although this formulation is analytic, the content of the equation is fundamentally a coherence condition for associativity.
Namely, Mac Lane’s pentagon axiom
[
17
]
is precisely the coherence constraint for the associator in a monoidal category. Moreover, in a monoidal category one finds morphisms satisfying the same formal pentagon equation: Street

[
21
]
proves that fusion operators satisfies the pentagon equation.
Moreover, Kashaev
[
11
]
proves that the pentagon equation plays for the Heisenberg double the same role that the Yang-Baxter equation plays for the Drinfeld double. Most importantly for this article Militaru
[
20
]
and Davydov
[
8
]
have shown that any finite-dimensional Hopf algebra corresponds to an invertible solution of the pentagon equation.

Lately there has been considerable interest in solutions where the underlying vector space
V
has a
k
-basis
\mathcal{B}
which is
f
-invariant in the sense that
f
restricts to a map between pure tensors in
\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
. In that case
f
induces a map
{\left.\kern-1.2ptf\right|_{\mathcal{B}}}\in\rm End(\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B})
which satisfies the set-theoretic pentagon equation.
A set-theoretic solution to the pentagon equation is often denoted by the pair
(S,s)
, where
s:S\times S\to S\times S
.
In
[
9
]
, set-theoretic solutions to the pentagon equation were used in symmetrically factorizable Lie groups, while in
[
12
]
they were considered in the context of the discrete Liouville equation.
Involutive set-theoretic solutions
(S,s)
of the pentagon equation, i.e. those with
s^{2}=\id
,
were completely classified by Colazzo–Jespers–Kubat
[
6
]
, while Castelli
[
4
]
recently studied further special families of bijective solutions.
Moreover, Colazzo–Okniński–Van Antwerpen
[
7
]
show that finite set-theoretic solution induces on
S
a semigroup structure of the form
S\cong E\times G
,
with
E
a left-zero semigroup and
G
a group, and then refine this to a decomposition

S\cong X\times A\times G
together with a hidden group structure on
A
and a matched product
structure on
(A,G)
that determines the solution.

In this article we investigate the connections between, potentially infinite, set-theoretic solutions and Hopf algebras and conversely to extract information on the Hopf algebras related to combinatorial solutions. There are two types of difficulties one is confronted with when describing (infinite) set-theoretic solutions of the PE.

(i)

Most constructions and dualities at a operator of Hopf algebra level do not preserve bases;

(ii)

There is no canonical (multiplier) Hopf algebra associated to an infinite solution.

One aim of this article is to contribute on circumventing the first difficulty. Another is to understand the finite dimensional Hopf algebras related to finite bijective set-theoretic solutions of the pentagon equation. One of our main results states that such Hopf algebras have very nice combinatorial properties: they are exactly those admitting a positive basis. Hopf algebras with a positive basis were defined by Lu–Yan–Zhu in
[
16
]
and classified over the complex numbers. Later the same authors constructed set-theoretic solutions of the Yang–Baxter equation in
[
15
]
from positive basis Hopf algebras. A third aim of this work is to investigate bases of potentially infinite dimensional Hopf algebras that canonically provide set-theoretic pentagon solutions. In particular, we classify such bases for the group algebra and also (infinite) cocommutative solutions.

In upcoming work, the framework of this paper, combined with representation theory, will be used for the study of combinatorial solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation. Among others, it will be used to construct an action of finite injective set-theoretic solutions of the YBE on the finite bijective set-theoretic solutions of the RPE.

In the following subsections we will now explain in more detail the main results of this paper.

1.2.
Connection set-theoretic solutions and positive basis property

Given a bialgebra
(H,\varepsilon,1,m,\Delta)
there is a canonical associated RPE solution, see
[
8
]
and
Proposition

4.5
, as following:

(1)

\Phi_{H}:=(1\otimes m)(\Delta\otimes 1)\in\rm End(H\otimes H).

We call a basis
\mathcal{B}
of the Hopf algebra
\Phi
-set theoretic
if
\Phi_{H}
sends pure tensors
b\otimes c\in\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
to a pure tensor in
\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
. In other words if the restriction of
\Phi_{H}
to
\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
yields a set-theoretic solution on
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}.
If
H
is a Hopf algebra, then the solution
\Phi_{H}
is bijective.

In
Section

5.1
we investigate some general properties of
\Phi
-set theoretic bases. For instance in
Corollary

5.2
we show that if
\varepsilon(b)\geq 0
for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
and also the unit element
1_{H}
decomposes into
\mathcal{B}
with positive coefficients, then
H
has the
positive basis property
. The latter means that
H
has a basis for which all structural constants of all structural maps are positive.

If
H
is finite dimensional, then there is somehow an inverse construction to
\Phi_{H}
. More precisely,
[
8
,
20
]
associated to any finite dimensional bijective RPE solution two finite dimensional Hopf algebras, called right (resp. left) coefficient Hopf algebra. This construction is an analogue of Baaj-Skandalis construction
[
2
]
of slices in the
C^{*}
-algebra setting. This construction yields a two-way construction between finite bijective RPE solutions and finite dimensional Hopf algebras
[
20
, Theorem 3.1]
. Given a finite bijective set-theoretic solution
(S,s)
, these Hopf algebras will be denoted
H_{r}(s)
and
H_{\ell}(s)
. Our first main result is that in the finite setting, set-theoretic solutions correspond to Hopf algebras having a positive basis.

Theorem A

(
Theorem

5.4
)
.

Let
(S,s)
be a finite bijective solution to RPE. Then
H_{\ell}(s)
and
H_{r}(s)
have a basis which is both positive and
\Phi
-set theoretic.

If the ground field
k
is the complex numbers, then finite dimensional Hopf algebras with the positive basis property have been classified by Lu-Yan-Zhu
[
16
]
. They showed that they are all isomorphic to a bicrossed product Hopf algebra
k[B]^{*}\bowtie k[N]
for some mashed pair of groups
(B,N)
. In
Section

4.3.1
we recall the necessary background on mashed pairs. More precisely, they show that given a positive basis
\mathcal{B}
of
H
, one can rescale the basis elements so that
\mathcal{B}
corresponds to a group
G
together with a factorization
G=B.N
, uniquely determined by
H
. The existence of such rescaling, obtained in
[
16
, Section 4]
, required some analysis on the spaces of bialgebra structures on a given vector space. As a consequence of
A
and
[
7
]
, we obtain a new proof of Lu-Yan-Zhu’s classification which has the advantage to work for any characteristic
0
ground field. More precisely, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary B
.

Let
H
be a finite dimensional Hopf
k
-algebra with
{\rm char}(k)=0
. Then the following are equivalent:

•

H
has a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis,

•

H
has the positive basis property,

•

H\cong k[B]^{*}\bowtie k[N]
for a mashed pair of finite groups
(B,N,\triangleleft,\triangleright)
.

Moreover, if above holds, then the mashed pair is uniquely determined.

We expect that for any Hopf algebra, the existence of a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis implies the existence of a (potentially different) basis which is positive, see
5.6
.

Remark
.

Conversely, one may attempt from the positive basis Hopf algebras classification in
[
16
]
and the methods above to obtain a Hopf theoretical proof of the classification in
[
7
]
of all finite bijective set-theoretic solutions
(S,s)
. However, this is more subtle as it first looks like. Indeed, if we denote by
(k[S],s^{v})
the associated vector space solution, then
[
8
, Theorem 5.7]
combined with
[
16
]
yields that
s^{v}
is equivalent to a solution
\Phi_{k[B]^{*}\bowtie k[N]}\otimes 1_{X}
for some finite dimensional vector space
X
. However, it is not clear to what the starting basis
S
of
k[S]
would correspond to in the space
k[B]^{*}\otimes k[N]\otimes X
. This for two reasons: (1) the tensor product
\Phi_{k[B]^{*}\bowtie k[N]}\otimes 1_{X}
arises after a use of the fundamental theorem of Hopf modules, which do not preserve the canonical bases and (2) the groups

and

are obtained after a non-explicit rescaling process. In order to solve this obstacles, one should describe all

-set theoretical bases of

1.3.
Infinite dimensional Hopf algebras yielding set-theoretic solutions

In case of infinite solutions there is no well-behaved analogue of the left coefficient Hopf algebras. In particular, there is no associated Hopf algebra in general. For instance it will follow from the main results below,
C
and
E
, that the solution from
Example

4.12
on an infinite abelian group
A
is not induced from a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis of a Hopf algebra. However, the construction (
1
) also works for multiplier Hopf algebras and the aforementioned solution originates so via the multiplier Hopf algebra
k_{\operatorname{fin}}^{A}
of finitely supported functions on
A
. We expect that this phenomenon holds for any bijective set-theoretic solution. A conceptual reason is that set-theoretic solutions, in contrary of vector space solutions, enjoy duality (cf.
Proposition

2.6
and
Proposition

2.8
).

In the remainder of the article we investigate which infinite solutions can be obtained from Hopf algebras. We call such solutions
reachable
. In practice this amount to the problem of determining whether a given Hopf algebra
H
has a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis.

Cocommutative Hopf algebras

One of our aims is to classify non-finite bijective cocommutative solutions of the RPE. To do so we relate in
Section

6.1
such solutions as following to Hopf algebras.

Theorem C

(
Theorem

6.1
)
.

Let
(S,s)
be a reachable cocommutative bijective solution of RPE on a set

. Then there exists a group

and
\Phi
-set theoretic basis
\mathcal{B}
of
k[G]
such that

s=\phi_{\mathcal{B}}\times 1_{X}

for some set

, where
\phi_{\mathcal{B}}
is the set-theoretic solution on
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}
associated to
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi_{k[G]}\right|_{\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}}}
.

It is tempting to believe that

follows from the work of Baaj-Skandalis
[
2
,
3
]
by lifting the linearised solution

to a multiplicative unitary on the Hilbert space

. Their result would provide a unitary to

for some locally compact group

and discrete space

. However, it is not clear where the basis

is send to in

Non-canonical bases of group algebras

C
reduces the problem of classifying all (reachable) cocommutative set-theoretic solutions to describing

-set theoretic bases of group algebras

. At first surprising, such bases do not need to be multiplicativly closed. For instance any mashed pair decomposition
A\bowtie N
of

a finite abelian group acting trivially on

using Fourier analysis.

Concretely, when

The set

is a

. Furthermore the right action of

which permutes the idempotents

(

)
.

Let

given by

Constructing the non-trivial

Non-existence for domains

A first, non-difficult but important, fact for

is that cocommutative solutions can only arise as the restriction to a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis of a solution
\Phi_{H}
if
H
is a cocommutative Hopf algebra. This allows to invoke Cartier-Konstant-Milnor-Moore classification saying that
H\cong U(\mathfrak{g})\rtimes k[G]
with
\mathfrak{g}:=P(H)
the Lie algebra consisting of primitive elements in

and
G:=G(H)
the group of group-like elements. For universal envelopings, and more generally domains, we show that no
\Phi
-set-theoretic basis exists.

Theorem F

(
Theorem

6.7
&
Corollary

6.8
)
.

Let
H
be a Hopf algebra which is a domain. If

has a
\Phi
-set-theoretic basis
\mathcal{B}
, then for each

we have that
\Delta_{H}(b)=\varepsilon_{H}(b)^{-1}\,b\otimes b
. Consequently, if
\mathfrak{g}
is a finite dimenisonal Lie algebra, then

has no

-set-theoretic basis.

In fact, in general by

such that

implies that the Hopf algebra must be isomorphic to a group algebra. By one of the notorious Kaplansky conjectures, a group algebra

2.
Overview and connection on the different types of Pentagon Solutions

Independent of the type of algebraic object one is dealing with we will study equations of the form

(RPE)

\displaystyle Z_{12}Z_{13}Z_{23}=Z_{23}Z_{12}

(PE)

\displaystyle Z_{23}Z_{13}Z_{12}=Z_{12}Z_{23}

where PE stands for
pentagon equation
and RPE for the
reversed pentagon equation
. In some parts of the literature the PE is called the
Hopf equation
, in which case the RPE is referred to simply as the pentagon equation. The meaning of the operator
Z_{ij}
, and hence of a solution to such equations, depends on the category in which we are working.

In this section we recall the necessary background on the various notions of solutions of the pentagon equation (for sets, vector spaces and algebras) that appear in the literature, and we explain how these notions are related. The content of this section is well-known to the experts, since we are not aware of a single reference that collects all these facts, we include it here.

2.1.
Background on pentagon equation

Let
S
be a set. In the set-theoretic setting, the (R)PE equation in this case is an identity in
\rm End(S\times S\times S)
.

Definition 2.1
.

A set-theoretic solution to the RPE (resp. PE) is a pair
(S,s)
where

(1)

S
is a set

(2)

s\in\rm End(S\times S)
satisfying RPE (resp. PE)

with the standard notation
s_{12}=s\times\id_{S},s_{23}=\id_{S}\times s
and
s_{13}=(\id_{s}\times\tau)(s\times\id_{s})(\id_{s}\times\tau)
where
\tau(u,v)=(v,u)
denotes the flip map.

If
(S,s)
is a set-theoretic solution to the RPE (resp. PE), it is convenient to denote it by

s(x,y)=(\psi_{y}(x),y\circ x)\quad(\text{resp. }s(x,y)=(xy,\theta_{x}(y))).

Expanding the RPE (resp. PE) for
(x,y,z)\in S^{3}
yields

(3)

\displaystyle z\circ(y\circ x)=(z\circ y)\circ x\quad

\displaystyle(\text{resp. }x\cdot(y\cdot z))

(4)

\displaystyle\psi_{z}(y\circ x)=\psi_{z}(y)\circ\psi_{z\circ y}(x)\quad

\displaystyle(\text{resp. }\theta_{x}(y\cdot z)=\theta_{x}(y)\cdot\theta_{x\cdot y}(z))

(5)

\displaystyle\psi_{\psi_{z}(y)}\psi_{z\circ y}(x)=\psi_{y}(x)\quad

\displaystyle(\text{resp. }\theta_{\theta_{x}(y)}\theta_{x\cdot y}(z)=\theta_{y}(z)).

In particular, (
3
), shows that
\circ
is an associative binary operation on
S
.

From the above identities one checks immediately that, given a set-theoretic solution
(S,s)
to the RPE, the map

t(x,y)=(x\circ y,\psi_{x}(y))

defines a set-theoretic solution
(S,t)
to the PE.
Moreover, since
t=\tau s\tau
(with
\tau(x,y)=(yx)
), it follows that
t
is bijective if and only if
s
is bijective.

Finally, let
(S,s)
be a solution of the RPE and write
s(x,y)=(\psi_{y}(x),y\circ x)
and
s^{-1}(x,y)=(xy,\theta_{x}(y))
. A direct computation shows that
ss^{-1}=\id_{S\times S}=s^{-1}s
is equivalent to the following relations:

(6)

\displaystyle\psi_{y}(x)(y\circ x)=x,

(7)

\displaystyle\theta_{\psi_{y}(x)}(y\circ x)=y,

(8)

\displaystyle\psi_{\theta_{x}(y)}(xy)=x,

(9)

\displaystyle\theta_{x}(y)\circ(xy)=y.

In
Section

5.1
we refer to theses as the
inverse relations
.

Given a set-theoretic solution, one can associate to it solutions in linear settings (vector spaces or algebras). For instance, linearising yields an operator on
V=k[S]
and hence an equation in
\rm End(V^{\otimes 3})
.

Definition 2.2
.

A
vector space solution
to the PE (resp. RPE) is a pair
(V,f)
where

(1)

V
is a
k
-vector space

(2)

f\in\rm End_{k}(V\otimes V)
satisfying PE (resp. RPE)

with
f_{12}=f\otimes\id_{V},f_{23}=\id_{V}\otimes f
and
f_{13}=(\id_{V}\otimes\tau)(f\otimes\id_{V})(\id_{V}\otimes\tau)

where
\tau(a\otimes b)=b\otimes a
is the bilinear extension of the flip map.

Some authors prefer to view the PE inside
\rm End_{k}(V)^{\otimes 2}
, or, in other words, to work in a
k
-algebra.

Definition 2.3
.

An
algebra solution
to the PE (resp. RPE) is a tuple
(A,R)
with

(1)

A
a
k
-algebra

(2)

R:=\sum_{i}R_{i}^{(1)}\otimes R_{i}^{(2)}\in A\otimes A
satisfying the PE (resp. RPE)

where
R_{12}=R\times 1_{A},R_{23}=1_{A}\otimes R
and
R_{13}=\sum_{i}R_{i}^{(1)}\otimes 1_{A}\otimes R_{i}^{(2)}.

Note that in
Definition

2.3
the PE is an equation in
A\otimes A\otimes A
.

2.2.
Connections between set-theoretic and vector space solutions

Starting from a set
S
there are two canonical ways to associate to it a
k
-vector space:

(1)

k[S]
, the free
k
-vector space on
S
;

(2)

k^{S}
, is the vector space of functions
S\to k
.

2.2.1.
Via linearisation.

We consider the canonical
k
-linear map

\psi_{(n)}:k[S]^{\otimes n}\rightarrow k[S^{n}]:s_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes s_{n}\mapsto(s_{1},\cdots,s_{n}).

Note that
\psi_{(n)}
is an isomorphism for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
with inverse induced by the universal property (this holds even when
S
infinite). Moreover, if
S
is a semigroup, then
\psi_{(n)}
is a
k
-algebra morphism.
Next given
s\in\rm End(S^{n})
, let
\overline{s}\in\rm End_{k}(k[S^{n}])
denote its
k
-linear extension. We then define from a set-theoretic the
k
-linear map
s^{v}:=\psi^{-1}_{(n)}\circ\overline{s}\circ\psi_{(n)}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

The pair
(S,s)
is a set-theoretic solution of PE (resp. RPE) if and only
(kS,s^{v})
is a vector space solution of the PE (resp. RPE).

Proof.

For
(i,j)\in\{(1,2),(1,3),(2,3)\}
one has
(s^{v})_{ij}=\psi_{(3)}^{-1}\circ\overline{s_{ij}}\circ\psi_{(3)}
.
The claim follows considering the conjugation by
\psi_{(3)}
.
∎

2.2.2.
Via the space of functions

Alternatively, one can also work with
k^{S}
. For
f\in{\rm Hom}(X,Y)
we write
f^{*}\in{\rm Hom}_{k}(k^{Y},k^{X})
for the pullback, defined by
f^{*}\circ\varphi=\varphi\circ f
for
\varphi\in k^{Y}
. In particular, if
s\in\rm End(S^{n})
, then
s^{*}\in\rm End(k^{S^{n}})
. Since
(\cdot)^{*}
is a contravariant functor from
Set
to
Vec
k
, it exchange the PE and the RPE. To express the pullback in a suitable way we introduce the follwing maps.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
S
be a set,
k
be a field, and
s\in\rm End(S^{n})
. Define
f_{s}=\theta_{n}^{-1}\circ s^{*}\circ\theta_{n}
where
\theta_{n}:k^{S}\otimes\cdots\otimes k^{S}\rightarrow k^{S^{n}}
is given by

\theta_{n}(f_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes f_{n}):S^{n}\longrightarrow k:(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\longmapsto\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x_{i}).

If
S
is finite, then
\theta_{n}
is an isomorphism; however its inverse is not canonical.

Proposition 2.6
.

The pair
(S,s)
is a solution of PE (resp. RPE) if and only if
(k^{S},f_{s})
is a solution of the RPE (resp. PE).

Proof.

A direct verification shows, for
(i,j)\in\{(1,2),(1,3),(2,3)\}
, that
(f_{s})_{ij}=\theta_{3}^{-1}\circ(s_{ij})^{*}\circ\theta_{3}
. Thus

(f_{s})_{12}(f_{s})_{13}(f_{s})_{23}=\theta_{3}^{-1}s_{12}^{*}s_{13}^{*}s_{23}^{*}\theta_{3}=\theta_{3}^{-1}(s_{23}s_{13}s_{12})^{*}\theta_{3}

and

(f_{s})_{23}(f_{s})_{12}=\theta_{3}^{-1}s_{23}^{*}s_{12}^{*}\theta_{3}=\theta_{3}^{-1}(s_{12}s_{23})^{*}\theta_{3}.

Therefore the RPE for
f_{s}
in
\rm End_{k}(k^{S}\otimes k^{S}\otimes k^{S})
is equivalent to the equation

(s_{23}s_{13}s_{12})^{*}=(s_{12}s_{23})^{*}

in
\rm End_{k}(k^{S\times S\times S})
. Now evaluating on delta-functions shows that this holds if and only if
s_{23}s_{13}s_{12}=s_{12}s_{23}
in
\rm End(S^{3})
, i.e.
(S,s)
satisfies the PE. The reverse implication and the swap of PE and RPE are analogous.
∎

Example 2.7
.

Let
G
be a group and consider the map
s\in\rm End(G^{2})
defined by
s(g,h)=(gh,h)
. This is a set-theoretic solution of the PE. We compute the pullback
s^{*}\in\rm End(k^{S\times S})
. For a basis element
\delta_{(g,h)}
we have, by definition, that

s^{*}(\delta_{(g,h)})(a,b)=\delta_{(g,h)}(s(a,b))=\delta_{(g,h)}(ab,b)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{if }h=b\text{ and }gb^{-1}=a\\
0&\text{else}\end{array}\right.

Therefore
s^{*}(\delta_{(g,h)})=\delta_{(gh^{-1},h)}
. Note that more generally
s^{*}(\delta_{(g,h)})=\delta_{s^{-1}(g,h)}
.
Consequently, on basis elements
\delta_{g}\otimes\delta_{h}
of

one obtains
f_{s}(\delta_{g}\otimes\delta_{h})=\delta_{gh^{-1}}\otimes\delta_{h}
, which gives that
(k^{G},f_{s})
is a vector space solution of the RPE.

2.2.3.
Connection between the two constructions

For a
bijctive

of the RPE the constructions above give two vector space solutions: (i)

Proposition 2.8
.

Let

be a set-theoretic solution of the PE. Then

f_{s}=(\sigma\otimes\sigma)(s^{-1})^{v}(\sigma^{-1}\otimes\sigma^{-1})

with

.

Proof.

It is enough to check the identity on the basis

of
K^{S}
.
On the one hand,

f_{s}(\delta_{g}\otimes\delta_{h})=\theta^{-1}_{(2)}s^{*}(\delta_{(g,h)})=\theta^{-1}_{(2)}\delta_{s^{-1}(g,h)}.

And on the other hand,

\left((\sigma\otimes\sigma)(s^{-1})^{v}(\sigma^{-1}\otimes\sigma^{-1})\right)(\delta_{g}\otimes\delta_{h})=(\sigma\otimes\sigma)(s^{-1})^{v}(g\otimes h)=(\sigma\otimes\sigma)\psi^{-1}_{(2)}s^{-1}(g,h)

which equals the expression above. This proves the claim.
∎

4.
Combinatorial RPE solutions arising from Hopf algebras

The aim of this section is to recall the canonical RPE solution associated to a Hopf module over a (not necessarily finite dimensional) Hopf algebra and investigate interesting examples. More precisely,
Section

4.2
contains background and introduce the core concept of a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis. In

4.3
we recall the construction of mashed pair of groups and Hopf algebras, yielding the bicrossed product Hopf algebra. Thereof we compute the associated RPE solution and propose a formal framework of mashed pair of RPE solutions.

4.1.
Background on multiplier Hopf algebra

We refer to
[
23
]
for a good account on multiplier Hopf algebras. Here we only recall the bare minimum to understand statements later in the paper.

Convention:
In the setting of multiplier algebras, we no longer assume that an algebra has an identity. Therefore in subsequent section, the terminology (Hopf) algebra will always mean unital, but when multiplier is added we do not assume unitality.

To start,

Definition 4.1
.

An associative
k
-algebra
A
is
non-degenerate
if
\mathrm{span}\{ab\mid a,b\in A\}=A
and for all
a\in A
one has

Aa=0\;\Rightarrow\;a=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad aA=0\;\Rightarrow\;a=0.

A
multiplier
of
A
is a pair
(L,R)
of
k
-linear maps
L,R:A\to A
such that for all
x,y\in A
holds

L(xy)=L(x)y,\qquad R(xy)=xR(y),\qquad xL(y)=R(x)y\quad(x,y\in A).

The set of all multipliers is denoted
M(A)
.

The set
M(A)
can be made into an algebra, called the
multiplier algebra of
A
.

Proposition 4.2
.

The set
M(A)
endowed with the operations

\displaystyle(L_{1},R_{1})+\lambda(L_{2},R_{2})

\displaystyle:=(L_{1}+\lambda L_{2},R_{1}+\lambda R_{2})

\displaystyle(L_{1},R_{1})\cdot(L_{2},R_{2})

\displaystyle:=(L_{1}\circ L_{2},R_{2}\circ R_{1})

is an algebra. Furthermore,
A
embeds into
M(A)
via
a\mapsto(L_{a},R_{a})
with
L_{a}(x)=ax
,
R_{a}(x)=xa
.

Note that a unital algebra is non-degenerate. In fact
A=M(A)
if and only if
A
is unital.

Give two non-degenerate algebras
A
and
B
. A homomorphism
\phi:A\rightarrow M(B)
is non-degenerate if
\mathrm{span}\{\phi(A)\,B\}=\mathrm{span}\{B\,\phi(A)\}=B
.

Definition 4.3
.

A
multiplier bialgebra
is an algebra
A
equipped with a non-degeneratealgebra homomorphism
\Delta:A\to M(A\otimes A)
such that

(i)

\Delta(a)(1\otimes b),\ (1\otimes b)\Delta(a),\ \Delta(a)(b\otimes 1),\ (b\otimes 1)\Delta(a)\in A\otimes A
for all
a,b\in A
.

(ii)

(\Delta\otimes\mathrm{id})\Delta=(\mathrm{id}\otimes\Delta)\Delta
in
M(A\otimes A\otimes A)
.

A
multiplier Hopf algebra
is a multiplier bialgebra
(A,\Delta)
such that the canonical maps
T_{1},T_{2}:A\otimes A\to A\otimes A
defined by

T_{1}(a\otimes b)=\Delta(a)(1\otimes b)\,\text{ and }\,T_{2}(a\otimes b)=(a\otimes 1)\Delta(b)

are bijective.

Any Hopf algebra is a multiplier Hopf algebra.

Example 4.4
.

Let
G
be a discrete group and denote by
k^{G}
the algebra of functions
G\rightarrow k
with the pointwise operations. We will write
k^{G}_{\operatorname{fin}}
for the
finitely supported functions.
Note that
k^{G}_{fin}=\bigoplus_{g\in G}k\,\delta_{g}.
where
\delta_{g}(h)=\delta_{g,h}
for any
g,h\in G.
Note that
\delta_{g}.\delta_{h}=0
if
g\neq h
and equal to
\delta_{g}
otherwise. Given
f\in k^{G}
one can consider
L_{f}=R_{f}:k^{G}_{\operatorname{fin}}\rightarrow k^{G}_{\operatorname{fin}}:g\mapsto f.
Then
T_{f}=(L_{f},R_{f})
is a multiplier on
k^{G}_{\operatorname{fin}}
. The associated map

k^{G}\rightarrow M(k^{G}_{\operatorname{fin}}):f\mapsto T_{f}

can be verified to be an isomorphism. Furthermore,
k^{G\times G}\cong M(k^{G}_{\operatorname{fin}}\otimes k^{G}_{\operatorname{fin}}).

The algebra
k^{G}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is a multiplier Hopf algebra. Concretely, using the aformentioned identification, the coproduct is given by

\Delta:k^{G}_{\operatorname{fin}}\to M(k^{G}_{\operatorname{fin}}\otimes k^{G}_{\operatorname{fin}})\text{ with }(\Delta f)(x,y):=f(xy).

Note that on the basis elements
\Delta(\delta_{g})=\sum_{ab=g}\delta_{a}\otimes\delta_{b}
which possibily is an infinite sum, i.e. it does not lie in

. The counit and antipode are
\varepsilon(f)=f(e)
and
(Sf)(g)=f(g^{-1})
.

Note that if

is finite, then the sum
\sum_{ab=g}\delta_{a}\otimes\delta_{b}
is finite. Hence in that case

is isomorphic to the linear dual

.

4.2.
RPE solutions from a bialgebra

Given a Hopf algebra
H
, following Davydov
[
8

one can associate to each Hopf

Hopf

if it satisfies the following:

•

•

Note that the definition of a Hopf module do not require the antipode of

and hence is also defined for bialgebras. When

is a bialgebra, then Davydov’s construction still yields a solution, but not necessarily bijective. For convenience of the reader we include a proof of the latter fact.

Proposition 4.5

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
B

(M,\mu_{M},\Delta_{M})

\Phi_{M}:=(I_{M}\otimes\mu_{M})\circ(\Delta_{M}\otimes I_{M}):M\otimes M\rightarrow M\otimes M.

Remark 4.6
.

Note that by definition if

is a multiplier bialgebra, then
(I\otimes m_{H})\circ(\Delta_{H}\otimes I)(x\otimes y)\in H\otimes H
for any

Remark 4.7

If

\Phi_{M}
is bijective with inverse

\Psi:M\otimes M\rightarrow M\otimes M:m\otimes n\mapsto m_{(0)}\otimes S(m_{(1)})n.

Furthermore, due to the fundamental theorem of Hopf modules,

This isomorphism induces an isomorphsim of RPE solutions between

Proof of
Proposition

4.5

The proof is a direct verification. Note that
\Phi_{M}(m\otimes n)=m_{(0)}\otimes m_{(1)}n

\Phi_{12}\Phi_{13}\Phi_{23}=\Phi_{23}\Phi_{12}.
For

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\Phi_{12}\Phi_{13}\Phi_{23}(m\otimes n\otimes l)&=&\Phi_{12}\Phi_{13}(m\otimes n_{(0)}\otimes n_{(1)}l)\\
&=&\Phi_{12}(m_{(0)}\otimes n_{(0)}\otimes m_{(1)}n_{(1)}l)\\
&=&m_{(0)}\otimes m_{(1)}n_{(0)}\otimes m_{(2)}n_{(1)}l\\
\end{array}

And the right hand side:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\Phi_{23}\Phi_{12}(m\otimes n\otimes l)&=&\Phi_{23}(m_{(0)}\otimes m_{(1)}n\otimes l)\\
&=&m_{(0)}\otimes m_{(1)}n_{(0)}\otimes m_{(2)}n_{(1)}l\end{array}

where in the last equality we used the compatibility of the structural maps of

∎

4.3.1.
Background on bicrossed products

Let

and
K

[
22
]
they are said to form a (left-right) matched pair if

•

There is a left action
\triangleright:K\otimes H\to H
making
H
into a left
K
-module coalgebra.

•

There is a right co-action
\rho:K\to K\otimes H:a\mapsto a_{K}\otimes a_{H}
making
K
into a right
H
-comodule coalgebra.

•

Compatibility identities, for all

and

:

\displaystyle a\triangleright(hh^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(a_{(1)}\triangleright h)\bigl(a_{(2)}\triangleright h^{\prime}\bigr),

\displaystyle\Delta(a_{K})\otimes a_{H}

\displaystyle=(a_{(1)})_{K}\otimes(a_{(2)})_{K}\otimes(a_{(1)})_{H}(a_{(2)})_{H}

\displaystyle\rho(ab)

\displaystyle=\rho(a_{(1)})(b_{K}\otimes(a_{(2)}\triangleright b_{H}))

\displaystyle\Delta_{K}(a\triangleright h)

\displaystyle=(a_{(1)})_{K}\triangleright h_{(1)}\otimes(a_{(1)})_{H}\,(a_{(2)}\triangleright h_{(2)})

and

a\triangleright 1_{H}=\varepsilon_{K}(a)1_{H}
,
1_{K}\triangleright h=h
.

The compatibility will ensure that the multiplication below is associative and that the bialgebra axioms hold.

Definition 4.15
.

Let
H
and

be Hopf algebras. The (left-right)

is the vector space

endowed with the following structural maps.

•

•

(h\otimes a)(y\otimes b)=h\,(a_{(1)}\triangleright y_{(1)})\ \otimes a_{(2)}\,b,

•

\varepsilon(h\otimes k)=\varepsilon_{H}(h)\varepsilon_{K}(k)
,

•

\Delta(h\otimes a)=(h_{(1)}\otimes(a_{(1)})_{K})\otimes(a_{(2)}(a_{(1)})_{H}\otimes a_{(2)}),

•

S(h\otimes a)=(1\otimes S_{K}(a_{K}))(S_{H}(h\,a_{H})\otimes 1)

It was proven in
[
22
]
that

5.
On relation between positive basis property and set-theoretic solutions

Convention:
In this section
k
will any field of characteristic
0
.

For such fields one can speak about positivity of scalars, e.g by viewing
k
as subfield of
{\mathbb{C}}
. Recall, cf
[
16
]
, that a Hopf algebra
H
is said to have
the positive basis property
if it has a basis

for which the structure constants of all structure maps are positive, i.e.:

(1)

the coordinates of the unit
1
are nonnegative;

(2)

the coordinates of the counit
\epsilon
(with respect to the dual basis
B^{\ast}
) are nonnegative, i.e.
\epsilon(b)\geq 0
, for any
b\in B
;

(3)

for any
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
, the coordinates of
b_{1}b_{2}
are nonnegative;

(4)

for any
b\in B
, the coordinates of
\Delta(b)
with respect to the tensor product basis
B\otimes B
are nonnegative;

(5)

for any
b\in B
, the coordinates of the antipode
S(b)
are nonnegative.

If all structural constants are positive except those for the antipode, then
H
is said to have the
nearly positive basis property
.

In this section we study the relation between Hopf algebras with the positive basis property and set-theoretic solutions. In the finite dimensional setting we show in

5.4
that such Hopf algebras correspond to finite bijection solutions of the RPE.

We start with
Section

5.1
where we show that any hopf algebra with a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis is close to be nearly positive.

5.1.

\Phi
-set theoretic bases

Now suppose that
\mathcal{B}
is a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis of some Hopf algebra H. In particular
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi\right|_{\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}}}
yields a set-theoretic solution of the RPE. Thus it can be written in the form

(20)

\phi_{\mathcal{B}}(b,c)=\bigl(\psi_{c}(b),\ c\circ b\bigr).

where for each

the map
\psi_{c}:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}
is a permutation and
\circ:\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}

is a binary operation.
Thus, for all

,

(21)

\Phi_{H}(b\otimes c)=\psi_{c}(b)\otimes c\circ b.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a

-set theoretic basis of a Hopf algebra

Then for all

,

(ii)

\varepsilon(c)b=\varepsilon(c\circ b)\psi_{c}(b)
,

(iii)

\Delta(b)=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda_{c}\,\varepsilon(c)\varepsilon(c\circ b)^{-1}b\otimes c\circ b

and if

(iv)

and
\varepsilon(c)S(b)(c\circ b)=\varepsilon(c\circ b)\varepsilon(b)c.
.

The expression for the (co-)multiplication in

directly imply the following.

be a Hopf algebra and

a

-set theoretic basis of

satisfies positivity with respect to the unit and co-unit, i.e.

for all

and
\lambda_{c}\geq 0
in the decomposition

has the nearly positive basis property.

It would be interesting to know whether under the conditions of
Corollary

5.2
the expression (
(iv)
) also yield positivity for the antipode.

Example 5.3
.

Lemma

5.1
implies that
\Phi_{H}(b\otimes c)=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}b\otimes bc
for some scalars
\lambda_{i}\in k.
Hence it is tempting to conclude that
\Phi_{H}
is equivalent to the group solution. This is however not the case. As good illustration thereof consider
H=k[G]^{*}
with
G
a finite group and the associated basis
\{\delta_{g}\mid g\in G\}.
In
Example

4.13
it was verified that
\Phi_{k[G]}(\delta_{g}\otimes\delta_{h})=\delta_{gh^{-1}}\otimes\delta_{h}.

The identity (
(ii)
) takes the form
\delta_{g}=\delta_{h,1}^{-1}\delta_{h,1}\delta_{gh^{-1}}=\delta_{h,1}\delta_{gh^{-1}}
. And the equation (
(i)
) is
\delta_{g,h}\delta_{h}=\delta_{gh^{-1},1}\delta_{h}
. Both statements are indeed correct and illustrate that the scalars
\lambda_{i}
can rewrite expression in a non-expected way.

Proof of
Lemma

5.1
.

We start with the equation
bc=\varepsilon(\psi_{c}(b))\,(c\circ b)
. For this apply
(\varepsilon\otimes\id)
to (
21
). On the one hand,

\displaystyle(\varepsilon\otimes\id)\Phi_{H}(b\otimes c)

\displaystyle=(\varepsilon\otimes\id)(\id\otimes m)(\Delta(b)\otimes c)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon(b_{(1)})b_{(2)}c

\displaystyle=m\bigl((\varepsilon\otimes\id)\Delta(b)\otimes c\bigr)

\displaystyle=bc,

using
(\varepsilon\otimes\id)\Delta=\id
.
On the other hand,
(\varepsilon\otimes\id)(\psi_{c}(b)\otimes(c\circ b))=\varepsilon(\psi_{c}(b))\,(c\circ b).

Now we prove that
b=\varepsilon(c)^{-1}\varepsilon(c\circ b)\psi_{c}(b)
in an analogue way. Namely compute
(\id\otimes\varepsilon)\Phi_{H}(b\otimes c)=\varepsilon(b_{(2)})\varepsilon(c)b_{(1)}=\varepsilon(c)b
using that
(\id\otimes\varepsilon)\Delta=\id.
On the other hand,
(\id\otimes\varepsilon)\Phi_{H}(b\otimes c)=\varepsilon(c\circ b)\psi_{c}(b)
, yielding the equation.

Next write
1_{H}=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda_{c}c
. Then

\Delta(b)=\Phi_{H}(b\otimes 1_{H})=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda_{c}\,\psi_{c}(b)\otimes c\circ b=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda_{c}\,\varepsilon(c)\varepsilon(c\circ b)^{-1}b\otimes c\circ b.

Finally we consider the antipode. For this note that

m(S\otimes 1_{H})\Phi_{H}(b\otimes c)=S(b_{(1)})b_{(2)}c=\varepsilon(b)c,

using the definition of
\Phi_{H}
and the convolution rule
m(S\otimes id)\Delta=\varepsilon
. Now by (

) and the second item, we also have
m(S\otimes 1_{H})\Phi_{H}(b\otimes c)=S(\psi_{c}(b))\,(c\circ b).
Therefore if we compare and use the second item,

\varepsilon(c)S(b)(c\circ b)=\varepsilon(c\circ b)S(\psi_{c}(b))(c\circ b)=\varepsilon(c\circ b)\varepsilon(b)c.

∎

5.2.
Finite dimensional Hopf algebras have positive basis

5.2.1.
Main result and consequences

The first main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
(S,s)
be a finite bijective solution to RPE. Then
H_{\ell}(s)
and
H_{r}(s)
have a basis which is both positive and
\Phi
-set theoretic.

The basis alluded to in
Theorem

5.4
will be refinement of the generating set in
Proposition

3.3
.

Recall that
Theorem

4.8
gives a correspondence between finite dimensional Hopf algebras and coefficient algebras of RPE solutions. Therefore we obtain as a consequence of
Theorem

5.4
that having the positive basis property and having a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis are equivalent in the finite dimensional setting.

In
[
16
]
finite dimensional Hopf algebras over the complexes
{\mathbb{C}}
with the positive basis property have been classified. It was namely shown that they are all isomorphic to a bicrossed product Hopf algebra
k[B]^{*}\bowtie k[N]
for some smashed pair of finite groups
(B,N,\triangleleft,\triangleright)
. More precisely, they show that given a positive basis
\mathcal{B}
of
H
, that one can rescale the basis elements so that
\mathcal{B}
corresponds to a group
G
with a factorisation
G=B.N
, uniquely determined by
H
. The existence of such rescaling, obtained in
[
16
, Section 4]
, however really makes use of the complex numbers in order to be able to work analytically on the spaces of bialgebra structures on a fixed vector space. Now, using the above together with the classification of finite bijective set-theoretic solutions
[
7
]
and
Example

3.5
we obtain that their classification also holds over arbitrary fields of characteristic
0
.

In summary, we have following generalization of
[
16
, Theorem 1]
.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
H
be a finite dimensional Hopf
k
-algebra with
{\rm char}(k)=0
. Then the following are equivalent:

•

H
has a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis,

•

H
has the positive basis property,

•

H\cong k[B]^{*}\bowtie k[N]
for a mashed pair of finite groups
(B,N,\triangleleft,\triangleright)
.

Moreover, if above holds, then the mashed pair is uniquely determined.

Combined with the content of
Section

5.1
we expect that the existence of a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis always imply the existence of a (potentially different) positive basis. However we do not expect the converse, because there exists examples of nearly positive Hopf algebras without a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis, such as the universal enveloping
U(\mathfrak{g})
of a finite dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, see
Corollary

6.8
.

Conjecture 5.6
.

Let
H
be a Hopf
k
-algebra with
{\rm char}(k)=0.
If
H
has a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis, then
H
has the positive basis property.

5.2.2.
Recollection of some structural results

To obtain a precise basis of the coefficient algebras, we will use some recent result by Colazzo-Okninski-Van Antwerpen
[

and Colazzo-Jespers-Kubat
[

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
(S,s)
be a finite bijective solution to the RPE. Then

is a left group, i.e. there exist a set

Moreover, for every

is bijective and either
\psi_{x}=\id_{S}
or
\psi_{x}
is fixed-point free.
Finally the set

\Psi=\left\{\left.\psi_{x}\right|x\in S\right\}=\left\{\left.\psi_{(e,1)}\right|e\in E\right\}

is a group (under composition).

Proof.

Applying
[
7
, Proposition 2.4]
to the set-theoretic solution of the PE given by

, we obtain that
(S,\circ)
is a left group.

Next, by
[
7
, Proposition 2.10]
applied to
(S,t)
, the map
\psi_{x}
is bijective for every
x\in S
. Combining
[
7
, Proposition 2.11]
with
[
6
, Lemma 2.4]
, we conclude that for each
x\in S
either
\psi_{x}=\id_{S}
or
\psi_{x}
is fixed-point free.

Finally, using (

) and the bijectivity of

, one checks that
\Psi
is closed under composition. Moreover,
\Psi
is finite, contains the identity (see
[
7
, Proposition 2.11]
), and each element is invertible; hence

is a group. The equality

\Psi=\left\{\left.\psi_{x}\right|x\in S\right\}=\left\{\left.\psi_{(e,1)}\right|e\in E\right\}

follows from
[

, Proposition 2.11]
.
∎

5.2.3.
Construction of the basis

From now on, since in a left group

coincides with the set of idempotents

, we will identify

with
(e,1)\in S
and simply write

.
Since

is a left group, the group

is isomorphic to

. As a consequence of the previous proposition, there exists an idempotent, which we denote by

We use

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
(S,s)
be a finite bijective solution to the RPE and

. Then:

\nu(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\left\{y\in S\mid\psi_{y}=\psi_{y^{\prime}}\right\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mu(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\left\{e\circ x^{\prime}\mid e\in E(S,\circ)\right\}.

Proof.

Let
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in S
and let
y\in\nu(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. Then
\psi_{y^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})=\psi_{y}(x^{\prime})
, and since
\psi_{y}
is bijective we obtain
\psi_{y}^{-1}\psi_{y^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})=x^{\prime}
.
Since
\Psi=\{\psi_{x}\mid x\in S\}

. Hence
\psi_{t}(x^{\prime})=x^{\prime}
, and by

.
Therefore

,

.

Now let

. Then
y^{\prime}\circ x=y^{\prime}\circ x^{\prime}
. Since
(S,\circ)
is a left group, we may write

. Write

,

, and

y^{\prime}\circ x=(e_{y^{\prime}},g_{y^{\prime}})\circ(e_{x},g_{x})=(e_{y^{\prime}},\,g_{y^{\prime}}g_{x})

and

y^{\prime}\circ x^{\prime}=(e_{y^{\prime}},g_{y^{\prime}}\circ g_{x^{\prime}})
. Let

. The reverse inclusion is immediate from

, for all

.
Hence
\mu(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\left\{e\circ x^{\prime}\mid e\in E(S,\circ)\right\}
.

, the dependence on

\nu_{\psi}(y^{\prime})=\left\{\,y\in S\mid\psi_{y}=\psi_{y^{\prime}}\right\},\qquad\mu_{\circ}(x^{\prime})=\left\{e\circ x^{\prime}\mid e\in E(S,\circ)\right\}.

(S,s)
be a finite bijective set-theoretic solution to the RPE. Now by

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
(S,s)
be a finite bijective set-theoretic solution of the RPE, written as

. Then the equivalence classes with respect to the retract relation all have the same cardinality.

Proof.

For

and

[e\circ g]=\{\,f\circ g\mid f\in E,\ \psi_{f}=\psi_{e}\,\},

and therefore

|[e\circ g]|=\left|\{\,f\in E\mid\psi_{f}=\psi_{e}\,\}\right|=|\nu_{\psi}(e)\cap E|.

Thus it suffices to show that

Let
e,f\in E
. By

5.7
, the set
\Psi=\{\psi_{x}\mid x\in E\}
is a group under composition. Hence

\psi_{e}^{-1}\psi_{f}\in\Psi

Define

Since

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
(S,s)
be a finite bijective set-theoretic solution of the RPE. Then

\displaystyle\left\{\left.\sum_{y\in\nu_{\psi}(y^{\prime})}S_{y\circ x^{\prime},y}\ \right|\ y^{\prime}\in\bar{E},\ x^{\prime}\in G\right\}.

To prove

A^{co-inv}=\left\{a\in A\ \left|\ (1\otimes a)R=1\otimes a\right.\right\}.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
(S,s)
be a finite bijective set-theoretic solution of the RPE. Then the algebra of left co-invariant is given by

A^{co-inv}=\operatorname{span}_{k}\left\{\left.\sum_{l\in d\circ S}S_{x,l}\ \right|\ x\in S,\,d\in\nu_{\psi}(1)\cap E\right\}.

In particular,

\dim_{k}A^{co-inv}\leq|S|\frac{|E|}{\bar{E}}.

Proof.

Write
a=\sum_{x,y\in S}\alpha_{x,y}S_{x,y}
. Then

\displaystyle(1\otimes a)R

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{u\in S}S_{u,u}\otimes\sum_{x,y\in S}\alpha_{x,y}S_{x,y}\right)\left(\sum_{c,d\in S}S_{\psi_{d}(c),c}\otimes S_{d\circ c,d}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,c,d,x,y\in S}\alpha_{x,y}\,S_{u,u}S_{\psi_{d}(c),c}\otimes S_{x,y}S_{d\circ c,d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{c,d,x\in S}\alpha_{x,d\circ c}\,S_{\psi_{d}(c),c}\otimes S_{x,d}.

Indeed,
S_{u,u}S_{\psi_{d}(c),c}=S_{\psi_{d}(c),c}
if

and

S_{x,y}S_{d\circ c,d}=S_{x,d}

0
otherwise.
Moreover,

\displaystyle 1\otimes a=\left(\sum_{c\in S}S_{c,c}\right)\otimes\left(\sum_{x,d\in S}\alpha_{x,d}S_{x,d}\right)=\sum_{c,x,d\in S}\alpha_{x,d}\,S_{c,c}\otimes S_{x,d}.

Since the elements

can match a term

6.1.
Relation (co)commutativity of Hopf algebras and solutions

A conceptual important property of set-theoretic solutions is that it has duality, as for multiplier Hopf algebras but unlike Hopf algebras, cf

Proposition 6.2

Let

Then

is commutative if and only if

Proof.

Write

. Then

. Note that if

s_{13}s_{23}=s_{23}s_{13}

A direct computation shows that the commutativity of

is equivalent to the two identities

x\theta_{y}(z)=xz,\qquad\theta_{y}\theta_{x}=\theta_{x}\theta_{y},\qquad\forall\,x,y,z\in S.

Now compute, for

t_{12}t_{13}(x,y,z)=\left(\theta_{y}\theta_{z}(x),\,y\,\theta_{z}(x),\,zx\right)

It follows that

Now, for

-set-theoretic basis.

\{\varepsilon^{-1}(b)b\mid b\in\mathcal{B}\}=G(H)
the group of group-like elements. Moreover,
H\cong k[G(H)]
.

Remark
.

As a consequence of
Theorem

6.7
and
Proposition

6.10
we obtain that a Hopf algebra which is a domain and posses a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis, must be isomorphic to a group algebra. Now, recall that if
G
is a group having a torsion element, then
k[G]
is not a domain. Indeed, a torsion element
g
yields a non-trivial idempotent
\hat{g}:=\frac{1}{o(g)}\sum_{i=1}^{o(g)}g^{i}
and hence a zero divisor. On other hand if
G
is torsion-free, it is a notorious conjecture of Kaplansky that
k[G]
is a domain. In conclusion, a basis as in
Proposition

6.10
can not exist if
H
is finite dimensional.

Finally, we point out that except for group algebras, a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis do not contain the unit element.

Proposition 6.11
.

Let
H
be a Hopf algebra and
\mathcal{B}
a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis of
H
such that
1\in B
. Then
H\cong k[G(H)]
with
G(H)
the group-like elements of
H
and
\{\varepsilon^{-1}(b)b\mid b\in\mathcal{B}\}=G(H).

Proof.

Take
b\in\mathcal{B}
. Then
\Phi_{H}(b\otimes 1)=\Delta(b)
. Hence, as
\mathcal{B}
is
\Phi
-set theoretic, this entails that
\Delta(b)=c\otimes d
for some
c,d\in\mathcal{B}
. Applying
\varepsilon\otimes\id
and
\id\otimes\varepsilon
, one obtains in the same way as in the proof of
Theorem

6.7
that
\varepsilon(c)d=b=\varepsilon(d)c
. Thus
D(b)=\lambda\,b\otimes b
for some
\lambda\in k^{*}
. The conclusion now follows from
Proposition

6.10
.
∎

6.2.2.
Proofs of the statements

To start we prove the statement on Hopf algebras that are a domain.

Proof of
Theorem

6.7
.

For
k
-vector spaces
V
and
W
and
T\in V\otimes W
, we say that
T
is written in left-reduced form if
T=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\otimes w_{i}

with
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in V
linearly independent and
n
minimal among all such expressions with linearly independent left factors.

Claim 1:

\Delta(y)
is a pure tensor in
H\otimes H
for each
y\in\mathcal{B}
.

Take a left-reduced form of
\Delta(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\otimes b_{i}
. Thus
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
are linearly independent and with
n
minimal. For any
z\in H
we have

(27)

\Phi_{H}(y\otimes z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\otimes b_{i}z.

Note that the left-reducedness implies that the
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
are linearly independent. Indeed, if the
b_{i}
satisfied a nontrivial relation, one could rewrite (
27
) with fewer summands and still keep the left factors independent.

Since
H
is a domain, right multiplication
\rho_{z}(h)=hz
is injective on
H
. This entails that also the
b_{1}z,\ldots,b_{n}z
are linearly independent. Now taking
z\in\mathcal{B}
and using that
\mathcal{B}
is
\Phi
-set theoretic we obtain Claim
1
.

Claim 2:
If
\Delta(y)
is a simple tensor, then
\Delta(y)=\varepsilon(y)^{-1}\,y\otimes y
.

Let
y\neq 0
and assume
\Delta(y)=u\otimes v
for
u,v\in H
. Applying
\varepsilon\otimes\id
and using that
(\varepsilon\otimes\id)\Delta=\id
gives

y=(\varepsilon\otimes\id)\Delta(y)=\varepsilon(u)\,v.

Hence
v=\varepsilon(u)^{-1}y
. Similarly applying
\id\otimes\varepsilon
and using that
(\id\otimes\varepsilon)\Delta=\id
, yields that
u=\varepsilon(v)^{-1}y
.
Therefore
\Delta(y)=\lambda\,y\otimes y
for some
\lambda\in k^{*}
. Finally, apply again
(\varepsilon\otimes\id)
to see that
y=(\varepsilon\otimes\id)\Delta(y)=\lambda\,\varepsilon(y)\,y.
Consequently,
\lambda\varepsilon(y)=1
, finishing the proof of Claim 2.
∎

Next, we consider the statement about Hopf algebras having a basis that is both
\Phi
-set theoretic and a coalgebra basis.

Proof of
Proposition

6.10
.

Take
b\in\mathcal{B}
. By the coalgebra-basis condition,
\Delta(b)=\lambda b^{\prime}\otimes b^{\prime\prime}
for some
b^{\prime},b^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
. Applying
\varepsilon\otimes\id
and
\id\otimes\varepsilon
, one obtains in the same way as in the proof of Claim 2 that
b^{\prime}=b=b^{\prime\prime}
and
\lambda=\varepsilon(b)^{-1}
.

With this property at hand we obtain that
H
must be a cocommative Hopf algebra. Indeed, take
h\in H
and decompose
h=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda_{b}b
into the basis. Then

\Delta(h)=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda_{b}\Delta(b)=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda_{b}\varepsilon(b)^{-1}b\otimes b=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\lambda_{b}\Delta^{op}(b)=\Delta^{op}(h).

Now, recall Cartier-Konstant-Milnor-Moore classification, saying that
H
is isomorphic to
U(P(H))\rtimes k[G(H)]
, where
\mathfrak{g}:=P(H)
is the Lie algebra consisting of primitive elements in
H
and
G:=G(H)
is the group of group-like elements. However the universal enveloping has no group-like elements, thus the Lie part must be trivial and
H\cong k[G(H)].

Finally, note that
\varepsilon^{-1}(b)b
is group-like for each
b\in\mathcal{B}
. This entails that
G(H)=\{\varepsilon^{-1}(b)b\mid b\in\mathcal{B}\}
.
∎

Finally, using the classification of cocommutative algebras, together with
Section

4.2
, we can relate classification of reachable cocommutative set-theoretic solutions of RPE to
\Phi
-set theoretic bases.

Proof of
Theorem

6.1
for reachable solutions.

Let
(S,s)
be a reachable set-theoretic solution of the RPE. By definition this means that there exists a Hopf algebra
H
and Hopf
H
-module
M
such that
s^{v}=\Phi_{M}\cong\Phi_{H}\otimes 1_{V}
for
V
a vector space with
\dim_{k}V=|S|-\dim_{k}H
. The fact that it reaches the linearisation of a set-theoretic solution implies that

must contain a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis
\mathcal{B}
such that
s\cong\phi_{\mathcal{B}}\times 1_{B(V)}
with
B(V)
a basis of
V
.

Thus it remains to show that
H
is a group algebra. Note that by
Lemma

6.6
the Hopf algebra
H
must be cocommutative. Therefore Cartier-Konstant-Milnor-Moore classification implies that

H\cong U(\mathfrak{g})\rtimes k[G]

with
\mathfrak{g}:=P(H)
the Lie algebra consisting of primitive elements in

and
G:=G(H)
the group of group-like elements. Hence by
Proposition

4.17
one has that

(28)

\Phi_{U(\mathfrak{g})}\#\Phi_{k[G]}=\Phi_{H}

However by
Corollary

6.8
the solution

can not be restricted to a set-theoretic solution. Thus

∎

7.1.
The abelian case: recollection on Fourier transform

Let
A
be a finite abelian group. From now on we assume that the ground field
k
satisfies
{\rm char}(k)\nmid|A|
and that
k
contains all
|A|
-roots of unity. As in
Example

4.12
we want to related
k[A]
with its dual through the Fourier transform. Concretely, define the
k
-linear map

(29)

\Theta_{A}:k[A^{\vee}]^{*}\longrightarrow k[A]:\delta_{\chi}\mapsto\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\chi(a^{-1})\,a.

The following fact is well-known.

Proposition 7.1
.

The map
\Theta_{A}
is an isomorphism of Hopf algebras. Its inverse
\Theta_{A}^{-1}:k[A]\to k[A^{\vee}]^{*}
is given on group elements
a\in A
by

(30)

\Theta_{A}^{-1}(a)=\sum_{\chi\in A^{\vee}}\chi(a)\,\delta_{\chi},

extended
k
-linearly.

We now show that the group solution
s(a\otimes b)=a\otimes ab
on
k[A]
seen in
Example

4.12
restrict to the solution from
Example

4.13
by choosing the basis
\{\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi})\mid\chi\in A^{\vee}\}
.

Proposition 7.2
.

For
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}\in A^{\vee}
one has

s\bigl((\Theta_{A}\otimes\Theta_{A})(\delta_{\chi_{1}}\otimes\delta_{\chi_{2}})\bigr)=\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi_{1}\chi_{2}^{-1}})\otimes\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi_{2}}).

Proof.

By definition

\displaystyle(s\circ(\Theta_{A}\otimes\Theta_{A}))(\delta_{\chi_{1}}\otimes\delta_{\chi_{2}})

\displaystyle=s\left(\frac{1}{|A|^{2}}\sum_{a,b\in A}\chi_{1}(a^{-1})\chi_{2}(b^{-1})\,(a\otimes b)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|A|^{2}}\sum_{a,b\in A}\chi_{1}(a^{-1})\chi_{2}(b^{-1})\,(a\otimes ab).

Now reindex the sum via
c:=ab
and hence
\chi_{2}(b^{-1})=\chi_{2}(c^{-1}a)=\chi_{2}(c^{-1})\chi_{2}(a)
. The latter sum becomes

\frac{1}{|A|^{2}}\sum_{a,c}(\chi_{2}\chi_{1}^{-1})(a)\chi_{2}(c^{-1})\,(a\otimes c)=\Bigl(\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a}(\chi_{2}\chi_{1}^{-1})(a)\,a\Bigr)\otimes\Bigl(\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{c}\chi_{2}(c^{-1})\,c\Bigr)

which exactly equals
\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi_{1}\chi_{2}^{-1}})\otimes\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi_{2}})
, as desired.
∎

For convenience of the non-expert reader we will now include a prove of
Proposition

7.1
. Recall that
k[A^{\vee}]^{*}
is equipped with the Hopf algebra structure dual to the group algebra
k[A^{\vee}]
:

\displaystyle\delta_{\chi}\delta_{\psi}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\chi,\psi}\,\delta_{\chi},\qquad 1=\sum_{\chi\in A^{\vee}}\delta_{\chi},

\displaystyle\Delta(\delta_{\chi})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta=\chi}\delta_{\alpha}\otimes\delta_{\beta},\qquad\varepsilon(\delta_{\chi})=\delta_{\chi,1},\qquad S(\delta_{\chi})=\delta_{\chi^{-1}}.

Proof of
Proposition

7.1
.

We will regularly use the character orthogonality relations which hold due to our assumptions on the ground field
k
:

(31)

\sum_{\chi\in A^{\vee}}\chi(x)=\begin{cases}|A|,&x=1,\\
0,&x\neq 1,\end{cases}\qquad\sum_{a\in A}\chi(a)=\begin{cases}|A|,&\chi=1,\\
0,&\chi\neq 1.\end{cases}

Algebra map.
By definition

\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi})\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\psi})=\frac{1}{|A|^{2}}\sum_{a,b\in A}\chi(a^{-1})\psi(b^{-1})\,ab.

The coefficient of a fixed
t\in A
equals

\frac{1}{|A|^{2}}\sum_{a\in A}\chi(a^{-1})\psi\bigl((a^{-1}t)^{-1}\bigr)=\frac{\psi(t^{-1})}{|A|^{2}}\sum_{a\in A}(\chi^{-1}\psi)(a).

By (
31
) this is
\frac{1}{|A|}\chi(t^{-1})
if
\chi=\psi
and
0
otherwise. Hence

\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi})\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\psi})=\delta_{\chi,\psi}\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi})=\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi}\delta_{\psi})
.
The orthogonality relations also yield

\Theta_{A}(1)=\Theta_{A}\Bigl(\sum_{\chi}\delta_{\chi}\Bigr)=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\Bigl(\sum_{\chi}\chi(a^{-1})\Bigr)a=1.

Coalgebra map.

We show
\Delta_{k[A]}(\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi}))=(\Theta_{A}\otimes\Theta_{A})\Delta_{k[A^{\vee}]^{*}}(\delta_{\chi})
and drop the indices of the coproducts.
The left-hand side is

\Delta(\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi}))=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\chi(a^{-1})\,(a\otimes a).

The right-hand side:

(\Theta_{A}\otimes\Theta_{A})\Delta(\delta_{\chi})=\sum_{\alpha\beta=\chi}\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\alpha})\otimes\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\beta})=\frac{1}{|A|^{2}}\sum_{\alpha\beta=\chi}\sum_{a,b\in A}\alpha(a^{-1})\beta(b^{-1})\,(a\otimes b).

Writing
\beta=\alpha^{-1}\chi
, the coefficient of a fixed
a\otimes b
is

\frac{1}{|A|^{2}}\sum_{\alpha\in A^{\vee}}\alpha(a^{-1})(\alpha^{-1}\chi)(b^{-1})=\frac{\chi(b^{-1})}{|A|^{2}}\sum_{\alpha\in A^{\vee}}\alpha(a^{-1}b)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|A|}\chi(a^{-1}),&a=b,\\
0,&a\neq b,\end{cases}

which equals
\Delta(\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi}))
.

Counit and antipode.

For the counit,

\varepsilon(\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi}))=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\chi(a^{-1})=\delta_{\chi,1}=\varepsilon(\delta_{\chi}).

For the antipode,

S(\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi}))=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\chi(a^{-1})\,a^{-1}=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\chi(a)\,a=\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi^{-1}})=\Theta_{A}(S(\delta_{\chi})).

Inverse.

We have show that
\Theta_{A}
is a Hopf algebra map and hence it remains to verify the inverse given as in
Equation

30
.

Fix
a\in A
. Then by (
29
),

\Theta_{A}(\Theta_{A}^{-1}(a))=\sum_{\chi\in A^{\vee}}\chi(a)\,\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi})=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{\chi\in A^{\vee}}\ \sum_{x\in A}\chi(a)\chi(x^{-1})\,x=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{x\in A}\Bigl(\sum_{\chi\in A^{\vee}}\chi(ax^{-1})\Bigr)x.

By orthogonality (
31
),
\sum_{\chi}\chi(ax^{-1})=|A|
iff
x=a
and
0
otherwise. Hence the above equals
a
.
Conversely, for
\delta_{\psi}
we compute using (
30
):

\displaystyle\Theta_{A}^{-1}(\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\psi}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{x\in A}\psi(x^{-1})\,\Theta_{A}^{-1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{x\in A}\psi(x^{-1})\sum_{\chi\in A^{\vee}}\chi(x)\delta_{\chi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\chi\in A^{\vee}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{x\in A}(\chi\psi^{-1})(x)\Bigr)\delta_{\chi}=\delta_{\psi},

again by (
31
). Thus
\Theta_{A}^{-1}
is indeed the inverse.
∎

7.2.
Matched pairs with a finite abelian factor

Suppsoe that
G

with

and

some subgroups. Consider the group solutions
\Phi_{k[G]}(g\otimes h)=g\otimes gh
and denote
\phi:=\Phi_{k[G]}
. The aim of this section is to construct a
\Phi
-set theoretic basis
\mathcal{B}
of

such that
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi\right|_{\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}}}
corresponds to the solution (
18

k[B]^{*}\bowtie k[N]
. However by

this is only possible if

abelian. Indeed

is cocommutative and thus by

6.6
so is
\Phi_{k[G]}
and its restriction to any

-set theoretic basis. In that case (

(32)

\phi_{B\bowtie N}\bigl((s,u)\,,\,(t,v)\bigr)=\Bigl(\bigl(s\,(t\triangleleft u^{-1})^{-1},u\bigr)\,,\,(t\triangleleft u^{-1},uv)\Bigr)

We first consider the case that

is finite abelian and denote it by

. We will make

appear using the maps introduced in
Section

7.1
.

7.2.1.
A dual like basis inside
k[A]\subset k[G]
:

for each
\chi\in A^{\vee}
denote

e_{\chi}:=\Theta_{A}(\delta_{\chi})=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\chi(a^{-1})\,a\in k[A].

Then by
Proposition

7.1
the standard relations

e_{\chi}e_{\psi}=\delta_{\chi,\psi}e_{\chi}
and
\sum_{\chi\in A^{\vee}}e_{\chi}=1
hold. Moreover,

(33)

b\,e_{\chi}=\chi(b)\,e_{\chi}.

for all

. Indeed,

b\,e_{\chi}=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{t\in B}\chi(t^{-1})\,bt=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{t\in B}\chi\bigl((b^{-1}t)^{-1}\bigr)\,t=\chi(b)\,\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{t\in B}\chi(t^{-1})\,t=\chi(b)\,e_{\chi}.

7.2.2.
Restriction of the group solution to the basis on
A^{\vee}\times N

We will now show that the basis
\mathcal{B}_{A^{\vee}}
is
\Phi_{k[G]}
-set theoretic, see
Proposition

7.3
below. First note that the right action
\triangleleft
of
N
on
A
induces a left action of
N
on
A^{\vee}
by pullback:

(34)

(u\cdot\chi)(a):=\chi(a\triangleleft u^{-1})

for

. This action permutes the Fourier idempotents
e_{\chi}
:

(35)

u\,e_{\chi}\,u^{-1}=e_{u\cdot\chi}.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
(A,N)
a mashed pair with
A
a finite abelian group acting trivially on
N
and let
G=A\rtimes N
. With notations as above, we have for all
\alpha,\beta\in A^{\vee}
and

,

(36)

\Phi_{k[G]}\bigl(e_{\alpha}u\otimes e_{\beta}v\bigr)=\bigl(e_{\alpha\,(u\cdot\beta)^{-1}}\,u\bigr)\ \otimes\ \bigl(e_{u\cdot\beta}\,uv\bigr).

In particular,
\Phi_{k[G]}
restricts to a set-theoretic map on
A^{\vee}\times N
,

\phi_{A^{\vee}\bowtie N}:\ (A^{\vee}\times N)\times(A^{\vee}\times N)\to(A^{\vee}\times N)\times(A^{\vee}\times N),

given by

(37)

\phi_{A^{\vee}\bowtie N}\bigl((\alpha,u),(\beta,v)\bigr)=\bigl((\alpha(u\cdot\beta)^{-1},u),\ (u\cdot\beta,uv)\bigr).

Before proving
Proposition

7.3
we explain why (
35
) holds. For this recall that by definition conjugation by
u
acts on
A
as the automorphism

a\mapsto a\triangleleft u^{-1}
. Thus
u\,e_{\chi}\,u^{-1}=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\chi(a^{-1})\,(a\triangleleft u^{-1}).
Now use that
(\cdot)\triangleleft u^{-1}
is a bijection of
A
to relabel the sum via
c=a\triangleleft u^{-1}
and obtain (
35
):

u\,e_{\chi}\,u^{-1}=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{c\in A}\chi\bigl((c\triangleleft u)^{-1}\bigr)\,c=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{c\in A}(u\cdot\chi)(c^{-1})\,c=e_{u\cdot\chi}.

Proof of
Proposition

7.3
.

Rolling out the definitions we readily obtain that:

(38)

\Phi_{k[G]}\bigl(e_{\alpha}u\otimes e_{\beta}v\bigr)=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\alpha(a^{-1})\,au\otimes(au)(e_{\beta}v).

By (
35
) and subsequently (
33
), we have

(au)(e_{\beta}v)=a\,e_{u\cdot\beta}\,(uv)=(u\cdot\beta)(a)\,e_{u\cdot\beta}\,(uv).

Substituting into (
38
) yields

\Phi_{k[G]}\bigl(e_{\alpha}u\otimes e_{\beta}v\bigr)=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\alpha(a^{-1})\,(u\cdot\beta)(a)\,au\otimes e_{u\cdot\beta}\,uv.

Since
\alpha(a^{-1})(u\cdot\beta)(a)=\bigl(\alpha(u\cdot\beta)^{-1}\bigr)(a^{-1})
, we can rewrite following sum as

\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\alpha(a^{-1})(u\cdot\beta)(a)\,au=\left(\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\bigl(\alpha(u\cdot\beta)^{-1}\bigr)(a^{-1})\,a\right)u=e_{\alpha(u\cdot\beta)^{-1}}\,u.

Substituting this in the obtained expression for
\Phi_{k[G]}\bigl(e_{\alpha}u\otimes e_{\beta}v\bigr)
, finishes the proof.
∎

7.2.3.
A solution on
A\times N

To obtain a solution on the set
A\times N
instead of the canonical
A^{\vee}\times N
we need to invoke a non-canonical isomorphism
\iota:A\cong A^{\vee}.
Such as isomorphism is equivalent to the choice of a nondegenerate bicharacter

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:A\times A\rightarrow k^{*}.

The associated isomorphism
A\cong A^{\vee}
is given by
s\mapsto\chi_{s}
where
\chi_{s}(t):=\langle s,t\rangle
. Then for
s\in A
consider

e_{s}:=e_{\chi_{s}}=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\left\langle s,a^{-1}\right\rangle a\in k[A].

In this setting one would consider the basis
\{e_{s}\,u\mid s\in A,u\in N\}
of
k[G]
. To however be able to recover the desired solution (
32
) one needs an
N
-equivariant isomorphism:

(39)

\iota(a\triangleleft u^{-1})=u\cdot\iota(a)\qquad(a\in A,\ u\in N),

where
u\cdot(-)
is the pullback action (
34
). In that case for
t\in A
we have that
ue_{\iota(t)}=e_{\iota(t)\triangleleft u^{-1}}u
using
Equation

35
. Such isomorphism unfortunately do not always exists.

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
(A,N,\triangleleft,\triangleright)
be a mashed pair of groups with
A
abelian. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

There exists an
N
-equivariant isomorphism
\iota:A\overset{\sim}{\to}A^{\vee}
,

(2)

there exists a nondegenerate bicharacter

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:A\times A\to k^{*}
such that

\langle a\triangleleft u^{-1},\ b\rangle=\langle a,\ b\triangleleft u\rangle\qquad(a,b\in A,\ u\in N).

(3)

A
and
A^{\vee}
are isomorphic as
{\mathbb{Z}}[N]
-modules.

Proof.

If
\iota
is given, define
\langle a,b\rangle:=\iota(a)(b)
.
Then (
39
) is equivalent to the displayed invariance by evaluating both sides at
b
and using (
34
).
Conversely, given such a pairing, define
\iota(a)(b):=\langle a,b\rangle
. Non-degeneracy makes
\iota
into an isomorphism and the invariance yields (
39
). Clearly also (
1
) and (

) are equivalent.
∎

Now suppose that there exists an
N
-equivariant isomorphism
\iota
.
Then the bijection

A\times N\xrightarrow{\sim}A^{\vee}\times N:(a,u)\mapsto(\iota(a),u)

transports (

) to a cocommutative solution on

.
Concretely, one obtains as desired the solution

\phi\bigl((a,u),(b,v)\bigr)=\bigl((a\,(b\triangleleft u^{-1})^{-1},\ u),\ (b\triangleleft u^{-1},\ uv)\bigr).

7.3.
Group algebra of infinite groups and non-existence criterion

Our aim now is to prove that a torsion-free group

-set theoretic basis other than
\lambda.G=\{\lambda g\mid g\in G\}
for some scalar
