Durvalumab plus anlotinib versus durvalumab alone as maintenance treatment in extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer (DURABLE): a multicenter, randomized, phase II trial and biomarker analysis
Authors: Bo Zhang, Runbo Zhong, Chunlei Shi, Tianqing Chu, Wei Zhang, Huimin Wang, Xin Gan, Zhihong Zhang, Changbin Zhu, Xing Li, Wenzhong Su, Juan Li, Yanwei Zhang, Baohui Han, Hua Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough first-line immunotherapy plus chemotherapy has made substantial progress in extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer (ES-SCLC), the survival benefit remains limited. DURABLE was a prospective, multicenter, open-label, randomized, phase II trial (NCT04985851) that evaluated the efficacy and safety of durvalumab plus anlotinib as consolidative maintenance therapy following first-line durvalumab plus platinum-etoposide chemotherapy in ES-SCLC. The primary endpoint was blinded independent central review-assessed progression-free survival (PFS) from randomization, with a two-sided p-value of <0.20 considered statistically significant. 66 patients were randomly assigned to maintenance therapy with durvalumab plus anlotinib (n = 34) or durvalumab alone (n = 32). Durvalumab plus anlotinib significantly improved PFS compared with durvalumab alone, with median PFS from randomization of 5.4 months versus 1.9 months (HR = 0.64; 80% CI, 0.44-0.94; p = 0.12). The incidence of grade 3-4 treatment-related adverse events in the combination and monotherapy groups was 24.2% and 12.5%, respectively. Patients with impaired antigen presenting capacity or low bTMB tended to show improved outcomes with combined maintenance therapy. While similar efficacy between the two groups was observed in patients with high antigen presenting capacity or high bTMB. These findings suggest that durvalumab plus anlotinib might be an effective and well-tolerated maintenance treatment option in ES-SCLC.Subject terms: Small-cell lung cancer, Phase II trials, Cancer immunotherapy

Introduction

Small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) is an aggressive neuroendocrine malignancy that accounts for about 15% of all lung cancer cases, with a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate of less than 7%1,2. Approximately 70% of patients with SCLC have developed extensive-stage SCLC (ES-SCLC) at the time of diagnosis due to its characteristics of poor differentiation, rapid proliferation, and early development of widespread metastases3. For over three decades, the standard first-line treatment for ES-SCLC has remained platinum-based chemotherapy (platinum-etoposide regimen)4,5. Despite its high response rate, most patients tend to experience rapid progression or relapse, leading to a median OS of only 9–11 months6–8. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) including durvalumab, atezolizumab, adebrelimab, or serplulimab in combination with platinum-etoposide chemotherapy as first-line treatment have prolonged OS of these patients, improvement is rather limited6,7,9,10. For patients with ES-SCLC who achieved objective response or stable disease (SD) after four to six cycles of first-line treatment, timely subsequent consolidative maintenance therapy may be a suitable option for delaying disease progression and further prolonging survival11–13. However, the current maintenance therapy with immunologic mono-therapy is inefficient and other attempts, including Rovalpituzumab Tesirine (Rova-T), programmed cell death-1 (PD-1) combined with cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4) and poly (adenosine diphosphate-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, or bevacizumab, have been largely unsuccessful14–18. Therefore, novel maintenance treatment regimens are warranted to address the unmet medical needs of patients with ES-SCLC.

Increasing evidence has revealed the synergistic anti-tumor effect of ICIs and anti-angiogenic agents19–22. Anti-angiogenic agents could restore the immune-supportive microenvironment and promote vessel normalisation19. In addition, anti-angiogenic therapy helps to overcome resistance to cancer immunotherapy by reducing immunosuppression and promoting effector cell infiltration, dendritic cell maturation, and T cell activation21. Anlotinib, a novel multitarget tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), exhibits anti-tumor growth and anti-angiogenic properties by targeting vascular endothelial growth factor receptor, fibroblast growth factor receptor, platelet-derived growth factor receptor, and stem cell growth factor receptor23. It has been approved by the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) of China as a third-line or subsequent treatment for SCLC based on the randomized, phase II ALTER 1202 trial24. In this trial, anlotinib mono-therapy as a third-line or subsequent treatment significantly prolongs progression-free survival (PFS; median: 4.1 vs. 0.7 months) and OS (median: 7.3 vs. 4.9 months) in patients with SCLC compared to placebo24. Moreover, the combination of anlotinib and ICI has exhibited encouraging anti-tumor activities and a manageable safety profile in multiple solid tumors25–27.

In this work, based on the potential synergistic effect of ICIs and anti-angiogenic therapy, the nonoverlapping toxicity of separate agents, and the unmet medical need for maintenance treatment in ES-SCLC, we propose our hypothesis that anlotinib combined with durvalumab may serve as a potential maintenance treatment in ES-SCLC patients and design this multicenter, randomized, phase II trial.

Results

Patient characteristics

From September 2021 to June 2023, 102 patients were screened and 90 were eligible for this trial and received induction therapy. 66 patients underwent randomisation (intention-to-treat [ITT] population; combination group, n = 34; mono-therapy group, n = 32). Disease progression during induction therapy was the main reason for non-randomisation to maintenance therapy (Fig. 1). One patient in the combination group didn’t receive any study treatment after randomisation, thus excluded from the safety analysis (Fig. 1). As of the data cut-off on June 13, 2024, the median follow-up duration from randomisation assignment was 18.0 months (range, 1.9–27.6). At the data cutoff, 15 patients (combination group, n = 8; mono-therapy group, n = 7) continued to receive maintenance therapy. Disease progression was the main reason for treatment discontinuation (Fig. 1). The median number of treatment cycles of durvalumab from randomisation in the combination and mono-therapy groups was 6.0 (range, 1.0–28.0) and 2.0 (range, 1.0–32.0), respectively. The median exposure cycle was 5.0 (range, 1.0–28.0) for anlotinib from randomisation.

Baseline characteristics were generally balanced between the two groups, except for a slightly higher proportion of male patients (96.9% vs. 73.5%), smokers (87.5% vs. 76.5%), and those with liver metastasis (18.8% vs. 8.8%) in the mono-therapy group (Table 1). The median age was 66.7 years (range, 41.3–75.6) in the combination group and 64.3 years (range, 50.7–72.9) in the mono-therapy group. Additionally, 5.9% and 6.3% of patients in the combination and mono-therapy groups were reported to have baseline brain metastasis, respectively. Most patients in the two groups had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 1 (91.2% vs. 93.8%; Table 1).

Efficacy

The primary endpoint was analyzed after blinded independent central review (BICR)-assessed disease progression or death occurred in 48 of the 66 patients. The BICR-assessed median PFS from randomisation was 5.4 months (95% confidence intervals [CI], 3.7–9.2) with combination therapy and 1.9 months (95% CI, 1.8–2.8) with mono-therapy (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.64; 80% CI, 0.44–0.94; p = 0.12; Fig. 2a). The 6-month PFS rate assessed by BICR was 46.9% (95% CI, 29.1–62.8) with combination therapy and 25.8% (95% CI, 12.2–41.8) with mono-therapy. The 12-month and 18-month PFS rates were 25.1% (95% CI, 11.2–41.9) versus 25.8% (95% CI, 12.2–41.8) and 20.1% (95% CI, 7.4–37.2) versus 21.5% (95% CI, 8.9–37.6), respectively. Investigator-assessed PFS confirmed these results (median: 5.4 months vs. 1.9 months, HR = 0.63; 80% CI, 0.44–0.92; p = 0.13; Supplementary Fig. 1).

The post-hoc median PFS from the start of induction therapy was 9.0 months (95% CI, 7.2–13.1) in patients receiving combination therapy versus 5.6 months (95% CI, 4.8–6.2) in patients receiving mono-therapy (HR = 0.66; 80% CI, 0.45–0.96; p = 0.15) as assessed by investigator, with 12-month PFS rates of 43.5% (95% CI, 26.2–59.7) versus 25.8% (95% CI, 12.2–41.8) and 18-month PFS rates of 18.5% (95% CI, 6.4–35.4) versus 25.8% (95% CI, 12.2–41.8; Supplementary Fig. 2a). The restricted mean survival time (RMST) for investigator-assessed PFS from the start of induction therapy was 11.8 months (95% CI, 9.4–14.2) with combination therapy versus 9.7 months (95% CI, 7.0–12.4) with mono-therapy (p = 0.02).

At the data cutoff, 58.8% of patients in the combination group and 62.5% of patients in the mono-therapy group received subsequent other anticancer therapies (Supplementary Table 1). In the mono-therapy group, five patients received anlotinib as the first subsequent treatment, and four patients received it as the second subsequent treatment. Additionally, four patients in each group continued maintenance therapy after disease progression. At the data cutoff, 16 (47.1%) of 34 patients in the combination group and 18 (56.3%) of 32 patients in the mono-therapy group had died. The median OS from randomisation was 17.4 months (95% CI, 13.3-not reached [NR]) in the combination group and 12.4 months (95% CI, 10.0-NR) in the mono-therapy group (HR = 0.65; 80% CI, 0.42–1.02; p = 0.22; Fig. 2b). The 6-month OS rates in the two groups were 91.0% (95% CI, 74.6–97.0) and 77.4% (95% CI, 58.4–88.5), respectively. The 12-month and 18-month OS rates were 71.5% (95% CI, 52.2–84.1) versus 50.8% (95% CI, 31.2–67.5) and 44.7% (95% CI, 24.7–62.9) versus 36.7% (95% CI, 18.4–55.2), respectively.

The median OS, 12-month and 18-month OS rates from the start of induction therapy in the two groups were 20.4 months (95% CI, 16.1-NR) versus 15.4 months (95% CI, 12.9-NR, HR = 0.65; 80% CI, 0.41–1.01; p = 0.21), 78.2% (95% CI, 59.6–89.0) versus 70.2% (95% CI, 50.4–83.3), and 67.3% (95% CI, 47.6–81.0) versus 46.5% (95% CI, 27.1–63.9), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2b). The RMST for OS from the start of induction therapy was 20.0 months (95% CI, 17.4–22.6) with combination therapy versus 17.4 months (95% CI, 14.3–20.5) with mono-therapy (p = 0.12).

The post-hoc ORR (20.6% [95% CI, 8.7–37.9] vs. 6.3% [95% CI, 0.8–20.8]; p = 0.15) and disease control rate (DCR; 82.4% [95% CI, 65.5–93.2] vs. 40.6% [95% CI, 23.7–59.4]; p < 0.001) during maintenance therapy favoured combination therapy compared with mono-therapy (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Safety

The safety overview during the maintenance phase is summarised in Supplementary Table 2. Of the 65 patients in the safety set (SS) population, 32 (97.0%) in the combination group and 27 (84.4%) in the mono-therapy group experienced at least one treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) of any grade, of which 10 (30.3%) and 7 (21.9%) were reported as grade ≥3. Any grade and grade ≥3 treatment-related AEs (TRAEs) were reported in 32 (97.0%) and 8 (24.2%) patients in the combination group, and 24 (75.0%) and 4 (12.5%) patients in the mono-therapy group. TRAEs leading to dose reduction were reported in 9 (27.3%) patients in the combination group versus 0 (0.0%) in the mono-therapy group, with treatment discontinuation in 0 (0.0%) versus 1 (3.1%) patient. During the maintenance phase, immune-related adverse events (irAEs) occurred in 7 (21.2%) patients in the combination group and 5 (15.6%) patients in the mono-therapy group, all of these events were grade 1 or 2. The most common irAEs in the combination and mono-therapy groups were hypothyroidism (12.1% vs. 3.1%), hyperthyroidism (6.1% vs. 0.0%), and dermatitis (0.0% vs. 6.3%). During the maintenance phase, 6 (18.2%) patients in the combination group and 3 (9.4%) patients in the mono-therapy group reported bleeding events of any cause. Detailed information on these bleeding events is provided in Supplementary Table 3.

The most frequently occurring TRAEs of any grade in the combination and mono-therapy groups were anemia (60.6% vs. 59.4%), blood thyroid stimulating hormone increased (36.4% vs. 3.1%), and platelet count decreased (21.2% vs. 25.0%). Besides, anemia (9.1% vs. 3.1%), hepatic function (6.1% vs. 3.1%), and platelet count decreased (0.0% vs. 6.3%) were the most common grade ≥3 TRAEs (Table 2).

Exploration of biomarkers in tissue transcriptome and ctDNA

Tumor biopsies from 24 patients (combination group, n = 16; mono-therapy group, n = 8; Supplementary Table 6) underwent whole-transcriptome sequencing. Based on Cox proportional hazards models and the interaction p-value, genes with differential prognostic value between the two treatment groups were identified, showing that deficiency of MHC class I-mediated antigen presentation (PSMA1, PSMB2, PSMB8, PSMC4, and PSMC6) and enrichment of platelet activation, together with collagen adhesion (PRKCB, NHLRC2, and COLA2), were associated with clinical benefits of combination therapy (Fig. 3a). Patients stratified by the median expression level of each corresponding gene showed that low expression of the MHC-I gene PSMB2 or high expression of the platelet-related gene NHLRC2 appeared to have reduced progression risk with combination therapy (Fig. 3b, c). Conversely, high PSMB2 or low NHLRC2 expression may favor durvalumab mono-therapy (Fig. 3d, e). Similar trends were observed for other MHC-I antigen presentation- and platelet activation-related genes (Supplementary Fig. 4). Molecular subtyping revealed that no significant PFS differences were observed among the different subtypes within either the combination or mono-therapy group (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) was detected in 29 patients (combination group, n = 16; mono-therapy group, n = 13; Supplementary Table 7) at baseline (T0). Patients with baseline blood tumor mutational burden (bTMB) below the median value of 6.67 mutations per megabase were classified as bTMB-low (combination group, n = 10; mono-therapy group, n = 7), while those with bTMB >6.67 muts/mb were classified as bTMB-high (combination group, n = 6; mono-therapy group, n = 6). Patients with baseline bTMB-low seemed to benefit from durvalumab plus anlotinib, with reduced progression (median PFS: 8.7 vs. 1.8 months, p = 0.005) and mortality (median OS: 9.6 vs. 6.4 months, p = 0.019) (Fig. 4a, b). Patients with bTMB-high appeared to show numerically improved OS (median: 18.1 vs. 6.4 months) with durvalumab mono-therapy (Supplementary Fig. 6). ctDNA dynamics, defined by log10 hGE in the 23 patients with continuous samples, might correlate with outcomes. Sustained responders, defined as patients with continued ctDNA decline or stabilization from first assessment (T1) to before randomisation (T2; log10 hGE at T2 ≤ T1), might have reduced recurrence with combination therapy (median PFS: 9.2 vs. 3.1 months, p = 0.01; Fig. 4c, d). Rapid relapsers, defined as patients with increased ctDNA concentration from T1 to T2 (log10 hGE at T2 > T1), showed no significant PFS difference between treatments (median PFS: 3.8 vs. 1.9 months, Fig. 4e, f). Similar trends were observed when ctDNA dynamics were assessed using maximum variant allele frequency (Mvaf; Supplementary Fig. 7a–d).

Discussion

This multicenter, randomized, phase II trial evaluated the efficacy and safety of maintenance therapy with ICI and anlotinib (an anti-angiogenic agent) in ES-SCLC patients. Our findings suggested that the combination of durvalumab plus anlotinib as maintenance therapy showed significantly improved PFS (median: 5.4 vs. 1.9 months), ORR (20.6% vs. 6.3%), and DCR (82.4% vs. 40.6%) compared with durvalumab alone, with a trend for improved OS (median: 17.4 vs. 12.4 months). Additionally, the safety profile of durvalumab plus anlotinib was manageable, with no new safety signals observed. These findings indicate that the combination of an ICI and anlotinib seems to be a reasonable maintenance treatment option in ES-SCLC patients.

As previously reported, maintenance treatment with ICIs alone just showed a slight improvement in median PFS16,17. Efficient consolidative treatment in patients with ES-SCLC who do not experience disease progression after induction therapy still represents an unmet medical need. Current exploration with antibody-drug conjugate (rovalpituzumab tesirine) failed to reveal OS benefit in the maintenance setting, with trials terminated early mainly due to high toxicity14. Maintenance treatment with multitarget anti-angiogenic agents, including pazopanib and sunitinib was also previously investigated. In the KCSG-LU12-07 study, maintenance treatment with pazopanib significantly prolonged PFS12. However, the benefit of PFS has not translated into improved OS due to toxicity, with 52.1% of patients in the pazopanib arm experiencing dose interruption and reduction due to AEs. In the CALGB 30504 study, maintenance sunitinib slightly improved the median PFS of 1.6 months (3.7 vs. 2.1 months)13. In our study, the addition of anlotinib improved median PFS and OS with a 3.5-month and 5.0-month extension, respectively. The potential explanation may be the synergistic effect of anti-angiogenesis and ICI therapy, induced by reprogramming of the tumor microenvironment19–22. In a recent study, improved PFS and OS were observed with poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitor (PARPi), but this slight benefit was only observed in SLFN11-positive patients28. In our study, crossover was permitted, however, only 28.1% of patients in the mono-therapy group were treated with anlotinib after disease progression. At the cut-off date, seven patients did not experience disease progression. In China, anlotinib was only approved as third- or further-line treatment, patients were usually administered chemotherapy as second-line therapy24. In addition, subjects who experienced 2 or more lines of treatment may not be the real candidates for anlotinib due to the corresponding contraindication.

This trial was originally designed to incorporate anlotinib into first-line treatment with chemotherapy and durvalumab, followed by maintenance anlotinib and durvalumab. However, toxicity concerns deterred this consideration. The ETER-701 trial investigated the efficacy and safety of PD-L1 inhibitors with anlotinib and standard chemotherapy in treatment-naive ES-SCLC29. In the ETER-701 trial, OS from the start of induction therapy was similar to our study (median: 19.3 months and 20.4 months); however, the incidence of grade ≥3 TRAEs and TRAEs leading to treatment discontinuation reached 93.1% and 16.7%, respectively. To mitigate potential toxicity, anlotinib was incorporated only during the maintenance phase of our study. Consequently, only 31.1% of our patients experienced grade ≥3 TRAEs, and 4.4% patients discontinued treatment due to TRAEs from the start of induction therapy, which was dramatically lower than that reported in the ETER-701 trial29. In addition, SCLC is usually presented with vascular invasion and bleeding symptoms (e.g., hemoptysis), and the addition of an anti-angiogenic agent to first-line treatment may improve the risk of hemorrhage2. After reducing the large burden of cancer, anlotinib may serve as a safe maintenance treatment option. In our study, 27.3% of patients experienced dose reduction during consolidative maintenance therapy, which was higher than the previous report (8.2%)30. Timely dose titration may help to manage AEs. In our study, dose adjustment was at the investigators’ discretion regardless of grade. The observed safety profiles of anlotinb and durvalumab were similar to previous reports when administered respectively31–33. No new safety signals were observed.

A key finding was that the deficiency in the MHC-I-mediated antigen presentation pathway, particularly PSMB2, may predict better outcomes with durvalumab and anlotinib combination therapy. Proteasome-related genes, PSMB2 and PSMB8, are critical for processing tumor antigens into peptides that activate CD8+ T cells34,35. In SCLC, loss of antigen-presenting machinery may reduce chemo-immunotherapy efficacy. Anlotinib enhances the anti-tumor microenvironment by promoting M1-like macrophage and dendritic cell infiltration36,37. This consolidative maintenance therapy may leverage a reactivated pro-inflammatory microenvironment to target SCLC with impaired antigen processing. Additionally, platelet activation, particularly α granules, is crucial in angiogenesis38. NHLRC2, essential for platelet α granule function, may characterize patients benefiting from this combined therapy.

ctDNA may provide preliminary insights into selecting maintenance treatments. High bTMB, linked to increased neoantigen release and enhanced immunogenicity39,40, is associated with similar efficacy of mono-therapy and combined therapy. However, low baseline bTMB was associated with longer PFS (median: 8.7 vs. 1.8 months) and OS (median: 9.6 vs. 6.4 months) in patients treated with durvalumab plus anlotinib. As discussed above, anlotinib may restore the antigen presenting capacity, especially for patients with low TMB (neoantigens) and lower antigen presenting capacity. Similar findings observed in triple negative breast cancer patients receiving anti-PD-L1 and anlotinib further supported this hypothesis41. Stratification by ctDNA dynamics revealed that sustained responders benefited from combined therapy, whereas rapid progressors experienced swift disease progression regardless of maintenance treatment. These findings suggest that ctDNA-based stratification may help optimise treatment strategies and refine patient selection. Further validation is warranted.

The study is limited by its relatively small sample size, therefore, further large-scale, randomized, phase III trials are warranted to verify the data. Due to the limited sample size, exploratory findings on ctDNA and tumor expression profile need to be further verified. In addition, this is an open-label study. However, the primary endpoint was assessed by BICR to minimise investigator bias. Furthermore, considering the slight imbalance in baseline characteristics between the two groups—in terms of sex, smoking status, and presence of liver metastases—likely due to the relatively small sample size, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Notably, the proportion of never-smokers in our study was higher than that reported in global cohorts (4.5%–8.0%) but was consistent with that reported in Chinese cohorts (20.8%–22.0%), consistent with prior reports of a higher prevalence of never-smokers among East Asian patients with SCLC compared with White populations.9,10,31,42,43 Moreover, the low statistical power may lead to imprecise estimation of the primary endpoint, and thus the results need to be interpreted with caution. Lastly, all patients enrolled in our study were Chinese patients, and the results need to be validated in a broader population. However, the efficacy and safety of durvalumab or similar multitarget tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as sunitinib, have been extensively validated worldwide, which to some extent supports the generalizability of our findings6,44.

In conclusion, this multicenter, randomized, phase II trial demonstrated that the combination of durvalumab and anlotinib as maintenance therapy significantly improved PFS compared with durvalumab alone in patients with ES-SCLC, with a trend for improved OS, although there was no obvious difference in long-term survival. Additionally, durvalumab plus anlotinib offered significant ORR and DCR benefits compared to durvalumab alone. These findings, along with an acceptable safety profile, support further evaluation of durvalumab plus anlotinib as a potential maintenance treatment option for ES-SCLC. Biomarker-based patient stratification may aid more precise decision making. Further validation in large-scale, randomized, phase III trials is warranted.

Methods

Study design and patients

The DURABLE study was a multicenter, open-label, randomized, phase II trial conducted in five sites in China. This trial had been registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (link: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04985851?term=NCT04985851&viewType=Card&rank=1; number: NCT04985851; date: June 7, 2021) and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice Guidelines. The protocol was approved by the ethical committee of Shanghai Chest Hospital (number, IS2150), and all patients provided written informed consent.

Eligible patients were aged 18–75 years with treatment-naïve histologically or cytologically confirmed ES-SCLC with an ECOG performance status of 0-1, having at least one measurable lesion per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours version 1.1 (RECIST 1.1). Patients should not experience disease progression after four cycles of cisplatin-based chemotherapy with durvalumab. Patients who achieved a complete response after induction therapy, resulting in no measurable lesions, were excluded from the study. Additionally, patients with only non-measurable lesions following induction therapy were also excluded. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria can be found in the study protocol (Supplementary Note 1).

Treatments and randomisation

Eligible patients were administered intravenous durvalumab (1500 mg, day 1), etoposide (80–100 mg/m2, day 1–3), and cisplatin (75–80 mg/m2, day 1) or carboplatin (area under curve = 5–6, day 1) of a 21-day cycle as induction therapy. After four cycles of induction therapy, patients who did not experience disease progression and had at least one measurable lesion were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive combination treatment (combination group) of durvalumab (1500 mg intravenously on day 1 of a 28-day cycle) plus anlotinib (12 mg orally on day 1–14 of a 21-day cycle) or durvalumab (1500 mg intravenously on day 1 of a 28-day cycle) alone (mono-therapy group) as maintenance therapy. Maintenance therapy was continued until disease progression, death, unacceptable toxicity, withdrawal of consent, or investigator decision, whichever occurred first. Continuation of maintenance therapy after disease progression was permitted if patients were judged by the investigator as deriving clinical benefits from the study drug. During the maintenance treatment phase, dose reduction was permitted for anlotinib but not for durvalumab; however, dose interruptions of durvalumab were allowed for management of toxicity. Anlotinib dose reductions may take place whenever toxicity was not controlled with optimal supportive care during the study. Two additional dose levels were available: 8 mg and 10 mg. The details on dose modifications are reported in the study protocol (pages 51–53). Patients who experienced disease progression in the mono-therapy group were permitted to receive anlotinib at the investigator’s discretion. Prophylactic cranial irradiation and thoracic radiotherapy were permitted at the investigator’s discretion.

Outcomes and assessments

The primary endpoint was BICR-assessed PFS of maintenance therapy, defined as the time from randomisation to disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Secondary endpoints included 12-month and 18-month PFS (from randomisation or start of induction therapy), OS (time from randomisation or start of induction therapy to death of any cause), 12-month and 18-month OS (from randomisation or start of induction therapy), and safety. The exploratory endpoint was biomarker analyses in the tissue transcriptome and ctDNA.

Efficacy assessment with computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging was performed every 6–8 weeks. Survival was evaluated every 3 months after discontinuation of study therapy. Safety was evaluated by recording the causality, incidence, and severity of AEs. AEs were assessed and graded during the induction and maintenance phases and 90 days after the last dose of maintenance therapy according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0.

Sample collection and analysis

Tumor tissue samples were collected at baseline. Peripheral blood was collected at four time points: baseline (T0), first assessment (T1), before randomisation (T2), and disease progression after randomisation (T3). From these blood samples, circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) was isolated and subjected to comprehensive genomic profiling covering 571 cancer-related genes (AmoyDx, China) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), as previously described45. The bTMB was calculated based on the number of detected somatic mutations as previously reported46. Meanwhile, RNA sequencing was performed on a tumor biopsy using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform after RNA extraction and cDNA library preparation (as described in detail in Supplementary Note 2).

ctDNA sequencing

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) was extracted from plasma samples using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Blood samples underwent plasma separation and ctDNA extraction via the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Peripheral blood lymphocyte DNA was extracted with the AmoyDx Blood and Leukocyte DNA Kit (AmoyDx, China) and used as a control. We conducted targeted NGS on these samples using a 571-gene cancer panel (AmoyDx, China) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000, targeting a depth range of 5600–17,500 times. Sequencing outputs, saved as FASTQ files, were processed with ADXMaster-DNA_v0.4.3 (AmoyDx, China). After adapter trimming and quality filtering, the data were aligned to hg19 using BWA-MEM. Duplicate removal was performed using FormatFastq (AmoyDx, China), with ctDNA duplicates identified via SSBC. Variant calling for single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and indels was executed using SSBC-VarScan and IndelCaller, respectively, and annotations were added with Annotator (AmoyDx, China). We classified ctDNA as positive (≥2 mutations) or negative (≤1 mutation) based on the detectability of somatic mutations; the dynamics of the maximum somatic allele mutation frequency (MSAF) were also tracked at baseline and post-treatment. Three criteria for competent mutations were applied: (i) somatic mutations only (not germline), (ii) mutations located in coding sequences, and (iii) nonsynonymous SNVs/indels. Then, blood-based tumor mutational burden (bTMB) was computed as the number of competent mutations divided by the length of the genomic region covered by the gene panel.

RNA sequencing of tissue and PBMCs

Total RNA was extracted from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks and peripheral blood mono-nuclear cells (PBMCs) using the AmoyDx FFPE RNA Kits (Amoy Diagnostics, Xiamen, China), following the manufacturer’s protocols. RNA concentration was quantified using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and RNA integrity was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with the RNA HS Kit (Cat. #5067-1504/5067-1511, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Based on the DV200 value obtained from the Bioanalyzer, RNA fragmentation was optimized and conducted at 95 °C for 0–15 min.

The fragmented RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA), followed by strand-specific library preparation using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (Cat. #E7760L, NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA). The resulting cDNA libraries were then sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

ctDNA quantitation

ctDNA levels were quantified as haploid genome equivalents per milliliter of plasma (hGE/mL). This was calculated by multiplying the total cfDNA concentration by the mean allele fraction of somatic mutations, with the final value expressed as the base-10 logarithm (log hGE/mL), as previously established47.

Statistical analysis

The sample size consideration was based on the PFS of maintenance therapy. Median PFS was expected to be 3.0 months in the mono-therapy group and 5.0 months in the combination group. Assuming an exponential distribution of PFS, this corresponded to a hazard ratio (HR) of approximately 0.60. With a two-sided significance level of 20%, approximately 64 patients were required for 50 PFS events to provide 70% power to detect an HR of 0.6 for PFS. Considering 20% of patients were not eligible for randomisation after four cycles of induction therapy31 and a 10% drop-out rate, a total of 90 patients were planned for enrollment in the induction phase. No interim analysis was planned for this trial.

Data on efficacy were analyzed in the ITT (all patients who underwent randomisation) populations. Data on safety were summarised in the SS population, defined as all patients who received at least one dose of maintenance treatment. For time-to-event endpoints, median values were calculated by the Kaplan-Meier method and compared by the log-rank test. HRs and 80% CIs for time-to-event outcomes were estimated using a Cox proportional hazards regression model. In case of non-proportional hazards, a post-hoc sensitivity analysis of RMST with a Fleming–Harrington test was also used to estimate the treatment effect size.48,49 The post-hoc ORR and DCR were summarised, and corresponding 95% CIs were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method. Differences in ORR and DCR were investigated using the Chi-square test or two-sided Fisher’s exact test. AEs were summarised descriptively. Only the analysis of the primary endpoint in the ITT population was based on formal statistical testing (a two-sided p-value < 0.20 was considered to be significant), whereas all other comparisons were exploratory and p-values were nominal.

For prespecified biomarker analyses, PFS according to gene expression in different treatment subgroups was evaluated using stratified log-rank tests, and HRs were estimated using stratified Cox proportional hazards models; interaction p-values were used to assess whether the prognostic value of gene expression differed between treatment groups.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 or R statistical software (v4.4.1). Prespecified biomarker analyses of associations with PFS and OS were analyzed using R, with the “survival” package employed for visualizations, while other analyses were conducted in SAS version 9.4.
