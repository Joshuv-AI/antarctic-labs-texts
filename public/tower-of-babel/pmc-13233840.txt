Debate: why surgery should remain central in the management of locally advanced (Stage III) non-small-cell lung cancer
Authors: Chris Aboujudom, David B Nelson, Inderpal Sarkaria, Kemp H Kernstine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStage III non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) comprises a broadly heterogeneous disease with historically poor survival, and treatment decisions must account for both pathophysiology and resectability. Despite conclusions from older trials that questioned the role of surgery, modern evidence supports surgical resection as an indispensable component of treatment when performed by experienced thoracic surgeons. Advances in neoadjuvant systemic therapy, particularly chemo-immunotherapy, have significantly improved outcomes and reinforced the added value of surgery in appropriately selected Stage III NSCLC patients. In PACIFIC, consolidation durvalumab after concurrent chemoradiation improved progression-free (16.9 vs 5.6 months) and overall survival (47.5 vs 29.1 months). Also, perioperative pembrolizumab reduced progression or death by 42% (*P* < 0.0001) and improved pCR and major pathologic response. Additional trials (NEOTORCH, NADIM 2) showed large reductions in events and higher pCR rates with checkpoint inhibitors plus chemotherapy. Collectively, these data underscore that Stage III NSCLC requires both systemic therapy and durable local control, and neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy followed by surgery can significantly improve oncologic outcomes without increasing perioperative mortality in carefully selected patients.

Introduction

Stage III non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) comprises a broadly heterogeneous group, ranging from bulky N2 disease to limited T3 lesions. Historically, survival for Stage III disease has been poor—less than one-third of patients are alive at 5 years according to Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) data [1]. Despite this sobering prognosis, only a minority ( < 5%) of Stage III patients receive the combination of neoadjuvant therapy and surgery that offers the best chance of long-term control [2, 3]. Modern multimodality trials now demonstrate that adding immunotherapy to chemotherapy before resection dramatically increases pathologic complete response (pCR) and event-free survival (EFS). However, outdated interpretations of older chemoradiotherapy trials have led some clinicians to dismiss surgery in favor of definitive chemoradiation. The purpose of this article is to convince the reader that, when delivered by experienced thoracic surgeons within a multidisciplinary team, contemporary surgery remains an indispensable component of therapy for appropriately selected Stage III NSCLC patients. We will review the heterogeneity of Stage III disease, describe the comprehensive work-up required to determine resectability, critique the limitations of historical trials that argue against surgery, highlight the unique advantages of surgical resection and the importance of high-quality surgical care, summarize modern evidence from neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy trials, and synthesize current guidelines to formulate a pragmatic treatment algorithm.

Heterogeneity of Stage III disease and the work-up for resectability

The International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) 9th edition staging system subdivides Stage III lung cancer into stages IIIA, IIIB, and IIIC, defined by distinct T and N groups that reflecting broad prognostic variation [4]. Prognosis varies among those with isolated mediastinal lymph nodes (cN2) and small primary tumors (cT1–T2) to those with bulky or multi-station N2 or T4 disease whose outcomes approximate unresectable disease [5]. A comprehensive staging workup includes high-resolution chest computed tomogram (CT), integrated positron emission tomography (PET)-CT to exclude occult distant metastases, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and either endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) or esophageal ultrasound (EUS)-guided fine-needle aspiration for mediastinal nodal sampling. Bronchoscopy helps to assess endobronchial extension and plan the operative approach for central tumors. Given that an increasing proportion of Stage III tumors harbor actionable oncogenic drivers, comprehensive molecular profiling for EGFR, ALK, ROS1 and other alterations is now standard of care. The decision to pursue resection requires evaluation by a multidisciplinary team that includes thoracic surgeons, medical oncologists, radiation oncologists, radiologists, and pulmonologists. Ideally, patients should have an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–1, adequate cardiopulmonary reserve, no active substance abuse or untreated psychiatric disease, and be compliant with therapy.

Major guidelines underscore the importance of multidisciplinary staging and resection for potentially operable Stage III patients. The 2024 American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guideline recommends neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy followed by surgery for any medically-operable, resectable stage II-III NSCLC after multi-disciplinary review, and whereas for unresectable concurrent chemoradiation with consolidative durvalumab is recommended [6, 7]. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) similarly advises neoadjuvant chemotherapy (with or without immunotherapy) or concurrent chemoradiation for non-bulky N2 disease, followed by reassessment and surgery when feasible [8]. For bulky or invasive cT3N2 and cT4N2 tumors, definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy with durvalumab consolidation remains the preferred strategy. Thus, the central question is not whether surgery should be universally applied, but rather which Stage III patients derive the superior benefit from resection and how to perform that surgery safely.

Limitations of historical chemoradiation trials

Between 1990 and 2012, several randomized trials concluded that adding surgery to chemoradiation failed to improve overall survival for Stage IIIA (N2) NSCLC [9]. These studies include Intergroup 0139, the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) 08941 trial, Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) 8901, and the ESPATUE trial. Intergroup 0139 randomized patients with Stage IIIA (pN2) disease to concurrent chemoradiation (45 Gy) followed by surgery versus a higher dose of chemoradiation (61 Gy) alone. Among 155 surgical patients, 35% underwent pneumonectomy and the operative mortality was 7.9%; fourteen of the sixteen postoperative deaths occurred after pneumonectomy [10]. Local-only relapses were dramatically improved in the operative cohort as compared with definitive chemoradiation alone (10 versus 22%), indicating the importance of surgery for local control. Although, the clinical relevance of improved local control with surgery was uncertain in this era, as the overall survival rates in the entire cohort were not different between the two treatment strategies, with both arms experiencing high rates of distant relapse. A post-hoc analysis indicated that progression-free and overall survival favored the surgery arm among those who received lobectomy, but not among those requiring pneumonectomy, further highlighting how much operative mortality influenced outcomes. Pneumonectomy, especially the right, is now rarely recommended. Both EORTC 08941 and ESPATUE similarly permitted pneumonectomy rates exceeding one-third and used outdated staging and induction regimens. The ESPATUE trial accrued only 246 patients over 13 years and allowed resection after high-dose radiotherapy up to 45 Gy, conditions rarely encountered in modern practice. RTOG 0617 attempted to improve outcomes by escalating radiotherapy from 60 to 74 Gy but found that higher doses worsened survival and increased toxicity [11, 12]. Too, providing post-operative radiation therapy (PORT) in patients with regional and mediastinal lymph node involvement showed no improvement in survival [13]. These trials predated PET-CT, modern chemotherapy doublets, immunotherapy, and minimally invasive surgery, so their negative conclusions about surgery cannot be extrapolated to contemporary treatment (Fig. 2).

Radiotherapy-only approaches provide inferior long-term control for Stage III NSCLC. For patients who are ineligible for chemotherapy or surgery, radiation alone yields a median overall survival of about 10 months, a 5-year survival rate near 5%, and can palliate hemoptysis, chest pain, and dyspnea [1, 14, 15]. Pre-immunotherapy concurrent chemoradiation improves outcomes modestly with a median overall survival of 20–30 months and a 5-year overall survival of approximately 20–30%, but still suffers from high rates of locoregional failure of 40-50% at 5-years [11, 16–19]. The PACIFIC trial demonstrated that consolidation durvalumab after concurrent chemoradiotherapy prolonged progression-free survival (16.9 vs 5.6 months) and overall survival (47.5 vs 29.1 months) [20]. Importantly, PACIFIC enrolled unresectable Stage III disease; the findings should not be misapplied to patients who could undergo complete resection after induction therapy. Thus, the poor outcomes associated with radiotherapy alone and the limitations of older chemoradiation trials underscore the need to revisit surgery in the modern era.

Unique benefits of surgical resection and the need for high-quality surgical care

Surgery offers several advantages that no non-operative modality can replicate. Complete resection removes the primary tumor and en bloc of all invaded structures without tumor spillage and all involved lymphatic tissue and the intrapulmonary lymphatic and venous drainage, eliminating the local microenvironment that may harbor chemo-resistant or immune-escape clones. Pathologic assessment of the resected specimen provides definitive staging (pCR, nodal downstaging), which guides decisions about adjuvant therapy [21]. Technically, adequate surgery achieves microscopic tumor-free margins of the primary (R0 resection) and clearance of nodal tissue basins and may provide durable remission and/or cure, while also obviating the need for long courses of radiation, which can cause esophagitis, pneumonitis, cardiac toxicity, and secondary malignancies [22]. Performing a systematic nodal dissection to include at least 3 mediastinal stations and 1 hilar lymph node station resection provides sufficient information for staging purposes [23]. Lobectomy or sleeve resection is preferred; pneumonectomy should be avoided whenever possible because it carries significantly higher morbidity and mortality [24–26]. The Intergroup 0139 experience showed that patients who underwent lobectomy had markedly better survival than those who required pneumonectomy, and that surgical deaths occurred almost exclusively among pneumonectomy recipients [27]. Although still a high risk procedure, more recent series highlight better survival rates than previously published among those who require a pneumonectomy, showcasing advancements in perioperative care among expert centers [28]. Sublobar resections, specifically wedge resection and segmentectomy, are a common and effective approach for some early-stage, small-sized non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) in selected patients. However, for more locally advanced disease, these limited resections are associated with higher rates of local recurrence compared to a standard lobectomy. This is primarily attributed to the inadequacy of these procedures for a complete oncologic resection, including a less thorough lymph node dissection, which is critical for clearing potential in-transit venous or lymphatic disease. A well-timed, sufficiently performed procedure provides the most accurate means of determining biology of the malignant process, improves local control, assesses response to any preoperative therapy, makes it easier to follow for local recurrence, and provides options for preparation of other therapies, such as adoptive cellular therapies.

Delivering these benefits requires that surgery be performed by experienced thoracic surgeons at high-volume centers. Data from the United Kingdom’s National Cancer Data Repository and other large databases, such as the Society of Thoracic Surgeons and several observational studies show that hospitals performing ≥150 lung resections per year have lower perioperative mortality and superior long-term survival [29, 30]. A multicenter analysis comparing general surgeons with thoracic surgeons found operative mortality after lobectomy to be 5.3% for general surgeons versus 3.0% for thoracic surgeons; among high-risk or elderly patients, mortality was 43.6% versus 25.4%, respectively [31]. Lung cancer surgeon specialists were found to have superior mediastinal lymphadenectomies than cardiac surgeons [32]. Logistic regression could not account for this difference, indicating that surgeon specialization independently influences outcomes [33]. Another study observed that centralizing lung cancer surgery in high-volume hospitals improved survival and reduced complications [34]. These data emphasize that the debate about the role of surgery must be coupled with the requirement that operations be performed in centers capable of providing advanced anesthesia, critical care, and multidisciplinary support [35]. A thorough patient selection process should assess pulmonary function (e.g., FEV1, DLCO), cardiac reserve (echocardiography, stress testing) and comorbidities; patients with severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart failure, uncontrolled diabetes, malnutrition, or substance abuse are poor surgical candidates. In borderline cases, prehabilitation and smoking cessation may improve surgical candidacy [36].

Modern evidence: neoadjuvant and perioperative chemo-immunotherapy

Recent phase II and III trials have revolutionized the management of resectable Stage III NSCLC by integrating immune checkpoint inhibitors into the neoadjuvant regimen. The CheckMate 816 trial randomized patients with resectable stage IB–IIIA NSCLC to neoadjuvant nivolumab plus platinum-doublet chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone. Neoadjuvant nivolumab increased the pathological complete response (pCR) rate from 2.2% to 24% and prolonged median event-free survival from 20.8 to 31.6 months; event-free survival at two years improved from 45.3% to 63.8% [37]. Importantly, the addition of nivolumab did not compromise surgical feasibility; conversion to unresectable status occurred in 12 patients receiving nivolumab versus 17 patients receiving chemotherapy alone, and the rate of minimally invasive surgery or lobectomy remained high [38].

The KEYNOTE-671 trial expanded upon this concept by adopting a perioperative strategy. Patients with resectable stage II–IIIB (N2) NSCLC were randomized to pembrolizumab plus platinum-doublet chemotherapy for four neoadjuvant cycles followed by surgery and adjuvant pembrolizumab versus placebo plus chemotherapy followed by adjuvant placebo. At the first interim analysis, perioperative pembrolizumab reduced the risk of disease progression, recurrence or death by 42% (hazard ratio 0.58; 95% CI 0.46–0.72; *P* < 0.0001) and the median event-free survival (EFS) was not reached compared with 17.0 months in the control group [39]. Pathologic complete response and major pathologic response rates were also improved (pCR 18.1% vs 4.0%; major pathological response (mPR) 30.2% vs 11.0%). A later exploratory analysis with longer follow-up reported a median EFS of 57.1 months with pembrolizumab versus 18.4 months with placebo and continued to show a robust hazard ratio of approximately 0.57 [40]. Surgical feasibility remained excellent, with 80% of patients proceeding to resection and no increase in perioperative mortality.

Other trials corroborate these findings. The phase III AEGEAN trial compared durvalumab plus chemotherapy with placebo plus chemotherapy in the perioperative setting and found that event-free survival and pCR were superior with durvalumab (EFS hazard ratio 0.68; 95% CI 0.53–0.88; *P* = 0.0039) [41]. The NEOTORCH trial conducted in Asia tested toripalimab with chemotherapy versus placebo and demonstrated a dramatic reduction in events (EFS hazard ratio 0.40; 95% CI 0.277–0.565) [42]. The NADIM-2 trial, which included only resectable Stage III patients, reported a pCR of 37% with neoadjuvant nivolumab-chemotherapy versus 7% with chemotherapy alone; median progression-free and overall survival were markedly improved [43]. Importantly, all these studies reported that adding an immune checkpoint inhibitor did not increase serious adverse events or compromise surgical resection. The rate of grade 3–4 treatment-related adverse events in CheckMate 816 was 33.5% with nivolumab versus 36.9% with chemotherapy alone, and in AEGEAN it was 42.3% with durvalumab versus 43.4% with placebo [37, 41]. Surgery remained feasible in roughly 80% of patients across trials, with some studies noting a higher rate of surgery in the immunotherapy arms than in the control arms [44].

These contemporary data refute the notion that surgery does not add value in Stage III NSCLC. By downstaging tumors and increasing complete resection rates, neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy leverages the immune system to eradicate micro-metastatic disease while still allowing surgeons to remove the primary tumor. In every trial cited, pathologic response translated into improved event-free survival, and early signals suggest an overall survival benefit. Therefore, the default approach for patients with non-bulky N2 disease and good performance status should be neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy followed by surgery and appropriate adjuvant therapy.

Integrating guidelines and formulating a treatment algorithm

In light of these data, the management of Stage III NSCLC should follow a structured algorithm [45]. All patients should undergo comprehensive staging with CT, PET-CT, brain MRI and mediastinal staging. Cases should be presented at a multidisciplinary tumor board to assess resectability and physiological fitness. Patients with non-bulky single-station N2 disease or limited T3 disease should receive neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy for three to four cycles [46]. If molecular profiling reveals an EGFR mutation or ALK fusion, consider either upfront surgery or if neoadjuvant treatment is needed then traditional platinum doublet chemotherapy [47]. Ongoing trials are evaluating neoadjuvant osimertinib, which has already been demonstrated benefit in the adjuvant setting (ADAURA trial) [48]. Recent data from NeoADAURA show that neoadjuvant osimertinib, with or without chemotherapy, significantly improves major pathologic response and nodal downstaging rates compared to chemotherapy alone while also achieving high rates of R0 resection and demonstrating potential early event-free survival in EGFR-mutated, resectable Stage II-IIIB disease []. After neoadjuvant therapy, patients are restaged; those without progression proceed to lobectomy or sleeve resection with systematic mediastinal lymphadenectomy. Although under some debate, adjuvant immunotherapy (durvalumab, pembrolizumab or nivolumab) continues for up to one year based on the regimen used [49]. Patients considered inoperable with bulky multistation N2 disease, cT4 tumors involving mediastinal structures, or poor physiologic reserve should be considered for concurrent chemoradiation followed by consolidation durvalumab [8]. Definitive radiotherapy is reserved for those unable to tolerate chemotherapy or surgery.

Special situations require nuance. Superior sulcus (Pancoast) tumors often invade the chest wall and brachial plexus; trimodality therapy with induction chemo-radiotherapy followed by en bloc resection remains standard [50, 51]. Investigational approaches include use of chemo-immunotherapy to spare radiation therapy and allow more treatment options [52] in the future, although the efficacy of this approach remains to be determined. Salvage resection after chemoradiation may benefit selected patients with persistent disease or recurrent local tumor, but associated morbidity is increased [52]. In all scenarios, high-volume centers with experienced thoracic surgeons should lead treatment decisions to optimize outcomes [53].

Future directions and unresolved questions

Several unresolved questions remain in the management of Stage III NSCLC. First, more precise tools are needed to refine surgical selection after neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy, including the integration of molecular biomarkers, circulating tumor DNA, radiographic response, and pathologic residual disease to predict long-term benefit. Second, the impact of neoadjuvant therapy on treatment-plan attrition remains poorly defined. Although surgery may be the original intent, treatment-related toxicity, frailty, fibrosis, or physiologic decline can delay or preclude resection, unintentionally diverting patients from potentially curative surgery. Critical unmet needs may be addressed by identifying patients at risk for loss of surgical candidacy and optimizing timing, intensity, and supportive care during induction therapy. Third, as outcomes increasingly depend on technical excellence, prospective validation of surgical quality metrics, including extent of lymphadenectomy, center volume, and surgeon specialization, is warranted. Finally, long-term outcomes, such as patterns of recurrence, functional recovery, and quality of life, following multimodality therapy, require further study to guide personalized treatment decisions. Addressing these unresolved questions through well-designed trials and multidisciplinary collaboration will be pivotal to fully individualize care for patients with Stage III NSCLC.

Conclusion

Stage III NSCLC is a heterogeneous disease in which achieving durable control requires both systemic therapy and local consolidation. Historical trials that dismissed surgery were limited by high pneumonectomy rates, outdated surgical and staging techniques and chemotherapy regimens, and lack of modern imaging and immunotherapy. Contemporary evidence demonstrates that neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy followed by surgery markedly improves pathologic response, local recurrence, and event-free survival without increasing perioperative mortality [54]. Complete resection provides critical benefits—removal of the primary tumor and lymphatic basin, biological staging and potential cure—that cannot be replicated by chemoradiation alone. High-quality surgery at specialized centers is essential to realize these advantages; general surgeons and low-volume hospitals have significantly higher mortality and non-general thoracic surgeons (general surgeons and cardiac surgeons) performing lung resections demonstrate inferior mediastinal lymphadenectomy quality. Current guidelines endorse surgery for resectable Stage III disease and chemoradiation with durvalumab for unresectable cases. Therefore, we argue that surgery should remain central in the management of appropriately selected Stage III NSCLC patients. Multidisciplinary evaluation, meticulous staging, proper patient selection and adherence to evolving trial evidence will ensure that patients receive the best chance for long-term survival.
