Somatic cell nuclear transfer as a complementary tool for genetic conservation of the Peruvian Paso horse
Authors: Jenin V. CORTEZ, Gleni T. SEGURA PORTOCARRERO, Nilton L. MURGA VALDERRAMA, William BARDALES, Ricardo ENCINA RUIZ, Carmen L. VELÁSQUEZ CASTILLO, Christopher G. GRUPEN, Jorge L. MAICELO QUINTANA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Peruvian Paso horse, a breed of national significance in Peru known for its unique gait, has been selectively bred for nearly 500 years and now faces potential risks due to reduced genetic diversity. To investigate the potential of somatic cell nuclear transfer to enhance genetic conservation strategies, a program was initiated to produce cloned Peruvian Paso foals. Skin biopsies from a highly awarded stallion with exceptional breed-defining traits were used as the source of donor somatic cells. Of the 15 cloned embryos reconstructed, seven blastocysts were produced, and five were transferred to five recipient mares. Three pregnancies ensued, two of which resulted in the birth of healthy foals at 333 and 347 days of gestation. Cloned foals were genetically and phenotypically identical to the donor animal, demonstrating the successful application of this approach to support genetic conservation in a protected horse breed.

Horses have long been valued for their athletic abilities and enduring historical and cultural significance in human societies. Many countries have preserved unique equine breeds that symbolize national identity. However, several equine breeds are currently threatened by declining populations and loss of genetic diversity. The Peruvian Paso horse is a breed of national importance in Peru, renowned for its characteristic body conformation and distinctive lateral four-beat gait, known as the Paso llano [1]. The breed descends from horses that were brought to South America during the Spanish conquest of the Inca Empire in the 16th century. Its unique phenotypic traits have been shaped over nearly 500 years of selective breeding in the mountainous, arid environments of Peru, making it one of the purest horse breeds in the world [2]. In 1992, the Peruvian government officially declared the Peruvian Paso horse a native species, conferring legal protection under national heritage legislation (Decree No. 25919 of Peru).

Genetic conservation of the Peruvian Paso horse is necessary because of its cultural significance and the demonstrable risk of genetic erosion. Analyses of inbreeding in a Peruvian Paso horse population revealed reduced genetic variability attributed to a low effective population size, bottleneck effects, and genetic drift [3]. Additionally, selection for chestnut coat color has been associated with reduced genetic diversity in this subpopulation, and some traits show early signs of inbreeding depression [2]. Recent sequencing of the mitochondrial genome has clarified this breed’s maternal ancestry and has provided valuable genomic resources for genetic management and conservation strategies [1]. Coordinated breeding interventions, including controlled mating schemes, semen cryopreservation, and embryo cryobanking, remain essential for effective genetic management. Additionally, somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) provides an additional strategy for recovering valuable genomes from elite, underrepresented, aged, castrated, or deceased individuals when no other reproductive options are available [4, 5].

The first equid clones produced using SCNT were reported in 2003 [6, 7], and this technology advanced rapidly, especially after the birth of the first horse clone in the United States in 2005 [8]. Since then, cloned horses have been produced in South America (Argentina, Brazil, and Colombia) [9], South Korea [10], and more recently Australia [11, 12]. Some laboratories have achieved commercially acceptable success rates in reproducing healthy cloned foals from elite performance animals [9, 11]. Although SCNT has considerable potential for conserving the genetics of rare and endangered species, its application is constrained by the limited access to suitable oocytes [11]. These oocytes are essential because they provide the nuclear reprogramming capacity and maternally derived factors needed for early embryonic development [13]. To address this limitation, donor nuclei may be transferred into enucleated oocytes from other breeds of the same species or, when necessary, from closely related species. This approach has enabled the successful cloning of several endangered species, including gaur [14], banteng [15], Esfahan mouflon [16], and Przewalski’s horse [17]. For the Przewalski’s horse clones, SCNT embryos were produced using enucleated oocytes from a domestic horse breed [17]. Given the cultural importance of the Peruvian Paso horse and the advanced state of equine cloning technologies, this study aimed to produce the first cloned Peruvian Paso foals. It also sought to evaluate the feasibility of SCNT for replicating the genome of an elite animal from this protected breed. Because the Criollo breed is comparable in size and body mass to the Peruvian Paso horse, Criollo mares were used as sources of oocytes and as embryo-transfer recipients.

Of the 90 cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs) recovered from slaughterhouse-sourced ovaries and selected for in vitro maturation (IVM), 20 completed nuclear maturation to the metaphase II stage and had an obvious first polar body (18% maturation rate). While the second birth proceeded without complications, the mare was subsequently diagnosed with mild placentitis, a relatively common condition in mares that may have contributed to the reduced birth weight [18]. The morphological traits of the foals were identical to those of the somatic cell donor, and genetic profiling confirmed an exact match for all microsatellite markers analyzed. The third pregnancy progressed normally, and the mare showed signs of labor after 326 days of gestation. However, the foal was stillborn, immature, and weighed 23 kg. The stillborn foal had inadequate development of the abdominal wall muscles, although the umbilicus appeared normal. Postmortem evaluation revealed severe placental insufficiency, including diffuse vascular congestion and fetal membrane edema, compatible with late intrauterine fetal death.

Conservation cloning aims to preserve the genetics of rare and endangered animals, such as the Przewalski’s horse [17]. It highlights the potential of SCNT to conserve genetically distinctive and culturally important equine breeds. Here, we demonstrate that an interbreed SCNT approach using Criollo mares as oocyte donors and ET recipients, and fibroblast cells derived from a Peruvian Paso stallion, is effective in producing cloned embryos and live foals. The primary value of SCNT lies not in increasing population size but in restoring genetic balance by enabling under-represented or otherwise inaccessible nuclear genomes to contribute to the germplasm through conventional breeding [17, 19]. Evidence from equine conservation programs indicates that SCNT can be incorporated without adverse population-level consequences if clone numbers and reproductive contributions are carefully managed [17]. In the Peruvian Paso horse, integrating SCNT into structured breeding programs may mitigate genetic drift and founder imbalance while preserving breed identity and functional traits [17].

The efficiency of cloned embryo production and in vivo viability was comparable to that recently reported for other horse breeds using the same methods [11, 20]. Recipient oocytes for SCNT can be obtained from slaughterhouse-sourced ovaries or live mares via OPU [11]. In the present study, slaughterhouse-derived oocytes had a lower maturation rate (18%) than those previously reported using similar methods [21], possibly because of the extended interval between ovary collection and COC recovery in the laboratory, which was unavoidable for logistical reasons. Using a conventional micromanipulation-based SCNT procedure [11], a blastocyst production rate of 47% was achieved. Following transfer of vitrified-warmed blastocysts to recipient mares on day 4 post-ovulation, considered the optimal timing for equine embryos produced in vitro [22], a 60% pregnancy rate was achieved. These outcomes compare favorably with those reported for horses using slaughterhouse-derived oocytes [11, 23, 24], although efficiency may be improved by using OPU-derived oocytes [11].

Of the three pregnancies established, two resulted in healthy live foals, whereas one pregnancy was affected by severe placental insufficiency, resulting in stillbirth. Abnormal placental development and adverse pregnancy outcomes are frequently reported in equine cloning and are commonly attributed to incomplete epigenetic reprogramming following SCNT [25, 26]. Adult fibroblasts, commonly used in equine cloning, were used as nuclear donor cells in this study [11, 20]. The epigenetic status of donor cells is a crucial factor influencing nuclear reprogramming efficiency [5], and improved neonatal outcomes have been reported when bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells were used instead of fibroblasts [27]. Therefore, bone marrow-derived stem cells may be preferable for equine conservation cloning.

The successful creation of the first Peruvian Paso foals through SCNT, marking the first equine clones produced in Peru, demonstrates the feasibility of using this technology to recapitulate the genome of a genetically valuable individual representative of the breed. After reaching sexual maturity, cloned foals can contribute their germplasm through conventional breeding methods. To maintain genetic diversity at the population level, the reproductive contributions of the cloned individuals should be carefully regulated within a structured breeding framework. When applied judiciously and within the appropriate ethical and welfare guidelines, SCNT can serve as a complementary tool to conventional genetic conservation strategies.

Methods

Animals

Cloned embryos were produced at the Animal Biotechnology, Reproduction, and Genetic Improvement Laboratory of the National University Toribio Rodríguez de Mendoza (UNTRM), Amazonas. The oocytes used to generate cloned embryos were recovered from the ovaries of Criollo mares collected from a slaughterhouse (Casa Blanca, Lima, Peru). Criollo mares from the Amazonas region aged 3–7 years were used as embryo recipients. The UNTRM Research Ethics Committee approved the study protocol (protocol number CIEI-No. 0093). All procedures were carried out with informed consent from the animal owner and in accordance with Peruvian National Law No. 30407, the Animal Protection and Welfare Act, and other relevant institutional guidelines and legislation. This study adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines for animal research.

Chemicals

All reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), unless specified otherwise.

Oocyte recovery

Immediately after collection at the slaughterhouse, the ovaries were placed in plastic bags within an insulated container, surrounded by gel packs to maintain a temperature of 20–22°C during transport [21], which included a 1.5 h flight from Lima to Chachapoyas. COCs were recovered in the laboratory within 10 h of ovary collection by aspirating follicles < 30 mm in diameter using an 18G needle and extensively scraping the follicular walls with a bone curette. The follicular material obtained was transferred to a 100 mm Petri dish containing Hepes-buffered Synthetic Oviductal Fluid (H-SOF) [28] at room temperature. Only COCs with at least two complete layers of cumulus cells were selected, washed three times with H-SOF, and transferred to 1.5 ml screw-top vials filled with holding medium at 20‒22°C. The holding medium consisted of a 1:1 mix of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 Ham (DMEM/F-12; D8437) and Medium 199 (M3769) supplemented with 20% heat-inactivated fetal calf serum (FCS; AU-FBS/PG; Cellsera, Rutherford, NSW, Australia). The vials were tightly sealed, wrapped in foil to avoid light exposure, and held overnight (12 h) in an insulated container at 20‒22°C.

Oocyte in vitro maturation

Oocyte in vitro maturation (IVM) was performed as previously described [11]. After holding overnight, the COCs were recovered from the sealed vials, washed 3 times with H-SOF, transferred to maturation medium, and incubated for 24 h at 38.2°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 in air. The maturation medium consisted of DMEM/F-12 supplemented with 0.1 IU/ml follicle-stimulating hormone and 0.1 IU/ml luteinizing hormone (Menopur; Ferring Pharmaceuticals, Copenhagen, Denmark), 50 ng/ml epidermal growth factor (EGF; E9644), 1 mM sodium pyruvate, insulin-transferrin-sodium selenium mixture (I1884), and 10% FCS.

Somatic cell nuclear transfer

SCNT embryos were reconstructed in a single day using oocytes obtained from a single ovary collection. Fibroblast cell lines were derived from skin biopsies of a prized stallion, and donor cells were prepared for SCNT as previously described [11]. Following IVM, cumulus cells were removed from the oocytes by vigorous pipetting in H-SOF supplemented with 1 mg/ml hyaluronidase. For micromanipulation, mature oocytes and donor cells were transferred to H-SOF microdroplets covered with mineral oil. The polar body and the metaphase plate of each oocyte were aspirated using an enucleation pipette connected to a piezoelectric drill (Eppendorf, Enfield, CT, USA). The donor cells were then loaded into an injection pipette, and a single cell was deposited into the perivitelline space of each cytoplast. The couplets were maintained in H-SOF at 38.2°C for 1 h before being transferred to fusion medium on a fusion chamber slide. The fusion medium consisted of 3.0 M D-sorbitol, 0.05 mM CaCl2, 0.10 mM MgCl2, and 0.05% (w/v) fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin (BSA; 700-102P; Gemini Bio-Products, West Sacramento, CA, USA).

A direct current (DC) pulse of 2.2 kV/cm intensity and 15 µsec duration was applied to the couplets. After pulsing, the couplets were washed three times in H-SOF, transferred to embryo culture medium, and incubated for 2 h. The embryo culture medium consisted of DMEM/F-12 supplemented with 10% FCS. Activation was achieved by incubating the fused couplets in 5 µM ionomycin (I0634) for 5 min, followed by treatment with 1 mM 6-dimethylaminopurine (D2629) and 5 µg/ml cycloheximide (C7698) in embryo culture medium for 4 h. After several washes in embryo culture medium, the reconstructed embryos were transferred to 10 µl droplets of embryo culture medium under mineral oil (maximum of 7 embryos in each droplet) and incubated in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2, 5% O2, and 90% N2 at 38.2°C. The cloned embryos were transferred to fresh droplets of culture medium every 2 days. Embryonic cleavage was assessed at the first change of culture medium. Blastocyst formation was assessed on day 7 of in vitro culture (IVC), and the morphological characteristics were used to classify the quality of each blastocyst (grade 1: excellent; grade 2: acceptable; grade 3: poor) [29].

Embryo vitrification and thawing

Grade 1 and 2 blastocysts obtained on day 7 of IVC were vitrified using the Cryotop method, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Vitrification Kit VT601-TOP; Kitazato BioPharma, Shizuoka, Japan), with each blastocyst loaded on a separate device for storage in liquid nitrogen. Once a recipient mare was deemed suitable, a single blastocyst was thawed immediately before embryo transfer (ET), again in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. After thawing, the blastocysts were transferred to equilibrated, warmed embryo culture medium and loaded into a 0.25 ml straw with 50 µl of embryo culture medium, which was sealed and transported at 38.2°C in a portable incubator to the site of ET (within 30 min).

Embryo transfer

The recipient mares were reproductively sound, had a history of normal estrous cycles and prior fertility, and were considered suitable for ET based on their clinical and ultrasonographic evaluations. Once in estrus, each recipient mare was scanned daily using transrectal ultrasound to monitor the day of ovulation, observing the disappearance of the preovulatory follicle and appearance of the corpus luteum (CL). Ultrasonographic examination the day before ET confirmed the absence of endometrial edema and excessive intrauterine fluid accumulation, and the presence of adequate uterine tone, a tight cervix, and at least one CL of expected echogenicity. Mares deemed suitable for ET were sedated with detomidine hydrochloride (4 mg i.v.; ~10 μg/kg; Dormosedan; Zoetis Peru, Lima, Peru), and the ET procedure was then performed. Each warmed blastocyst was transcervically transferred to a recipient mare on day 4 after ovulation. Following the procedure, mares were treated with long-acting progesterone (1.5 g i.m.; P4-300, 300 mg/ml; Botupharma, Botucatu, Brazil), administered every 7 days thereafter until day 100 of gestation. On day 14 post-ovulation (day 10 after ET), and again on days 42 and 90 of gestation, the mares were examined ultrasonographically to diagnose pregnancy. Additional scans were subsequently performed to monitor the progress of the ongoing pregnancies.

Genotyping

For genotyping, blood samples from cloned foals were analyzed at an independent laboratory (Genexa ADN Evolutivo, Montevideo, Uruguay) to compare their genetic profiles with that of the somatic cell donor. The analysis was based on the amplification of short tandem repeat (STR) loci using a panel of microsatellite markers (VHL20, HTG4, AHT4, HMS7, HTG6, AHT5, HMS6, ASB23, ASB2, HTG10, HTG7, HMS3, HMS2, ASB17, LEX3, HMS1, CA425), as recommended by the International Society for Animal Genetics (ISAG) to assess equine parentage [30].
