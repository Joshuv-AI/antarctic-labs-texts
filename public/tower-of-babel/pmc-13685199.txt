Interpretable machine learning model for brain metastasis in breast cancer: a large-scale, multi-center study

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBrain metastasis (BM) is a devastating complication of breast cancer (BC) with a poor prognosis. Early identification of high-risk patients is essential but currently lacks accurate predictive tools. This study aimed to develop a stable machine learning model for predicting BM in a large, diverse patient population.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 186,351 primary BC patients from the SEER program, the National Cancer Database, and a multi-center Chinese cohort. Patients were split into training and validation sets. Candidate predictors included demographic, clinicopathological, biomarker, and treatment-related variables, including surgery, chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, neoadjuvant therapy, and radiotherapy. LASSO regression was used for feature selection. Nine machine learning models were developed and compared using area under the curve (AUC) and decision curve analysis. The optimal model was deployed as an online tool.ResultsAmong 186,351 patients, 2,982 (1.6%) developed BM. In baseline comparisons, patients who developed BM more frequently exhibited advanced disease characteristics and different treatment patterns, likely reflecting more aggressive disease presentation. Key predictors retained in the final parsimonious model included AJCC N/M/T stage, age, and surgery status. The LASSO-regularized Logistic Regression model achieved superior discrimination in the validation set, with an AUC of 0.907 (95% CI: 0.857–0.958). The model demonstrated good calibration, clinical net benefit, and interpretability, with SHAP analysis confirming the contribution of each retained predictor to individual risk estimates. Survival analysis confirmed the poor prognosis associated with BM (p < 0.001). An interactive online tool was developed for real-time risk estimation.ConclusionWe developed and internally validated a accurate, clinically translatable model for predicting BM in BC using over 186,000 patients from North American and Asian cohorts. This web-based tool can assist clinicians in identifying high-risk individuals, enabling personalized management and potentially improving patient outcomes.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) stands as a paramount global health challenge, representing the most prevalent malignancy among women and a principal driver of cancer-related mortality [1, 2]. The clinical trajectory of BC has been transformed by advancements in early detection and systemic therapies, leading to remarkable improvements in survival for patients with localized disease [3, 4]. However, this progress is sharply contrasted by the persistent lethality of metastatic BC, where the dissemination of tumor cells to distant organs remains the ultimate cause of death [5, 6]. Among these distant sites, the central nervous system (CNS) presents a unique and formidable therapeutic frontier. The brain, shielded by the highly selective blood-brain barrier, acts as a sanctuary site where malignant cells can evade systemic treatments, proliferate, and establish devastating metastases [7–9].The incidence of brain metastasis (BM) in BC patients is alarmingly high, affecting an estimated 10–30% of those with advanced disease and rising to as high as 50% in aggressive molecular subtypes such as HER2-positive and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) [10, 11]. The onset of BM marks a critical turning point in a patient’s illness, invariably leading to a dire prognosis with a median overall survival often measured in mere months [12–14]. Beyond its impact on survival, the clinical burden of BM is immense, inflicting a constellation of debilitating neurological symptoms—from severe headaches and seizures to profound cognitive impairment and focal motor deficits—that catastrophically erode a patient’s quality of life and functional independence [15–17]. This grim reality underscores a pressing clinical dilemma: how to identify, from the vast population of BC patients, the subset of individuals destined to develop this complication. Proactive identification of this high-risk group is not merely an academic exercise; it is the cornerstone of a potential paradigm shift in patient management, enabling a move from reactive treatment of symptomatic disease to proactive surveillance and pre-emptive therapeutic strategies.

Historically, efforts to stratify this risk have relied on a handful of well-established clinicopathological factors. Younger age, high-grade tumors, advanced nodal status, and specific molecular subtypes have been consistently implicated in an elevated risk of CNS relapse [18, 19]. While these factors provide a coarse level of risk assessment, they are insufficient for precise, individualized prediction. Traditional statistical models, such as logistic regression, have been developed to integrate these variables, yet they often fall short. These models are frequently constrained by methodological limitations, including derivation from small, single-institution cohorts with inherent selection biases, a failure to account for the complex, non-linear interplay between predictors, and, most critically, a pervasive lack of stable external validation across diverse ethnic and geographic populations [20–22]. Consequently, their performance in real-world clinical settings is often disappointing, leaving clinicians without a reliable tool to guide critical decisions, such as the timing of brain MRI surveillance or the consideration of CNS-penetrant systemic agents. In addition to conventional clinicopathological variables, treatment-related factors may also carry important clinical information in the context of BM risk stratification. Among them, chemotherapy is of particular interest because its use often reflects both underlying disease aggressiveness and therapeutic selection in patients with higher tumor burden or biologically unfavorable subtypes.

The confluence of “big data” in oncology and the maturation of machine learning (ML) presents an unprecedented opportunity to overcome these limitations [23, 24]. Unlike conventional statistical methods that often rely on pre-specified assumptions, ML algorithms excel at autonomously learning complex patterns and high-order interactions directly from vast datasets [25, 26]. This capability is perfectly suited to the multifactorial nature of metastatic progression, allowing for the creation of more granular, accurate, and personalized predictive models. While the potential of ML is immense, its application to the prediction of breast cancer BM remains in its nascent stages. Existing ML-based studies have often been limited to smaller datasets or have not performed a comprehensive, head-to-head comparison of various algorithms to determine the optimal modeling strategy for this specific clinical problem [27, 28].

Therefore, this study was designed to definitively address these long-standing challenges. By assembling a massive, uniquely heterogeneous cohort of over 186,000 patients—integrating comprehensive data from two major North American population-based registries (SEER and NCDB) with a multi-center, real-world Asian cohort—we aimed to construct a model. Our primary objectives were to: (1) comprehensively delineate the clinical and pathological determinants of BM on an unprecedented scale; (2) systematically develop, compare, and validate a suite of machine learning models to identify the most accurate and reliable predictive tool; and (3) ensure the clinical translatability of our findings by deploying the final model as an interactive, open-access web-based calculator, thereby empowering clinicians with a practical instrument for evidence-based risk stratification.

Methods

Data sources and study cohort

This retrospective cohort study integrated data from three distinct sources to construct a large and heterogeneous patient population. The final cohort comprised 186,351 patients with primary BC. The primary analysis was anchored in a multi-center, real-world cohort of 54,232 patients from nine tertiary cancer centers in China. To increase the statistical power and assess the generalizability of our findings, this cohort was augmented with data from two major United States cancer registries, including 81,376 patients from the National Cancer Database (NCDB) and 50,743 patients from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program.

To enhance the generalizability and external validity of our findings across different ethnic populations, we integrated a third source: a multi-center, real-world cohort from nine tertiary cancer centers in China. These centers included: Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital, Jilin Cancer Hospital, Nanyang Central Hospital, Quanzhou First Hospital Affiliated to Fujian Medical University, Sanming First Hospital, The 900th Hospital of Joint Logistic Support Force, The First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University, The First Hospital of Putian City, and The Second Affiliated Hospital of Shantou University Medical College. Data from these institutions were retrospectively collected following institutional review board approval from each participating center. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective and de-identified nature of the data analysis.

The final combined cohort included female patients diagnosed with primary invasive BC between 2010 and 2018. Key inclusion criteria were a pathologically confirmed diagnosis of BC and the availability of essential data on tumor characteristics, treatment, and follow-up. Patients with a prior history of other malignancies, male BC, or those with extensive missing data on core variables such as AJCC stage or biomarker status were excluded from the analysis.

Variables and outcome definition

The primary endpoint of this study was the development of BM at any point during the follow-up period. Patients were categorized into two groups: the BM group (patients who developed BM) and the non-BM group (patients who did not). The primary endpoint was documented brain metastasis recorded at any time during the available observation period. This endpoint included both brain metastasis identified at initial breast cancer diagnosis and brain metastasis documented subsequently during follow-up, when such information was available in the corresponding data source. Patients without any recorded evidence of brain metastasis during the available follow-up were classified as the non-BM group. We extracted a comprehensive set of variables for each patient, categorized as follows:

Demographics and patient history

Age at diagnosis (continuous), sex, menopausal status, family history of malignancy, and key comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes).

Tumor characteristics

Primary tumor site (left, right, bilateral/other), American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition stage group, and individual T, N, and M stages.

Biomarkers and molecular subtype

Estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status. Based on these markers, patients were categorized into four estimated molecular subtypes: Hormone Receptor-positive/HER2-negative (HR+/HER2-), HR+/HER2+, HR-/HER2+, and Triple-Negative (HR-/HER2-).

Primary treatment modalities

Information on primary tumor surgery (breast-conserving surgery, mastectomy, or no surgery), receipt of chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, neoadjuvant therapy, and radiotherapy to the breast/chest wall. Chemotherapy was coded as a binary treatment exposure variable (yes/no) according to the treatment records available in each source database and was entered into the initial candidate feature pool for model development. Because detailed information on chemotherapy regimen, treatment line, dose intensity, and timing was not consistently available across all data sources, chemotherapy was analyzed as an overall treatment exposure rather than a regimen-specific variable.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between the BM and non-BM groups. Continuous variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. To quantify the magnitude of difference between groups independent of sample size, the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was calculated for all variables, with an SMD > 0.1 considered to indicate a meaningful imbalance. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All baseline statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3).

Machine learning model development and validation

Data preprocessing and feature selection

Prior to model development, the combined dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%) and a validation set (30%) while maintaining the original proportion of BM cases in each subset. This constitutes an internal hold-out validation rather than independent external validation. To build a parsimonious model and prevent overfitting, we employed the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) logistic regression on the training data. LASSO applies an L1 penalty to shrink the regression coefficients of less important features towards zero, effectively performing automated feature selection. The optimal penalty parameter (λ) was determined through 10-fold cross-validation, selecting the value that minimized the mean cross-validated error. Features with non-zero coefficients at the optimal λ were selected for inclusion in the primary logistic regression model.

Missing data handling: Patients with missing values in core variables (AJCC stage components, biomarker status) were excluded during cohort assembly as described in Section “Data sources and study cohort”. For the remaining analytical variables, the proportion of missing data was below 2% for all features. Complete-case analysis was performed, as multiple imputation was deemed unnecessary given the minimal missingness.

Feature encoding: Categorical variables were encoded using integer coding for ordinal features (T stage: 1–4; N stage: 0–3; M stage: 0–1) and dummy coding for nominal features (surgery type: no surgery, breast-conserving, mastectomy). Age was entered as a continuous variable.

Preprocessing and scaling: For algorithms sensitive to feature scale (SVM-L, SVM-R, KNN, NN), all input features were standardized to zero mean and unit variance using parameters derived exclusively from the training set. Tree-based models (RF, GBM, DT) and Naive Bayes did not require scaling.

Hyperparameter tuning: For LASSO, the regularization parameter λ was selected via 10-fold cross-validation on the training set, choosing the λ that minimized mean cross-validated deviance (lambda.min). For Random Forest, the number of trees was set to 500 and mtry was tuned via out-of-bag error. For GBM, learning rate, tree depth, and number of boosting iterations were tuned via 5-fold cross-validation grid search. For SVM, the cost parameter C (and gamma for radial kernel) was selected via 5-fold CV. For KNN, k was selected by evaluating odd values from 3 to 25. For NN, a single hidden layer with varying node counts was evaluated via 5-fold CV.

Cross-validation settings: 10-fold cross-validation was used for LASSO feature selection; 5-fold CV was used for hyperparameter tuning of other algorithms. All CV was performed exclusively within the training set.

Model training

We systematically developed and compared nine machine learning algorithms: Logistic Regression with L1/Elastic Net regularization (LR(L1/EN)), Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Support Vector Machine with linear and radial kernels (SVM-L, SVM-R), a multi-layer perceptron Neural Network (NN), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Decision Tree (DT), and Naive Bayes (NB). All models were trained exclusively on the training dataset. The LR(L1/EN) model in our comparative framework refers specifically to a logistic regression model with L1 (LASSO) penalization applied during the training phase for simultaneous feature selection and coefficient estimation.

Model evaluation

The performance of each trained model was rigorously assessed on the unseen validation dataset. The primary metric for discriminative ability was the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), with 95% confidence intervals (CI). We also calculated several other performance metrics at the optimal threshold (determined by the Youden index), including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, and the F1-score.

Clinical utility and model calibration

To evaluate the potential clinical value of the models, we performed Decision Curve Analysis (DCA). DCA quantifies the net benefit of using a model to guide clinical decisions across a range of risk thresholds, comparing it to the default strategies of treating all or no patients. A model with a higher net benefit is considered more clinically useful. Model reliability was assessed using calibration curves, which plot the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed event frequencies. A well-calibrated model will have a curve that lies close to the 45-degree diagonal line.

In addition to visual calibration curves, quantitative calibration metrics were calculated for the final model. The Brier score was used to assess the overall accuracy of probabilistic predictions, with lower values indicating better performance. Calibration intercept and calibration slope were further estimated by regressing the observed BM status on the logit-transformed predicted probabilities. An intercept close to 0 indicates minimal systematic overestimation or underestimation, whereas a slope close to 1 indicates good agreement between predicted and observed risks across the full probability range. Calibration was evaluated separately in the training and validation sets.

Model interpretability

To provide transparency and understand the drivers of the model’s predictions, we employed the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) framework. SHAP is a game-theoretic approach that assigns an importance value to each feature for each individual prediction. We generated SHAP summary plots to visualize global feature importance and dependence plots to explore the marginal effect of individual features on the model’s output.

Survival analysis and web tool development

Overall survival (OS), defined as the time from primary diagnosis to death from any cause, was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed with the log-rank test. Finally, the best-performing predictive model was implemented as a user-friendly, interactive web application using the R Shiny framework to facilitate its use in a clinical setting.

The analysis revealed significant baseline disparities. Patients in the BM group were diagnosed at a younger age (median 49 vs. 52 years; SMD = 0.158) and demonstrated a profoundly more aggressive tumor phenotype. This was evidenced by a higher burden of advanced disease (AJCC Stage III/IV: 55.2% in BM vs. 24.6% in non-BM), a finding underscored by a large standardized mean difference (SMD = 0.912).

Marked disparities were also observed in tumor biology. The BM cohort had a higher prevalence of hormone receptor-negative status (ER-negative: 54.6% vs. 29.7%; PR-negative: 62.2% vs. 36.7%). Consequently, the distribution of molecular subtypes differed starkly: Triple-Negative (31.5% vs. 20.5%) and HR-/HER2+ (23.8% vs. 9.0%) subtypes were disproportionately enriched in the BM group, whereas the HR+/HER2- subtype constituted a much smaller fraction (24.1% vs. 53.9%) (p < 0.001 for all).

Significant differences were also observed in treatment patterns between the two groups. Patients in the BM group were substantially more likely to have received chemotherapy than those in the non-BM group (95.0% vs. 82.7%, p < 0.001; SMD = 0.512). In contrast, endocrine therapy was less frequently administered in the BM group (41.9% vs. 64.5%, p < 0.001), whereas radiotherapy was more common (60.8% vs. 42.4%, p < 0.001).

LASSO-driven feature selection for a parsimonious predictive model

To construct a stable and clinically actionable predictive model while mitigating the risk of overfitting, we employed the LASSO regression algorithm for feature selection. From an initial pool of clinicopathological variables, 10-fold cross-validation identified an optimal regularization parameter (λ) that shrunk the coefficients of non-informative features to zero (Fig. 2A and B). This process distilled the feature set down to five pivotal predictors with the strongest association with BM development: AJCC N Stage, AJCC M Stage, Age at Diagnosis, AJCC T Stage, and primary tumor surgery (Fig. 2C). These selected features formed the basis for the final LASSO-regularized logistic regression model used for clinical deployment and subsequent interpretation.

Although chemotherapy showed a significant imbalance in the baseline comparison, it was not retained after LASSO regularization, suggesting that its predictive information was largely captured by more dominant variables such as stage, age, and surgery status. In the validation set, the LASSO-regularized logistic regression model (labeled LR(L1/EN) in Table 2; Fig. 3), leveraging the five LASSO-selected features, emerged as the top-performing model. It achieved an AUC of 0.907 (95% CI: 0.857–0.958), coupled with a sensitivity of 0.866 and specificity of 0.745 at the optimal threshold. The RF model also demonstrated excellent, albeit slightly lower, discrimination with an AUC of 0.894 (95% CI: 0.825–0.962). Other models, including the DT and NN, showed strong performance with AUCs of 0.844 and 0.834, respectively. In contrast, algorithms such as GBM and SVM-R were less effective in this classification task, yielding significantly lower AUCs. The superior performance of the final LASSO-regularized logistic regression model highlights the efficacy of the LASSO selection process in identifying the most salient predictors.

To provide a holistic view of model efficacy, we visualized multiple evaluation metrics—including AUC, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and F1-score—across all nine algorithms using a comprehensive model performance heatmap (Fig. 3C). This multidimensional assessment highlighted that while tree-based models like Random Forest achieved near-perfect scores in the training set (indicating a potential tendency for overfitting), the final logistic regression model maintained a balanced and stable profile across all clinical metrics. Furthermore, an AUC comparison bar plot (Fig. 3D) was constructed to directly juxtapose the discriminative performance of each model between the training and validation cohorts. This visual comparison demonstrated that the final logistic regression model exhibited consistency between the training and validation sets (AUC from 0.863 to 0.907), maintaining stable performance compared to models like KNN or SVM-R, which experienced notable performance degradation on unseen data.

Evaluation of clinical utility and model reliability

To translate predictive accuracy into clinical relevance, we performed Decision Curve Analysis (DCA). As illustrated in (Fig. 4), the final LASSO-regularized logistic regression model and the RF model demonstrated superior net benefit across a broad and clinically practical range of threshold probabilities in both the training and validation sets. In the validation set, the final LASSO-regularized logistic regression model provided positive net benefit across approximately 0.05 to 0.75 threshold probabilities, indicating that model-guided risk stratification may be more clinically useful than default strategies of assuming all patients or no patients are at high risk for BM. These findings suggest that the model may support individualized decisions regarding intensified neurological assessment or enhanced surveillance in selected high-risk patients.

Survival outcomes underscore the clinical significance of brain metastasis

To contextualize the clinical importance of BM prediction, we performed exploratory survival analyses. These analyses were not intended as a primary endpoint but rather serve to demonstrate the devastating prognostic impact of BM—median overall survival was profoundly shorter in the BM group compared to the non-BM group (p < 0.001)—thereby establishing the clinical urgency that motivates the development of an accurate prediction tool. The severe survival penalty associated with BM validates the premise that early identification of high-risk patients, before neurological symptoms manifest, could create a window for proactive intervention (enhanced surveillance, CNS-directed therapy) that may improve outcomes. Without such prognostic context, the clinical motivation for investing in prediction would be unclear. Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed the devastating prognostic impact of BM. A stark and statistically significant divergence in overall survival (OS) was observed between the BM and non-BM groups, with BM patients experiencing a profoundly poorer prognosis (p < 0.001) (Fig. 8C). This finding validates the clinical urgency of predicting and managing this condition. Survival was also heavily stratified by molecular subtype, with Triple-Negative and HR-/HER2 + patients exhibiting the worst outcomes, consistent with their higher propensity for developing BM (Fig. 8D). Among patients who developed BM, the administration of endocrine therapy was associated with a significant survival advantage (p = 0.021), highlighting a potential therapeutic avenue in this poor-prognosis subgroup (Fig. 8B).

To facilitate the clinical application of our predictive model and promote its use in practice, we developed and deployed a user-friendly, open-access web-based calculator based on the final LASSO-regularized logistic regression model. Discussion

The development of BM represents a watershed moment in the clinical course of BC, signifying a transition to a phase of high morbidity and profoundly limited survival. In this large-scale, multi-database study, we leveraged the power of machine learning to address the critical challenge of predicting this devastating event. By integrating data from over 186,000 patients across North American and Asian cohorts, we have not only delineated the clinicopathological landscape of BM on an unprecedented scale but also developed and validated a accurate, interpretable, and clinically translatable prediction model, which we have deployed as an open-access web tool.

Our analysis of baseline characteristics reaffirms and strengthens the existing understanding of the BM-prone phenotype. The strong association with younger age, advanced T and N stages, and aggressive molecular subtypes—particularly Triple-Negative and HER2-positive disease—is consistent with previous literature [29, 30]. However, the sheer scale of our cohort provides more precise and stable estimates of these effects. For instance, the dramatic enrichment of TNBC and HER2-positive subtypes in the BM group underscores the intrinsic biological propensity of these tumors for CNS tropism, possibly mediated by specific cellular adhesion molecules or an ability to co-opt the neuro-inflammatory microenvironment. The large standardized mean differences (SMDs) observed for variables like AJCC stage (0.912) and N-stage (0.768) highlight that tumor burden and lymphatic dissemination are not merely correlational but are overwhelmingly powerful determinants of future CNS relapse.

The central achievement of our study is the development of a superior predictive model using a systematic, data-driven approach. While we evaluated nine machine learning algorithms, the LASSO-regularized logistic regression model emerged as the top performer, achieving an AUC of 0.907 in the validation set. This finding is particularly insightful. While more complex, “black-box” models like deep neural networks or gradient boosting machines are often presumed to be superior, the success of a more parsimonious, regularized linear model suggests that the risk of BM in BC is driven by a few dominant, well-defined factors whose interactions are largely additive. The LASSO algorithm’s ability to perform feature selection was crucial, distilling the predictive signal down to five core variables: N-stage, M-stage, T-stage, age, and surgery. This inherent simplicity is a significant advantage, enhancing the model’s transparency, robustness against overfitting, and ease of clinical implementation.

The inclusion of M-stage as a top predictor warrants careful interpretation. While its high predictive power, confirmed by SHAP analysis of the final logistic regression model, it also presents a potential logical circularity for predicting the initial metastatic event to the brain in M0 patients. Its primary utility lies in quantifying risk for patients who already have extracranial metastases, a clinically distinct and important question. For M0 patients, our model’s strength relies on the other four variables—age, T-stage, N-stage, and surgery status. The strong negative coefficient for age is a particularly compelling finding, suggesting that the biology of BC in younger women may be intrinsically more aggressive or possess a higher affinity for CNS dissemination, a hypothesis that merits further biological investigation. Surgery status was associated with lower observed BM risk, although this association likely reflects disease extent, operability, and treatment selection rather than a direct anti-metastatic effect of surgery itself.

An additional finding that merits discussion is the marked imbalance in chemotherapy exposure between the BM and non-BM groups. In the baseline analysis, chemotherapy was administered substantially more often in patients who subsequently developed BM than in those who did not. However, chemotherapy was not retained in the final LASSO-selected model. The marked imbalance in chemotherapy exposure between the BM and non-BM groups likely reflects confounding by indication: patients with more advanced stage, greater tumor burden, and more aggressive molecular subtypes are both more likely to receive chemotherapy and more likely to develop CNS relapse. Therefore, chemotherapy in this context functions as a surrogate marker of disease severity rather than an independent predictor or causative factor. In addition, the binary coding of chemotherapy may have attenuated its predictive contribution by failing to capture clinically relevant heterogeneity in regimen type, treatment setting, dose intensity, and timing relative to BM occurrence.

Beyond raw accuracy, the clinical applicability of a prediction model is paramount. Our Decision Curve Analysis demonstrated net benefit for the final logistic regression model across a range of clinical risk thresholds, supporting its potential utility for guiding decision-making. Clinically, the threshold probability should be interpreted according to the balance between the potential benefit of early BM detection and the cost, anxiety, and false-positive findings associated with brain MRI surveillance. Because routine brain imaging is not generally recommended for asymptomatic BC patients, the present model should not be used as an automatic indication for MRI screening. Instead, it may serve as a risk-stratification aid to support individualized surveillance decisions. In practical terms, patients with predicted BM risk below 5% may continue standard follow-up, whereas those with predicted risk between 5% and 10% may warrant closer neurological symptom assessment and individualized follow-up. Patients with predicted risk ≥ 10% may be considered for enhanced surveillance, including individualized brain MRI assessment, particularly among younger patients or those with advanced nodal disease, extracranial metastasis, HER2-positive disease, or triple-negative breast cancer. Patients with predicted risk ≥ 20% may represent a very-high-risk subgroup suitable for multidisciplinary discussion or enrollment in prospective CNS surveillance studies. These thresholds are exploratory and should be prospectively validated before being adopted as definitive clinical decision cutoffs. This is not a trivial statistical point; it means that using our model to, for instance, select high-risk patients for enhanced MRI surveillance could lead to better outcomes than the current standard of care.

The model is intended to support the following clinical applications: (1) Risk-stratified surveillance: Patients identified as high-risk may benefit from more frequent brain MRI screening. Current NCCN guidelines do not recommend routine brain imaging in asymptomatic patients; however, a validated risk stratification tool could identify a subset for whom periodic surveillance (e.g., every 6–12 months) may be justified, particularly among younger patients with advanced nodal disease. (2) Treatment planning: For high-risk patients, clinicians may consider systemic agents with demonstrated CNS penetrance (e.g., tucatinib, capecitabine) earlier in the treatment sequence. (3) Patient counseling: The tool facilitates transparent risk communication, allowing patients to understand their individualized probability and participate in shared decision-making regarding surveillance intensity. (4) Clinical trial eligibility: The predicted risk score could help identify candidates for prevention-oriented clinical trials targeting CNS relapse.

This study possesses several key strengths that distinguish it in the field. First, its massive sample size, derived from three distinct and complementary databases, provides unparalleled statistical power and enhances the generalizability of our findings across different healthcare systems and ethnic backgrounds. Second, the head-to-head comparison of multiple machine learning algorithms provides a stable, unbiased assessment of the optimal modeling strategy for this clinical problem. Finally, our commitment to transparency, through the use of SHAP for interpretability and the deployment of an open-access tool, sets a new standard for translational research in this area.

Nevertheless, we must also acknowledge the limitations inherent in our study design. The retrospective nature of the data sources introduces the potential for unmeasured confounding variables and coding inaccuracies. We lacked granular data on specific chemotherapy regimens, radiation fields, and the precise timing of BM diagnosis relative to treatment, all of which could influence outcomes. Furthermore, while our multi-national cohort is a major strength, it does not constitute a formal prospective external validation. Future research should aim to validate our model in a prospective cohort, which remains the gold standard for assessing clinical prediction tools. Additionally, the future of risk prediction will likely involve the integration of multi-omic data; incorporating genomic classifiers, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) dynamics, or radiomic signatures from primary tumor imaging could further refine our model and unlock deeper biological insights into the mechanisms of BM.

In conclusion, this study developed and internally validated a LASSO-regularized logistic regression model for predicting BM in BC using a large, multi-source dataset. The resulting model is interpretable, parsimonious, and has been translated into an accessible clinical tool. It is inevitable that this study has certain limitations. BM was modeled as a binary outcome rather than a time-to-event endpoint. This approach does not account for censoring, competing risks, or differences in follow-up duration between the BM and non-BM groups (median 55.1 vs. 65.4 months). Patients with longer observation periods had more opportunities for BM detection, potentially introducing ascertainment bias. A time-to-event framework such as Cox proportional hazards regression or Fine-Gray competing risk modeling would be methodologically preferable and should be pursued in future studies where precise event timing is available across all data sources. Furthermore, this study lacks detailed treatment regimens related to HER-2.This may have affected the estimation of HER-2 related risk. This unmeasured variable represents an important limitation and may partly explain why molecular subtype was not retained in the final model despite its strong biological rationale. Moreover, follow-up duration differed between the BM and non-BM groups, and BM was modeled as a binary outcome rather than a time-to-event endpoint. This may have introduced bias in event ascertainment, because patients with longer observation windows had more opportunities to be identified as developing BM.

Future studies should incorporate time-dependent modeling frameworks to better account for follow-up heterogeneity and event timing. Because the hold-out split was not stratified by data source, patients from all three databases were represented in both the training and validation sets. This may yield moderately optimistic performance estimates relative to a fully independent external cohort. Source-stratified validation or leave-one-source-out cross-validation would provide a more stringent test of cross-population generalizability and is planned as a priority in future work. While further prospective validation is warranted, this work represents a significant step forward in our ability to stratify patients by their risk of CNS relapse, paving the way for more personalized surveillance and management strategies that may ultimately improve the dismal outcomes associated with this devastating complication.
