Surgical Cardiac Sympathetic Denervation in Active Inflammatory Cardiac Sarcoidosis
Authors: Vishal Gavali, Rahul Chhabria, Ameya Udyavar, Upendra Bhalerao, Ashwin B. Mehta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcoidosis is a systemic inflammatory disorder characterized by noncaseating granulomas, with clinically overt cardiac involvement occurring in 5% to 10% of cases. Cardiac sarcoidosis (CS) may present with malignant ventricular arrhythmias and electrical storm.Case SummaryA previously healthy 35-year-old man presented with palpitations and was diagnosed with CS following multimodality imaging and histopathology. He developed recurrent ventricular tachycardia consistent with electrical storm despite medical therapy. An implantable cardioverter-defibrillator was inserted for secondary prevention. Ongoing arrhythmic instability prompted bilateral video-assisted thoracoscopic sympathectomy, resulting in complete cessation of ventricular arrhythmias.DiscussionCS exhibits heterogeneous clinical manifestations, commonly including conduction abnormalities and ventricular arrhythmias. Diagnosis often requires integration of advanced multimodal cardiac imaging. Immunosuppression is central to controlling active myocardial inflammation, while autonomic modulation may be effective in refractory cases.Take-Home MessagesCS can masquerade as refractory electrical storm. Multidisciplinary management is essential, and cardiac sympathetic denervation may be a valuable therapeutic option when conventional therapies fail.

History of Presentation

A 35-year-old man presented to the emergency department of this hospital with history of intermittent palpitations, diaphoresis, and diffuse chest discomfort for 1 hour. On examination, he was conscious and oriented, afebrile with the blood pressure of 132/74 mm Hg, the heart rate was 212 beats/min and regular. The oxygen saturation was 96% while the patient was breathing ambient air. No cardiac murmur or added sounds were detected on auscultation. A 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) obtained at the time of arrival showed wide QRS complex tachycardia suggesting sustained ventricular tachycardia (VT) (Figure 1A). Amiodarone, as 150-mg bolus followed by infusion of 1 mg per minute and 2 mg of metoprolol tartrate were administered intravenously; this restored his rhythm to sinus (Figure 1B), and he was transferred to the cardiac intensive care unit.Figure 112-Lead Electrocardiograms During Hospitalization(A) A 12-lead electrocardiogram revealing wide complex tachycardia suggesting ventricular tachycardia (VT). Clinical Course and Management

A transthoracic echocardiography was performed that showed mild generalized left ventricular (LV) hypokinesis; predominantly in basal septal region and minimal pericardial effusion; left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) reported to be 45%. About 2 hours after the initial presentation, he developed palpitations again, and his blood pressure fell to 80/40 mm Hg, and he became unresponsive. An ECG repeated showed sustained VT (Figure 1C). Patient was cardioverted to sinus rhythm with biphasic shock at 150-J. Norepineprine by intravenous infusion was initiated. He regained his consciousness and became alert thereafter. Approximately 6 hours of hospitalization the cardiac monitor showed another episode of sustained ventricular arrythmia (VA), and he required electrical cardioversion. Patient had 3 episodes of sustained VAs within 24 hours, diagnosing as ventricular electrical storm (ES). Pharmacological treatment was escalated. He remained hemodynamically stable on vasopressors. Over next 24 hours no further episodes of VT were reported, and norepinephrine was discontinued. Oral amiodarone and beta-blocker were initiated. For secondary prevention of sudden cardiac death (SCD) he underwent single-chamber implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) on the fifth day of hospitalization. His coronary angiography did not reveal any presence of obstructive coronary artery disease. On the second day post-ICD insertion, he developed episodes of sustained ventricular tachycardia which required antitachycardia pacing as well as multiple ICD shocks. He was transferred to the intensive care unit due to hemodynamic deterioration. The patient was intubated and mechanically ventilated and deep sedation was administered to combat ES. Intravenous vasopressors, amiodarone, and lignocaine were commenced. Magnesium, at a dose of 2 g was administered intravenously. Patient received multiple ICD shocks over the next 2 hours. ICD interrogation was performed. Attempts were also made to cardiovert the patient externally. With aggressive pharmacological treatment his rhythm was restored to sinus.

A decision of sympathetic denervation was made, and he underwent bilateral video-assisted thoracoscopic sympathectomy. It involves removal of the distal half of the stellate ganglion (T1) and thoracic ganglia (T2-T4), thereby diminishing the noradrenergic input to the LV. The histopathological examination of the lymph node was consistent with sarcoidosis (Figure 4). Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient remained asymptomatic without any further episodes of ventricular arrhythmias after bilateral cardiac sympathetic denervation. He was commenced on guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure. He was subsequently discharged on day 17 of hospitalization. Oral antiarrhythmic drugs and immunosuppressive agents were continued. On follow-up after 2 weeks the patient showed significant clinical improvement; repeat echocardiography obtained showed recovery of the LV function to baseline. The patient was further followed up after 6 months where his FDG-PET demonstrated significant regression in the previously observed diffuse, increased metabolic activity in the LV myocardium and significant metabolic response was seen of the multiple mediastinal lymph nodes (Figure 3: post-therapy panel). A gradual de-escalation of antiarrhythmic drugs was planned on subsequent follow-up.

Discussion

Sarcoidosis is an inflammatory, multisystemic disease characterized by the presence of granulomas in any organ in the body. The incidence of sarcoidosis is highest in Scandinavian countries (11-24 cases per 100,000 population per year) and lowest in Asian countries (1 case per 100,000 population per year). Incidence is highly variable and depends on age, sex, ethnicity, and environmental and genetic factors.1 The incidence has been reported to peak at 30 to 50 years and at 50 to 60 years of age in men and women, respectively.2 Granulomatous inflammation is proposed to be a dysregulated antigenic response to unknown environmental exposure in a genetically susceptible person.3 Sarcoidosis has a multifaceted clinical involvement with variable organ damage and granuloma burden.1 The lung is the predominantly involved site in 90% to 95% of cases.4

CS can be coexisting with extracardiac involvement or can be the first or even isolated sign of sarcoidosis.5 CS diagnosed at autopsy, with dominant extracardiac involvement and clinically manifest CS are reported phenotypes in the literature.6 Clinically overt CS has been reported in 5% to 10% of cases.4 The prevalence of isolated CS has varied from 20% to 25%.6 The clinical presentation depends on degree of myocardial inflammatory activity. Patients present with palpitations, pre-syncope, syncope, or chest pain. Breathlessness disproportionate to the extent of respiratory disease may be due to cardiac involvement.7

In 15% of patients, SCD is the initial presentation of the disease and about 20% to 25% of patients may remain asymptomatic.8 High-grade atrioventricular block and VAs are the most common electrocardiographic manifestations. Other ECG features in CS include fragmented or prolonged QRS complex, abnormal Q-wave, T-wave inversion, epsilon-wave, frequent ventricular premature beats and atrial tachyarrhythmia.6 Sustained VT results from re-entry circuits in inflamed and scarred myocardial areas; however, automatic and triggered arrhythmias are also likely mechanisms.9 Multiple VT morphologies are common. Patients can also present with cardiac failure due to extensive LV infiltration and systolic impairment. CS can also involve coronaries, heart valves, and pericardium.6 Myocardial ischemic syndromes, other inflammatory cardiomyopathies (giant cell myocarditis and lymphocytic myocarditis), various infectious myocarditis, cardiac lymphomas, and certain genetic cardiomyopathies (arrhythmic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, laminin A/C, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, etc) can mimic the presentation of CS.10

The diagnosis of sarcoidosis is challenging because the disease may involve any organ and has varied clinical presentation. Endomyocardial biopsy is considered the gold standard to establish a diagnosis of CS, but the reported yield of unguided myocardial biopsy is ∼25%.11 However, due to the patchy nature of the disease, the sensitivity of endomyocardial biopsies remains low.7 Multimodality Imaging (with PET/CMR) is essential to establish the diagnosis. Echocardiography has a low specificity and sensitivity in detecting CS; regional wall motion abnormalities associated with wall thinning or thickening may be observed.6 The Heart Rhythm Society (HRS) 2014 expert consensus statement for the diagnosis of CS requires the evidence of extracardiac biopsy proven sarcoidosis, abnormalities of cardiac rhythm or structure, as well as characteristic features from advanced cardiac imaging modalities.12 An updated Japanese Circulation Society criteria set in 2019 included CMR and cardiac FDG-PET abnormalities in the major diagnostic criteria set.13 Late gadolinium enhancement on CMR has a diagnostic as well as prognostic role in CS, a nonischemic distribution with subepicardial and midwall involvement of the basal septum and/or inferolateral wall are most frequently reported. A mismatch pattern of perfusion defects and myocardial inflammation is characteristic of CS on FDG-PET imaging and is an imaging of choice to identify inflammation and monitor treatment response.6

Treatment of CS aims to control myocardial inflammation and mitigate its clinical consequences, that is, arrythmia and ventricular dysfunction. Glucocorticoids constitute the first-line therapy and steroid-sparing alternatives/antimetabolites are considered second line/in the management of patients with CS.11 The detailed pharmacotherapy of CS is reviewed extensively elsewhere.6,11

VAs may be the presenting symptom of CS and carry significant mortality; its incidence in patients with CS is not well defined.9 Last, Te and colleagues14 studied 2,237 patients with systemic sarcoidosis followed for a mean of 11.4 years and showed an incidence of VT of 0.94%. Ventricular ES complicating CS is published in a very few case reports.15, 16, 17, 18 Management of ES integrates provision of advanced cardiac life support, antiarrhythmic drugs, beta-blockade, sedation, catheter ablation, and identifying reversible causes.

ICD use in CS to prevent SCD is a potentially life-saving intervention. HRS expert consensus gives class I indication for ICD insertion in patients with spontaneous sustained ventricular arrhythmias, including prior cardiac arrest or LVEF <35%, despite optimal medical of immunosuppression (if there is active inflammation).12 2017 American Heart Association suggested Class IIa recommendations for individuals with CS and LVEF >35% who have syncope or myocardial scar on CMR or FDG-PET, indication for permanent pacing, or inducible sustained VT at electrophysiology study.19 Kron et al20 found that 36% of patients who had an ICD inserted for primary vs secondary prevention received appropriate ICD therapy over a mean follow-up of 4.2 years. The HRS recommends a period of 3 months of immunosuppression before ICD insertion in case of active myocardial inflammation. In the present case, a phase of transient arrythmia control was observed following initial immunosuppression therefore, decision of ICD insertion was planned. However, patient experienced recurrence of VT later. The data on timing of ICD insertion is scarce in the literature and considerable gap exists between research and clinical practice. Hence, the decision to proceed with ICD placement was individualized. Furthermore, the presence of late gadolinium enhancement on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, a well-recognized risk factor for ventricular arrhythmias and SCD in CS, supported the decision to proceed with ICD insertion.

Catheter ablation is associated with high rates of recurrence in VT storm associated with CS, notably, if there is active inflammation. Kaur et al21 reported a higher rate of recurrence among patients with ongoing inflammation (58.8%) than in patients with scar (14.2%). In a recent meta-analysis, acute success was achieved in 57% of the cases and complications in 5.7%.22 The patient in our case report was found to be in an inflammatory phase as confirmed on imaging and histopathological examination of lymph node biopsy; therefore, we decided to defer the catheter ablation and intensified immunosuppressive therapy.

The overlap between clinical and radiological features between pulmonary sarcoidosis and tuberculosis (TB) poses a significant challenge for the treatment. Therefore, the exclusion of pulmonary TB is mandatory for the diagnosis of pulmonary sarcoidosis, particularly in a high-prevalence areas of TB. IGRA test is a whole blood test that is based on the principle of persistent immune response to stimulation by Mycobacterium tuberculosis antigens, detected by interferon-γ assay. The 2 commercially available tests are QuantiFERON-TB Gold In-Tube test (QFT-G-IT), which uses enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, and T SPOT.TB, which involves an enzyme-linked immunospot assay. The positivity of IGRA occurs in both latent TB infection and sarcoidosis with some evidence claiming that IGRAs could identify latent TB infection in sarcoidosis patients both in high-prevalence and low-prevalence of TB areas.23 A positive IGRA indicates latent or active TB infection. Importantly, immunosuppression in an undiagnosed latent TB carries a risk of reactivation. The patient reported in our case was started on antitubercular drugs considering the prevalence of the disease in our country.

Cardiac autonomic modulation with percutaneous stellate ganglion blockade (PSGB), thoracic epidural anesthesia (TEA), and surgical cardiac sympathetic denervation (CSD) are established modalities in the management of ES. The autonomic nervous system plays key role in the genesis and maintenance of VAs, and this forms the basis for neuraxial modulation in the management of cases with refractory VAs. TEA involves administration (bolus and continuous) of anesthetic agents into the epidural space to achieve sympathetic blockade from T1 to T4. PSGB is a minimally invasive procedure, typically performed bedside with an ultrasound guidance at C7 to T1, that can temporarily suppress arrhythmias.24 TEA or PSGB may offer additional advantages by maintaining prolonged autonomic modulation, sustaining autonomic modulation, achieving superior control of ventricular arrhythmia by continuous infusion.25 Both procedures often serve as a bridge to a definitive therapeutic alternative (such as CSD) in treating refractory arrythmias.

CSD consists of a removal of the lower one-half of the stellate ganglion as well as the T2 to T4 ganglia of the thoracic sympathetic chain. Our patient underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic bilateral sympathectomy, permitting cessation of further ventricular arrhythmia. Bilateral sympathectomy achieves greater efficacy in reducing arrhythmic triggers, compared with left alone. TEA and PSGB were considered as potential therapeutic options in our patient, but we deferred both in view of the patient's presumed TB infection., along with limited institutional expertise. In addition, in patients with unstable arrhythmias, diffuse substrate, or recurrent ES, surgical sympathectomy offers a broader and more systemic modulation of sympathetic tone, reducing arrhythmic triggers at the autonomic level.25 Accordingly, CSD was pursued as a therapeutic strategy in this case, with the aim of providing a more durable solution to mitigate persistent ES.

Our case uniquely demonstrates the feasibility and clinical efficacy of CSD during active inflammatory disease. This approach diverges from traditional stepwise management and suggests that earlier consideration of surgical autonomic modulation maybe warranted in carefully selected, high-risk patients with refractory ES.

Okada et al26 reported that CSD may be a feasible adjunct therapy and may reduce the burden of VAs and ICD shocks. Our case differs from that of the Okada et al26 series in several key aspects. First, refractory ventricular ES was the initial clinical manifestation leading to the diagnosis of CS, rather than occurring in the setting of previously diagnosed disease. Second, advanced imaging and histopathology confirmed that our patient was in an active inflammatory phase, whereas many patients in the Okada et al26 case series cohort had predominantly scar-mediated arrhythmias. Third, catheter ablation was intentionally deferred in our patient because of diffuse myocardial inflammation, and bilateral thoracoscopic sympathectomy was employed early as a rescue strategy rather than as a late intervention after failed ablation. Finally, this case highlights additional complexity related to a TB-endemic setting, including positive IGRA testing and the need for concurrent antitubercular therapy during immunosuppression. To our knowledge, this is the fourth case in the literature in which a patient underwent CSD for ventricular ES in CS, making it a rare finding.

Conclusions

The clinical presentation of CS is heterogeneous and often challenging to diagnose. It can sometimes manifest in the form of life-threatening ventricular arrhythmia. Diagnosing CS is often complex and requires advanced multimodality imaging as well as tissue biopsy. Although lymph node biopsy in our case revealed predominantly noncaseating granulomas, the overlap between tuberculosis and sarcoidosis can pose a significant challenge in the evaluation of CS. Device therapy and immunosuppression form the basis of CS treatment complicating as VT. In addition, this case highlights the importance of sympathetic ganglionectomy to modulate cardiac autonomic function and reduce arrhythmic risk of refractory ES.
