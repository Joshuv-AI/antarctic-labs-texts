Uncertainty and unmet needs in older high-risk blunt trauma survivors and their caregivers: a multi-centre mixed methods study
Authors: Ting Hway Wong, Hazirah Mohamad, Ad Maulod, Stella Jinran Zhan, Mihir Gandhi, Grace Meijuan Yang, Dennis Chuen Chai Seow, Chetna Malhotra, Chek Hooi Wong, Angelique Wei-Ming Chan, Li-Tserng Teo, Sachin Mathur, Ling Tiah, Tze Tec Chong, Charyl Jia Qi Yap, Marcus Eng Hock Ong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Quality-of-life is reduced in older patients surviving major blunt trauma. Pre-injury frailty, chronic illness, low falls, or head injury, increase the risk of unplanned healthcare utilization and post-discharge mortality. However, post-hospitalization needs and experiences of patients and caregivers from this high-risk group are not well-understood. This mixed-methods longitudinal study aims to understand participants’ perspectives on needs, prognosis, quality-of-life and end-of-life planning, during the post-injury trajectory. High-risk patients > 55-years-old, and their caregivers, were recruited from three hospitals after surviving blunt trauma. All participants allowed the study team access to medical records, including unplanned readmission and death, for 36 months. Participants who consented to longitudinal follow-up were assessed for quality-of-life and caregiver burden at regular intervals. If unplanned readmission or death occurred, the longitudinal group participants were contacted for a semi-structured interview. Out of 155 participants recruited, 71 patients experienced > = 1 unplanned readmission and 33 died. Twelve semi-structured interviews were conducted after unplanned readmission or death. Five unmet needs were extrapolated from qualitative interviews: uncertainty of prognosis; access to financial support; unaddressed social needs; inconsistent access to healthcare and inadequate structural support. Qualitative findings corroborated with and provided context for the quantitative analysis. Patients with high (> = 2) unplanned readmissions, higher co-morbidities or poorer function had lower quality-of-life. For caregiver burden, high-readmission caregivers experienced increasing financial stress over time, but other domain trajectories were similar between high- and low-readmission groups. The study was limited by recruitment and access difficulties caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, and over-representation of the ethnic majority. Older high-risk blunt trauma survivors and caregivers need better support to mitigate uncertainties in their disease trajectory. Future directions include a system-level prompt to identify at-risk patients at discharge, offering access to designated care teams at different time-points to support the patients and caregivers to navigate the many uncertainties in their healthcare journeys.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47587-3.

Introduction

Injury in older adults is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide1,2. While previous studies focused on improvement of in-hospital mortality and morbidity3,4, there is now increasing interest in post-discharge quality-of-life, function, and long-term mortality5–12. For older survivors of moderate and severe injury, quality-of-life and functional outcomes are reduced post-injury, especially for patients with the following risk factors: pre-injury frailty, co-morbidities, low fall mechanism of injury, serious head injury, or higher overall injury severity scores5–8. These risk factors exacerbate any pre-morbid frailty, increasing mental and physical disability, resulting in higher risk of unplanned healthcare utilization and post-discharge mortality5,9–11.

However, little is known about the unmet clinical, social or psychological needs for these patients at high risk of unplanned readmission or death during the post-discharge trajectory. The goal of this mixed-methods study was to understand participants’ unmet needs or concerns, perspectives and awareness of disease severity and prognosis for recovery, changes in quality-of-life, function and overall health, and end-of-life planning, during the post-injury trajectory, for high-risk patients and their caregivers.

While many quantitative studies have examined post-injury older patients4–11,13–15, there are few qualitative studies16,17. The mixed methods approach was chosen to understand the specific needs and stressors taking place in this high-risk group, in the extended post-discharge phase, allowing the journey through unexpected and unplanned events over time to be triangulated with the trends of quality-of-life measures at defined time-points, for both individual participants, and for the cohort as a whole.

Better understanding of unmet needs would inform potential mechanisms of support for these high-risk patients.

Methods

Mixed-methods study design

For this mixed-methods study, convergent parallel design was applied, utilizing qualitative semi-structured interviews (after unplanned readmission or bereavement) and quantitative surveys (baseline demographics, follow-up measures at pre-defined time-points of quality-of-life and caregiver burden).

For the qualitative analysis, the analytical method adopted was reflexive thematic analysis, outlined by Braun and Clarke18. Purposive sampling and inductive analysis were performed. The twelve interviews, involving participants with direct, high-salience experience of unplanned readmission and / or death, carried sufficient analytical weight to support rigorous inductive analysis.

Transcripts were checked for quality. One study team member (H.M), who conducted one of the two bereavement interviews, did an initial round of coding the data. H.M read the transcripts several times and generated initial codes, by exploring needs verbalized by patients and caregivers both in the hospital readmission and bereavement interviews, using QSR International’s NVivo14 software for coding. Two study team members (H.M. and W.T.H.) then reviewed these preliminary codes and developed potential themes based on the first round of coding. These potential themes were iteratively debated by H.M., a qualitative research scientist experienced in end-of-life studies, and W.T.H., a surgeon researcher studying frailty in trauma patients. The themes were subsequently defined, named, discussed with other team members and modified again. We then selected suitable quotes supporting the main points of each theme.

The quantitative component was a descriptive analysis focused on charting the mean trajectories of quality-of-life and caregiver burden over the follow-up period for the cohort. The quality-of-life (QOL) score used was the World Health Organization Quality of Life Brief Version (WHO-QOL-BREF)19, a self-report questionnaire measuring subjective quality-of-life across the four domains of physical health, psychological health, social relationships, and the environment, plus a score for general quality-of-life. The modified Caregiver Reaction Assessment (mCRA) uses a Likert-scale tool to measure both negative and positive aspects of caregiving, in the domains of disturbed schedule and poor health, lack of finances, lack of family support, and caregiver esteem20. Both scales have been validated in Singapore.

Qualitative and quantitative analysis occurred iteratively and concurrently. The qualitative team analyzed the responses from the semi-structured interviews. The quantitative team charted the WHO-QOL-BREF and caregiver burden trajectories. After the initial concurrent and separate analysis, both qualitative and quantitative teams then held several joint discussions, triangulating the themes found in the qualitative data with the trends in quantitative data. Then, to understand underlying patterns of change, the teams analyzed the specific trajectories of individual participants, focusing on changes before and after unplanned readmission, and in the lead-up to death, again triangulating this with the interview data.

Study setting and participants

The research protocol was reviewed and approved by Singhealth Centralised Institutional Review Board (CIRB Ref: 2018/2184). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment.

Participants were recruited from three major public acute care hospitals in Singapore, from November 2018 to February 2022, during the index trauma admission, prior to discharge. The inclusion criteria were: age > = 55 years, blunt injury with Injury Severity Score (ISS) or New Injury Severity Score (NISS) ≥ 10 ref.21, with any of these risk factors associated with post-discharge readmission and mortality5–8: Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) > = 2 ref.22, low fall (< 0.5 m) ref.10, or head injury with Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) > = 3 ref.10,13,23. Low fall mechanism of injury was defined as falls from standing, same-level, or low height up to 0.5 m. This is based on previous analysis of the national trauma registry data, where detailed fall height was captured from standardized interpretation of furniture and building storey heights, to extrapolate injury mechanism fall height data. Poorer outcomes, adjusted for injury severity, comorbidity, gender and age, were found for fall heights from and including ground-level, up to and including 0.5 m (chair height), disproportionate to the low velocity of injury, hence this definition of low fall has been used for our subsequent studies23. Charlson comorbidity index (without the age factor), a score for co-morbidities ranging from 0 (no co-morbidities) to 33 (1 point each for myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular accident, dementia, chronic pulmonary disease, connective tissue disease, peptic ulcer disease, diabetes mellitus without end-organ damage; 2 points each for hemiplegia, severe kidney disease, localized solid tumor, leukaemia, lymphoma, diabetes mellitus with end-organ damage; 6 points for HIV or metastatic cancer), was chosen based on widespread use in trauma and geriatric studies.

Patients who died during the index hospitalization were excluded.

Caregivers were recruited separately from patients, with patients’ explicit permission if patients were able to communicate, or recruited directly if patients were unable to communicate their preferences.

At recruitment, WHO-QOL-BREF19, frailty pre-injury (modified Fried’s criteria)14,24, modified Barthel’s Index (a 100-point scale marking 10 domains, each on a scale of 0 for unable to 10 for independent, namely feeding, bathing, grooming, dressing, bowel control, bladder control, toilet use, bed-to-chair-to-bed transfers, mobility on level surfaces and stairs)25 and the Mini-Mental State Examination (a 30-point questionnaire screening for cognitive impairment and dementia, assessing orientation, memory, attention, language, and visuospatial skills)26 were administered to patients. Caregivers completed the mCRA20. Attending physicians were asked the surprise question at the time of recruitment (“Would I be surprised if this person died within the next 12 months?”)27.

All participants granted permission to the study team to access the patient’s medical records for the next three years, specifically looking for unplanned hospitalization and death, to obtain long-term unplanned hospitalization and mortality rates. Annual unplanned readmission and death data were obtained.

Participants were given the option to participate in the longitudinal active follow-up component, scoring WHO-QOL-BREF at 3-months, and then 6-month intervals till 36 months post-injury, and mCRA for caregivers at the same time-points. For each WHO-QOL-BREF and mCRA domain, mean scores were calculated at each follow-up time-point (0, 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36 months) using available observations and plotted over time to visualize trajectories. These trajectories were stratified by readmission (low: 0–1 vs high: ≥ 2), as well as by the following known risk-factors for readmission and low quality-of-life: frailty (by modified Fried’s criteria, low: 0–2 vs high: ≥ 3), function (Barthel’s index, low: < 80 vs high ≥ 80), and co-morbidities (Charlson Co-morbidity Index, low: 0–1 vs high ≥ 2). Trajectories were plotted using ggplot2 package in R (version 4.3.2) and RStudio (version 2024.09.0 + 375). These mean trajectories, showing the trends of quality-of-life and caregiver burden over time, by these specific risk-factors were then presented with individual experiences within the at-risk groups.

For the qualitative component, after unplanned readmission or bereavement, participants in the longitudinal follow-up group were invited for a semi-structured interview. We asked patients and/or caregivers questions related to their awareness of disease severity, changes in quality-of-life, function and overall health, assessing needs, concerns and supports, and end-of-life planning (Supplementary Table S1a and b). Open-ended questions followed by probes were asked when required. Interviewers included trained qualitative researchers with prior engagement with end-of-life populations. Quality of Death and Dying (QODD) questionnaire28 was administered to bereaved caregivers prior to the bereavement semi-structured interview. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and translated into English.

Characteristics of participants stratified by frequency of unplanned readmissions (low: 0–1, high: ≥ 2) and mortality status.

Almost one-third of patients (50, 32.3%) were unfit to answer questions at the time of recruitment, and questionnaires were filled out by their caregivers. This corresponds to the MMSE scores, with 21 (13.5%) patients unable to perform the MMSE, 82 (52.9%) MMSE < 24, and only 52 (33.5%) with MMSE > −24. Of these 82 MMSE < 24 patients, 64 had a head AIS score of > = 3, 21 patients had a formal diagnosis of dementia charted in their medical records at recruitment, including 7 patients with both formal a diagnosis of dementia and a head AIS score of > = 3. For the 21 patients who were unable to perform the test at all, 15 (71.4%) had a head AIS score of > = 3, 8 (38.1%) had a formal diagnosis of dementia charted at recruitment, and 6 (28.6%) had both a formal diagnosis of dementia and a head AIS score of > = 3.

Twelve semi-structured interviews were conducted via telephone or face-to-face. Eleven were recorded, transcribed and translated, while one transcript was based entirely on extensive fieldnotes by the interviewer, due to technical issues with the recorder. Most interviews were conducted in English (n = 8), the rest in Mandarin (n = 3) or a mixture of Mandarin and Cantonese (n = 1). Audio data was transcribed and translated by research staff.

The interviews were with: patients only (n = 2; no caregivers present), caregivers only (n = 7; patient unfit to participate), patient and caregiver together (n = 1, indicated preference), patient and caregiver interviewed separately (n = 2; indicated preference). Ten interviews pertained to readmission, and two were conducted with caregivers a few months after bereavement, to allow time and space to grieve.

All interviewees were Chinese. For patients, mean age was 82 years, majority female (82%), injured by same-level fall (91%), and not working (91%). Uncertainty of prognosis

After the initial hospitalization for physical injury, the resulting functional decline could be disproportionate to the duration of the index hospitalization. Hence, there was minimal time to accept and adapt to changes, particularly the loss of mobility. Rapid loss of function came as a shock to some participants, especially if falls prior to the index hospitalization had been non-injurious, and neither caregiver nor patient had realized that the prior frequent falls could be a harbinger of impending functional decline. This contributed to the sense that there was not much they could do, with the sense of unpreparedness compounding the sense of loss. This lack of prognostic awareness caused anxiety, as patients and caregivers were surprised by the trajectory of deterioration.

There appeared to be less uncertainty when caregivers expressed understanding or acceptance that the prognosis was poor. They appeared to have lower decisional conflict, more confidence in decision-making, and experienced a smoother transition towards the end-of-life care trajectory.

Similarly, when caregivers were aware of patients’ care goals and needs, they appeared more confident and accepting of the trajectory of deterioration. This was the case even if end-of-life decision-making could no longer be explicitly discussed with the patient. Caregivers were more prepared if there had been another recent encounter with serious illness or death.

Uncertainty of access to financial support

All patients and caregivers mentioned financial concerns. Unplanned readmissions corresponded to more bills, additional deterioration in health status, and increasing functional dependence. This gave rise to multiple dimensions of financial uncertainty: (a) whether they would receive financial support after the readmission; (b) whether there would be further functional decline and hence increased future financial needs; and (c) especially when the prognosis and remaining lifespan was unclear – how long the financial burden would go on for.

Caregivers faced onerous processes and constant follow-up appointments that they had to go through to access public financial support schemes, which are means-tested. While the financial subsidy programs for the older adults aged 55 years and older30 were reassuring for some interviewees, it was insufficient for others.

It was particularly stressful when there were multiple potential sources of financial support or reimbursement, each with its own opaque set of rules, limits and unpredictable processing times.

Unaddressed needs for family and social support

Family dynamics and social support had a substantial effect on caregiver stress. Caregiving could cause tension in social relationships, affect sleep, and have an impact on work.

Some caregivers felt isolated and could not share their challenges with anyone, due to the lonely nature of caregiving and difficulty of verbalizing these challenges. This sometimes culminated in the sole or main caregiver getting burnout. There could be discordance between who the main caregiver and the main decision-maker was, influenced by gender, education and generation. This likely compounded any existing relationship strain.

In contrast, some caregivers cherished the closer familial bonds catalyzed by caring for the patient, experiencing reduced caregiver stress. Caregivers who had siblings to share costs had a reduced burden, financial and otherwise.

Inconsistent access to healthcare and information

Patients and caregivers encountering supportive healthcare professionals, catering to both informational and healthcare needs, were more confident in navigating the post-hospitalization journey. Unfortunately, this was not the case for many participants. If healthcare was siloed into different specialties or services (for example surgical disciplines for trauma, medical disciplines for co-morbidities), interviewees could feel neglected, unless they encountered a service with a holistic approach.

One caregiver mentioned how fragmentation of care, without a clear sense of purpose, led to confusion as they were shunted between one healthcare provider to the other, only for their anxiety to be finally resolved once they were referred to a home hospice program, for which the care they received was perceived to be very different.

Some reported a language barrier when trying to access information, compounding information asymmetry, leading to confusion or difficulties in communication with healthcare providers.

Many brought up how access to care was affected by limitations during the COVID-19 pandemic. This reduced contact with family for patients who needed it most, causing regret due to the unexpectedness with which patients passed on, without all family members by their side.

Inadequate structural support

Patients and caregivers mentioned how access to daycare or active ageing centers helped to give a sense of purpose, allowing patients to participate in activities that provided a break for some caregivers. However, these options were mostly limited to patients who still retained some mobility.

For patients who had deteriorated beyond the point that caregivers were able and willing to care for them, the nursing home appeared to be the only solution. However, many caregivers encountered difficulty in getting patients placed in a nursing home due to a nationwide shortage. They had to make alternative arrangements in the interim, compounding the stress, in addition to concerns on the affordability of a nursing home.

Another major point was the role of live-in migrant domestic workers (MDWs, or “helpers”) to support increasing home care demands. Some caregivers described how they were almost at breaking point before they managed to get support from MDWs. MDWs were crucial in easing the load faced by many caregivers, especially for patients who became less mobile after a fall. While the MDWs provided relief for many caregivers, hiring MDWs came with its own set of challenges, which many felt helpless to address, as caregivers were dependent on them for the day-to-day physical caregiving.

Access to services such as specialized physiotherapy programs and mobility aids such as rollators, motorized scooters and grab bars greatly aided in the transition-to-home for patients post-injury. However, this appeared limited to the participants who had been referred by the healthcare team to the relevant program, advised on the necessary equipment, or had the foresight to tap into infrastructural support such as senior-friendly “community care apartments” that allowed them access to these services.

For participants experiencing a successful transition out of institutional care, many expressed relief after returning home.

Quantitative analysis: unplanned readmission and divergent trajectories of quality-of-life and caregiver burden

For the 155 participants, unplanned readmissions ranged from 0 to 15 in the first year, and 0–44 over the 3-year follow-up period. While 84 (54.2%) had no unplanned readmissions, for the remaining 71 patients, many readmission episodes occurred beyond the first year (the standard follow-up period in many studies)9,12,14,15,23, into the subsequent two years. This corroborates with the theme on uncertainty in the interviews, highlighting the unpredictability of their trajectory.

Furthermore, the cumulative yearly death rate was similar between the low- and high- readmission groups (not differing greatly from the overall 11.0, 17.4 and 21.3% first-, second- and third-year cumulative mortality respectively). This suggests that impending death and increasing symptoms were not the only underlying driver of the readmissions.

Trajectories of quality-of-life and caregiver burden among interview participants

Of the participants who gave interviews, all patients were frail (Table 2). This patient had seven unplanned readmissions, likely a major contributing factor to the low WHO-QOL-BREF and mCRA. From the interviews (see theme 2), Q4p and Q4cg had better financial and structural support, although there could be other contributory factors.

Of the patients who were well enough to participate in the study and give interviews themselves, all three had CCI < 2. Two patients survived the study period, with above average and relatively stable WHO-QOL-BREF scores despite their pre-injury and injury risk factors (S5p and R59p, Fig. 2b and c). S5p suffered declining psychological and social health over time, despite high, stable scores in other domains, possibly reflecting the financial concerns mentioned in the interview. R59p suffered a decline in all domains prior to readmission, although the overall trajectory reflected high WHO-QOL-BREF otherwise. One (S11p) died during the study period, experiencing a WHO-QOL-BREF trajectory below the mean, declining before death (Fig. 2d). This patient had a reasonable Barthel score (75) at baseline, signaling a rapid decline after the injury. The interview with this patient’s caregivers mentioned uncertainty several times, across different domains (function, prognosis), and it may be harder to adapt to the post-injury state for a pre-morbid high-functioning person without pre-existing supports.

There was a similar decline in the WHO-QOL-BREF trajectory for R79p, who died during the study period, also frail with CCI < 2. However, the mCRA remained stable throughout for this caregiver (R79cg, Caregiver, Son), with only a slight deterioration in the lead-up to the patient’s death (Fig. 2e). For R79cg, family support (theme 3) was mentioned in positive light early in the interview, providing one explanation for the stable mCRA trajectory.

Of the two patients for whom participant caregivers agreed to a bereavement interview, and completed the QODD questionnaire, both had sustained moderate injury (NISS and / or ISS 10–15) after a low fall, and were aged over 75. The first patient, male (R65p), had the lowest pre-morbid Barthel score (25) of the interviewees, mainly due to a severely disabling pre-injury stroke, and had just been recently widowed. For the caregivers in their interview, although the pandemic restrictions blocked access to the patient at the end, it appeared that they had been prepared for his passing, and felt that the patient had already suffered greatly pre-injury. The caregiver rated his QODD as 50 (a moderate quality of death), and he died soon (46 days) after his injury.

In contrast, for the other patient, female (R79p), the premorbid Barthel was 100. The QODD score was rated as high (92.5, high quality of death), and the patient survived almost two years post-injury (704 days).

In both cases, family support featured positively in the interview, although it may well have been that the caregivers who felt prepared to give the interview were the ones with better support, hence not being representative of other caregivers.

Integrating qualitative and quantitative findings

The qualitative findings identified five domains of unmet need shaping the post-injury experience, with uncertainty as a cross-cutting thread – about prognosis, financial entitlements, and whether adequate support would be available or accessible. The quantitative findings show, for the cohort with at least two unplanned readmissions — unpredictable in both frequency and timing — declining patient QOL across all domains, while caregiver burden increased primarily in the financial domain for the high-readmission cohort. When comparing the WHO-QOL-BREF 3-year trajectories discretely by the risk-factors of comorbidity, functional status, and frailty at baseline, comorbidity burden emerged as the strongest differentiator of QOL trajectories across all domains, followed by functional status, yet QOL improved over time in many domains. In comparison, there was not much difference in QOL over time between low- and high- frailty groups, apart from high-frailty patients having slightly worse social relationships QOL. For caregiver burden, the greatest differentiator was function, with caregivers of functionally dependent patients experiencing greater burden in the domains of disturbed schedule and poor health, and lack of finances. Hence, comorbidity, function, and frailty intersect with QOL, caregiver burden and readmission, compounding the unmet needs caregivers described.

Discussion

The needs and concerns of older high-risk blunt trauma survivors and their caregivers included: uncertainty of prognosis, financial, social, access to healthcare, and structural support. While the overall unplanned readmission rate was high in these high-risk patients, there was broad variation as to how many unplanned readmissions occurred in the post-injury trajectory, and when in the post-discharge trajectory they occurred. For the group with high unplanned readmission, WHO-QOL-BREF trended lower over time. Compared to the low unplanned readmission group, caregiver burden for the high unplanned readmission group increased over time in the domain of finances, but there was no difference in the other domains. While comorbidity and baseline functional status emerged as strong differentiators of quality-of-life across time for the cohort, differences in frailty had less of an effect. This highlights the importance of studying these correlated but nevertheless independent risk factors (comorbidities, functional independence and frailty) separately, and the difficulty of predicting which patients would need more support, and when.

Uncertainty was a recurrent theme. The loss of control from not knowing the prognosis, not knowing their eventual financial obligations, or not knowing if they would obtain the necessary medical, social and structural support, all contributed to a reduction in QOL.

The poor correlation between physician expectations from the surprise question, and actual one-year mortality, highlighted how difficult prognostication can be. In a recent systematic review, the surprise question performs poorly to modestly as a predictive tool for death, with worse performance in non-cancer illness27.

Overall, our findings resonate with those of studies performed in different disease, cultural, and healthcare contexts. A major physical injury often causes a sudden functional decline, increasing any pre-injury mental and physical deterioration. The disease trajectory of patients in our study appears similar to those of chronic disease patients, whose health services needs differ from cancer patients. Compared to cancer patients, chronic disease patients have a different disease trajectory – usually a more gradual decline interrupted by acute exacerbations33.

The importance of family and community support for both patients and caregivers has been echoed in other recent trauma studies16,17. Our finding that the social domain for quality of life differs between the frail and non-frail group suggests additional opportunities for community-level support.

Similar to our findings, the desire for information and guidance on life post-injury has also been reported in another recent study of older head-injured patients17.

Financial stress, a major concern for our participants, has been studied in other end-of-life settings34. A study of cancer patients in Singapore showed a strong correlation between socioeconomic status and place of death35. Financial burden is associated with negative outcomes for both patient and caregiver, and there are unquantified costs associated with caregiving in the community, compared to institutional care34.

Crucially, while other studies have highlighted financial difficulties and rising costs, many of our participants expressed anxiety over whether they qualified to receive financial support. Although financial assistance schemes are available, the complexity of the application process meant that participants may not have been aware of their eligibility status, how much subsidy they would receive, or which scheme to apply for. This stress was amplified by the uncertainty of not knowing the prognosis, leading to the conundrum of not knowing how much time was left, and how long the financial strain will last. End-of-life healthcare costs increase, especially in the last three months of life, particularly if societal costs are taken into account36. Not knowing whether the patient’s function might improve, how much lifespan there might be left, and uncertainty over what cognitively impaired patients would have wanted – all serve to exacerbate stress.

In our study, generation differences affected financial burden and caregiver dynamics. In other studies, unmet needs varied by gender, marital status, income, place of death, previous hospitalization, memory-related diseases, self-rated health, and chronic diseases, with the proportion of home versus acute hospital deaths varying by healthcare system and cultural context37. As family sizes shrink over successive generations, especially in rapidly aging societies, with fewer potential caregivers, the burden on the next generation would only increase, while the dynamics between genders and generations evolve. To address the unmet needs of this high-risk population, any system-level interventions will have to be tailored to diverse patient and caregiver profiles.

Participants reported anxiety when hiring a foreign domestic worker for the first time after an injury. Prior studies show that family caregivers meet the needs of disabled older adults better than non-family caregivers38. Sustainable community-based, institutional or other long-term solutions for an aging society are needed, to reduce uncertainty among patients and caregivers.

Trajectories of QOL and mCRA

When QOL and mCRA trajectories were examined over time, stratifying the cohort by high- and low- readmission, and by high- and low- risk factors (comorbidity, functional independence, and frailty), the differences in QOL and caregiver burden varied by domain and by time-points, a reflection of trajectories with different needs at different periods. For example, over the 3-year study period, the high-morbidity group showed improvements in psychological health, social relationships and environmental health, which could be accounted for by gradual adaptation and/or access to support. Similarly, when comparing high- vs low- functioning groups, the low-functioning group also experienced gradual improvements in the QOL domains of psychological health, social relationships and environmental health, but to a lesser extent than when comparing high- and low- comorbidity groups. The potential for improvements in QOL may represent opportunities for additional intervention or support.

This difference in trajectory was also evident when comparing individual trajectories and interviews of participants with similar risk-factors, showing the complex interplay between comorbidity, function, frailty, caregiving, and accessing support within the healthcare system.

Our study included patients and their caregivers, representing both journeys on the post-injury trajectory. Post-injury caregiving is a field of research that is gaining increasing interest39–41. Similar to our study, other authors have found high unmet needs among caregivers, difficulty in navigating the healthcare system, and an appreciation for personal, familial or workplace relationships as important sources of informal support40,41.

Future directions

Interventions worth considering include advance care planning outreach for high-risk patients, access to information at different time-points, and designated care teams to support patients and caregivers to navigate the many uncertainties in their healthcare journeys. Physicians may not be aware of the poor prognosis in this profile of patients, hence a system-level prompt based on risk-factors, rather than depending on individual healthcare providers, may be helpful in identifying high-risk patients.

In a systematic review of unplanned admissions from care homes42, additional system-level support appeared to reduce admissions, and decision-making was a source of tension among healthcare providers, patients and caregivers. In a study of patients with end-stage heart failure, a disease with a similarly uncertain prognosis as our study population, advance care planning did not affect the likelihood of the desired place of death for the patient. However, decisional conflict was reduced after advance care planning43.

Goals of care discussions after hip fractures are recommended during the index hospitalization44,45. Similar principles should apply to our study population, who have higher injury severity scores and a worse prognosis than hip fracture patients. Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) has been associated with improved 1-year mortality in trauma patients, and advance care planning conversations can be integrated into CGAs12,46. In a study of major trauma survivors in Singapore, most long-term deaths occurred in the acute hospital (< 30% dying at home)10. The proportion of home deaths was lower compared to patients dying after stroke (50.3%) or cancer (35.8%), even though the majority of patients and caregivers in Singapore would prefer a home death47,48. This gap suggests that care needs required for a home death are not being met.

Hence, future interventions would be best designed to be longitudinal, rather than single-point supports, to better accommodate the disparate trajectories seen in our participants. Holistic and accessible post-discharge follow-up for high-risk older blunt trauma survivors and their caregivers would enable early assessment of their needs, to provide the appropriate informational, financial, social and structural support.

Limitations

Participant recruitment and access

Our study was limited by the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, necessitating change from in-person interviewing to telephone interviews. The poorer response and recruitment rates than in previous studies13–15 was probably due to the increased anxiety over unnecessary contact with research teams during the pandemic.

In addition, the change to a telephone interview may have influenced the depth of qualitative data, as well as the willingness of participants to answer sensitive questions. Nevertheless, only two of the interviews were done via telephone; while we acknowledge that a telephone interview would not be able to match a face-to-face interview, research assistants were trained using a semi-structured interview guide and the questions posed were still answered through the telephone interview.

Ethnicity and representation

We were only able to recruit English- and Chinese-speaking participants, likely due to lack of research team members proficient in Malay, unlike previous studies. This affects the generalizability of our study in several ways: we were unable to hear first-hand accounts of unmet needs specific to ethnic minorities in the at-risk population, which limits the generalizability of the findings to ethnic minorities. This limitation also affects the generalizability of our study findings to other multi-ethnic societies.

While our findings capture the experiences of Singaporean Chinese, the majority ethnic group, ethnic minorities make up approximately 25% of the at-risk population. It is likely that the stressors faced by patients from ethnic minorities would, at the minimum, be greater than those faced by the ethnic majority. For example, if members of the majority ethnic group express problems with language barriers, then it is likely that patients and caregivers from minority ethnic groups would face even greater barriers.

In addition, there may have been other stressors that our study did not capture at all, specifically, stressors uniquely faced by members of ethnic minority groups. For these stressors, our study findings would not have captured these, and hence our results may not be fully representative of the life journey and experience of patients from an ethnic minority.

Inclusion of caregiver participants as proxies of cognitively impaired

Many of the high-risk patients in our study had pre-injury cognitive impairment, or sustained head injuries affecting their ability to participate, as indicated by the number of participants too severely impaired to perform the MMSE. Hence, their QOL questionnaires were done by their surrogates (caregivers), and may not be a true reflection of participants’ own QOL. We explored the effect of this in the additional analyses (Supp. Fig. 3: WHO-QOL-BREF by proxy vs non-proxy responders), whereby, as with other papers in the literature31,32, QOL by proxy was generally lower than participants who could respond themselves. However, it is difficult to know how much the difference is due to the surrogate responder underestimating QOL, and how much it is because the participants unable to respond are generally more impaired.

Nevertheless, the inclusion of caregivers in our study as proxy respondents, as well as direct stakeholders, enabled our study to be inclusive of the cognitively impaired (either pre-injury, or as a result of injury) patient population that are at high risk of post-injury morbidity and mortality. This enabled us to explore the journey of patients who, if their caregivers were not recruited, would otherwise not be studied at all, because they were too impaired to participate, either due to pre-existing cognitive impairment, head injury after a frailty-related fall, or both.

Finally, our study focused on patients at high risk of unplanned readmission or post-discharge mortality, as our previous studies examined risk factors at the index admission for these poor outcomes. Nevertheless, there may be other patients who did not experience unplanned readmission or post-discharge death, but who nonetheless had unmet needs, whose journeys would not be included in the qualitative part of our study. In addition, although the cohort size would permit more advanced trajectory modelling approaches (e.g., latent class analysis), this was beyond the scope of the present study, which focused on descriptive trajectory patterns and their integration with qualitative findings.

Conclusion

Quality of life for older high-risk blunt trauma survivors and their caregivers may be improved with better access to informational, financial, social, healthcare, and structural support, while also mitigating the myriad uncertainties faced in their disease trajectory.
