Self-charging organic flow batteries based on multivalent metal negative electrodes
Authors: Tao Wang, Guo Yang, Mingjin Cui, Huitang Xia, Chenlu Jiang, Yuheng Xia, Ke Chen, Menghao Yang, Jiwoong Bae, Cheng Gu, Yu Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelf-charging batteries, which integrate energy conversion and storage within a single system, represent a promising technology for building a reliable and intelligent energy network. However, the charging rate of conventional self-charging energy systems that use solid-state electrodes is limited by slow solid-gas reaction processes at the electrode-air interface. A complete charging procedure typically requires several hours. Here we show a self-charging organic redox flow battery to address the limitations of solid-state reaction kinetics. A high charging rate is achieved, with 94% of the total capacity reached within 8 minutes, owing to the rapid kinetics of liquid-phase redox reactions. Using manganese oxide-based catalysts to reduce side reactions, the flow battery exhibits nearly 99.98% capacity retention over 1,600 cycles. Even in a harsh environment of -10 °C, the battery can run more than 2,500 cycles at a current density of 20 mA cm-2. The redox chemistry underlying the self-charging mechanism is investigated through computational modeling and in situ characterization, revealing that fast outer-sphere electron transfer during the enolization reaction contributes significantly to the reaction kinetics. In the proof-of-concept demonstration, we further extend the system from zinc to magnesium and aluminum as the negative electrodes, demonstrating a potential pathway for constructing sustainable energy systems.Subject terms: Batteries, Batteries

Introduction

Rechargeable batteries are essential across a broad array of applications and stand out as a key technology for effective energy storage1–3. By supporting the integration of renewable energy sources into power grids, they play a pivotal role in balancing energy generation and consumption4,5. From mobile phones to electric vehicles and large-scale grid storage, rechargeable batteries are woven into our daily lives. Yet, many such batteries rely on a direct grid connection for charging, which poses challenges in remote or harsh environments where the grid may be unreliable or inaccessible6. To overcome these limitations, various energy harvesting technologies, such as photovoltaic cells, thermoelectric generators, and nanogenerators, are now paired with rechargeable batteries to create self-powered systems7–9. However, these devices depend on intermittent energy sources, such as sunlight, heat, or movement, limiting their utility in certain applications. Furthermore, their integration often demands complex configurations with additional components. This challenge has spurred interest in developing streamlined self-charging batteries that operate independently of external environmental factors. Integrating energy conversion and storage offers a simplified solution for creating reliable and autonomous energy networks.

Among available resources, oxygen is ubiquitous in the air and can chemically charge batteries for integrated energy conversion and storage applications, and a variety of air-enabled self-charging batteries have been reported. For example, Wei et al. developed a self-rechargeable Zn ||polyaniline@Pt/C (PANI@Pt/C) battery, which shows 75% capacity retention after a self-charging period of 9 h10. Comparably, Liu et al. presented a Zn | |HCLHAYN (hexachlorohexaazatrinaphthylene) self-rechargeable battery that achieves 82% of its theoretical capacity following a 15 h self-charging duration11. Guo et al. introduced a Zn | |V2O5 self-rechargeable battery, which exhibits a capacity retention of 92% after a 6-hour self-charging process12. These progresses highlight the potential of air-enabled self-charging batteries toward broad applications beyond conventional power sources13–18.

Despite these advances, the majority of reported self-charging batteries rely on solid-state electrodes, and the charging rate is seriously restricted by the sluggish solid-gas reaction kinetics at the electrode-air interface19–21. On one hand, the dense solid electrodes composed of active materials, conductive carbon, and binders obstruct the diffusion of O2, giving rise to limited reaction interface areas. Additionally, the charging process of electrodes by O2 is accompanied by the deintercalation of Zn2+ ions in solid electrodes. Such diffusion-controlled processes further impede the reaction kinetics22,23. Accordingly, the charging process generally requires several hours to complete, and such a time-consuming procedure restricts widespread applications where rapidly delivering or absorbing power within a short timeframe is needed.

Considering the higher mobility and faster reaction kinetics of liquids than solids, it is expected to enhance self-charging rates by employing liquid-state electrodes24,25. Herein, we report a self-charging flow battery (SCFB) with redox species dissolved in liquids to overcome the diffusion barrier. Therefore, O2 can diffuse through the liquid phase to oxidize the reduced polyol rapidly. Moreover, charge carriers, like Zn2+, are readily mobile in liquids, and it is reported that the diffusion coefficient can reach up to 10-5 cm2 s-1, which is several orders of magnitude larger than that in solids (10-12 to 10-8 cm2 s-1)26–30. As a result, Zn2+ ions can rapidly diffuse through the system, facilitating charge balance and thereby enabling fast recharging of batteries. This enhanced diffusion process is critical for sustaining the efficiency of the charging cycle.

Additionally, flow batteries have high scalability with redox species stored in outer reservoirs, and such an open cell structure matches well with the air-infusion process of self-charging batteries31–34. Merely, an air pump is needed to introduce O2 into the reservoir for self-charging, circumventing disassembly and air circulation in reported self-charging batteries based on solid-state electrodes31. After rational screening to tailor characteristics of organic electroactive materials and solvation environments of electrolytes, we achieved a high charging rate for self-charging batteries, reaching 94% of theoretical capacity within 8 min. The high charging rate is attributed to the rapid outer sphere electron transfer of organic redox species in liquid electrolytes, as well as the efficient and uniform oxygen interaction with active substances35–37. The SCFB, which utilizes organic redox species, base metals, and ubiquitous oxygen from the air, is constructed entirely from earth-abundant elements as building blocks38. This type of battery architecture may present potential benefits for the development of next-generation self-powered energy systems.

Results

Distinguished from conventional batteries based on solid-state electrodes, redox flow batteries have electroactive materials stored in outer reservoirs while redox reactions take place on electrodes, which enables decoupled control over energy and power39,40. Such an open cell configuration correlates well with the self-charging batteries, where air infusion is needed. Figure 1a shows a schematic of the SCFB. Unlike conventional self-charging solid-state-electrode batteries, the SCFB system only requires an air pump to infuse oxygen into the reservoir, allowing it to charge without disassembling the cells. This simple and effective design can reduce both the complexity of the device and the overall system cost.

From an electrochemical perspective, the design principle of air-enabled SCFBs hinges on the redox potentials of electroactive materials, which should be lower than oxygen reduction reactions. In this work, we systematically investigated a variety of organic redox species featuring different functional groups, namely aminoxyl radicals, organic halides, heterocyclic organic compounds, and carbonyl derivatives. The standard electrode potential for oxygen reduction ranges from 0.401 to 1.23 V vs. SHE (standard hydrogen electrode), depending on the pH of the solution36. For a molecule to be compatible with self-charging processes, it must possess a redox potential lower than the oxygen reduction potential, allowing it to undergo electron transfer to oxygen and complete the self-charging process. Figure 1b shows cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves of various electroactive materials, tested in both O2 and Ar atmospheres, including 4-OH TEMPO, 1-amino-pyridine iodide, 8-hydroxypyridine-1,3,6-trisodium trisulfonic acid, and 3,7-bis(dimethylamine) clofenazolium. Under an Ar atmosphere, the current peaks observed in the voltammograms correspond to well-defined reduction and oxidation processes. These peaks reflect the electrochemical behavior of the molecules, indicating their redox potentials ranging from 0.2 to 0.9 V (vs. Ag/AgCl). It is noted that these CV curves in oxygen can almost overlap with those in Ar, suggesting that oxygen did not participate in the redox reaction due to the high reduction potentials of the redox species explored in Fig. 1b.

In comparison, we switched from high-potential redox species to low-potential alternatives, and the quinone family was adopted as a model study. Figure 1c presents the CV curves of a series of quinone derivatives, including sodium anthraquinone sulfonate 2,7-anthraquinone disulfonic acid (2,7-AQDS), 2-anthraquinone sulfonic acid (2-AQS), 1,5-anthraquinone disulfonic acid (1,5-AQDS), and 1-anthraquinone sulfonic acid (1-AQS), and notable differences can be detected when tested in Ar or O2-infused solutions. For instance, 2,7-AQDS exhibits symmetrical current peaks in the Ar atmosphere, indicating good electrochemical reversibility. When exposed to oxygen, however, the current responses showed a marked change, as the reduced 2,7-AQDS species rapidly reacted with oxygen existing in the surrounding electrolyte. This interaction regenerated 2,7-AQDS, reducing its mass transport in the solution and increasing the current. The oxidation peak shows a corresponding decrease, and continuous oxygen infusion leads to oscillations in CV curves. Similar results can also be gained from other quinone derivatives in Fig. 1c, further validating the design principle of SCFBs, and the key to enabling self-charging is the meticulous choice of redox-active materials with finely tuned redox potentials.

To gain more insights into the chemistry of quinone derivatives, we calculated their highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) levels (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Data 1). A strong correlation between the reduction potential and LUMO energy levels of quinones was observed (Supplementary Fig.1), and 2,7-AQDS exhibits the highest redox potential and the lowest LUMO energy, attributed to the strong inductive effect of two electron-withdrawing groups attached. The energy gap between HOMO and LUMO was calculated as well, and 2,7-AQDS shows the smallest gap, indicating the fastest electron transfer upon reduction in light of the frontier molecular orbital theory. In comparison, it is noted that 1,5-AQDS, featuring two electron-withdrawing groups, exhibits a lower voltage in contrast to 2-AQS. This finding suggests that the position of substituents plays a significant role in modulating electrochemical behaviors as well. In particular, the sulfonate group can serve as a carbonyl orthosis to donate electrons to the carbonyl group, weakening its electron-accepting ability. This interaction contributes to increased LUMO energy and altered electrochemical behavior of the molecules. Among all studied molecules, 2,7-AQDS, possessing the lowest LUMO energy and the smallest energy gap, is expected to maintain electrochemical performance with rapid electron transfer and self-charging reaction kinetics.

The electrochemical characteristics of quinones were further characterized using the rotating disk electrode (RDE) test. The limiting currents at different rotating rates correspond to a diffusion-controlled process, yielding diffusion coefficients of 2.954 × 10-5 cm2 s-1, 3.970 × 10-5 cm2 s-1, 2.245 × 10-5 cm2 s-1, and 2.051 × 10-5 cm2 s-1 for 2,7-AQDS, 2-AQS, 1-AQS and 1,5-AQDS, respectively (Supplementary Table. 1). The derived diffusion coefficient, being several orders of magnitude larger than values typical of solid-state electrodes (10-12 to 10-8 cm2 s-1), underscores the inherent advantage of liquid-phase energy systems. We further derived kinetic currents from Koutecky-Levich plots (Supplementary Figs. 2–5) and reaction rate constants of quinones from the Butler-Volmer Eq. 2,7-AQDS possesses the highest reaction kinetics with a rate constant (k) of 5.723 × 10-3 cm s-1, by the fast outer-sphere electron-transfer process. The well-delocalized π-electron system within the anthraquinone ring coupled with two sulfonate groups contributes to the rapid electron transfer, with minimal reorganization energy. The fast kinetics of 2,7-AQDS also correlate well with the lowest HOMO-LUMO energy gap derived from DFT calculations (Fig. 1d). The high reaction rate constant as well as the fast diffusion coefficient of 2,7-AQDS lays the foundation for high-power energy storage systems.

The amount of redox-active material dissolved in the electrolyte is directly proportional to the battery’s charge storage capacity, and Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy was employed to evaluate the solubility of the quinone derivatives (Supplementary Figs. 6-9). Among the candidates, 2,7-AQDS exhibits the highest solubility, reaching 1.1 M (equivalent electron concentration: 2.2 M), which is a primary determinant of the energy density in redox flow batteries. The solubility of 1,5-AQDS and 2,7-AQDS, both containing two hydrophilic functional groups, was higher than that of 1-AQS and 2-AQS, containing only one hydrophilic group. Meanwhile, 2,7-AQDS demonstrated greater solubility than 1,5-AQDS due to differences in their lattice structures and Coulombic forces between sodium ions and sulfonate groups. Figure 1e and Supplementary Table. 1 summarize the physicochemical characteristics of quinones, and it is concluded that 2,7-AQDS, maintaining the highest redox potential, solubility, diffusion coefficient, and reaction rate constant, represents a promising electroactive molecule for SCFB applications.

After the rational screening of organic redox species, the SCFB was built with 2,7-AQDS as the positive and Zn as the negative electrode. The open-circuit voltage (OCV) was further tested to validate the self-charging mechanism (Fig. 2a). The battery’s OCV is stabilized at 0.56 V after discharging, and infusion of Ar shows no notable voltage changes. Upon the injection of oxygen, the OCV increased, and the cell voltage subsequently rose to and stabilized at 1.08 V. This demonstrates that oxygen plays a key role in oxidizing 2,7-AQDS2- to 2,7-AQDS. To gain further insight into the redox chemistry of the self-charging process, we performed UV-Vis spectroscopy (Fig. 2b). After the first discharge cycle, the absorption peak at 390 nm, corresponding to the C-O groups of 2,7-AQDS2-, reaches its maximum value. After pumping air to oxidize 2,7-AQDS2-, the absorption peak at 320 nm (C = O groups of 2,7-AQDS) gradually intensifies, while the absorption peak of C-O groups experiences a decrease in intensity, indicating structural changes due to the redox reaction. With continued air exposure, the absorption peak of 2,7-AQDS2- diminishes sharply, while the 2,7-AQDS (C = O) peak increases, reaching the maximum within eight minutes, and 2,7-AQDS2- is fully oxidized at this point. These results confirm that the discharged positive electroactive species can be oxidized by dissolved oxygen in the air, enabling self-charging without an external power supply. It is noted that the absorption spectra of 2,7-AQDS2- after the second cycle align closely with that of the first cycle, highlighting the reversibility of such a redox reaction for long-term use in energy storage applications.

Furthermore, operando fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to reveal how the local operational chemical structure of reduced redox species evolves during the self-charging process. As shown in Fig. 2c, the FTIR spectra of 2,7-AQDS2- reveal a significant enhancement in the absorption intensity within the 1600–1700 cm-1 range, corresponding to the C = O stretching vibration of regenerated 2,7-AQDS during oxidation. Likewise, the absorption peak at 1650 cm-1, as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 10, shows a notable intensity increase, confirming the oxidation process of 2,7-AQDS2- into 2,7-ADQS, as well. After air injection, the stretching vibration of water molecules (υ(OH), 3000 ~ 3800 cm-1) was enhanced. This change may be related to the formation of hydrogen bonds between oxygen molecules with strong electronegativity and water molecules, thus changing the vibrational energy level of water molecules. The signal of O-H deformation vibration of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) at 1400–1450 cm-1 was also recorded. These spectral changes reflect the underlying molecular transformations of the redox reaction and offer crucial insights into the redox chemistry during the self-charging process of the 2,7-AQDS-based SCFB. Especially, the fast outer sphere electron transfer of the enolization reaction, involving the interconversion of C = O and C-O bonds, plays a key role in facilitating the reaction kinetics of the electrochemical reactions. Additionally, similar adsorption changes due to the redox reaction within the 1600–1700 cm-1 range can also be observed in other quinone derivatives, as shown in Fig. 2d–f and Supplementary Figs. 11–13. EPR (electron paramagnetic resonance) measurements were further performed on the solution after self-charging. Figure 2g shows the EPR spectrum with DMPO (5, 5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline n-oxide) as the trapping agent, showing the DMPO-OH signal, where •OH comes from the decomposition of H2O2, which should be the reaction product after oxygen reduction. Based on the in situ and ex-situ characterizations to elucidate the electrochemistry of 2,7-AQDS, Fig. 2h illustrates the mechanism of the redox reaction during the self-charging process. 2,7-AQDS can be reduced to 2,7-AQDS2- to deliver power during discharging, and the reduced state of 2,7-AQDS2- can be oxidized in contact with air to regenerate 2,7-AQDS, enabling repeated discharge cycles. During the self-charging process, molecular oxygen oxidizes reduced 2,7-AQDS2- to 2,7-AQDS, with the energy stored as chemical potential in the chemical bonds of the material. However, the reaction produces hydrogen peroxide as a by-product, which is highly oxidative and detrimental to the battery’s performance. To mitigate this issue, a manganese-based carbon felt was developed to decompose the hydrogen peroxide efficiently, completing the self-charging cycle. Meanwhile, it is expected that the manganese-based carbon felt can not only inhibit side reactions but also promote the electron-transfer process, giving rise to enhanced electrochemical performance.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping demonstrates the uniform distribution of manganese and oxygen elements across the felts, contributing to high active surface area and enhanced catalytic efficiency (Fig. 3a–d). The XRD patterns show that the diffraction peak at 27° corresponds to the carbon substance in the carbon felt, and the other diffraction peaks correspond to the standard spectrum of Mn3O4 (Fig. 3e). We also conducted contact angle measurements on carbon felt before and after Mn3O4 modification (Supplementary Fig. 14), and it was observed that the pristine carbon felt maintains a hydrophobic nature before treatment. By contrast, H2O can penetrate Mn3O4-carbon felt upon contacting the surface, and the substantially enhanced hydrophilicity should be ascribed to the polar bonds between manganese cations and oxygen anions. The boosted wetting behavior, allowing for more active reaction sites and better charge transfer, is crucial for efficient electrochemical reactions within the battery. According to the electrochemical impedance diagram, the charge transfer resistance of the Mn3O4-coated carbon felt-based SCFB can be reduced in comparison with the carbon felt-based counterpart (Fig. 3f), which further confirms the enhancement of reaction kinetics facilitated by the Mn-based catalyst. To elucidate the origin of interactions between 2,7-AQDS and catalysts, the local density of states (LDOS) simulations were conducted, revealing that the d-band center of Mn3O4-carbon felt is positioned at -0.688 eV (Supplementary Fig. 15). Orbital hybridization near the Fermi level was also observed. The calculated adsorption energies of 2,7-AQDS on carbon felt and Mn3O4-carbon felt are 0.3529 and -5.6372 eV, respectively, reflecting a substantial enhancement in interfacial interactions between organic molecules and catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 16). Additionally, the extended electron interaction region in Mn3O4-carbon felt (Supplementary Fig. 16) provides evidence of broadened charge transfer pathways, establishing a clear link between interfacial interactions and accelerated electron transfer. Moreover, Mn3O4-coated carbon felt can give rise to inhibited side reactions, and Fig. 3g illustrates its hydrogen peroxide-based byproduct scavenging capabilities. The colorimetric analysis shows that no hydrogen peroxide was detected in the posolyte of the 2,7-AQDS-based SCFB before self-charging. Nevertheless, the concentration of H2O2 increased to approximately 10 mg L-1 after O2 infusion owing to the two-electron oxygen reduction reaction, correlating well with the redox chemistry elucidated by operando FTIR and EPR test. In comparison, the Mn3O4-carbon felt can completely scavenge the H2O2 byproduct efficiently, with no noticeable H2O2 residues in the posolyte despite the infusion of O2. On the carbon felt, the decomposition pathway of H2O2 to generate O2 requires overcoming a higher energy barrier, resulting in kinetically hindered reaction dynamics. In comparison, the Mn3O4-carbon felt can effectively stabilize the transition state through its catalytically active sites, significantly reducing the activation energy for O-O bond cleavage (Supplementary Fig. 17). Therefore, it is demonstrated that during the H2O2 decomposition process, the Mn3O4-carbon felt effectively stabilizes the transition state, significantly reducing the activation energy required for O-O bond cleavage and ultimately enhances the overall decomposition efficiency of H2O2. This effective depletion of hydrogen peroxide mitigates its adverse effects on the stability of 2,7-AQDS and demonstrates the feasibility of Mn3O4-carbon felt to enhance the durability of SCFB. These results provide a foundation for optimizing cell components and highlight a pathway to improve the long-term performance of flow battery systems.

In the proof-of-concept battery test, UV-Vis spectroscopy was first employed to study electrochemical processes. (Fig. 4a–c). The results indicate that, during charging, the absorption peak of 2,7-AQDS (C = O) increases, while that of 2,7-AQDS2- (C-O) decreases; and the opposite trend is observed during the discharging process. These results align with UV-Vis spectra during the self-charging process (Fig. 2b), confirming the same enolization-based redox reactions taking place involving carbonyl groups. Specifically, during discharging, 2,7-AQDS undergoes a two-electron reduction, resulting in the formation of the enolate ions of 2,7-AQDS2-, which corresponds to a visible color change in the electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 18). This provides compelling evidence for the efficacy of self-charging mechanisms, demonstrating that the pivotal aspect of energy storage resides in the reversible transformation of the 2,7-AQDS and 2,7-AQDS2-. This conversion between C-O and C = O bonds induces a reduction in the conjugation of the molecular structure, lowering the energy required for electron transitions, which leads to a redshift in the absorption peak. The redox chemistry elucidated by UV-Vis spectroscopy reveals that the fast and reversible outer-sphere electron transfer involved in the enolization reaction plays a pivotal role in facilitating the charge transfer reactions.

Figure 4e presents the charge and discharge curves at current densities ranging from 10 mA cm-2 to 120 mA cm-2, and the Coulombic efficiency (CE) remains close to 100% across different current densities. At a current density of 10 mA cm-2, it can achieve 92% of the theoretical capacity with an energy efficiency (EE) of 94%. Compared to 10 mA cm-2, when the current is increased to 20, 40, 80, and 120 mA cm-2, the capacity retention rates are 96, 90, 86, and 81% respectively. Even at a low current density of 10 mA cm-2, the CE remains close to 100%. The energy efficiency can reach 65% even at a current density of 120 mA cm-2. The CE (approaching 100%) underscores the system’s high stability, which effectively suppresses hydrogen evolution reactions (HER). The battery’s rate capability is partly contributed by the intrinsic nature of the 2,7-AQDS with a small HOMO-LUMO energy gap as well as the high diffusion coefficients and reaction kinetic constants. Additionally, the adoption of Mn3O4-catalyst substantially promotes the electron transfer process as well. The decorated Mn3O4 serves as an active site to lower the activation energy of the redox reactions, and the synergistic interaction between Mn and the carbon matrix further enhances the reaction kinetics and ensures the structural stability of the electrode. The long-term performance evaluation was conducted at a current density of 120 mA cm-2, and the battery retains a capacity retention rate of 99.98% after 1600 cycles (Fig. 4d). We provided the capacity and voltage profiles at key intervals (Supplementary Fig. 19), and the noticeable capacity variations should be due to corrosion of the Zn negative electrode and crossover of redox species, in addition to the temperature fluctuations. The corrosion of the Zn negative electrode and passivation effects over prolonged cycling are evidenced by the increasing charge transfer resistance revealed in EIS measurements (Supplementary Fig. 20)41,42. In comparison, the battery without the utilization of manganese oxide-coated carbon exhibited a limited cycle life of only 1000 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 21). Therefore, the as-fabricated catalyst not only accelerates the electrochemical procedures but also inhibits side reactions.

Temperature variations pose another significant challenge to the performance of energy storage systems. Especially at low temperatures below the freezing point, the performance of batteries can be severely impacted, and it is highly demanded to design batteries operating under harsh environments. The solvation environment of an electrolyte is a critical factor in dictating the electrochemical performance in harsh conditions. Herein, urea, a cost-effective and environmentally benign additive, was adopted to prevent the formation of an ordered ice crystal structure and lower the freezing point of electrolytes. The battery can be stably operated at -10 °C with a current density of 20 mA cm-2 for 2500 cycles, while maintaining over 99% CE (Fig. 4f). Figure 4g illustrates the charge-discharge voltage profiles of the battery at 200, 300, 400, and 500 cycles, showing a slight increase in capacity as the battery undergoes activation. This indicates that the manganese oxide-coated carbon felt exhibits efficient electrocatalytic activity and stability even at low temperatures. Additionally, we provided the CE and EE for low-temperature testing (Supplementary Fig. 22). The results demonstrate that the CE remains stable throughout the test, consistently exceeding 99%. In contrast, the EE decreases gradually from ~74 to ~68% over 2500 cycles, which should be associated with the corrosion of the Zn negative electrode and the resulting interfacial resistance changes as well, which is a common phenomenon in the community of Zn-based batteries43,44. When temperature decreases, the electrolyte exhibits increased viscosity and reduced ionic conductivity, which collectively impede charge transfer kinetics and result in slight capacity decrease (Supplementary Table. 2). Increasing urea concentration progressively reduces ionic conductivity due to lifted viscosity of the resulting electrolyte. Concurrently, 2,7-AQDS solubility displays non-monotonic behavior, showing enhanced solubility at moderate urea concentrations (5 M) but diminishing beyond 8 M, likely linked to concentration-dependent solvent-solute interactions (Supplementary Table. 3). To evaluate the impact of urea concentration on battery performance, electrolytes containing urea additive at various concentrations were tested. It is noted that urea has little effect on CE, while its effect on EE is substantially more pronounced due to the modulated ionic conductivity and diffusion of redox species. Therefore, EE decreases from 76.7 to 64.9% when increasing urea concentration from 3 to 8 M. Increasing the concentration of redox species enhances the energy density of the battery, which is crucial for its practical application. The Zn | |2,7-AQDS battery with a higher concentration of 0.5 M was tested, and the capacity of 24.91 Ah L-1 can be achieved. This capacity remains at 78.67% after 600 cycles, corresponding to a degradation rate of 0.0355% per cycle (Fig. 4h). We also systematically analyzed the influence of 2,7-AQDS concentration on efficiency, cycling stability, and cost of the battery. Notably, increased concentration of 2,7-AQDS leads to reduced energy efficiency, probably due to increased viscosity of the electrolyte, while CE remains consistently at almost 100%. Meanwhile, the cost of the energy system exhibits a concentration-dependent relationship, decreasing from 500 to 650 $ (kWh)-1 at 0.1 M 2,7-AQDS to 200–250 $ (kWh)-1 at 0.5 M, mainly due to lowered electrolyte and stack cost per unit of energy (Supplementary Table 4). The Zn | |2,7-AQDS batteries can achieve a capacity of up to 39.7 Ah L-1 with a concentration of 0.8 M and maintain a capacity of 21 Ah L-1 with a concentration of 0.6 M (Supplementary Figs. 23-24). Nevertheless, the batteries based on other quinone derivatives with lower solubility, namely, 1-AQS, 2-AQS, and 1,5-AQDS, present much worse stability (Supplementary Figs. 25-27), which may be due to the precipitation and crossover of electroactive materials during battery cycling (Supplementary Fig. 28). It is expected that further molecular engineering to tailor the characteristics of redox species can give rise to higher energy density.

To pinpoint the intrinsic durability of the 2,7-AQDS-based SCFB, NMR spectra of the electrolyte before and after cycling were collected in Fig. 4i. The absence of new absorption peaks in the aromatic region suggests no noticeable degradation of 2,7-AQDS during cycling. Therefore, longer cycling of 2,7-AQDS-based SCFB is confined by the deterioration of zinc, exacerbated by side reactions including zinc dissolution and dendrite growth (Supplementary Figs. 29–31). The replacement of Zn with other negative alternatives is promising to alleviate such concerns, which will be discussed in the next section.

After the rational screening of the redox species and tuning of the solvation environments, the air-enabled self-charging properties of 2,7-AQDS-based SCFB were studied. The discharge voltage profiles under different oxidation time intervals are presented in Fig. 5a, and a high charging rate is achieved, reaching 94% of capacity within 8 min. The high self-charging rate is attributed to the rapid outer-sphere electron transfer of organic redox species in liquid electrolytes, as well as the efficient and uniform interaction of oxygen with active substances. In addition to the fast reaction kinetics corroborated by DFT modeling and RDE testing, the Mn3O4 decorated carbon felt also enables fast electron transfer in the hydroxyl outer sphere, laying the foundation for efficient self-charging. SCFB with such a fast self-charging rate shows great potential when instant delivering or absorbing power is required. In comparison, it typically takes a couple of hours to complete self-charging for conventional energy systems involving more sluggish charging processes (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table. 5) in reported work. In addition to rapid charging capability, our assembled SCFB demonstrates the ability to undergo 20 self-charging and discharge cycles through oxygen oxidation (Fig. 5c). After multiple self-charge cycles, the electrolyte pH increased from an initial value of 1.10–2.15. 1H NMR spectra of the electrolyte collected before and after the self-charging process show no new emerging absorption peaks, confirming that 2,7-AQDS undergoes no degradation during operation (Supplementary Fig. 32). This stability originates from the effectively suppressed side reactions enabled by the Mn3O4 catalyst. To demonstrate the versatility of the SCFB design, we switched from Zn to Mg or Al, thereby broadening the system’s operational capabilities and highlighting the robustness of this approach. Figure 5d illustrates the voltage profiles of 20 self-charging and discharging cycles conducted with Al serving as the negative electrode. Notably, the battery maintains a stable discharge plateau at a cell voltage of 0.7 V throughout cycling with negligible capacity fading. Despite minor fluctuations in the discharge profiles inherent to the open-system configuration, these observations collectively demonstrate the stability of the system. Similarly, magnesium can also be used as the negative electrode to achieve 20 self-charging and discharging cycles (Supplementary Fig. 33). The successful implementation of SCFB utilizing three distinct negative electrodes underscores the efficacy of our strategy. The self-charging capability of the battery can be achieved simply by incorporating an air pump, which reduces both additional manufacturing costs and operational complexity compared to self-charging batteries based on solid-state batteries. Consequently, we possess the capability to tailor and adapt the negative composition to suit a variety of operational scenarios. The availability of a broader spectrum of negative alternatives significantly enhances the prospects for developing SCFBs that exhibit decent performance while maintaining cost-effectiveness. Supplementary Table. 6 presents systematic performance comparisons in terms of cell performance and capital cost. Notably, the as-fabricated SCFB in this work exhibits comparable energy, power density and cost. Meanwhile, SCFB can deliver higher power density in comparison with conventional solid-state self-charging systems thanks to the fast reaction kinetics in liquid phases. While the reduced organic molecule in the positive electrode achieves self-charging under oxygen, the negative electrode can achieve reversible electrochemical reduction via charging of batteries or electroplating in the electrolyte.

As a proof-of-concept demonstration, a battery stack composed of three 2,7-AQDS-based SCFBs connected in series was assembled, which can charge a mobile phone, demonstrating their practical viability (Supplementary Figs. 34–35). During the discharge process, the posolyte underwent a distinct color change from bright yellow to black, corresponding to the reduction of 2,7-AQDS to 2,7-AQDS2-. This visible transformation, consistent with Supplementary Fig.18, served as a clear indicator of the electrochemical reaction taking place. Once the air pump was activated to initiate the self-charging process, the electrolyte reverted to its original yellow color, reflecting the oxidation of 2,7-AQDS2- back to 2,7-AQDS. This reversible color transition highlights the robust and repeatable redox activity of 2,7-AQDS, enabling continuous charge-discharge cycling. The seamless repetition of this process underscores the efficiency of the self-charging mechanism, with the air pump introducing oxygen, which drives the oxidation reaction. Notably, the system not only supports mobile phone charging but also demonstrates reliable power supply capability for electric fans, confirming its multi-scenario practicality (Supplementary Fig. 36). This proof-of-concept demonstration not only showcases the potential of SCFB for practical applications but also its capability to achieve sustainable and long-term energy storage through a highly efficient and reversible electrochemical process.

Discussion

In this study, a SCFB is developed to address the challenge of sluggish solid-state reaction kinetics in existing self-charging systems. A series of organic redox species were screened, and the highly water-soluble 2,7-AQDS was selected to achieve an effective capacity balance with the zinc negative electrode. The redox chemistry of quinone-based SCFBs was investigated through in-situ and ex-situ characterizations along with computational modeling. The results indicate that the delocalized π-electron system in 2,7-AQDS facilitates fast outer-sphere electron transfer with low reorganization energy. By incorporating a manganese oxide-based catalyst, parasitic reactions were suppressed, and reaction kinetics were improved, allowing the battery to maintain nearly 99.98% of its capacity over 1600 cycles. Even at -10 °C the battery can run more than 2500 cycles at a current density of 20 mA cm-2. The system attains 94% of its theoretical capacity within 8 min of self-charging, performing favorably compared to other self-charging systems based on solid-state reactions. The versatility of the system is also demonstrated through the substitution of Zn with Mg and Al as negative electrodes. The as-fabricated SCFB, utilizing bio-derived organic redox species, earth-abundant metals, and atmospheric oxygen is promising for applications in scenarios devoid of external power sources.

Methods

Chemicals

For this study, sodium anthraquinone-2-sulfonate (97%), anthraquinone-2,7-disulfonic acid disodium salt (97%), 1,5-anthraquinone disulfonic acid disodium salt (98%), 1-anthraquinone sulfonic acid sodium salt (98%), 3,7-bis(dimethylamine) clofenazolium (98%) and 1,4-Dihydroxy-2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (99%) were purchased from Meryer(Shanghai)Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. 1-amino-pyridine iodide (98%), 8-hydroxypyridine-1,3,6-trisodium trisulfonic acid (85%), Zinc trifluoromethanesul-fonate (99%), Ammonium chloride (99.8%), and D2O (99.9% at.%D) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Zinc foil (99.99% purity) and aluminum foil (99.99% purity)was purchased from Suzhou Sinero Technology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). Magnesium foil (99.95% purity) was purchased from Kunshan Xuanzuan Electronics Technology Co., Ltd.

Instrument

This work measured the 1H NMR spectra on a Bruker AVANCE III 400 MHz. The EPR spectra were collected using Bruker EMX plus instrument. Ultraviolet-visible spectra were collected on a VU-8000 from METASH. Electrochemical measurements involving CV and linear sweep voltammetry were recorded utilizing CHI 760E. The electrochemical performance of batteries was examined using a Neware battery test system (CT-4008Tn-5V6A-S1, China). Operando FTIR was measured on a BRUKER VERTEX 70 V. Then, the after contact angle test was done using CA100C form INNUO.

CV test was conducted in N2 and O2 atmosphere using a three-electrode system. The electrolyte consisted of 10 mM of the active substance and 0.1M ammonium chloride, which was dissolved in 0.1 M sulfuric acid. The glassy carbon electrode was the working electrode, the platinum mesh electrode was the counter electrode and the silver/silver chloride electrode was the reference electrode, the distance between each electrode was approximately 1 cm. Ar atmosphere electrolyte: The electrolyte in the beaker was continuously purged with high-purity nitrogen for 10 min to eliminate the dissolved oxygen in the electrolyte, and then the beaker was sealed to make it completely airtight. O2 atmosphere electrolyte: To keep the dissolved oxygen concentration in the electrolyte constant, the electrolyte was purged with oxygen throughout the test. All CV measurements were conducted at a scan rate of 10 mV/s. The applied potential windows for each compound versus Ag/AgCl were as follows: 3,7-Bis(dimethylamine)phenoxazinium: 0 to 0.5 V; 4-OH TEMPO: 0.2 to 1 V; 1-Aminopyridine iodide: -0.1 to 1 V; 8-Hydroxypyrene-1,3,6-trisulfonic acid trisodium salt: 0.3 to 1 V; 1,5-AQDS: -0.3 to 0.3 V; 1-AQS: -0.2 to 0.2 V; 2-AQS: -0.25 to 0.2 V; 2,7-AQDS: -0.2 to 0.2 V.

Operando FTIR measurements

This study utilized a BRUKER VERTEX 70 V spectrometer and BRUKER cell to conduct the required spectroscopic test. The positive active material is mounted with the cell near the test window. During the test, infrared light is reflected off the surface of the positive active material and penetrates the sample at an appropriate depth to obtain an infrared spectrum. An X-ray spectrometer was utilized. The positive active substance after discharge is used as a sample solution, and air is gradually injected during the test. The relationship between transmittance difference before and after scanning and air injection time was recorded. For the operando infrared measurements, a flow cell configuration was employed. The electrolyte consisted of 0.1 M electroactive species, 1 M NH4Cl and 0.05 M H2SO4. Carbon felt was used as the electrode material alongside a Nafion 212 membrane as the separator.

Electrodeposition

Electrodeposition Experiments were conducted using a ChenHua electrochemical workstation (CHI 760E) with multi-current steps, with the parameters set as follows: deposition current of 7 mA cm-2, deposition time of 1200 s, and deposition temperature of 95 °C. The electrodeposition solution comprised 0.3 M anhydrous manganese sulfate and 0.36 M sulfuric acid. The deposition process utilized a three-electrode system, consisting of working, reference, and counter electrodes.

SEM

SEM experiments were carried out using a ZEISS Sigma 500 and photographed electro-deposited carbon felts, showing the state of deposition of manganese oxides in them. EDS mapping images of the carbon felt electrodes showed the distribution of the elements C, O, and Mn.

NMR

Ex situ 1H NMR spectra (400 MHz, D2O) of 2,7-AQDS before and after battery testing at the oxidized state were recorded to analyze the structural changes.

Battery performance

Full-cell tests were carried out using an CT-4008Tn-5V6A-S1 (Neware Technology Co., China). The flow battery (Model YTH-1, obtained from Wuhan Zhisheng New Energy) was assembled with the positve and negative electrodes separated by a Nafion 212 membrane. The positive electrode employed Mn3O4-carbon felt, while the negative electrode utilized carbon felt in combination with a metal cube. The carbon felt was in direct contact with Nafion 212. Zinc, magnesium, and aluminum metals were cut into 1 × 1 cm squares. Their thickness is approximately 2 mm. Prior to cell assembly, the surface of the metal electrode was mechanically polished in an ethanol medium using 1000-grit sandpaper to thoroughly remove the native oxide layer and any surface contaminants, thereby ensuring a clean and electrochemically active interface. Metal electrodes were prepared prior to battery assembly. The electrolyte was pumped through a peristaltic pump. For the cycling tests of 2,7-AQDS, the posolyte was formulated with 0.1-0.5 M 2,7-AQDS, 1 M NH4Cl, and 0.05 M H2SO4. The negolyte consisted of 1 M Zn(OTf)2, 1 M NH4Cl and 0.05 M H2SO4. For the long-term cycling test of 2,7-AQDS at -10 °C, 5 M urea was further added. All cells were fabricated with negative electrodes containing an identical concentration of zinc salt. During cycling tests, the low-concentration cells exhibited a negative-to-positive capacity ratio of 10:1, whereas the high-concentration cells demonstrated the ratio of 2:1. The electrolyte was subjected to mild ultrasonication at room temperature to ensure complete dissolution. The prepared electrolyte was stored in a sealed glass bottle equipped with an airtight polytetrafluoroethylene cap liner. All solution transfers were conducted using polypropylene pipette tips. The time interval between electrolyte preparation and cell assembly was maintained within 10 min. In this study, carbon felt (5 mm thickness, 20% compression ratio) was purchased from Wuhan Zhisheng New Energy Co., Ltd. The material was pretreated in a muffle furnace at 450 °C for 4 h. The Mn3O4-carbon felt composite was first pre-treated in a muffle furnace at 450 °C, followed by electrodeposition. Subsequently, the sample was thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and dried. A peristaltic pump (adjustable-flow type, flow rate: 20 mL/min) and a diaphragm air pump (flow rate: 1 L/min) were employed. Ambient air was introduced into the electrolyte via the air pump to drive the self-charging process. The electrochemical tests were conducted under galvanostatic conditions. The cells were assembled without the requirement for a specific gaseous atmosphere. Electrochemical measurements of the low-temperature cells were conducted at -10 °C within a climate-controlled chamber. Except for these low-temperature tests, all other cells were tested at an average ambient temperature of 25 °C ± 2 °C. For all ex situ measurements, electrolyte was collected from the circulation pump and transported to characterization instruments using an argon-atmosphere glove box as a standard procedure. For high-concentration posolyte, the measurement was conducted using the static method. DFT theoretical calculation

All quantum chemical calculations, including geometry optimizations and total energy evaluations, were conducted using DFT through the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)45. Electron-ion interactions were modeled with the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method46, and the exchange-correlation energy was accounted for using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional⁴⁷ within the spin-polarized generalized gradient approximation (GGA)47.

Calculations was performed for CF and CF with Mn3O4. The graphene structure was used to simulate the CF, and the Mn3O4 unit cell was applied to the graphene surface to simulate the effect of Mn3O4 doping on CF. The structure of graphene, AQDS and Mn3O4 was generated from Materials Project (MP) database48.The cutoff plane-wave kinetic energy of 520 eV was used in calculations. The conjugate-gradient method was used in electronic relaxation, with the total energy convergence criterion is 10-2 eV. Gamma-point-centered k-meshes were used for all calculations, and the k-point grid size was 1 × 1 × 1.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurements

The perturbation mode was potentiostatic, with a sinusoidal amplitude of 5 mV. The frequency range spanned from 0.01 to 100000 Hz. Data were collected at 12 points per decade of frequency. All impedance measurements were performed under a steady-state DC potential of 0 V (vs. open circuit voltage). Prior to each measurement, the system was stabilized under open-circuit conditions for 10 s.
