Nutritional Applications of Green Magnesium Nanoparticles as Functional Feed Additives in Broiler Diets: Towards Sustainable Poultry Production
Authors: Fayiz M. Reda, Ayman S. Salah, Ayman E. Taha, Frank Idan, Zhongxin Zhou, Samar S. Bassiony, Laila A. Mohamed, Mahmoud Alagawany
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNanoparticles, including green magnesium nanoparticles, facilitate passage through cellular membranes in animals and interact efficiently with biological systems, improving their functional activity. Consequently, the utilization of green magnesium nanoparticles is recommended to enhance the availability of magnesium, thus boosting its metabolism and uptake.ObjectivesThe current research aimed to examine the impacts of biological magnesium nanoparticles (BNMg) as a feed additive on growth efficiency, haematological parameters, biochemical parameters, antioxidant efficacy, immune system activities, blood minerals, digestive enzymes, carcass traits and caecal microbiota in broiler chickens.MethodsA total of 250 unsexed Ross 1‐day‐old broiler chicks were randomly allocated into five groups, each containing 50 birds. Five replicates of each group were established, with each replicate comprising 10 birds. The control group was fed a diet devoid of BNMg, but the rest of the groups were provided diets enriched with BNMg at levels of 150, 300, 450 and 600 mg/kg, respectively.ResultsThe outcomes demonstrated a substantial improvement in all performance parameters. BNMg administration significantly elevated carcass and dressing, and decreased abdominal fat percentage compared to control. BNMg supplementation markedly decreased glucose levels and elevated haemoglobin and red and white blood cells. BNMg administration elevated blood total protein and globulin, while concurrently decreasing aspartate aminotransferase and alanine. The inclusion of BNMg in broiler diets increased blood magnesium levels and noticeably enhanced lipid profile, immunity markers, antioxidant activities and digestive enzymes. Moreover, BNMg supplementation increased the concentration of copper, potassium and zinc in the blood. BNMg markedly elevated the population of lactic acid bacteria while dramatically reducing the overall counts of total bacteria, coliforms, Escherichia coli, Salmonella, total yeast and mould counts.ConclusionBNMg supplementation enhances growth performance, haematological parameters, blood chemistry, antioxidant efficacy, immune system status, blood minerals, digestive enzymes and caecal microbiota without any adverse effects on broiler health.

Introduction

The fields of livestock and veterinary sciences are currently witnessing the development of nanobiotechnology for several beneficial applications, including nutritional, therapeutic and diagnostic (AL‐Ruwad et al. 2024). Nanoparticles (NPs) of important minerals, sized between 1 and 100 nm, may provide a substitute for traditional elemental forms in animal nutrition (Kociova et al. 2020). The administration of feed additives in poultry production improves growth performance (Alagawany et al. 2024; Gomaa et al. 2025), immunity (Alagawany et al. 2025; Salah et al. 2025b) and antioxidant activity (Salah et al. 2025a). Conventional applications of feed additives in poultry nutrition are largely in elemental form, while recent advances emphasize NP‐based delivery systems. Research has indicated that significantly reduced quantities of NPs may be sufficient to meet the elemental requirements of animals compared to bulk mineral sources (Amr et al. 2025), thereby mitigating the environmental effects associated with high concentrations of inorganic minerals (Ouyang et al. 2021). Furthermore, nanoforms of minerals may enhance bioavailability in the gastrointestinal tracts of animals owing to their diminutive size, greater resemblance, large surface area and elevated physical activity (Hidayat et al. 2021). The characteristics of NPs, including reduced dosage, diminished toxicity, enhanced absorption and accelerated tissue dispersion rate, can be advantageous for avian species. NPs can assist birds in various ways, including administering smaller doses, reducing toxicity, enhancing absorption and facilitating rapid distribution across tissues. Numerous studies on animal nutrition have revealed that NPs possess significant potential, particularly in minimal quantities. This research demonstrates that NPs can enhance growth, feed efficiency and general health of poultry. Micro‐ and macro‐mineral NPs consistently enhance body weight (BW), average daily gain and feed conversion efficiency (Reda et al. 2023). Nutritional additives are utilized to fulfil an animal's dietary requirements, enhance productivity, bolster the immune system and microbial equilibrium, and mitigate the risk of illness. NPs are recognized for their several advantages, including antiviral, antifungal, antibacterial, antiprotozoal and antioxidant properties (Ahmad et al. 2022).

Alternatives to antibiotics that promote growth and health include silver, copper, selenium, zinc and cobalt nanoparticles (CoNPs) (El‐Maddawy et al. 2022). In animal nutrition, nanotechnology is applied for disease detection and treatment, as well as for supplementing vitamins, minerals and medications (Fesseha et al. 2020). Examples of nanotechnology applications in animal feed include nanominerals, nanoenzymes and various additives (Marappan Gopi et al. 2017). The application of nanotechnology in essential minerals such as copper, zinc, selenium, silver, gold and chromium has garnered significant scrutiny and interest. This technology has a broad application in the development of vaccines, immunological enhancers, diagnostic tools, antivirals, antibacterials, antiparasitics, disinfectants, antifungal agents and antimycotoxin agents (Anwar et al. 2019).

In the food industry and poultry product packaging, nanobiosensors are utilized for food manufacturing and conservation to evaluate food safety, thereby enhancing quality control (Sadr et al. 2023). Investigative findings show that the utilization of nanotechnology in nutrition for essential minerals such as silver, iron, zinc, manganese, selenium, magnesium (Mg) and copper is more advantageous and dependable, addressing problems (Ahmad et al. 2022; Hassan et al. 2020). The potential of nanotechnology to transform several facets of nutrition, encompassing production, processing, storage, transportation, security and food safety, has been acknowledged for an extended period (FAO 2022). Research suggests that intravenous delivery of NP medicines into the bloodstream can achieve up to 100% bioavailability for some compounds that exhibit considerable systemic elimination.

For instance, nano‐sized minerals rapidly reach tissue sites such as the liver and spleen via the bloodstream and exhibit enhanced diffusion (Geraets et al. 2014). Nonetheless, nanotechnology enables the delivery of nutrients at substantially reduced quantities, which in turn mitigates pollutant release and enhances environmental sustainability. Notwithstanding, this technology enhances production efficiency, avian health and food safety within the poultry sector (Hassan et al. 2020). Mg NPs are gaining popularity in poultry nutrition due to their superior bioavailability and absorption efficiency compared to conventional Mg sources, allowing for lower dietary inclusion levels while supporting metabolic functions and overall productive performance (Patra and Lalhriatpuii 2019). However, the information on incorporating biological Mg NPs into poultry diets and their impact on performance and health is limited. One novel approach to designing and manufacturing nanomaterials is to apply well‐established principles of green chemistry, thereby circumventing the reliance on procedures that pose dangers to human well‐being and the ecosystem. Consequently, emphasizing green nanotechnology across the life cycle can enhance users' preparedness to mitigate novel dangers arising from the unforeseen implications of nanotechnology (Iavicoli et al. 2017). Therefore, it was hypothesized that the addition of dietary biological Mg nanoparticles (BNMg) would have a positive impact on broiler growth performance, carcass traits and immune response. This current trial aimed to measure the antimicrobial properties of BNMg and their impact on the growth performance, carcass features, blood chemistry, renal and hepatic functions, immune response, antioxidant status, lipid profile, intestinal microbial community and digestive enzymes of broiler chickens.

Materials and Methods

Animals, Design and Diets

A total of 250 unsexed Ross 1‐day‐old broiler chicks were randomly allocated into 5 treatment groups in a completely randomized design, with 50 birds in each group. Each treatment group was divided into five replicates, each consisting of 10 chicks housed in separate pens of 100 × 120 cm. The treatments consisted of a basal control diet (Table 1) containing 0 mg/kg BNMg and four experimental diets supplemented with BNMg at 150, 300, 450 and 600 mg/kg to evaluate dose–response effects on the experimental birds. Birds were provided ad libitum access to feed and water, and maintained under standard temperature, ventilation and lighting conditions. BNMg was supplied and analysed by the Department of Agricultural Microbiology at the Faculty of Agriculture, Zagazig University, Zagazig, Egypt.

Growth Performance and Carcass Traits

Live body weight (LBW) was recorded at 1, 3 and 6 weeks of age. Bird growth performance metrics were monitored at three intervals (1–3 weeks, 1–6 weeks and 3–6 weeks of age). At 42 days of age, 25 birds were randomly selected to assess carcass traits. Each bird was weighed and then humanely slaughtered. The weights of the heart, liver and gizzard were calculated and expressed as a percentage of the LBW before slaughter.

Blood Metabolites

To estimate the impact of BNMg administration on haematological parameters and blood chemistry, five birds were randomly selected from each group for blood sampling. The birds were humanely euthanized following standard industry practices and thereafter underwent cervical dislocation. Blood samples were collected from the euthanized birds to assess biochemical markers. Blood specimens for laboratory analysis were collected in EDTA tubes (Vacutainer tubes, EDTA‐Italian Kima, Lab Supply Categories, Lab Supply Company, Egypt). The haemoglobin (Hb) concentration, red blood cells (RBCs) and white blood cells (WBCs) counts in the whole blood were evaluated following Dein (1984) within one hour post‐collection. In addition, blood glucose levels were measured in freshly collected samples using a home glucose monitor (Accu‐Chek Active, Roche Diagnostics, Germany) with disposable test strips.

The RBC (106/µL) and WBC (103/µL) counts were determined utilizing a Neubauer Improved Haemocytometer (Marienfeld Superior, Germany) following suitable dilution with Natt–Herricks solution (Irizaary‐Rovira 2004). The Hb concentration was measured using the acid haematin (Sahli's) process as outlined by Dacie and Lewis (1991). Blood haematology was conducted with a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV‐1800, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan) at a wavelength of 540 nm, utilizing a commercial acid haematin reagent (Catalogue No. H2500, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA). The residual blood samples underwent centrifugation for 10 min at 5000 rpm, after which the blood plasma was isolated and preserved at −20°C for subsequent biochemical examination. Commercial kits were employed according to the manufacturer's instructions (Labtest Diagnóstica S.A, Lagoa Santa, Brazil) to measure total protein (TP) (g/dL), albumin (ALB) (g/dL), creatinine (mg/dL), urea (mg/dL), uric acid (mg/dL), triglycerides (TG) (mg/dL), total cholesterol (TC) (mg/dL) and high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol (mg/dL). The activities of liver enzymes AST (IU/L) and ALT (IU/L) were assessed using an automated chemistry analyser (Mindray BS‐240, Shenzhen Mindray Bio‐Medical Electronics Co., China).

Plasma globulin was calculated by subtracting plasma ALB from total plasma protein. TG were divided by five to calculate the plasma very‐low‐density lipoprotein (VLDL) cholesterol (mg/dL). Low‐density lipoprotein (LDL, mg/dL) cholesterol was calculated by subtracting HDL and VLDL from TC (Friedewald et al. 1972). Plasma levels of critical oxidative stress and antioxidant activities were quantified spectrophotometrically (Shimadzu UV‐1800 spectrophotometer) utilizing specific commercial kits (Abcam, UK) for glutathione (GSH), superoxide dismutase (SOD), total antioxidant capacity (TAC) and malondialdehyde (MDA). The activity of catalase (CAT) was evaluated using an ultraviolet‐based technique as outlined by Mueller et al. (1997). Plasma immunity indices were quantified in plasma utilizing a sandwich ELISA kit (My BioSource Company, San Diego, USA), following the methodology of Bianchi et al. (1995) for IgG (mg/dL), IgM (mg/dL) and IgA (mg/dL). However, blood concentrations of Complement 3 (C3) and lysozyme activity (mg/dL) were determined according to the manufacturers’ instructions using a microplate reader (BioTek ELx800, Winooski, VT, USA).

Minerals

Blood plasma mineral concentrations were measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Biochrom Libra S22, Biochrom Ltd., UK) with specific kits from Spectrum Diagnostics (Egypt) for Mg (mg/dL), Ca (mg/dL), Cu (mg/dL), K (mg/dL) and Zn (mg/dL).

Digestive Enzymes

Digesta samples from the small intestine were obtained from each euthanized chicken by compressing the intestine. Every sample was weighed, diluted (1:10) with ice‐cold PBS (pH 7.2, Sigma‐Aldrich, Catalogue No. P2272), homogenized by an IKA T18 Ultra‐Turrax homogenizer (IKA Works, Germany) and centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 20 min at 4°C using an Eppendorf 5810R centrifuge (Eppendorf AG, Germany). The supernatant was preserved at 4°C for subsequent analysis utilizing a sandwich ELISA kit (My BioSource Company, San Diego, USA) for amylase, lipase, protease and trypsin with a microplate reader (BioTek ELx800, USA) (Somogyi 1960; Tietz and Fiereck 1966; Lynn and Clevette‐Radford 1984).

Caecal Microbiome

For the caecal microbiome analysis, five birds per treatment were sacrificed, and their caecal contents (1 g) were transferred to 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks. The flasks also contained 90 mL of sterile peptone (0.1%) and a saline solution (0.85% NaCl). To ensure the contents in the flasks were evenly distributed, they were thoroughly mixed. Microbiological testing was carried out using BD‐specific selective agar media Difco (USA) to ascertain the quantities of Escherichia coli (MacConkey Agar; Catalogue No. 212123), Salmonella (XLD Agar; Catalogue No. 223410), lactic acid bacteria (MRS Agar; Catalogue No. 288210), total bacterial count (TBC; Plate Count Agar; Catalogue No. 247940) and total yeast and moulds (TYMC; Sabouraud Dextrose Agar; Catalogue No. 210930). At the recommended temperatures for each species, the plates were placed in an incubator manufactured by Memmert GmbH of Germany, specifically the Memmert IN30. The procedures were based on those of Xia et al. (2004).

Statistical Analysis

Using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test, the Gaussian normal distribution was examined before data analysis. All data for growth metrics, carcass traits, blood parameters and digestive enzymes, minerals and bacteriology were subjected to a one‐way ANOVA as a completely randomized design using the GLM procedure of SAS (SAS Institute 2001). The model used for the analysis is presented below: Y ij = μ + T i + e ij, where Y ij = observation, μ = overall mean, T i = BNMg effect and e ij = random error term.

Results

Growth Performance

The effects of BNMg on the growth performance of broiler chickens are presented in Table 2. From the results obtained, dietary BNMg supplementation in broiler diets significantly improved BW at 3 (p = 0.0008) and 6 (p < 0.0001) weeks of age. Birds fed 600 mg/kg recorded the highest BW (891.58 g) at 3 weeks of age, while those on 450 mg/kg recorded the highest (2204.45 g) at Week 6. However, no differences (p > 0.05) in BW were observed in birds receiving 300–600 mg/kg at Week 3. Final BW peaked at 450 mg/kg supplementation and dropped slightly, signifying dose‐dependent effects of BNMg. Moreover, BNMg dietary supplementation significantly (p < 0.0001) increased body weight gain (BWG) in all experimental periods (weeks: 1–3, 3–6 and 16), with birds fed 450 mg/kg recording the highest (58.35 g) overall gain at Weeks 1–6. Furthermore, there were no substantial variations (p = 0.1307) in feed consumption during the experimental period (1–6 weeks) with BNMg supplementation. This indicates that BNMg supplementation did not have any influence on feed intake, even though the birds on the 0 mg/kg supplemented diet tended to have the highest intake throughout the experimental period. Moreover, feed conversion ratio was significantly improved with BNMg supplementation at Weeks 3 (p = 0.0046) and 6 (p = 0.0014), as presented in Table 2. Results indicate better FCR at 450 mg/kg (1.77 and 1.70) compared to the 0 and 150 mg/kg groups at Weeks 3 and 6, respectively.

Carcass Traits

Carcass characteristics of broilers supplemented with BNMg showed significant differences, especially for carcass (p = 0.0008), dressing (p = 0.0003) and abdominal fat (p = 0.0007) percentages. The highest carcass (81.61%) and dressing percentage (87.77%) were recorded in birds fed 600 mg/kg. Moreover, the control group exhibited the highest abdominal fat percentage (0.95 %) compared to the BNMg‐supplemented groups, suggesting that BNMg supplementation reduces abdominal fat deposition. Besides, giblet percentage increased significantly (p = 0.0109) with BNMg supplementation, as birds fed 0 and 150 mg/kg exhibited lower values compared with those receiving higher inclusion levels. However, BNMg supplementation had no significant effects on the relative weights of liver (p = 0.6557), gizzard (p = 0.0635) and heart (p = 0.3858), as shown in Table 3.

Haematological Parameters

The results of the haematological parameters demonstrated that BNMg supplementation significantly affected glucose concentration (p = 0.0007), Hb levels (p = 0.0275), RBC counts (p = 0.0063) and WBC counts (p = 0.0002), as presented in Table 4. The results showed a significant reduction in glucose concentration with increasing levels of BNMg supplementation, with the lowest level (255.66 mg/dL) recorded in the 450 mg/kg group. Moreover, a dose‐dependent effect of BNMg supplementation was observed in Hb levels and RBC counts, with birds on 600 mg/kg recording the highest (11.45 g/dL and 3.88 × 106/mm3, respectively). In addition, birds fed 450 mg/kg BNMg had an increased WBC count (26.64 × 103/mm3) compared with the control group.

Table 5 shows that dietary BNMg supplementation significantly influenced several liver and kidney function indices in broiler chickens. From the results, TP increased significantly (p = 0.0044) with increasing levels of BNMg, with birds fed 600 mg/kg recording the highest value (4.30 g/dL), which was comparable to 450 mg/kg but higher than the 0 mg/kg group (2.97 g/dL). In contrast, ALB levels were not significantly influenced (p = 0.2957) by BNMg supplementation. However, the albumin‐to‐globulin (A/G) ratio decreased significantly (p = 0.0109) at higher inclusion levels (450 and 600 mg/kg) compared to the control and 150 mg/kg groups.

Lipid Profile

The effect of increasing levels of dietary BNMg supplementation on the lipid profile of broiler chickens is presented in Table 6. From the results derived, dietary BNMg significantly affected (p ≤ 0.0051) all measured lipid parameters, including TC, TG, LDL, HDL, VLDL and LDL/HDL ratio. TC concentration significantly decreased (p = 0.0004) in response to the supplementation of BNMg. Birds fed 600 mg/kg (130.42 mg/dL) had the lowest TC value, while the highest value was recorded in the control diet (179.84 mg/dL). Comparably, TG levels declined progressively with increasing BNMg levels (p = 0.0002). The birds in the 0 mg/kg group had the highest TG concentration (86.62 mg/dL), whereas those receiving 450 and 600 mg/kg had the lowest levels (53.48 and 49.60 mg/dL, respectively). A similar trend was observed for LDL (p < 0.0001), VLDL (p = 0.0002) and LDL/HDL (p ≤ 0.0001). The VLDL and LDL/HDL levels followed a distinct pattern, with birds on the control diet having the highest values (17.32 mg/dL and 2.01, respectively) and gradually dropping to (9.92 mg/dL and 0.91) in the 600 mg/kg group. Even though LDL levels decreased with supplementation of BNMg, no clear pattern was observed. Nevertheless, birds fed the control diet had the highest value (108.39 mg/dL), with those on 600 mg/kg having the lowest (48.89 mg/dL). On the contrary, HDL concentrations increased significantly with BNMg supplementation (p = 0.0051). The lowest HDL level was observed in the control group (54.07 mg/dL), while the highest values were recorded at 300 and 600 mg/kg (69.15 and 71.61 mg/dL, respectively).

Immunological Status

Dietary BNMg supplementation in broiler diets significantly enhanced the immunological status and weight of immune organs, as shown in Table 7. The results showed a significant elevation in the percentages of immune organs, including the spleen (p = 0.0001), thymus (p < 0.0001) and bursa Fabricius (p < 0.0001). Dietary supplementation of BNMg significantly increased the relative weights of the spleen, thymus and bursa of Fabricius in a dose‐dependent manner up to 450 mg/kg diet, reaching peak values of 0.31%, 0.44% and 0.30%, respectively. However, a decline in these parameters, except the thymus, was observed at the 600 mg/kg inclusion level. Furthermore, IgY, IgM and IgA plasma levels increased significantly (p = 0.0003, p = 0.0001, p = 0.0077, respectively) in response to BNMg supplementation compared to the control diet. Results showed that plasma IgY and IgA concentrations peaked at 450 mg/kg BNMg supplementation, reaching 1.15 and 1.13 mg/dL, respectively. In contrast, the inclusion level of 600 mg/kg resulted in the highest IgM concentration (0.97 mg/dL). In addition, C3 and lysozyme levels were substantially increased with BNMg supplementation (p = 0.0009, p < 0.0001, respectively). Discussion

Mg is a crucial macro‐mineral in poultry nutrition, acting as an essential cofactor for over 300 enzymes and playing a vital role in skeletal growth, energy metabolism and eggshell formation (Amein et al. 2025). Recently, nano‐magnesium (Nano‐Mg) has surfaced as an excellent substitute for conventional Mg sources, such as mass MgO and MgSO4. The principal benefit of Nano‐Mg is its extremely small particle size (1–100 nm), which significantly amplifies its surface area and improves bioavailability, facilitating reduced inclusion rates while augmenting biological impacts. Our findings demonstrated a notable enhancement in BW, weight gain and feed conversion ratio with BNMg supplementation, particularly in the group that received 450 mg/kg supplementation. This aligns with the results of Shastak and Rodehutscord (2015), who reported improvements in BW, weight gain and feed conversion ratio with Mg administration in broiler diets. Furthermore, results of the present study agree with those of Reda et al. (2025), who demonstrated a significant increase in BW, BWG and an improvement in feed conversion ratio in broilers supplemented with biological nano‐cobalt (BNCo). Furthermore, these results are consistent with those reported by Reda et al. (2023), who indicated that the optimal dosage of biological nano‐selenium (BNSe) increased BW and weight gain metrics by 0.4 g/kg of feed. In addition, those authors observed improvement in feed conversion with the incorporation of BNSe in quail feed. Furthermore, our discoveries reflect those of V. Mohammadi et al. (2015), who noticed enhanced growth performance in hens administered nano‐zinc‐methionine at a dosage of 80 mg/kg of feed. Nanomaterial forms of calcium and phosphorus can enhance feed conversion ratio, augment BW, and concurrently decrease the excretion of Ca and P into the external atmosphere (Samanta et al. 2019). The consumption of minerals in nanoparticulate form can enhance production and decrease contaminants, while generating nutritious animal products, such as poultry meat and eggs (Gupta et al. 2024). Nano‐silver treatments at 4 mg/kg increased BWG and optimal feed conversion ratio in broilers (Elkloub et al. 2015). Andi et al. (2011) demonstrated that silver, functioning as an antibacterial agent against harmful gut microbes, may improve gut health, leading to increased nutrient absorption, as evidenced by elevated BW, feed intake and feed conversion efficiency in broiler chickens receiving feed containing silver‐nanoparticles (Ag‐NP). The observed improvement in performance could be attributed to the crucial role of Mg in amino acid metabolism, bone development and the activation of numerous enzymatic systems in poultry. In addition, Mg supplementation is reported to impact muscle coordination and poultry growth, particularly in young chicks (Shastak and Rodehutscord 2015).

The findings of the current study exhibited a significant enhancement in carcass attributes, specifically in carcass yield, dressing %, giblet weight and abdominal fat proportions. This observation supports the results of Esfahani et al. (2015), who reported that the inclusion of zinc oxide NPs in a dry chicken diet improved carcass attributes and the relative weight of digestive and lymphoid organs compared to a moist diet during the first phase (1–21 days). Conversely, previous studies indicate that the addition of 0.3–0.5 mg/kg of nano‐selenium to feed did not influence the carcass characteristics and organ weights of broilers, supporting our conclusions (Ashour et al. 2025). In addition, Wang et al. (2011) revealed that there were no significant differences in carcass traits after supplementation with nano‐selenium at doses of 50, 150 and 300 µg/kg. Moreover, the incorporation of various BNSe doses (0.2, 0.4, 0.6 g/kg) demonstrated a negligible effect on all carcass characteristics (Reda et al. 2023).

The findings of the present research reveal a substantial rise in Hb, RBC and WBC counts in the groups supplemented with BNMg. These results corroborate those of Reda et al. (2025), who reported that BNCo treatments markedly elevated Hb, RBCs and WBCs in broilers. The results are consistent with those of Reda et al. (2023), who reported that dietary treatment with BNSe (0.4 and 0.6 g/kg) substantially enhanced WBC counts and RBC counts in quails. Furthermore, the present study substantiates previous reports by Diaz et al. (1994), who observed an increase in erythrocytes and Hb in broilers subjected to a cobalt‐enriched feed. In contrast, Kato et al. (2003) indicated that the haematocrit, Hb, erythrocyte and leukocyte counts remained unchanged following cobalt and vitamin B12 dietary treatment. However, erythrocyte count and Hb levels were unaffected by cobalt‐only supplementation.

The liver function test markedly improved with elevated concentrations of TP, ALB and globulin in the blood plasma of broiler chickens, whereas AST and ALT plasma levels were reduced by BNMg supplementation. Meanwhile, all treatments with BNMg levels displayed a substantial reduction in plasma hepatic function enzymes (ALT and AST) and kidney function tests (creatinine, urea and uric acid). Our results are consistent with those of Reda et al. (2025), who demonstrated that in broiler chickens, BNCo administration significantly elevated plasma concentrations of TP, ALB and globulin, while concurrently decreasing plasma concentrations of AST and ALT. Moreover, the results obtained in the current study confirm those of Reda et al. (2023), who indicated that dietary interventions with BNSe (0.4 and 0.6 g/kg) significantly improved AST activity, along with TP and globulin levels, in quails. The improved liver and kidney functions observed in broilers may be ascribed to the diverse effects of BNMg, which include antioxidant capabilities and metabolic regulation. Our findings align with those of Shokraneh et al. (2020), who demonstrated that nano‐selenium significantly decreased AST and ALT levels. Qin et al. (2016) opined that dietary supplementation with nano‐selenium substantially enhanced the liver and kidney function tests of rabbits. The current trial demonstrated that feed supplementation with BNMg markedly decreased plasma levels of creatinine, uric acid and urea in broiler chickens. These observations were substantiated by Sheiha et al. (2020), who reported that the blood serum of Cobb chickens treated with nano‐selenium at concentrations of 0.15, 0.075 and 0.0375 ppm exhibited reduced concentrations of AST, ALT and creatinine. Consequently, the incorporation of nano‐selenium into broiler diets may have significantly reduced both ALT and AST levels, which was attributed to the moderating effects of nano‐selenium on fat metabolism mediated by thyroid hormone (Dalia et al. 2017).

This current study indicates that the lipid profile levels of broilers were significantly influenced by dietary BNMg supplementation, resulting in a notable decrease in cholesterol, TG, LDL and VLDL. These findings contradict those of Reda et al. (2023), who asserted that dietary BNSe supplementation significantly improved lipid indices and increased selenium concentrations in the blood of quails. However, the present findings correlate with previous research, particularly the study by Mohapatra et al. (2014), which showed that broilers receiving 0.3 mg/kg of nano‐selenium exhibited significantly reduced serum cholesterol and TG concentrations. Saleh and Ebeid (2019) confirmed that supplementing broiler feeds with 0.5 mg/kg of dietary nano‐selenium substantially reduced plasma TG and total cholesterol.

The overall weight of immunological organs, specifically the spleen, thymus and bursa of Fabricius, increased substantially with BNMg supplementation in the diet of broilers. The findings we reported correspond with those of Reda et al. (2025), who demonstrated that BNCo supplementation significantly increased the weight of immune organs, notably the spleen and thymus, in broilers. These findings also align with those of Reda et al. (2023), who reported that dietary BNSe supplementation substantially boosted immune status and increased blood selenium concentrations in quails. Furthermore, our results concurred with Grodzik and Sawosz (2006), who reported that the administration of Ag‐NPs at 10 ppm influenced the embryonic bursa of Fabricius and its development. Even though there was no significant impact on overall chicken growth, a reduction in follicle size and number was documented. Ahmadi and Kurdestany (2010) explored the alterations in the proportional weight of the bursa following supplementation with Ag‐NPs at concentrations of 20, 40 and 60 ppm, revealing a reduction in both the number and size of follicles because of the antibacterial action of Ag‐NPs impacting the gut's microbial multiplication. From their study, Ag‐NPs clearly inhibited the proliferation of anaerobic bacteria and facilitated the transfer of available oxygen. The inflammatory response induced by NPs may affect the immunological state and stability of T helper 1 and T helper 2 cells. According to the results of Matsumura et al. (2003), a comparable effect of silver zeolite may result from Ag absorption into bacterial cells upon contact, which disrupts biological processes and leads to cell demise. However, it may be attributed to the release of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can impair and delay normal cellular function. Logically, intestinal microbiota play an essential role in the growth and maturation of the bursa of Fabricius in healthy chickens, contributing to proper immune development and function. The antiviral efficacy of an Ag‐NP solution against the virus responsible for infectious bursal disease in chicken embryos was established in a separate study (Pangestika and Ernawati 2017).

The immunological status of birds in the present study was enhanced by BNMg supplementation, particularly for IgA, IgY and IgM. Furthermore, C3 and lysozyme activities were also positively influenced. Contrary to our study, IgM and IgG levels do not appear to be affected by Ag‐NPs as reported by Pineda et al. (2012). The immune system is vital for optimal health and is closely interconnected with other physiological systems. It protects the host animal from infections (Gao et al. 2014) and continuously monitors the cells for abnormalities, which may pose a threat, such as tumorigenic cells (Pan Na et al. 2011). Previous research has indicated that trace minerals play a pivotal role in regulating the host's resistance against invading pathogens and facilitating innate immune recognition. This highlights their significance in modulating the immune responses and resistance to infection (Anwar et al. 2019). According to Sahoo et al. (2014), a novel medicinal method utilizing metal‐based composites has been suggested for disease treatment, suggesting that these compounds may regulate autophagy, which is a crucial host immunological response (Sahoo et al. 2014).

The findings of the present study showed that antioxidant levels were significantly enhanced with BNMg supplementation. The results we found were in accordance with those of Reda et al. (2023) who reported that dietary BNSe supplementation substantially boosted antioxidant activities and increased selenium concentrations in the blood of quails. The inclusion of BNCo in feed increased blood cobalt levels and markedly improved lipid parameters, immune responses and antioxidant capacity, as documented by Reda et al. (2025). These findings align with those of Scott et al. (2016), who claimed that CoNPs markedly augment lipid formation and peroxidation, in addition to ROS creation.

The enhancement of caspase‐3 enzymes contributes to cellular protection against oxidative stress. In addition to their moderate total anti‐oxidative capacity (TAC) and overall decreasing impact, the BNCo showed remarkable radical scavenging capacity (Matinise et al. 2018). Furthermore, Shahzadi et al. (2019) stated that bioinspired CoNPs exhibit radical scavenging activity, suggesting that antioxidant activity and scavenging power are dose‐dependent. This indicates that as the activity increases, so does the concentration of CoNP. The green‐synthesized NPs exhibited substantial free radical‐scavenging activity, yielding noteworthy findings (Hou et al. 2020). Moreover, Elkhateeb et al. (2024) confirmed that the MDAs decreased considerably as the nano‐selenium concentration increased to 0.4 mg/kg, and the TAC exhibited a substantial linear increase with the elevation of nano‐selenium concentration at 0.4 mg/kg. In addition, Hafez et al. (2020) indicated that the antioxidant status of broiler chickens was enhanced by the incorporation of zinc oxide NPs at 40 or 80 mg/kg into their feed, as evidenced by increased SOD and CAT activities and reduced MDA levels. Zhao et al. (2014) confirmed that the group fed 20 mg/kg nano‐ZnO exhibited a reduction in serum and liver MDA levels, an improvement in TAC action in both blood and liver tissue, and elevated serum CAT activity. The antioxidants capacity of Mg, as noted by Afanas'ev et al. (1995), depends on its anti‐radical properties and the repression of the xanthine oxidase enzyme and the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide. Liu et al. (2007) consistently proposed that Mg may limit lysyl oxidase (LOX) in chicken muscles by decreasing the generation of ROS. Consequently, Mg deficiency is recognized to enhance hydrogen peroxide synthesis and decrease CAT levels in hepatocytes in chick embryos in vitro, ultimately resulting in oxidative stress and biological harm.

The results from the current study illustrated a significant increase in blood minerals, including copper, potassium, calcium and zinc. These findings align with those of Estevez and Petracci (2019), who reported that Mg supplementation interacted significantly with calcium metabolism and deposition in both blood and breast muscle. However, this interaction did not adversely impact any of the production metrics examined. Nevertheless, the results of the present study corroborate the findings of Stillmak and Sunde (1971), who suggested that circulating Mg concentration is the most dependable biomarker of Mg absorption and physiological availability. Multiple processes may elucidate the reported effects of dietary Mg on calcium metabolism, including the possibility that dietary Mg may impede the intestinal absorption of calcium ions. Intestinal absorption of Ca and Mg happens via the exact mechanism for divalent ions. Empirical evidence suggests that increasing Mg intake may lead to a gradual reduction in absorption, potentially because of the formation of insoluble Mg‐phosphate complexes. Although such interactions have been demonstrated to interfere with the calcification process, no evidence of this effect was observed in the present study.

The digestive enzymes, including amylase, lipase, protease and trypsin, were significantly enhanced with BNMg supplementation. These findings were consistent with those of Reda et al. (2025), which indicated that BNCo supplementation significantly boosted digestive enzyme activity. These observations align with earlier studies by Brugger and Windisch (2016), who reported that Zn administration markedly increased amylase activity on Day 35. Zinc metabolism in exocrine glands, including the pancreas and bile ducts, regulates the balance of zinc in homeostasis through the secretion of pancreatic digestive enzymes into the GIT. Hu et al. (2022) reported that dietary supplementation of organic zinc sources improved the activities of pancreatic amylase, lipase and trypsin in broiler chickens, thus facilitating improved zinc absorption and utilization (Hu et al. 2022). Nonetheless, Park et al. (2015) confirmed that the administration of coated zinc oxide at 125, 3125 mg/kg or 139 mg of liquid‐encapsulated Shield Zn did not result in significant differences in amylase or trypsin activity. These studies suggest that while uncoated zinc supplemented at 2000 mg/kg may suppress the activity of pancreatic enzymes (Lü and Combs 1988), encapsulation of zinc may help mitigate these adverse effects.

The gut microbiota is crucial for enhancing the growth‐promoting effects and health benefits of feed additives in poultry. The research results of the current study proved that BNMg addition in broiler diets considerably raised the overall number of lactic acid‐producing bacteria while substantially decreasing the TBCs, coliforms, E. coli, Salmonella and TYMC. The present findings reinforce the observations of Reda et al. (2023), who reported that the addition of BNSe at concentrations of 0.4 and 0.6 g/kg substantially increased the population of lactic acid‐producing bacteria while concurrently decreasing the overall counts of harmful microbes, thereby mitigating gastroenteritis in developing quail. NPs demonstrate antibacterial capabilities that have garnered considerable interest, especially in recent years (Khajeh Bami et al. 2020). Nano‐mg can effectively reduce microbial infections and simultaneously improve the growth performance and overall well‐being of birds. These NPs can inhibit the growth of both Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative bacteria by eradicating the biofilm of microbial organisms (Hariharan et al. 2012). Stanley et al. (2015) revealed a substantial inhibitory impact of nano‐Se on pathogenic microbes, including E. coli, while promoting beneficial bacteria, such as Lactobacillus, in the avian digestive tract. An earlier study by Nabi et al. (2020) revealed that nano‐Se supplementation at 0.9 mg/kg in poultry feed improved intestinal health by stimulating the formation of short‐chain fatty acids and increasing the proliferation of beneficial microorganisms. This illustrates the ability of nano‐Se to modify the intestinal microbiota, which can significantly improve digestive health and immune function (Nabi et al. 2020). The supplementation of green nano‐selenium decreased coliform population in the caecum, elevated the ratio of lactic acid‐producing bacteria to coliforms, as well as increased the levels of lactic acid bacteria in the ileum (E. Mohammadi et al. 2020). Equally importantly, Sheiha et al. (2020) reported that BNSe inhibited the proliferation of deleterious bacteria while specifically enhancing the multiplication of beneficial bacteria in the rabbit caecum. Beyond this, Malyugina et al. (2021) elucidated that nano selenium possesses a substantial surface area, thereby enhancing its contact with microbes. Furthermore, Se NPs are more likely to penetrate the bacterial body due to their nanoscale dimensions, allowing for more efficient antibacterial activity. Specific investigations have demonstrated that the germicidal impact of nano‐Se may involve the production of ROS, leading to protein degradation, DNA destruction and compromise of cellular stability, thereby impeding bacterial proliferation (Shaaban and El‐Mahdy 2018). Interestingly, the current research provides insight into the role BNMg plays in improving the growth performance, carcass traits and immune response in broiler chickens, an observation that aligns with many NPs discussed in this manuscript.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study demonstrates that the inclusion of BNMg in broiler diets significantly improves growth performance, carcass characteristics, blood parameters, biochemical indices and the immunity status of birds. Furthermore, the findings contribute to the growing body of evidence that BNMg supplementation enhances antioxidant status and improves blood mineral concentrations of broiler chickens. Coupled with these, BNMg markedly stimulated digestive enzyme activities and promoted healthier intestinal microbiota by enhancing the growth of beneficial bacteria, while reducing the population of pathogens. In addition, the utilization of BNMg enhances Mg bioavailability, thereby increasing its absorption and uptake while minimizing the release of other compounds into the environment and advancing sustainable poultry production. The evidence presented herein highlights promising nutritional strategies for incorporating Mg NPs for improving the growth performance, gut health and overall productivity and sustainability in poultry production.
