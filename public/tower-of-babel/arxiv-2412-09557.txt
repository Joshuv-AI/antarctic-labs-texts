Experimental Machine Learning with Classical and Quantum Data via NMR Quantum Kernels
Vivek Sabarad, Vishal Varma, T. S. Mahesh

Abstract
Kernel methods map data into high-dimensional spaces, enabling linear algorithms to learn nonlinear functions without explicitly storing the feature vectors. Quantum kernel methods promise efficient learning by encoding feature maps into exponentially large Hilbert spaces inherent in quantum systems. In this work, we implement quantum kernels on a 10-qubit star-topology register in a nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) platform. We experimentally encode classical data in the evolution of multiple quantum coherence orders using data-dependent unitary transformations and then demonstrate one-dimensional regression and two-dimensional classification tasks. By extending the register to a double-layered star configuration, we propose an extended quantum kernel to handle non-parametrized operator inputs. Specifically, we set up a kernel for the classification of entangling and non-entangling operations and then validate this kernel first numerically by computing it on a double-layered star register and then experimentally by computing it on a three-qubit NMR register. Our results show that this kernel exhibits an ability to generalize well over unseen data. These results confirm that quantum kernels possess strong capabilities in classical as well as quantum machine learning tasks.



I
Introduction

Powered by advanced computing hardware and ingenious algorithms, machine learning is recently making great strides in several walks of modern civilization, from drug discovery to self-driving vehicles. Kernel methods are fundamental in classical machine learning, particularly in algorithms like support vector machines, as they enable the analysis of data in high-dimensional feature spaces without explicit transformations
Schölkopf and Smola 2002
. This is achieved through the ‘kernel trick’, which involves computing inner products in feature spaces, facilitating efficient handling of nonlinear relationships in data. In classical kernel methods, predefined kernel functions, such as the polynomial or Gaussian kernels, compute the inner products in feature space
Shawe-Taylor and Cristianini 2004
.

Quantum machine learning is a promising interdisciplinary field that combines quantum computing with machine learning to address diverse computational challenges
Cerezo et al. 2022
;
Mangini et al. 2021
. A key strategy involves leveraging quantum systems to represent data in high-dimensional feature spaces that are beyond the reach of classical computation. Some notable early contributions to quantum machine learning include quantum algorithms for solving linear systems
Harrow et al. 2009
, quantum principal component analysis
Lloyd et al. 2014
, and quantum support vector machines
Rebentrost et al. 2014
. Foundational surveys by Biamonte
et al.

Biamonte et al. 2017
and Schuld
et al.

Schuld et al. 2015
offered in-depth overviews, establishing the theoretical groundwork of the field. In recent years, progress has been made in the development of variational quantum algorithms, which have become central to near-term QML implementations
Cerezo et al. 2021
;
Tacchino et al. 2021
;
Banchi et al. 2021
, and in realizing quantum-enhanced feature spaces for classification tasks using superconducting quantum devices
Havlíček et al. 2019
. Furthermore, studies such as
Huang et al. 2021
have examined the informational power of input data to identify scenarios where the quantum advantage is achievable. More recent theoretical advances have introduced a unifying framework for quantum machine learning models as linear models in a quantum feature space, clarifying the relationship between parametrized quantum circuits or Quantum Neural Networks (QNNs) and Quantum Kernel Methods
Jerbi et al. 2023
.

Analogous to a classical kernel, a quantum kernel maps classical data to quantum states or operators in a high-dimensional Hilbert space. Here, the kernel function is calculated as the overlap between these states or operators. Such a quantum kernel can capture intricate data structures that may be challenging for classical kernels
Schuld and Killoran 2019
. The quantum kernel can then be used with classical algorithms for supervised machine learning like support vector machines (SVMs)
Schuld 2021
;
Rebentrost et al. 2014

II
Theory

We start with a familiar machine learning model, namely linear regression
Montgomery et al. 2012
.
Consider a dataset
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the feature vector of the
i
-th observation, and
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the corresponding target value. The linear regression model assumes that the relationship between the input features and the output can be described by a linear function:

y_{i}=f(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\epsilon_{i},

(1)

where
f(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}+b
,
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the weight vector,
b\in\mathbb{R}
is the bias term, and
\epsilon_{i}
accounts for any noise or error in the data. For simplicity, we can represent it,

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{x}_{i}\end{bmatrix},\quad\tilde{\mathbf{w}}=\begin{bmatrix}b\\
\mathbf{w}\end{bmatrix},

(2)

which allows us to write the model as

f(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}.

(3)

Our objective is to find the optimal weight vector
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}
that minimizes the difference between the predicted values
\hat{y}_{i}=f(\mathbf{x}_{i})
and the actual target values
y_{i}
, usually taken in the form

J(\tilde{\mathbf{w}})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(y_{i}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\right)^{2}.

(4)

Minimization of J with respect to
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}
leads to
normal
equations,

\tilde{\mathbf{w}}=(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{X}})^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{y},

(5)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
is the design matrix formed by stacking the augmented feature vectors
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
as rows, and
\mathbf{y}
is the vector of target values
Montgomery et al. 2012
.

While linear regression is powerful for linearly separable data, it struggles with datasets where the relationship between features and targets is nonlinear. To address this, we can transform the input features into a higher-dimensional space wherein linear regression can capture the nonlinear patterns in the data.

II.1
Kernel Methods

Kernel methods extend linear algorithms to handle nonlinear problems by mapping the input data into a high-dimensional feature space using a nonlinear transformation. However, instead of explicitly performing this transformation, kernel methods rely on computing inner products in the feature space directly using a kernel function (see Fig.
1
)
Schölkopf and Smola 2002
.
Consider a non-linear map
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
, which transforms the input features into a vector space
\mathcal{F}
known as the feature space. The linear model of a non-linear function
f(\mathbf{x})
in the feature space is

f(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\mathbf{w}_{\phi}^{\top}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i}),

(6)

where
\mathbf{w}_{\phi}
is the weight vector in the feature space. The cost function becomes

J(\mathbf{w}_{\phi})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(y_{i}-\mathbf{w}_{\phi}^{\top}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i})\right)^{2}.

(7)

Solving for
\mathbf{w}_{\phi}
directly is often computationally expensive due to the high dimensionality of
\mathcal{F}
. Instead, we leverage the representer theorem
Schölkopf et al. 2001
, which states that the optimal solution
\mathbf{w}_{\phi}
can be expressed as a linear combination of the transformed training samples

\mathbf{w}_{\phi}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i}),

(8)

with scalar coefficients
\alpha_{i}
.
Substituting the above into the model in Eq.
6
, we obtain

f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i})^{\top}\phi(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}),

(9)

where
k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})=\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i})^{\top}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{j})
is the kernel function.
For clarity, let us consider kernel regression without regularization. The cost function simplifies to

J(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[y_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}k(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{x}_{i})\right]^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{K}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\right\|^{2},

(10)

where
K_{ij}=(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})
are elements of the kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}
.
Our objective is to find the coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{N}]^{\top}
that minimize
J(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
.
Minimizing
J(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
leads to the normal equations

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{y}.

(11)

Once we have
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, we can make the prediction for any new input
\mathbf{x}
using Eq.
9
.
Thus, by using kernel functions, we can work in very high-dimensional (even infinite-dimensional) feature spaces without explicitly computing the mapping
\phi(\mathbf{x})
. Common kernel functions include:

•

Linear kernel:
k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{j}
.

•

Polynomial kernel:
k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})=(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{j}+c)^{d}
, where
c
is a constant and
d
is the degree.

•

Gaussian kernel:
k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})=\exp\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)
.

These kernels allow us to capture complex, nonlinear relationships in the data using algorithms designed for linear models
Shawe-Taylor and Cristianini 2004
.

II.2
Quantum Kernel Methods

Quantum kernel methods extend classical kernel techniques by utilizing quantum registers to compute kernel functions, capturing complex patterns in data that may be intractable classically. The principal idea is to map the input data into the operator space of the quantum system that exploits the rich algebraic structure of quantum operators without necessitating a full-scale quantum computer
Schuld 2021
. In our work, we employ nuclear spin qubits in an NMR setup to experimentally extract the quantum kernel.

Quantum kernel methods generally consist of two key steps: quantum feature mapping and quantum kernel computation
Wang et al. 2024
. The quantum feature mapping involves encoding input data points
\mathbf{x}_{i}
into quantum operators
A(\mathbf{x}_{i})
. We achieve the encoding by associating each data point with a specific unitary transformation
U(\mathbf{x}_{i})
acting on the quantum
system

A(\mathbf{x}_{i})=U(\mathbf{x}_{i})A_{0}U^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),

(12)

where
A_{0}
is a reference operator in the system. Here,
U(\mathbf{x}_{i})
are designed such that they efficiently explore the operator space. However, care must be taken in feature map design since overly expressive maps can lead to exponentially concentrated kernel values
Thanasilp et al. 2024
, making the model effectively independent of the input.
The quantum kernel is then computed using the Frobenius inner product between operators

k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})=\text{Tr}\left[A(\mathbf{x}_{i})A(\mathbf{x}_{j})\right].

(13)

This kernel function measures the similarity between data points in the operator space, effectively capturing intricate relationships inherent in the data. The protocol for quantum feature mapping and quantum kernel computation is illustrated in Fig.
2
. Detailed methodologies and specific implementations within the NMR framework are discussed in the next section.

in order to ensure the symmetry of the quantum kernel since only the
C
qubit is measured at the end.

III.2
Quantum Kernel for Quantum Data

As an example for a quantum ML task, we take up the task of determining whether a given unitary transformation
U
entangles any given initial mixed state
\rho
.
The entanglement classification task is a binary classification problem where the input data is a unitary transformation and the output is a binary label indicating whether the transformation is entangling or not for a particular initial mixed state. It is a nontrivial problem, which is discussed by Serrano-Ensástiga and Martin
Serrano-Ensástiga and Martin 2023
.

We now approach the above problem using the quantum kernel method by taking a set of unitary transformation
U
as the quantum input data. Quantum data offers significant advantages over classical data. Firstly, unitary transformations do not require parameterization (except for training), as they can be fed directly into the kernel model. Secondly, the quantum kernels with quantum inputs have the ability to predict the labels of
inputs that are categorically different from the training inputs (generalizability). To handle a quantum input, we propose a quantum kernel

k(U_{i},U_{j})=\text{Tr}(A(U_{i})A(U_{j})),

(21)

where
A(U_{i})=V(U_{i})A_{0}V^{\dagger}(U_{i})
is the quantum feature map for the unitary
U_{i}
with the encoding unitary
V(U_{i})=U_{i}U_{e}U_{i}^{\dagger}
and
A_{0}
is a suitable reference operator in the system.
The kernel function
k(U_{i},U_{j})
measures the similarity between the unitaries
U_{i}
and
U_{j}
in the operator space.
We now assume a quantum register of the geometry shown in Fig.
6
(a). The central qubit C is connected to A qubits, and each A qubit is connected to a B qubit.
The input unitaries
U_{i}
act on A and B qubits as shown in Fig.
6

U_{e}
is a CNOT-like operation that entangles A qubits with C qubit after a Hadamard on C.
The quantum kernel for the entanglement classification task is computed using

16

We classify the unitary operators acting on the thermal equilibrium state

\rho_{0}=e^{-\beta H_{Z}}/4,

(22)

of a two spin-
\frac{1}{2}

\beta=1.5
. The input unitaries

The training is done with 30 operators, chosen from only the lower part of the parameter space as indicated by circles. The kernel model is then tested on 196 operators uniformly distributed over the entire parameter space. The model is found to classify the operators with an accuracy of 95%. Additionally, as mentioned before, unlike classical kernels, the quantum kernel can also handle non-parameterized unitaries. We tested the above quantum kernel (trained on the same set as above) with 100 non-parameterized random unitary operators. We found it to correctly classify up to 84% of the random unitary operators, once again confirming its ability to handle quantum data directly.

IV
Conclusions

Quantum kernels offer a practical approach for exploring the high-dimensional feature spaces inherent in quantum systems. In this work, we have demonstrated the implementation of quantum kernel methods using NMR-based spin registers. By leveraging the symmetries of a 10-qubit star-topology NMR system, we encoded classical data into multiple-quantum coherence orders through data-dependent unitary transforms and computed the kernel function through experimental measurements. Our results show that the quantum kernel method can effectively perform classical machine learning tasks such as one-dimensional regression and two-dimensional classification. Furthermore, by extending the register to a double-layered star configuration, we introduced a quantum kernel capable of handling non-parametrized unitary operator inputs, which can then perform quantum classification tasks and be validated experimentally. We illustrated the power of the quantum kernel by successfully classifying unitary operators depending on whether they generate entanglement or not when acting on a thermal state. When a large set of high-dimensional unitaries needs to be classified, the standard method of tomography is no longer feasible. In such a scenario, a well-trained quantum kernel can be far more efficient in classification. This work opens new possibilities for processing quantum data directly, marking a step toward realizing fully quantum machine learning systems.
