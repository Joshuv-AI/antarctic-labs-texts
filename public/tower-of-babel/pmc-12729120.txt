Ginsenoside Rh2 inhibits mesenchymal stem cell senescence by regulating mitochondrial and lysosomal function
Authors: Jianjian Zhuang, Yue Li, Yi Ling Huang, Xiang Wang, Neng Ming Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) undergo senescence after expansion and in vitro culture under oxidative stress, which limits their clinical application. Ginsenoside Rh2 has been confirmed to regulate mitochondrial function, but its role in modulating the senescence of MSCs has not been clearly investigated.PurposeThis study aims to explore the effects and underlying mechanisms of Rh2 in inhibiting the senescence of MSCs.MethodsTransmission electron microscopey (TEM) and fluorescence staining assays were used to monitor changes in mitochondrial and lysosomal morphology and function in Rh2-treated senescent MSCs. Introduction

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are a type of adult stem cell that have received extensive attention in cell therapy and tissue regeneration in recent years due to their strong capacities for self-renewal, differentiation, pluripotency, and immune regulation [1]. In clinical applications, MSCs must be expanded and cultured in vitro before being transplanted in vivo to compensate for functional loss. However, MSCs are influenced by various environmental factors, such as oxidative stress, when introduced into target tissues. This stress can induce senescence and apoptosis of MSCs, thereby hindering their therapeutic efficacy [2]. Increasing evidence shows that mitochondrial dysfunction caused by oxidative stress often occurs in senescent MSCs and is typically associated with elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS), decreased mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), and disrupted mitochondrial metabolism [3].Conversely, mitochondrial dysfunction can further increase ROS levels, which may also impair lysosomal function [4]. Mitophagy contributes to mitochondrial quality control by removing damaged mitochondria and reducing intracellular ROS, thereby regulating cellular metabolic homeostasis and ultimately delaying MSC senescence [5]. Additionally, lysosomes are critical organelles for maintaining cellular homeostasis and degrading dysfunctional mitochondria through autophagy [6]. In recent years, sirtuins have been shown to regulate mitochondrial quality by mediating mitophagy in senescent cells. Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), a member of the sirtuin family, plays an important role in delaying senescence by modulating mechanisms such as mitochondrial biogenesis, homeostasis, autophagy and oxidative stress [7]. However, the specific role and underlying mechanisms of SIRT1 in senescent MSCs remain largely unexplored.

Ginseng, the root of Panax ginseng C.A. Meyer, has been widely used in the Far East, including Korea and China, for its medicinal properties [8].Ginsenoside Rh2 is one of the main bioactive components of P. ginseng and exhibits anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-senescence effects [9]. Studies have shown that Rh2 can upregulate SIRT1/PGC-1α and PINK1–Parkin-mediated mitophagy to enhance antioxidant capacity, thereby protecting cells from senescence [10]. In addition, Rh2 can influence lysosomal function through modulation of ROS, which can indirectly affect mitochondrial function [11]. Thus, Rh2 has the potential to simultaneously regulate the functions of cellular mitochondria and lysosomes. However, whether Rh2 can inhibit MSC senescence and the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. In this study, we investigated the inhibitory effect of Rh2 on the senescence of MSCs and its impact on mitochondrial and lysosomal functions, providing new insights into the potential of the traditional Chinese medicine monomer Rh2 in mitigating the senescence of MSCs. Furthermore, this study also explored the interactions among cellular organelles during the MSC senescence process.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reagents

MSCs were purchased from PH Biotechnology, and MSCs at passage numbers 2 to 5 were used for experimental assays [12, 13]. The MSCs were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12; Shanghai BasalMedia Technologies Co., Ltd.) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Hyclone). Ginsenoside Rh2 (≥ 98% purity) was obtained from Zhejiang Yak Drug Co., Ltd.

Cell proliferation assays

MSCs were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured until reaching 80–90% confluence. Cells were then treated with varying concentrations of Rh2 (10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 µM) for 48 h. After treatment, 100 µL of culture medium containing 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added to each well, and cells were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance of each sample was measured at 450 nm using an MK3 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Senescence-associated beta-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining

MSCs were seeded in 48-well plates and cultured until reaching 80–90% confluence. For the senescence model, cells were treated with 10 g/L D-Gal for 72 h. In the drug intervention groups, cells were subsequently treated with different concentrations of Rh2 (14 µM and 28 µM) for 48 h following D-Gal exposure. SA-β-gal staining was performed using a β-gal staining kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) [14]. Staining results were observed under an IX71 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell cycle assays

The treatment procedure for the experimental groups are described in Sect. 2.3. For cell cycle analysis, cells were collected, fixed with cold 70% ethanol overnight at 4 °C, and stained with propidium iodide (PI) for 30 min at 4 °C (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Fluorescence intensity (Ex/Em = 535/615 nm for PI) was then analyzed using a FACSVerse flow cytometer (BD, New York, NY, USA).

Assessment of lysosome and mitochondria morphology

Mitochondrial morphology in MSCs was visualized by staining with 100 nM Mito-Tracker Green (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C for 20 min. Lysosomal morphology was assessed by staining with 50 nM Lyso-Tracker Red (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C for 15 min. Fluorescence changes were observed using an IX71 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (against LC3B and COXIV, (1:100, Proteintech). After washing, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488- or 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:400, Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature. Cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst before fluorescence (imaging using an Olympus IX71) microscope. Confocal images were captured, and ImageJ software was used for quantitative analysis.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The collected cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The samples were then post-fixed 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h, followed by dehydration, embedding, and sectioning into 60–80 nm ultrathin sections. Sections were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate and examined using a HT7700 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Measurement of ROS and mitochondrial ROS (mtROS)

The production of intracellular ROS and mtROS were assessed using the oxidation-sensitive fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and MitoSOX Red Mitochondrial Superoxide Indicator (Yeasen, Shanghai, China), respectively. The treatment procedures for the cell groups are described in Sect. 2.3. For ROS measurement, cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin, suspended in PBS, and incubated with DCFH-DA for 20 min at 37 °C. Fluorescence intensity (Ex/Em = 488/525 nm) was measured by flow cytometry (BD, New York, NY, USA), and fluorescence changes were observed using an IX71 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

For mtROS measurement, cells were incubated with 5 µM MitoSOX Red (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) for 20 min at 37 °C. Fluorescence intensity (Ex/Em = 396/610 nm) was measured by flow cytometry (BD, New York, NY, USA), and fluorescence changes were simultaneously observed using the IX71 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement

MSCs were prepared for JC-1 staining according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), following the treatment procedures described in Sect. 2.3. Fluorescence intensity was measured by flow cytometry (Monomer: Ex/Em = 514/529 nm; J-aggregates: Ex/Em = 585/590 nm, BD, New York, NY, USA), and JC-1 fluorescence changes were observed using an IX71 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantification of intracellular ATP, NAD+/NADH, glucose and lactate

The treatment procedures for the experimental groups are described in Sect. 2.3. Intracellular ATP levels were measured using an ATP Content Assay Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China), with absorbance recorded at 340 nm. NAD+ and NADH levels, as well as the NAD+/NADH ratio, were determined using an NAD+/NADH Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), with absorbance measured at 450 nm. Glucose content was measured using a Glucose Test Kit (Sangon, Shanghai, China) at 505 nm, and lactate levels were determined using a Lactic Acid Assay Kit (Sangon, Shanghai, China) with absorbance measured at 570 nm.

Molecular docking of Rh2 and SIRT1

To investigate the molecular interaction between Rh2 and SIRT1, semi-flexible docking simulations were performed using Glide. The protein structure was optimized with MacroModel (OPLS2005 force field), and the Protein Preparation Wizard was employed to optimize the hydrogen-bonding network. The most likely binding conformations were explored, and docking scores were calculated to evaluate interaction strength.

Real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

The treatment procedures for the experimental groups are described in Sect. 2.3. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using TRUEscript H Minus M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase (Aidlab, China) to generate cDNA for quantitative PCR (qPCR). Primers were purchased from General Biol (Anhui, China), and qPCR was conducted in triplicate using 2×SYBR Green qPCR Mix (Aidlab, China) on an ABI 7500 real-time PCR instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). GAPDH was used as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Western blot

The treatment procedures for the experimental groups are described in Sect. 2.3. Treated cells were harvested by centrifugation and lysed in Radio Immunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime, China) containing the protease inhibitor phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; Aladdin). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 1 h, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the appropriate primary antibodies, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h. After washing five times with TBST, signals were detected and analyzed using an integrated chemiluminescence imaging system (ChemiScope, Shanghai, China).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. All in vitro experiments were conducted at least 3 independent experiments and data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistically significant differences were determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Results

Ginsenoside Rh2 inhibited the senescence of MSCs

To investigate the effect of ginsenoside Rh2 on the senescence of MSCs, we first determined the non-toxic concentration of Rh2. MSCs were treated with Rh2 at concentrations of 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 µM for 48 h. As shown in Fig. 1A, the non-toxic concentration (IC₁₀, 28 µM) was selected for subsequent senescence-related experiments. An oxidative stress-induced senescence model was established by treating MSCs with 10 g/L D-Gal for 72 h [15, 16]. To evaluate the senescence phenotype, the activity of the senescence-specific enzyme SA-β-gal was measured. Results confirmed increased SA-β-gal activity in senescent MSCs, which was inhibited by Rh2 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1B). Senescent cells are also characterized by cell cycle arrest. Our results showed that senescent MSCs were arrested in the G0/G1 phase, and Rh2 treatment partially reversed this arrest (Fig. 1C). Additional analyses of biological changes associated with senescence included the expression levels of senescence-related genes (IL-3, IL-6, IL-8, MMP3, IL-1α, GM-CSF) [17]. In senescent MSCs, inflammatory cytokines IL-3, IL-6, IL-8, MMP3, and IL-1α were upregulated, while the hematopoietic cytokine GM-CSF was downregulated. Rh2 reversed the increase in inflammatory cytokine expression, suggesting that it can partially restore the biological characteristics of senescent MSCs (Fig. 1D). Finally, analysis of senescence marker proteins revealed that p21, p16, and p53 were significantly upregulated in senescent MSCs. Rh2 treatment reduced the expression of these proteins, consistent with the observed effects on cell cycle progression (Fig. 1E, F). Overall, these results confirmed that D-Gal successfully induced MSC senescence, and Rh2 was able to mitigate the senescent phenotype of MSCs.

Effects of ginsenoside Rh2 on mitochondrial structure and function

To investigate the effects of Rh2 on mitochondrial morphology and function in senescent MSCs, we first examined mitochondrial ultrastructure using TEM. Senescent MSCs displayed condensed nuclear chromatin, numerous cytoplasmic vacuoles, and a reduced number of mitochondria, which appeared enlarged or swollen. Treatment with increasing concentrations of Rh2 improved these cellular features (Fig. 2A). MitoTracker Green staining further revealed that mitochondria in senescent MSCs were fragmented, whereas Rh2 treatment restored a continuous and uniform mitochondrial distribution (Fig. 2B, C). Since the integrity of mitochondrial structure directly affects function, including ROS, MMP, and mitochondrial metabolism, we next assessed these parameters. Rh2 inhibited the increase in both mitochondrial ROS and total ROS in senescent MSCs (Fig. 2D, G) and attenuated the decline in MMP (Fig. 2E). Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) is an important cofactor in mitochondrial energy production. Previous studies have shown that NAD+ supplementation can delay the senescence of MSCs [18]. Consistently, D-Gal treatment reduced the NAD+/NADH ratio, which was restored by Rh2 treatment (Figure S1A). Mitochondrial dysfunction can also shift cellular metabolism toward glycolysis [19]. We measured glucose consumption and lactate production and found that Rh2 significantly increased the production of lactate (Fig. 2F) and reduced the consumption of glucose (Fig. 2H). Additionally, D-Gal reduced the level of ATP, and Rh2 treatment restored ATP content (Figure S1B). Collectively, these results suggest that Rh2 alleviates mitochondrial dysfunction and reduces the glycolytic shift observed in senescent MSCs.

Effects of ginsenoside Rh2 on lysosome structure and function

Senescent cells are typically characterized by the upregulation of lysosomal proteins and an increase in lysosome content [20]. Consistent with this, we observed a significant increase in lysosome number in senescent MSCs, which was reversed by Rh2 treatment (Fig. 3A). In addition to lysosome quantity, loss of lysosomal acidification reduces autophagic activity, contributing to cellular senescence [21, 22]. Using the LysoSensor yellow/blue dextran probe, we found that lysosomal acidification decreased in senescent MSCs but was restored with Rh2 treatment (Fig. 3B). Dysfunctional lysosomes can also promote exosome secretion by impairing the degradation of multivesicular proteins [23]. Immunofluorescence colocalization analysis showed that exosome secretion was markedly increased in senescent MSCs, whereas Rh2 treatment inhibited this secretion (Fig. 3C). Exosomes collected in vitro were further analyzed for surface markers ALIX, CD63, and TSG101; Rh2 significantly reduced exosome secretion in senescent MSCs (Fig. 3D). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed that exosome size remained around 100–130 nm across all treatments. While Rh2 had little effect on exosome size, it significantly reduced their number in senescent MSCs (Fig. 3E).

Rh2 activated SIRT1 to inhibit mitochondrial and lysosomal functions

RNA sequencing analysis revealed that Rh2 influenced senescent MSCs through pathways including cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, NF-κB signaling, cellular senescence, and ferroptosis (Fig. 4A, B). Protein expression is typically dysregulated in senescent cells [24]. RNA-seq results showed that among the NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase SIRT family genes, SIRT1 expression was significantly increased under Rh2 treatment (Fig. 4C). These findings were further validated at both the gene and protein levels. Rh2 significantly upregulated SIRT1 gene expression (Fig. 4D) and increased SIRT1 protein activity in senescent MSCs (Fig. 4E, I). The chemical structure of Rh2 is shown in Fig. 4F. Molecular docking further confirmed the interaction between Rh2 and SIRT1, indicating that Rh2 forms hydrogen bonds with R247 and N417 in SIRT1, and is stabilized by hydrophobic interactions between its steroid scaffold and surrounding residues, including E249, T209, R446, F414, G415, and H423 (Fig. 4G). The addition of the SIRT1 inhibitor EX-527 significantly reversed the inhibitory effects of Rh2 on the phenotypes of senescent MSCs (Figure S2). Both SIRT1 siRNA and SIRT1 inhibitor EX-527 enhanced the expression of senescence-related proteins p16, p21, and p53 in Rh2-treated senescent MSCs (Fig. 4I, Fig. S5). Regarding mitochondrial function, the inhibition of SIRT1 reversed the Rh2-induced decrease in mitochondrial ROS (Figure S3A) and the increase in MMP (Figure S3B). In studies examining SIRT1’s role in mitochondrial metabolism, inhibition of SIRT1 promoted lactate accumulation (Figure S4A), increased glucose consumption (Figure S4B), decreased the NAD+/NADH ratio in Rh2-treated senescent MSCs (Figure S4C). For lysosomal function, SIRT1 inhibition increased exosome secretion in Rh2-treated senescent MSCs (Figure S3C). Collectively, these results indicate that Rh2 regulates both mitochondrial and lysosomal functions in senescent MSCs by activating SIRT1.

Rh2 activated SIRT1-mediated mitophagy inhibit MSCs senescence

Mitophagy is an important mechanism for maintaining mitochondrial quality by eliminating damaged mitochondria [25]. TEM was used to assess mitophagy, revealing an increase in the number of autophagosomes that encapsulated dysfunctional mitochondria following Rh2 treatment of senescent MSCs (Fig. 5A). Immunofluorescence staining further verified the activation of mitophagy, as Rh2 enhanced the colocalization of LC3 and COX-IV in mitochondria (Fig. 5B). These results collectively demonstrated that Rh2 significantly enhanced the mitophagy of senescent MSCs.The PINK1–Parkin pathway is a classical signaling mechanism regulating mitophagy [26]. In this study, mitophagy-related markers (PINK1, Parkin, LC3, and p62) and senescence related factor (NF-κB, IL-8, IL-6) were detected by qPCR and Western blot. The results showed that the gene and protein expression of PINK1, Parkin and LC3 was increased and p62 was decreased, the senescence related factors showed the downregulated trend (Fig. 5C, D). All results confirming that Rh2 can activate mitophagy in senescent MSCs.

According to previous research, the release of senescence-related factors can be regulated by the PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy pathway [7]. We further explored the role of mitophagy by using mitophagy inhibitors and activators in Rh2-treated senescent MSCs and in senescent MSCs, respectively. Immunofluorescence staining preliminarily verified the changes in mitophagy under the mitophagy inhibitors and activators (Fig. 6A). The expression of mitophagy-related genes and proteins showed a decreasing trend when mitophagy was inhibited in Rh2-treated senescent MSCs (Fig. 6B, D). Meanwhile, the gene expression and protein activity levels of IL-8 and IL-6 increased after the inhibition of mitophagy in Rh2-treated senescent MSCs, indicating that mitophagy can regulate the release of senescence-related factors (Fig. 6B, C). It has been found that there is bidirectional regulation between SIRT1 and autophagy in senescent cells [37]. We further explored the regulatory roles of mitophagy and SIRT1 in senescent MSCs. The results demonstrated that the protein expression of SIRT1 decreased after the inhibition of mitophagy in Rh2-treated senescent MSCs, indicating that mitophagy could regulate the stability of SIRT1 in these cells. Meanwhile, we also explored the regulatory role of SIRT1 in mitophagy by introducing SIRT1 siRNA (SIRT1-830) in Rh2-treated senescent MSCs (Fig. 7A). Immunofluorescence staining preliminarily verified that the inhibition of SIRT1 could reverse the activation of mitophagy in Rh2-treated senescent MSCs (Fig. 7B). The expression of autophagy-related molecules increased, while senescence-related factors decreased at both gene and protein levels (Fig. 7C&D&E). The activity of senescence factors (IL-6, IL-8) was also increased in Rh2-treated senescent MSCs (Fig. 7F). At the same time, SIRT1 was overexpressed to further verify its role in senescent MSCs (Fig. S6). These results indicate that the secretion of senescence-related factors is regulated through SIRT1/PINK1–Parkin mitophagy-mediated activation of NF-κB in senescent MSCs. Overall, these findings suggest a potential feedback loop between SIRT1 and mitophagy in senescent MSCs, in which Rh2 is involved.

Rh2 activated SIRT1-mediated exosome secretion to inhibit the senescence of MSCs

Previous studies have shown that impaired lysosomal function in SIRT1-deficient cells is associated with decreased expression of the V-type proton ATPase catalytic subunit A (ATP6V1A), which is critical for late endosomal and lysosomal acidification. Dysregulated lysosomal function can, in turn, promote exosome secretion [21, 27]. Consistent with this, we observed decreased ATP6V1A expression in senescent MSCs, which was reversed by Rh2 treatment (Fig. 6B, D). To investigate the role of SIRT1 in regulating lysosomal function, ATP6V1A expression was measured when SIRT1 was inhibited in Rh2-treated senescent MSCs (Fig. 7C–E, Fig. S7) and when SIRT1 was overexpressed in senescent MSCs (Figure S6). The results indicated that ATP6V1A expression trends were consistent with SIRT1 levels, suggesting that SIRT1 positively regulates ATP6V1A in MSCs. These findings indicate that Rh2 regulates lysosomal function by activating SIRT1 in senescent MSCs. Overall, Rh2 inhibits MSC senescence by modulating both the secretion of senescence-associated factors and exosomes, a process mediated through activation of mitophagy and restoration of lysosomal acidification.

Discussion

The senescence of MSCs is a complex process, accompanied by the accumulation of ROS, changes in sirtuin family protein activity, and other cellular alterations. Currently, strategies to inhibit or delay senescence primarily involve nutritional supplements, drug interventions, or modulation of cytokines and other signaling molecules [28]. Ginsenoside Rh2, a major safe and non-toxic bioactive saponin in ginseng, has recently gained attention for its potential role in regulating cell senescence [10]. However, its effects and mechanisms in combating the senescent of MSCs remain largely unexplored. In this study, we established a D-Gal-induced oxidative stress model of senescent MSCs and demonstrated that Rh2 effectively inhibits the senescence of MSCs while restoring mitochondrial and lysosomal morphology and function. Specifically, Rh2 suppressed the secretion of senescence-associated factors by activating the SIRT1-PINK1/Parkin mitophagy pathway and reduced exosome release by enhancing lysosomal acidification in senescent MSCs. These findings highlight the potential of Rh2 as a therapeutic agent to alleviate MSC senescence.

Mitochondrial dysfunction also leads to metabolic disturbances, characterized by enhanced glycolysis, increased glucose consumption, lactic acid accumulation, and a decreased NAD+/NADH ratio. MSCs typically rely on anaerobic glycolysis to generate energy in damaged tissues [29]. In this study, we confirmed that Rh2 could inhibit glycolysis in senescent MSCs, consistent with previous findings showing that Rh2 suppresses glycolysis in non-small-cell lung cancer and cervical cancer cells [30, 31]. SIRT1, which regulates various cytoplasmic and nuclear targets, plays a critical role in energy metabolism [32]. Inhibition of SIRT1 in senescent MSCs enhanced glycolytic activity, supporting our conclusion that Rh2 could inhibit glycolysis in these cells via SIRT1 activation. Previous studies have reported that ginsenoside monomers act as activators of SIRT family proteins [32]. Liu et al. have already verified that Rh2 can enhance the antioxidant capacity and improve mitochondrial dysfunction by regulating the gene expression of SIRT1,PGC-1α and SOD1 in senescent porcine oocytes [10]. In this study, we also conducted further research on how Rh2 regulates cellular metabolism by activating SIRT1.

Senescent cells mediate the aging process by secreting senescence-associated cytokines [33]. Activation of mitophagy not only repairs mitochondrial damage in senescent MSCs but also directly inhibits the secretion of these cytokines [34]. Our results were consistent with previous studies reporting the paracrine effects of Rh2 on senescent MDA-MB-231 and MCF-10 A cells [35]. Mitophagy plays an important role in maintaining mitochondrial function. Previous studies have shown that Rh2 inhibits senescent phenotypes by reducing ROS and activating the PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy pathway in doxorubicin (DOX)-induced human mammary epithelial cell senescence models [36]. Additionally, Rh2 upregulates the SIRT1/PGC-1α pathway to enhance antioxidant capacity, protecting porcine oocytes from senescence and oxidative stress [10]. However, whether Rh2 regulates mitophagy through SIRT1 activation had not been confirmed. In this study, we demonstrate for the first time that Rh2 preserves mitochondrial function by upregulating SIRT1-mediated PINK1–Parkin mitophagy and activating downstream NF-κB signaling, which regulates the secretion of senescence-related factors such as IL-6 and IL-8. This finding further supports the role of SIRT1 in Rh2’s modulation of mitophagy and its impact on cytokine secretion. Another active ginsenoside monomer, Rg1, has also been shown to prevent the senescence of MSCs [37], mainly through its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, which aligns with our findings. In terms of basic research and clinical application, Rh2 and Rg1 differ in their primary effects: Rh2 predominantly enhances immunity and exerts antitumor effects [38, 39], whereas Rg1 is mainly used to improve neurological function and support liver disease treatment [40, 41]. Regarding availability and efficacy, Rg1 is the main ginsenoside component and can be obtained in large quantities, while Rh2 is a secondary ginsenoside with lower natural abundance but higher bioavailability and efficacy compared to Rg1 [42]. In conclusion, Rh2 exhibits a more advantageous effect than Rg1 in inhibiting MSC senescence.

The deficiency of SIRT1 leads to decreased lysosomal acidification and increased exosome production [27]. In gastric cancer, Rh2 has been shown to regulate lysosomal pH and membrane integrity, inhibiting the proliferation of HCG-27 cells by inducing lysosomal dysfunction [43]. Additionally, Rh2 significantly increases ROS production in HepG2 cells, resulting in early lysosomal membrane permeabilization and the release of lysosomal proteins, which activates the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway [11]. However, the role of Rh2 in regulating lysosomal function in senescent MSCs and its underlying mechanism remained unclear. In this study, we confirmed that Rh2 restores lysosomal acidification and inhibits exosome production in senescent MSCs through SIRT1 activation. This effect is mediated by upregulating ATP6V1A expression. These findings indicate that Rh2’s regulation of lysosomal function contributes to its inhibitory effect on exosome secretion. Although early studies reported that Rh2 can modulate lysosomes [44]. our study expands on these findings by specifically investigating lysosomal acidification and its functional consequences in senescent MSCs.

Our study confirmed that Rh2 reversed the senescence of MSCs by regulating both mitochondrial and lysosomal functions by modulating the secretion phenotype, including the SASP and exosome production. Although previous studies have demonstrated that Rh2 can inhibit glycolysis in MSCs, the detailed mechanisms by which Rh2 activates SIRT1 to regulate metabolism in senescent MSCs remain to be elucidated. In addition, further studies are needed to investigate how Rh2 modulates exosomal content and its subsequent effects on recipient cells.

Conclusion

This study confirmed that oxidative stress-induced senescence of MSCs leads to mitochondrial and lysosomal dysfunction and, for the first time, demonstrated the role of the natural Chinese medicine monomer Rh2 in inhibiting the senescence phenotype by restoring these organelle functions. Rh2 regulated mitophagy by activating SIRT1 to inhibit the release of senescence-associated cytokines and concurrently by enhancing lysosomal acidity through SIRT1 regulation. By simultaneously reducing the release of senescence-associated cytokines and exosomes, Rh2 effectively alleviated the senescence of MSCs. These findings not only advance our understanding of the mechanisms by which Rh2 inhibits senescence but also suggest a potential therapeutic strategy for oxidative stress-induced senescence of MSCs.
