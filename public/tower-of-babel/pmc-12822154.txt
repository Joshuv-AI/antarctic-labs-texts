Odor repellency and neuronal responses of commercial products towards Anopheles mosquitoes
Authors: Minh N Nguyen, Julia M Johnson, Inioluwa Olaleye, Christopher J Potter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe bites from infected female Anopheles mosquitoes lead to hundreds of thousands of deaths annually. To find a human, mosquitoes rely on their keen sense of smell. Repellents are chemicals that can target a mosquito’s chemosensory senses. Therefore, targeting olfaction using repellents can be an effective way to prevent their attraction to humans and subsequent biting. However, the precise mechanisms underlying the mode of action for most commonly used repellents is surprisingly not well known. Recent evidence suggests that commonly used insect repellents target Anopheles mosquitoes by either activating mosquito olfactory neurons to trigger aversive responses or masking natural attractant cues emitted by the host to reduce attraction.MethodsIn this study, we used a combination of olfactory behavioral assays, calcium imaging of antennal neurons, and volatile organic compounds measurement in Anopheles mosquitoes to determine the specific mechanism by which one commercial repellent might outperform another. In our experiments, we assessed commercial products containing synthetic (DEET, IR3535, picaridin) or natural (p-menthane-3,8-diol, essential oils) ingredients.ResultsWe found that natural products performed better than synthetic repellents on rapid odor-based repellency assays. On odor-based assays that allowed for longer time periods and odor accumulation, both products performed well, with p-methane-3,8-diol products showing consistent performance in both odor-based assays. Synthetic products demonstrated longer-lasting odor concentrations compared to more volatile natural products. The antennal olfactory neuron responses demonstrated clear differences between the two categories. We found that for Anopheles mosquitoes, the most effective products often combined masking and odor repellency, activated a number of antennal neurons, and evaporated at a slow rate.ConclusionsThis approach provides insights into rational development of more effective Anopheles vector controls. Understanding the influence by which different mechanisms contribute to a repellent’s function can improve vector control strategies for malaria-endemic regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05730-x.Keywords: Insect repellents, Olfaction, Olfactory responses, Spatial repellent, DEET, Picaridin, IR3535, PMD, Olfactory neuron

Background

The Anopheles genus of mosquitoes can be vectors for a parasite that gives rise to malaria, which caused 597 000 deaths in 2023 [1]. Mosquito control methods, such as indoor residual spraying and insecticide-treated bed nets, can be effective measures for indoor use, but do not protect humans from outdoor biting [2–4]. Insect repellents can provide personal protection and prevent mosquito biting when applied correctly [3, 5–7].

Commercial repellents are most commonly comprised of synthetic ingredients—such as DEET, IR3535, or picaridin—or naturally-derived ingredients—such as essential oils. Despite their widespread use, their modes of action are largely unknown. Previous studies suggest that these ingredients repel mosquitoes by interacting with their olfactory and/or taste systems [8, 9]. The peripheral olfactory system of Anopheles mosquitoes consists of three sensory appendages – a pair of antennae, a pair of maxillary palps, and one labellum at the tip of the proboscis [10]. The taste system of Anopheles mosquitoes includes the tarsal segments of the legs, the labellum and labrum of the mouthparts and the cibarium, an internal organ [11].

There are several ways a repellent can deter mosquito bites – spatial repellency, contact repellency, or masking repellency. A repellent can act at a distance (spatial repellency) by directly activating olfactory receptor neurons on the antenna of mosquitoes and triggering aversive behaviors to drive them away [8, 12–16]. Repellents can also act by contact (contact repellency) either via activating gustatory receptors on different parts of the mosquito or by stimulating the mosquito’s tarsi mechanically, so that they will fly away after contacting the repellent [17–21]. A repellent could simply block the scent of the host from activating the sensory system of the mosquitoes (masking repellency), thus ‘hiding’ the host from olfactory-based detection [5, 22, 23]. Finally, repellents might be able to modulate olfactory receptors’ responses to host odors, scrambling the insect odor code, and thereby acting as a ‘confusant’ for host odor sensing [24–26].

With a large number of repellent products available, it can be challenging for customers to pick which to purchase. The Consumer Reports® is a non-profit organization that provides independent testing and reporting on consumer products and services, including insect repellents. The Consumer Reports® yearly ranking of mosquito repellents is based on arm-in-cage assays for Aedes aegypti and Culex quinquefasciatus mosquitoes [27] (Fig. 1). Since the two tested species belong to Culicinae subfamily, while Anopheles belongs to the Anophelinae subfamily, there might be differences in their responses to repellents. Here, we assessed 16 out of 53 Consumer Reports® products tested in 2023 to determine product efficiency in repelling Anopheles mosquitoes as this has not been previously extensively tested. These products span 5 main categories of active ingredients: DEET, IR3535, picaridin (also called icaridin), p-Menthane-3,8-diol (PMD), and essential oils with reported rankings from low to high. In Anopheles mosquitoes, previous studies suggested that DEET, IR3535, and picaridin work primarily through an odor masking mechanism while the natural ingredients, including PMD and essential oils would function primarily through odor repellency [8, 23]. Therefore, we expected that commercial products comprised of these ingredients would share the respective mechanism of these active ingredients.

Repellent products are most often tested using the arm-in-cage assay [28, 29]. In this assay, the repellent is directly applied to a human arm, which is then placed into a cage of hungry, blood-seeking mosquitoes. Repellency is measured by the protection time from biting or blood-feeding. However, the arm-in-cage assay does not separate out the three different mechanisms of repellency and likely emphasizes products that promote contact repellency (given the measured outcome is biting or blood-feeding). This can make it challenging to distinguish if other mechanisms also underlie the effectiveness of each repellent [5, 30]. In this paper, we utilized behavioral assays to specifically test the odor repellency of commonly used repellent products.

We used two different behavior assays: the close proximity response assay and the tube assay. In the close proximity response assay, we observe the immediate responses of individual mosquitoes to repellents within 30 s of exposure [23, 31]. This determines whether the tested product only needs a short period of time to act as an odor repellent. The tube assay, on the other hand, examines the group response of mosquitoes to repellents in a small tube over the course of one hour [31, 32]. In combination, the results from the two assays determine if a repellent can function through odor repellency. Furthermore, a key signature of odor repellents is their activation of the mosquitoes’ olfactory neurons. To examine this we further tested if odor repellency of different products might be correlated with the activity of different groups of antennal neurons. To do this, we used calcium imaging in transgenic Anopheles mosquitoes to record the responses of odorant receptor neurons on the antenna to the repellents [33]. We found that the most effective repellent products against Anopheles likely combine masking and odor repellents to activate small subsets of olfactory neurons with the antenna.

Methods

Mosquitoes rearing

Anopheles coluzzii (Wild type Ngousso strain; Orco-QF2, QUAS-GCaMP6s) and Anopheles gambiae (Keele) were reared in a chamber maintained at 26–28 °C, 70–80% RH and L14:D10 cycle. Eggs were submerged in Reverse Osmosis (RO) water in a pan and larvae were fed fish food (TetraMin) daily (~ 150 larvae per pan). Once emerged into adults, mosquitoes were collected into a cage and fed with a 10% sugar solution. For all experiments, non blood-fed, freely mated female mosquitoes (7-12 days old) were used.

Products chosen from Consumer Reports®

A total of 16 products ranked by Consumer Reports® were chosen for the study in order to sample a wide range of ranked products, to examine all active ingredient categories, and based on commercial availability (Table 1). All products were purchased using the links provided by the Consumer Reports® website.

Behavior assay

All behavior assays were performed 1–5 h before lights off, at 24 °C, 22% RH.

Close proximity response assay (Fig. 2A): Female mosquitoes were tested individually. A mosquito was transferred by aspirator from a rearing cage to a small experiment cage with meshed sides (3D designed, 6 cm × 6 cm × 6 cm) and given 3 min to rest on one of the cage mesh walls. The mosquito was approached by the larger end of a 1000 μL pipette tip containing a piece of filter paper (75 mm × 15 mm) saturated with 10 µl testing odor or control solvent (mineral oil). For products in lotion form or wipe papers, we saturated the filter paper fully with the products. Mosquitoes that did not move after 30 s of solvent control exposure were further used for test odorants. The mosquito was observed for 30 s and the time when it flew away was recorded. The mosquito was given 1–2 min between odorants, until it rested on the cage mesh again for at least 30 s. One mosquito was tested with 4 odorants.

Analysis: A ‘Kaplan—Meier Survival Estimates’ was used to analyze the time that tested mosquitoes took to fly away in response to odorants. A Cox Proportional Hazard Model was used to assess the relationship between the time to fly away and the repellents. All analyses were performed using standard libraries and external libraries (pandas, Matplotlib, scikit-survival) in Python.

Tube repellency assay: 10–12 individual mosquitoes were transferred from a rearing cage into prepared glass tubes. The tubes were enclosed, semi-sealed on both sides using nylon mesh and covered by clear conical polypropylene caps. The mosquitoes were given 15 min to habituate to the tubes before the experiments. Subsequently, filter papers saturated with control or testing odors were placed inside the conical caps out of direct contact reach from mosquito appendages. The spatial distributions of the mosquitoes were recorded for one hour. The repellency index (RI) was calculated as RI = [(C-T)/(C + T)] * 100 where C is the number of mosquitoes in the control area (the tube half farthest from the treated filter paper) and T is the number of mosquitoes in the treatment area (the tube half closest to the treated filter paper). At 10 min, all mosquitoes were motile and making odor-guided choices. At time-points great than 30 min, some animals became immobilized or knocked-out by the odor. Only mosquitoes that could stand on their legs were included in repellency index calculations at all time points. The tube assay utilizes a semi-closed environment to maximize spatial repellency but may not reflect naturalistic real-world situations.

Analysis: One-sample t-test was used to compare mean RI values against a null hypothetical mean of 0.

Calcium imaging assay

Mosquito preparation: A female mosquito was immobilized on ice for 1 min, and its wings and legs removed by fine forceps. The mosquito was placed sideway on a piece of double-sided tape in the center to the side of the glass slide and its body and one eye were stuck to the tape. The mosquito’s maxillary palps and antennae were fixed by two glass capillary tips onto the slide – one at each end of the olfactory organs. All recordings are from the 11th antennal segment.

Imaging system: Calcium imaging was performed using a 50 × objective (LD EC Epiplan-Neofluar 50 ×/0.55 DIC) mounted on a Zeiss Axio Examiner D1 microscope. A Zeiss Illuminator HXP 200 C light source and an eGFP filter cube (FL Filter Set 38 HE GFP shift free) were used for fluorescence. An EMCCD camera (Andor iXon Ultra, Oxford Instruments) using Andor Solis software (Oxford Instruments) was used to record videos of 20 s at 512 × 512 pixel resolution. The exposure time was 200 ms at 5 Hz.

Odorant preparation and delivery: For products in liquid form, 10 µl of the product was pipetted on a piece of filter paper. For products in other forms (lotion, wipe papers), a piece of filter paper was saturated with the products. The filter paper was placed in a Pasteur pipette which was connected to a 1000 µl pipette tip. The Pasteur pipette was inserted into a hole in a plastic serological pipette (Denville Scientific Inc) carrying a continuous stream of purified air directed towards the antenna. A stimulus controller (Syntech) was used to divert a 1 s pulse of filtered air into the Pasteur pipette approximately 10 s after the start of video recording. The sequence of odors was randomized for each set of experiments and new odor pipettes were prepared for each recording session. Each animal was tested to all selected repellents, with mineral oil as control odor.

Analysis of calcium imaging recordings: Responses of each animal to all odors were concatenated into one stack of images. Fiji software was used to stabilize the stack and correct for movement using the ImageStabilizer plugin. Regions of interest (ROIs) were selected based on the fluorescence intensity and used for analysis of the GCaMP6s signal. Once raw fluorescence values were extracted for each cell, df/f values were calculated using a custom Python script where df/f = (f-f0)/f0. To determine baseline fluorescence f0, 20 frames were averaged before stimulus presentation. A Python DataFrame containing peak responses of each cell to each odor was generated. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was used for visualizing the responses of all cells on one antenna to all the odors. All the cells from 5 females were pooled and subjected to hierarchical clustering using Euclidean distance with Ward linkage. Clustering was based on the cells’ responses to all the odors (including positive and negative controls).

Volatile organic compound measurement assay

The MiniRAE 3000 photoionization detector (Honeywell RAE Systems) was used to determine the bulk concentration of odorants through time. The photoionization detector was calibrated to a reference gas (ethyl acetate) and was positioned at the end of a 30 cm glass tube. A filter paper soaked with 10 µl of an odorant (75 mm × 1.5 cm) was put into a plastic cap and placed at the other end of the glass tube.

Results

Selection of consumer products has been influenced by the choice of behavioral assay

The Consumer Reports® used arm-in-cage assays for Aedes and Culex mosquitoes (Fig. 1A) to rank the effectiveness of 53 commercial products [27]. From this list we chose 16 products (Table 1) with rankings ranging from excellent (> 90/100 score) to poor (< 20/100 score) across 5 categories of active ingredients (Fig. 1B). As the arm-in-cage assay makes it challenging to distinguish between contact or spatial repellency—and thus to evaluate repellents that effectively deter mosquitoes over long distances— we focused our studies on behavioral assays that are odor-based to identify which repellents function as spatial repellents. Effective spatial repellents would greatly complement many of our existing masking and contact repellents and provide a better understanding of the underlying chemosensory mechanism for each product type. Spatial repellents can also complement traditional contact-based mosquito control methods such as insecticide-treated bed nets to increase protection against malaria infection, whereas contact repellents demonstrated little additional benefit under these conditions [5, 6, 34]. According to the ranking of Consumer Reports®, products containing DEET, IR3535, picaridin, and PMD offered the greatest protection, whereas mixtures of essential oils did not protect humans from mosquito bites, resulting in lower scores [27] (Fig. 1B). In contrast, several studies have shown that essential oils can provide adequate protection for a longer period of time [35–38] and are also less damaging to common materials [27] (Fig. 1C).

Natural products outperform synthetic products in an odor-based repellent assay

To specifically test for odor-based repulsion, we first used the close proximity response assay (Fig. 2A). In this assay, a single mosquito was allowed to come to rest for 3 min on the mesh wall of a testing cage. It was then slowly approached using a pipette tip containing a piece of filter paper saturated with the repellent product. The time until the mosquito flew away was recorded. Each individual mosquito was exposed to five consecutive odorants (including solvent control) and the sequence of the odorants randomized. We tested 30 Anopheles gambiae (Keele strain) mosquitoes in total for each odorant. To avoid non-specific movement of mosquitoes, we proceeded with the experiment only if the animal remained still for at least 30 s after exposure to negative control (mineral oil). The proportion of mosquitoes that remained on the wall of the cage over the course of 30 s is represented by a survival plot (Fig. 2). This assay determines the immediate effect of the products on Anopheles mosquito behavior. Across the 5 groups of ingredients, synthetic products containing DEET, IR3535 and picaridin either did not repel Anopheles mosquitoes or repelled around 15%—40% of mosquitoes within the same period of time (Fig. 2B-D), consistent with previous findings [8, 23] (with the exception of product 3; see Discussion). Consumer synthetic products outperformed the pure chemicals at similar concentrations, suggesting additional ingredients in the consumer products increased the spatial repellency of these products. In contrast, PMD and essential oils performed well and repelled 80% of the mosquitoes within 30 s (Fig. 2E-F). There were also differences within the same group of products. For example, product 13 contains 10% of PMD and did not repel Anopheles mosquitoes within 30 s while other PMD products containing around 19.5% of PMD repelled at least 80% of the insects (Fig. 2E).

To compare the protection score from Consumer Reports® with the performance of the products in the close proximity response assay, we similarly scored the products from 1 to 5 based on the percentages of mosquitoes repelled at the end of 30 s (Fig. 2G; Score = percentages of mosquitoes repelled/100 *5). We observed a large discrepancy between the Consumer Reports® protection score and the close proximity response assay score (compare Fig. 1B to Fig. 2G). In particular, the synthetic products have scores of 1 to 2 in the close proximity response assay while their Consumer Reports® scores are on average 3. On the other hand, essential oils products score only 1 in protection score but have scores of 5 in the close proximity response assay. Only PMD products have equivalent performance in both assays. We observed similar results for this assay with Anopheles coluzzii mosquitoes (Additional file 1). These results suggest that repellent protection from these two types of products- synthetic and natural- likely represent different modes of action, with the Consumer Reports® arm-in-cage assay biasing toward contact repellency, whereas the close proximity response assay preferencing for immediate odor-based spatial repellency [5].

A tube assay reveals a range of repellency for consumer products

We used the tube assay to examine spatial repellency of the commercial products over a longer time scale (1 h) that allows animal group interactions and odor accumulation (Fig. 3A). Overall, compared to our negative control using mineral oil (Fig. 3A), all commercial products showed some level of repellency, contrasting results from the close proximity response assay. In particular, IR3535, DEET, and picaridin commercial products clearly showed repellency after 10 min in the tube assay (Fig. 3B-D), with some products demonstrating as strong spatial repellency as natural products (Fig. 3E-F), such as products 2, 3, 4, 6, and 9 (see Table 1). Of the three synthetic ingredients, IR3535 product exhibited the weakest spatial repellency in the tube assay. Interestingly, pure DEET, IR3535, and picaridin tested at concentrations found in commercial products led to minimal repellency in this assay (Fig. 3B-D). Meanwhile, natural products containing PMD or essential oils demonstrated strong odor repellency in the tube assay like they did in the close proximity response assay.

For comparison to the arm-in-cage and close proximity response assay, we also scored the products from 1 to 5 based on their repellency indices at 10 min in the tube assay (Score = RI at 10 min/100 * 5). In the tube assay, the repellency scores of synthetic products were not significantly different (p = 0.54, two-sample t-test) from those of natural products.

Volatility of consumer products correlates with the timescale of repellency periods

For odor repellency, an important consideration is how long the product will last. This is determined by the volatility of the product which determines how rapidly the repellent will evaporate over time. To address if volatility of the product might influence the spatial repellency results, we measured the concentration of volatile organic compounds using a photoionization detector over time (Additional file 2). Overall, all products except products 1, 13, 14, and 16 (which already started with low concentrations) decreased in their concentrations. Aligning with our assumption that essential oils products work primarily through spatial repellency, this drop in concentration could explain why essential oil products receive a low protection score from Consumer Reports®. Rapid volatilization could provide an initial window of high repellency, followed quickly by a period of low protection once the product has evaporated. We also found that when a synthetic product was mixed with a natural one, it slowed the decrease over time of the natural product’s concentration (Additional file 3).

Olfactory activation patterns related to spatial repellency

As the behavioral assays identify products that could function as spatial repellents, we suspected that they were activating olfactory neurons to drive this repellency. Therefore, we examined the responses of Anopheles antennal olfactory neurons to determine the neuronal activity profiles of the consumer products (Fig. 4).

Using calcium imaging on the antenna of Anopheles mosquitoes, we determined the activation of odorant receptor expressing neurons in response to commercial repellents (Fig. 4A). We found that the products containing essential oils and PMD activate a large number of cells across the antenna at a high level (Fig. 4B). Meanwhile, the products containing synthetic chemicals such as DEET, IR3535 and picaridin activate a smaller number of antennal cells and at a lower level (Fig. 4B). This was in contrast to the pure synthetic chemicals which we previously demonstrated showed little to no antennal olfactory neuron activities even at 100% [23]. From the population level responses of each antenna, for each animal, multivariate analysis was used to study the representation of tested repellents and their relationship. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) of mean df/f show a clear clustering of the synthetic and natural products into two groups, suggesting that these two products can be distinguished by antennal neuron activation patterns (Fig. 4B).

Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of the dataset was performed to classify the neurons from 5 females into 3 categories based on their response profiles to the 16 commercial products. Cluster 1 and 2 (Fig. 4C, D) contain strong and medium responding cells that can support distinguishing synthetic and natural products. Cluster 3 consists of weaker responding cells that respond to all repellents, not just the natural plant-based repellents, possibly reflecting an activation pattern for odors to function as spatial repellents. This data further supports our hypothesis that natural products function to drive repellency by activating antennal neurons. Interestingly, there are also a number of cells activated by both natural and synthetic products. This could help explain why in both the close proximity response assay and the tube assay, some synthetic products could still spatially repel mosquitoes. These overlapping cells might play a critical role in triggering the avoidance response of Anopheles mosquitoes.

Discussion

We have used the Consumer Reports® guide of available repellent products for Aedes and Culex mosquitoes to examine if these products might also function as repellents against Anopheles mosquitoes. In particular, we tested the spatial behavioral response of these repellents towards Anopheles mosquitoes to determine which could likely be the most effective odor repellents. The close proximity response assay identified PMD and essential oils as the most potent odor repellents, whereas most commercial repellents scored well in the olfactory tube assay. The activity patterns of olfactory neurons responding to repellents indicated clear differences in how the Anopheles mosquito detects and likely perceives synthetic vs plant-based repellents, highlighting neurons that may directly guide odor-based repulsion. Synthetic consumer products (such as DEET product 3) might also contain additional ingredients that act as odor-based repellents to boost their efficiency and distinguish them from other commercial products.

When choosing a mosquito repellent, consumers are highly influenced by the stated protection time of a product. The arm-in-cage assay is the most widely adopted method to test for repellent-based protection [28, 29]. It is simple to set up and provides a direct measure of how long a repellent can protect humans from mosquito bites. However, this assay may not adequately test all the modes of action for repellents. By focusing on the number of landings and bites from many mosquitoes in a small space, it primarily measures if mosquitoes are repelled based on contact with the repellent and undervalues if the repellent can also act as a potent spatial repellent. A recent study that tracked mosquitoes in the arm-in-cage assay found mosquitoes have to touch the arm treated with repellents such as DEET, picaridin and PMD in order to be repelled [30]. This supports the hypothesis that this behavioral assay biases positive results toward contact repellents.

Understanding how each type of consumer repellent product functions to repel mosquitoes can inform which repellent product might work best for different situations, and could also be useful for developing better repellents or repellent mixtures. Spatial repellents can be sensed by mosquitoes from a distance, activating sensory neurons on their olfactory appendages to drive them away. In contrast, contact repellents are low or non-volatile chemicals and can either mask the odor of hosts to hide humans from mosquitoes [23] or activate sensory neurons on a mosquito’s legs when it touches the treated area [9, 39]. Depending on which mode of action is engaged, a repellent could be used to repel mosquitoes away from an area like a doorway or table (spatial repellent) or have minimal effect on preventing mosquito attraction but highly effective at preventing mosquito bites when directly applied to skin (e.g., contact repellent).

The two assays used in this study focus on volatile spatial repellency. The close proximity response assay examines the immediate responses of an individual mosquito to an odor. Given the short observation time in an open space, the assumption is that it is a direct measurement of the spatial repellency induced by the chemical. The tube assay examines the response of a group of mosquitoes to odor in a closed arena. With a longer time scale and an enclosed environment, chemicals can slowly accumulate in the air inside the tube. It is also possible that the volatile odorant could gradually deposit onto the glass tube and trigger contact repellency as well. Interestingly, one product tested (PMD, product 13) did not evoke responses from individual mosquitoes in the close proximity response assay but could repel mosquitoes in the tube assay. It is possible a repellent odorant in product 13 needed to reach a certain odor level before it became repellent, or needed time to deposit onto the glass surface, which only occurred in the tube assay.

The three behavioral assays when considered together reveal how different ingredients might function as mosquito repellents. Products containing DEET have the highest score on average in the arm-in-cage assay for Aedes, Culex, and Anopheles [40, 41] mosquitoes. This means that DEET products can protect humans from mosquito bites for at least two hours but only on skin directly covered with DEET [42] reflecting their contact repellency and low volatility. With these characteristics, the DEET products tested in the close proximity response assay did not repel mosquitoes as quickly as natural products; however, given enough time to accumulate in the tube assay, they perform as well as natural products. This likely reflects the inclusion of spatial repellents into the consumer product as pure DEET showed minimal repulsion in the tube assay. The identity of these additional ingredient(s) is unknown, but could possibly be from the EPA 25(b) list of essential oils known to functional as mosquito spatial repellents [43]. IR3535 and picaridin products share similar trends in their performance across the three assays, with commercial products outperforming pure IR3535 or picaridin. When applied to human skin under typical real-world applications, however, these products would not have sufficient enclosed space to accumulate with time as in the tubes and thus likely function primarily as contact repellents instead of as spatial repellents. On the other hand, natural products containing PMD or essential oil mixtures outperform synthetic products in close proximity response and tube assays, but do not surpass DEET products in arm-in-cage assay. In fact, essential oils seem to fail to prevent biting in two hours in arm-in-cage assays according to the results from Consumer Reports®. These results suggest that natural products, with their high volatility, promote spatial repellency at the expense of long-lasting protection. Interestingly, some PMD products do perform well in all three assays. This indicates that these products have adequate volatility or odor-based potency to repel mosquitoes spatially while the essential oil mixture limits evaporation for longer lasting protection, and arm-in-cage results suggest they might also function as contact repellents. Future studies, such as those aimed at testing contact repellency directly, will be necessary to determine to what extent the effect of PMD is from spatial or contact repellency. Also, future studies could examine the benefits of including fixative ingredients (such as those used in perfumes to promote their longevity) with essential oil products to help lower the volatility of the essential oils and extend their odor-based protection.

Spatial repellent products likely trigger aversion from mosquitoes by directly activating olfactory neurons. The calcium imaging data which monitored the response of olfactory neurons in the antenna to the consumer products suggests that there is some overlap in the cells activated by synthetic products and natural products. The identification of the olfactory receptors activated by spatial repellents could allow for large scale screens to identify chemicals with improved characteristics, such as minimal odor to humans at concentrations that still robustly repel mosquitoes. While there are groups of olfactory neurons that exhibit higher responses to natural products than synthetic ones (likely representing neurons that mediate behavioral repulsion to plant-based products), there is also a group of cells activated at low level by both types of products. These results suggest that the synthetic products could contain other unspecified ingredients that allow them to activate sensory neurons and thus act as a spatial repellent. A caveat of this assay is that it only examines responses of Orco positive neurons, and it is possible that other chemosensory neurons (such as those expressing ionotropic receptors or gustatory receptors) may also contribute to the ability of the repellents to mediate repellency. A future direction will be to identify which specific neuronal populations within these activity profiles directly drive spatial repellency.

Investigating the mode of action for different repellent products could be valuable in designing improved repellents or repellent mixtures. An ideal repellent would combine the contact repellency of synthetic ingredients with the spatial repellency of natural ingredients, while also maintaining the harmless-to-materials feature of essential oils. Ideally, it should also work across multiple pest arthropods (mosquitoes, ticks, bed bugs, etc.). To develop a better repellent with advantages from both types of ingredients, there is the need for standard assays to quantify both contact protection and spatial repellency. One caveat of both tube and close proximity response assays is they do not include a human factor in the experiment. It is possible that attraction to humans could counteract odor repulsion. Thus, it can be hard to predict from these assays how a repellent would perform when tested on human skin. Nonetheless, these assays can be useful for identifying potent spatial repellents, but would then need to be tested with other assays, such as hand-in-cage [15, 44] and arm-next-to-cage [45] to determine the effects of human attraction on their spatial repellency. Assays used to test consumer products are also typically performed in laboratory-controlled environments. Thus, variations in environment temperature, humidity, different human skin conditions and other factors in a real-world setting could affect their efficiency. For this reason, testing topical repellents in the field would provide useful behavioral data. Consumers could also benefit if each repellent product specified whether it could function at a distance and/or only upon direct contact.

Conclusions

The category ‘mosquito repellent’ represents a broad range of chemicals which do not reveal the mode of action that enables their function to prevent mosquito attraction or biting. Most consumer products, such as those ranked by Consumer Reports®, rely on the ‘arm-in-cage’ assay to evaluate protection, which biases selection towards contact repellents (DEET, picaridin, IR3535). Behavioral assays that specifically examine spatial repellency suggest plant-based repellents (PMD, essential oils) typically outperform synthetic products. Optimal repellents might be produced by a mixture of these two categories. Anopheles mosquitoes, in particular, are sensitive to plant-based PMD-containing products suggesting PMD products might be a preferred consumer choice in malaria-endemic countries.
