Introduction

Candida and cytomegalovirus (CMV) are infectious agents that can cause severe intraocular inflammation, particularly in immunocompromised individuals [1, 2]. Their sequential occurrence in the same eye is exceptionally rare and presents complex diagnostic and management challenges.

Intraocular Candida infection can lead to endogenous endophthalmitis characterized by insidious multifocal, cream-colored, retinal lesions that can involve both deep and superficial layers with indistinct borders. These lesions eventually extend into the vitreous and develop into fungal abscesses, forming a characteristic “string of pearls” appearance [3, 4]. Oude et al. [3] reported ocular involvement in 16% of candidemia patients, emphasizing the importance of ophthalmologic screening in at-risk individuals. Risk factors include indwelling catheters, parenteral nutrition, and immunosuppression [3, 4].

Intraocular CMV infection typically presents as a unilateral hypertensive uveitis, mimicking Posner–Schlossman syndrome. As the virus spreads hematogenously, CMV retinitis in its hemorrhagic form predominately affects areas surrounding major blood vessels. It is characterized by full-thickness retinal necrosis with hemorrhagic borders [5].

This case report describes an immunocompromised patient who developed presumed endogenous candida endophthalmitis followed by presumed CMV retinitis, emphasizing the role of advanced retinal imaging, surgical interventions, and long-term management in preserving visual function.

Case presentation

A 46-year-old immunocompromised Caucasian male patient, initially hospitalized and treated with intravenous antibiotics for Klebsiella bacteremia, was referred to the ophthalmology service for floaters and blurry vision in the left eye. His medical history included Candida discitis from recent hospitalization, rheumatoid arthritis treated with long-term oral prednisone (10–50 mg daily for over 3 years, with poor tolerance to tapering below 20 mg), steroid-induced diabetes, hepatitis B and C, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) negative status, and past intravenous drug use.

Initial examination revealed visual acuity of 20/30 OD and 20/40 OS. Fundus examination of the left eye showed vitritis, a hyperemic disc, and two creamy-colored patches of retinitis (1–2 disc diameters in size) located superotemporally and inferonasally to the disc, with overlying fungal balls in the vitreous, consistent with presumed Candida endogenous endophthalmitis (Fig. 1). Intrahospital blood cultures later confirmed Candida fungemia, though initial cultures during the hospitalization were negative for fungi.

Wide-field fundus photograph of the left eye. Multiple cream-colored, fluffy vitreous opacities (fungal balls) are visible. Blue arrows indicate two prominent fungal balls overlying retinitis, one superotemporal and one inferonasal to the optic disc

Despite empiric treatment with intravitreal ceftazidime (2.25 mg/0.1 ml), vancomycin (1 mg/0.1 ml), amikacin (400 mcg/0.1 ml), amphotericin B (5 mcg/0.05 ml), and voriconazole (100 mcg/0.1 ml), administered sequentially over several weeks, the patient’s condition deteriorated. Visual acuity in the left eye declined to hand motion, with enlarging vitreous opacities forming a “string of pearls” appearance. Subsequently, the patient underwent pars plana vitrectomy with silicone oil tamponade. Intraoperatively, white fluffy balls were noted with focal vitreoretinal traction and a small retinal break causing a localized combined tractional and rhegmatogenous retinal detachment, particularly in the superotemporal region. These were managed by releasing tractions and applying barrier endolaser. Vitreous sampling for Gram stain and bacterial and fungal cultures yielded negative results, showing only 1+ white blood cells. Postoperatively, the retina remained attached. Following silicone oil removal and intraocular lens implantation, visual acuity improved to 20/70.

Two months post-surgery, the patient developed recurrent episodes of hypertensive uveitis in the left eye. Over four distinct flare-ups, he presented with anterior chamber cells, elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) up to 42 mmHg, and retinal hemorrhages along an inferior retinal vessel (Fig. 2). Slit-lamp examination revealed granulomatous keratic precipitates on the corneal endothelium (Fig. 3). Visual acuity deteriorated to 20/400 over the course of these episodes, which lasted several months, each partially responding to local steroid and IOP-lowering agents.

The magnified view (B) details these microhemorrhages, highlighting the absence of typical widespread retinal whitening or necrosis associated with fulminant cytomegalovirus retinitis. Subtle vascular changes, including early signs of vascular sheathing and potential capillary dropout areas, are visible

Slit-lamp photograph of the left eye demonstrating granulomatous keratic precipitates on the corneal endothelium. These appear as small, whitish-gray deposits on the inner surface of the cornea, characteristic of granulomatous uveitis

Fluorescein angiography revealed retinal vascular leakage, with shunt vessels, telangiectasias, and microaneurysms predominately along the same inferior vessel (Fig. 4). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) angiography showed corresponding areas of capillary dropout. Notably, fundus examination lacked typical retinal necrosis of fulminant CMV retinitis. In addition, anterior chamber paracentesis for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was inconclusive owing to an insufficient sample.

Fluorescein angiography of the left eye. A Wide-field view demonstrating diffuse retinal vascular leakage in the inferior retina. B Magnified view of the inferior retina showing areas of vascular leakage, microaneurysms, and telangiectasia along a major inferior retinal vessel. Laser spots and healed retinitis demonstrate staining

Despite the absence of classic retinal whitening, the combination of recurrent unilateral uveitis with markedly elevated intraocular pressure and localized retinal hemorrhages along a major vessel suggested an atypical presentation of presumed CMV panuveitis. Empiric treatment with topical ganciclovir gel and oral valganciclovir was initiated, leading to swift improvement. Within a week, the patient experienced resolution of uveitis, normalization of IOP, and visual acuity improvement to 20/80 (Fig. 5). At the 6-month follow-up, the patient’s best-corrected visual acuity in the left eye had improved to 20/40 and remained flare free while on prophylactic CMV treatment.

Discussion

This case of sequential rare intraocular infections in an immunosuppressed patient presents a complex scenario in retinal practice, highlighting the importance of vigilance in high-risk patients.

Our case highlights the importance of clinical diagnosis and prompt treatment in managing complex ocular infections. The initial presumed Candida endophthalmitis did not respond to intravitreal injections, necessitating surgical intervention. The subsequent presumed CMV-associated hypertensive uveitis, presenting as a milder form of CMV retinitis, responded well to medical management. While laboratory confirmation was not obtained for either infection, this case underscores the value of careful clinical assessment when paraclinical results are inconclusive or unavailable.

It is worth noting that laboratory tests, while helpful, are not always definitive in these conditions. The yield for positive vitreous sample culture in Candida endophthalmitis is relatively low, around 38% [6]. In contrast, PCR of anterior chamber fluid for CMV uveitis is quite accurate, with a false-negative rate of only 3.5% [7] . However, in our case, insufficient sample volume prevented PCR analysis. This scenario illustrates the challenges that clinicians may face in diagnosing and managing complex ocular infections when laboratory confirmation is not possible.

In cases of endogenous endophthalmitis with severe vitritis, early surgical intervention offers both diagnostic and therapeutic advantages [8, 9]. Sallam et al. found that early vitrectomy was associated with better visual outcomes in cases with endogenous Candida endophthalmitis [9].

The subsequent development of presumed CMV panuveitis presented a significant diagnostic challenge, particularly owing to its atypical presentation. While CMV retinitis typically presents with both necrosis and hemorrhage in its fulminant form [5], our case revealed only hemorrhage with concomitant anterior segment involvement. This underscores the potential for variant presentations in immunocompromised hosts.

The sequential occurrence of these infections can be attributed to the patient’s severely compromised immune system due to long-term steroid use and complex medical history. The initial Candida infection may have altered the local immune environment in the eye, potentially creating conditions favorable for CMV reactivation. Romani [10] described how fungal infections can modulate host immune responses, and the concept of “trained immunity” in innate immune cells could play a role in altering the response to subsequent viral challenges [11]. Additionally, recent research by Doan et al. [12] suggests that disturbances to the ocular microbiome due to the initial infection and treatment can favor CMV reactivation.

Despite the challenges of this case, the final visual outcome was remarkably favorable. The patient’s vision fluctuated significantly—from hand motion due to candida endophthalmitis, improving post-vitrectomy, then worsening with CMV retinitis, before ultimately recovering to 20/40. This nonlinear trajectory underscores the potential for visual recovery in severe, consecutive intraocular infections when managed aggressively. The improvement from hand motion to 20/40, despite setbacks, highlights the retina’s resilience and the importance of persistent, tailored treatment. However, it also emphasizes the need for long-term follow-up in these complex cases, as recurrent infections can rapidly reverse initial visual gains.

This case report provides valuable insights into managing sequential, rare intraocular infections in immunocompromised patients, highlighting the importance of vigilant clinical assessment and aggressive treatment. While the detailed documentation of the clinical course and favorable outcome are strengths of this report, the lack of microbiological confirmation and limited follow-up period are notable limitations. Nonetheless, this case contributes to the literature by illustrating the complexities of managing such challenging scenarios in clinical practice.

Conclusion

This case provides valuable insights into the management of sequential severe and atypical intraocular infections in immunocompromised patients. It highlights the importance of early diagnosis, aggressive treatment, and the utilization of advanced imaging techniques in guiding therapeutic decisions. Furthermore, it underscores the need for a multidisciplinary approach in managing these complex cases, involving close collaboration between retinal specialists, infectious disease experts, and immunologists.
