Systematic interrogation of drug sensitivities in melanoma reveals potent synergistic and antagonistic drug combinations with translational potential
Authors: Pilar Ayuda-Durán, Robert Hanes, Leiv Rønneberg, Tine Norman Alver, Geir Frode Øy, Sissel Hauge, Aram N. Andersen, Eivind Valen Egeland, Siri Juell, Sigve Nakken, Theophilus Quachie Asenso, Manuela Zucknick, Randi G. Syljuåsen, Gunhild M. Mælandsmo, Eivind Hovig, Jorrit M. Enserink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTargeted therapies have become mainstream anticancer treatments, although their efficacy is limited by low response rates, drug resistance, and therapy-related toxicities. Drug combinations have the potential to overcome these challenges. However, identifying effective drug combinations is a major challenge.MethodsFocusing on melanoma, we systematically interrogated drug-drug interactions to identify synergistic and antagonistic drug combinations. We screened 23 melanoma and 2 melanocyte cell lines with a library of 61 drugs belonging to 11 different drug classes.ResultsWe have identified multiple antagonistic and synergistic drug interactions. For instance, the CDK inhibitor abemaciclib strongly reduces the antiproliferative effect of nucleosides. Interestingly, this antagonistic interaction can be converted into a potent synergistic interaction by modifying the sequence of treatment; treating cells first with abemaciclib followed by nucleoside treatment results in antagonism, whereas pretreatment with nucleoside analogs followed by abemaciclib results in strong synergy. Furthermore, strong synergy is observed between combinations of Chk1 inhibitors with nucleoside analogs, especially in cell lines with defects in DNA damage response pathways, such as mutations in TP53 or CDKN2A. A combination of low doses of a Chk1 inhibitor and a nucleoside analog also synergizes in a mouse model of melanoma using female mice and significantly reduces tumor growth without noticeable toxic side effects.ConclusionsTaken together, our study underscores the importance of careful design of treatment sequence algorithms and identifies actionable drug-drug interactions for treatment of melanoma with translational potential.

Introduction

The development of targeted therapy has had a profound impact on the treatment of many types of cancer. One example is melanoma, a malignant disease that often involves constitutive activation of the Ras-Raf-MEK-ERK pathway, such as mutations in NRAS and BRAF1,2. Several drugs have been developed that inhibit components of the Ras pathway, such as the Raf inhibitors vemurafenib and dabrafenib, and the MEK inhibitors trametinib and cobimetinib3,4. Some of these targeted therapies have demonstrated increased progression-free survival in randomized phase III clinical trials5,6. However, as with most targeted therapies, acquired resistance to these single drug treatments is a major problem, and only a minority of patients showed durable responses5,6. Resistance to targeted therapies can occur due to a variety of reasons, including mutations within the target or in the target’s pathway, overexpression of pathway components or the drug target itself, protective interactions between cancer cells and their microenvironment, and activation of compensatory mechanisms7–9. For instance, reactivation of the MAPK pathway is a commonly observed mechanism for resistance to B-Raf inhibitors10.

The use of combinations of drugs may overcome at least some of these resistance mechanisms and result in more durable responses11,12. One example is the combination of BRAF inhibitors with MEK inhibitors, which can overcome resistance to single-drug targeted therapy13. Similar effects have been observed for many other cancers, such as breast cancer and various hematological cancers14–16. Another potential advantage of drug combinations is that they may reduce dose-limiting single agent toxicity17. Thus, survival rates and quality of life of cancer patients may be significantly improved by treatment with drug combinations.

On the other hand, systematically identifying antagonistic drug interactions, where one drug suppresses the effect of another drug, would also provide useful information and may prevent treatment of patients with drug combinations that reduce overall survival12.

Given the large number of potential drug combinations, identifying and analyzing drug combinations with synergistic and antagonistic effects is a major challenge18. To be able to accurately identify synergistic and antagonistic drug interactions, it is important to empirically generate high quality datasets that can be used to train bioinformatics models. Molecularly annotated cancer cell lines are commonly used for this purpose19–25. Given the sheer number of potential drug combinations, most studies have implemented strategies to reduce the number of treatments in the screen, such as equimolar ray designs, anchored approaches, and line designs20,21. Although this limits the number of treatments and thereby the required resources, the use of reduced designs can result in relatively high false discovery rates. For instance, identification of synergistic drug combinations in preclinical studies is often studied only at a relatively narrow range of drug concentrations centered on the midpoint (IC50) of a sigmoidal dose–response curve; however, at these drug concentrations, the target coverage is incomplete, and drug responses are suboptimal12. Screens aimed at identifying synergistic drug combinations will therefore yield limited insight if the drug concentrations fall outside the active range. This is particularly problematic if curve fitting strategies are applied to impute IC50 values using drug response data that are located far outside the active concentration range of a given drug, which is not uncommon in large-scale screens involving multiple cell line panels derived from different cancer types, each with potentially very different drug sensitivities. Technical variability is another problem that limits the reproducibility of high-throughput drug screens, especially if large cell line panels derived from different cancer types and very large numbers of drug combinations are tested26–28.

To overcome these challenges, in this study, we have generated a full matrix drug combination dataset focused on a single cancer type, melanoma. This reveals a range of drug interactions, including a synthetic lethal drug interaction between Chk1 inhibitors and nucleoside analogs in cells with a compromised DNA damage response, which we have validated in vivo. In contrast, CDK4/6 inhibitors strongly antagonize the effect of nucleosides, although we demonstrate that this antagonistic effect can be converted into strong synergy depending on the order of sequential single-drug treatments.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culturing of cells

The melanoma cell lines used in this study were provided by the Department of Tumor Biology, Institute for Cancer Research, Oslo University Hospital, Oslo, Norway. Cell line information is described in Supplementary Data 1. Mutational status of the cell lines was derived from Cellosaurus29 or from internal analysis: HM1930, Melmet1, and Melmet531, MM382, and MM38832. The cell and drug screening work was covered by Regional Ethics Committee approval REK 2015/1012.

All cell lines were grown at 37 °C, 5% CO2, 95% humidity, and regularly tested for mycoplasma infection (LT07-705, Lonza).

All the cell lines, except MM382 and MM388 (as they are newly established from melanoma lymph node metastases after informed consent32 under a project approved by the Regional Committee for Medical Research Ethics of South-East Norway, approval number 2014/2208, 2015/2434, see Supplementary Data 1), were authenticated by the provider parties in the genotyping core facility at the Oslo University Hospital, Oslo, Norway using the following protocol as stated in the reports:

DNA from 10⁶ to 10⁷ cells was extracted using the Qiagen MagAttract DNA Mini M48 Kit by an automated procedure on the BioRobot M48. The PowerPlex 16 kit (Promega, Nerliens Meszansky) was used to amplify the thirteen core CODIS short tandem repeat (STR) loci plus Penta E, Penta D, and the gender-determining locus, amelogenin. Alleles were determined by capillary electrophoresis on a MegaBACE 1000, followed by data analysis with the software GeneMarker HID. Appropriate positive and negative controls were used throughout the analysis.

Additionally, the cell lines A375, MeWo, Skmel28, and WM1366 were authenticated a second time by Eurofins Genomics using this protocol, as stated in the reports. In brief, DNA isolation was carried out from cell pellets. Genetic characteristics were determined by PCR-single-locus-technology. 16 independent PCR-systems D8S1179, D21S11, D7S820, CSF1PO, D3S1358, TH01, D13S317, D16S539, D2S1338, AMEL, D5S818, FGA, D19S433, vWA, TPOX and D18S51 were investigated. In parallel, positive and negative controls were carried out, yielding correct results.

The cell lines were cultured in RPMI 1640 GlutaMAX medium (61870044, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (F7524, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (15276355, Gibco) except for Hermes 3C and Hermes 4C, which were cultured in 254 medium (M254500, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (F7524, Sigma-Aldrich), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (15276355, Gibco), 200 nM phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate (TPA), (P8139, Sigma-Aldrich), 200 pM (Hermes 3C) and 2 pM (Hermes 4C) cholera toxin (C8052, Sigma-Aldrich), 10 ng/ml human stem cell factor (hSCF) (15319100, Invitrogen) and 10 nM endothelin (EDN1) (E7764, Sigma-Aldrich)33

Drug sensitivity screening

Drug screening was essentially performed as previously described34. The drugs were purchased from different vendors (Supplementary Data 2) as powders of >98% purity and dissolved at the recommended maximum concentration. The drug stock solutions were dissolved in DMSO (5895690100, Sigma-Aldrich) except Chloroquine, which was dissolved in sterile Milli-Q water. The drug libraries were pre-dispensed in white 1536-well plates with opaque bottom (CLS3727-50EA, Corning) using acoustic droplet ejection technology (LABCYTE Echo acoustic dispensers) at the High-Throughput Chemical Biology Screening Platform, University of Oslo, or at the High Throughput Biomedicine Unit, University of Helsinki.

Before the screening, we determined the optimal seeding density for each cell line analyzing growth curves with the live-cell imaging system Incucyte® S3 (Sartorius AG) (Supplementary Data 1). Cells were grown for at least two passages, treated with Trypsin (85450C, Merck), filtered with a 40 µm strainer (732–2757, VWR), counted in a Countess automated cell counter (Invitrogen), and placed in a single-cell suspension at the appropriate concentration. 5 µl of cell suspension was seeded per well directly into the pre-drugged plates using a Certus-Flex liquid handling device (Fritz Gyger AG), and the plates were covered with Breathe-Easy® sealing membranes (Z380059, Sigma-Aldrich) to avoid evaporation. The plates were incubated as described previously34, to ensure an even temperature distribution. After a 72 h incubation, the plates were equilibrated at room temperature for at least 30 min and cell viability was estimated measuring ATP levels with the bioluminescent CellTiter-Glo 2.0 cell viability assay (G9243, Promega) as CPS (counts per second, which refers to the number of individual photons detected per second) with the EnVision® 2105 multimode plate reader.

Drug screen design and set up

Drug combination screens were set up using the screenwerk pipeline35. First, active drug concentration ranges were determined by dose-finding single drug screens, which were carried out at five 10-fold serial dilutions ranging from 1 nM to 10,000 nM in single replicates.

For the drug combination screen, drugs were dispensed both as single drug treatments and as drug combination treatments, where each drug was combined with every other drug. Redundant permutations of combinations were removed, leading to unique drug pairs. Drugs were combined at four concentrations ranging from 10 to 90 percent of its maximum inhibitory effect, denoted by the IC10 and IC90, respectively. Single-drug treatments were dispensed with two additional concentrations (one lower and one higher) to allow for more accurate curve fitting. Single-drug treatments were dispensed with three replicates, while drug combinations were tested as single replicates.

Eight positive and negative controls were dispensed to each plate, in addition to a set of untreated controls, where neither a drug nor any other chemical substance was dispensed. The positive control consisted of 100 μM benzethonium chloride (BzCl) (sc-239299, Santa Cruz Biotech) and the negative control of 0.2% DMSO (5895690100, Sigma-Aldrich). All drug treatments were randomized across a set of 23 plates. The outer wells were excluded to avoid inaccurate results that may occur due to evaporation.

The plate layout was exported as dispensing instructions for the LABCYTE Echo 550 acoustic dispenser. After drug response measurement as CPS (counts per second), the dataset representing the dispensing layout was consolidated with the readout based on CPS to associate each measurement with the corresponding drug treatment or control.

Data processing and analysis

Both single-drug and drug combination screens were processed, analyzed, and visualized with screenwerk version 0.2.0-1 running on R version 3.6.0 (2019-04-26) [R Core Team (2021) R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria. https://www.R-project.org]. Quality control analysis was performed using screenwerk by determining the variance and signal distribution between individual controls. The Z’ factor was used as a measure of assay performance36. Raw measurements of the positive and negative control were used to calculate the Z’ factors for each plate. The Z’ cutoff was set at 0.5, with an average Z’ score of 0.73 and a median Z’ score of 0.76 across all samples. Raw measurements (CPS, counts per second) were normalized to median positive (BzCl) and median negative (DMSO) controls using the equation: (drug – positive control)/(negative control – positive control). Relative IC50 was calculated using a four-parameter logistic function37 based on non-linear regression analysis using “drc”38 R package version 3.0-1. Area under the concentration curve (AUC) was calculated using a linear trapezoidal summation rule. Drug interactions, such as synergy and antagonism, were estimated using bayesynergy39. Heatmaps were generated with “ComplexHeatmap” R package version 2.2.040,41. Hierarchical clustering was performed using the complete linkage method with a Euclidean distance-based similarity measure based on the area under the curve (AUC) for the clustering of single drug responses, while for the clustering of standardized synergy and antagonism scores, the absolute median volume under the surface (VUS) was used.

Drug interaction prediction and data processing with bayesynergy: The data was processed using version 2.5.1 of the bayesynergy R package using a new robust model formulation (see Supplementary Text). All other prior distributions, hyperparameter choices, and Markov chain Monte Carlo settings were taken at their default values39. Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical significance was tested using Welch’s two-sided Student’s t-test unless stated otherwise. For mouse experiments, statistical analysis was performed as follows. Tumor growth was modeled as a function of time using a linear mixed effects model, taking log tumor volume as the response and accounting for repeated measurements. For fixed effects, we included time, treatment group (untreated, rabusertib, cytarabine, combination), and their interaction, taking as baseline the untreated group. Random intercepts were included to account for the nested structure of the experiment—tumor site within animal. Model fit was evaluated via residual plots stratified by group and Q-Q-plots, with no substantial deviations observed. P-values for the fixed effects were obtained via Satterthwaite’s approximation as implemented in the R package “lmerTest” (version 3.1-3); analysis was conducted in R (version 4.4.3, 2025-02-28).

To assess reproducibility of the drug screening results across the dataset, we run three experimental replicates of the same cell line, MeWo, and we analyzed all 1830 drug combinations from the MeWo dataset using the bayesynergy R package. For each replicate, we extracted the posterior mean response from the Bayesian model. Pairwise reproducibility between replicates was quantified using Pearson’s correlation coefficient, computed on the posterior mean responses across all combinations. In the Live-cell microscopy experiments in Figs. 4B and 5D, E the p-values are not adjusted for multiple comparisons and only tests the difference of endpoints.

Live-cell microscopy experiments

Live-cell imaging experiments were conducted in an Incucyte® S3 instrument (Sartorius). Cell death was assessed using Incucyte® Cytotox Green Dye (4633, Sartorius) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded at the appropriate density in black-walled, clear-bottom 96-well plates (353219 Falcon, Corning) and incubated overnight (16–24 h) to allow attachment. The day of the experiment drug solutions were prepared in fresh medium containing Incucyte® Cytotox Green Dye and dispensed manually into a 96-well plate according to a predefined treatment layout. For each biological replicate, we included three technical replicates per treatment, except for cytarabine single-drug treatments in Fig. 5D and Supplementary Fig. S6B, where there was only sufficient space on the multiwell plates for two technical replicates.

At time zero, after cell attachment for 16–24 h, the cell plate was scanned with the Incucyte® S3 (Sartorius), the medium removed, and the medium containing the drugs added using a PLATEMASTER® (Gilson), enabling simultaneous administration of all treatments. The plate was returned into the Incucyte® S3 and scanned every 3 h. For sequential treatment experiments, following the 24 h scan, the plate was removed, washed twice with fresh medium, and new treatments were added using the PLATEMASTER®. The plate was then returned into the Incucyte® S3 for continued imaging until the 48 h timepoint. Image analysis and quantification were conducted using the Incucyte® analysis software.

Flow cytometry

For DNA profiles and γH2Ax analysis, we run three independent experiments in different days. Cells were fixed in ice-cold 70% ethanol and stained with mouse anti-phospho-H2Ax (Ser139) at 1:500 (05-636, Millipore; Clone JBW301 Lot: 4156777), followed by Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-mouse (A21202, Invitrogen, Lot: 2563848) or Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-mouse (A-21235, Invitrogen, Lot 1786359) secondary antibodies at 1:1000, and the DNA stain Hoechst 33258 (1.5 μg/ml) or FxCycle Far Red (200 nM FxCycle and 0.1 mg/ml RNase A) (F10348, Invitrogen). Flow cytometry analysis was performed on a LSRII flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and using FlowJo software version 10.

Mouse xenograft analysis

Cell lines

The Melmet5 cell line is derived from melanoma lymph node metastases from a patient treated at the Oslo University Hospital, The Norwegian Radium Hospital, Oslo, Norway, and established as described previously31.

Animal ethics

Ethical approval for the study, project license number FOTS29842, was given by the Norwegian Food Safety Authority, according to the Norwegian National Legislation on animal experimentation. All procedures and experiments involving animals were carried out in accordance with institutional guidelines, national legislation, and the European Union Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. Personnel handling the animals were trained and educated following the described functions A/B/C in article 23 of Directive 2010/63/EU7, equivalent to the FELASA (Federation of Laboratory Animal Science Associations) category B/C.

Mouse strain, husbandry, and environmental parameters

Female Hsd: Athymic Nude-Foxn1nu mice were locally bread at the Department of Comparative Medicine at Oslo University Hospital—The Norwegian Radium Hospital, Oslo, Norway, from a breeding stock licensed from Envigo (US). Only female mice were used to reduce aggression-related stress, limit stress-induced physiological variability, and promote more consistent tumor growth patterns across groups, thereby reducing variability unrelated to treatment. The mice were housed in Eurostandard type III cages placed in shelves behind plexiglass curtains. A minimum of two and a maximum of eight mice were housed in each cage. The mice were fed ad libitum “Rat and Mouse No.3 Breeding pellets” (Special Diet Services, UK) and given water acidified to pH 3. All cages were provided with Aspen wood bedding (Tapvei, Estonia), nesting paper, paper rodent homes for shelter, and wooden sticks for chewing. The cages were routinely changed once per week, water twice per week, and food refilled when needed. The environmental parameters in the animal rooms were as follows; temperature 22 °C (±1 °C), humidity 62% (±5%), 12 h light cycle with 70 lux at day time and 1 lux at night time and changing of air every 15 h.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the resource equation method as previously described43, resulting in a group size of six mice per group (24 mice total). In addition, to minimize the risk of an insufficient number of animals due to potential attrition, six extra mice were included, giving a total of 30 mice.

Tumor cell inoculation and tumor growth

Melmet5 cells were cultivated in Gibco RPMI1640 medium w/GlutaMax (Life Technologies) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (Sigma-Aldrich). The cells were grown to 80% confluency, washed once with dPBS (VWR) at room temperature, before being detached by adding Gibco 0.05% Trypsin-EDTA solution (Life Technologies) pre-heated to 37 °C. Detached cells were resuspended in dPBS (VWR), and the number of viable cells was determined using 0.4% Trypan Blue (NanoEntek) and the Invitrogen Countess 2 automated cell counter (Life Technologies). The cell suspension was centrifuged at 500 × g and resuspended in Gibco RPMI1640 w/GlutaMax at a concentration of 2 × 106 viable cells/0.1 ml and placed on ice. Injection of the tumor cells was done as follows; 30 athymic nude mice were placed in light anesthesia using 5% sevoflurane (Baxter Medical AB) with 3 L O2/minute as auxiliary gas. When the mice were unconscious, they were ear labeled and given a 100 µl subcutaneous (sc) injection of 2 × 106 Melmet5 cells on the right and left lateral flanks. Tumors were allowed to grow before treatment began during a latency period of three weeks, to a volume of approximately 60–130 mm³. This was done to ensure that all animals had clearly established measurable disease. On Day 0, both tumors in each mouse were measured, and mice were assigned to treatment groups so that the average baseline tumor size was comparable across groups (90–100 mm³). This step was taken to avoid differences in starting tumor burden that could influence growth rates and confound interpretation of treatment effects. Tumors that did not establish measurable growth at baseline or became unevaluable during the study were excluded from analysis, as including non-engrafted or irregularly progressing tumors could have distorted group averages. In mice bearing tumors on both flanks, exclusion of one tumor did not automatically result in removal of the entire animal unless both tumors were unevaluable or the animal reached predefined humane endpoint criteria. This approach allowed us to maintain data quality while adhering to ethical standards.

Treatment experiments

The mice were divided into four treatment groups consisting of 5 control mice/9 tumors; 4 rabusertib mice/8 tumors; 4 cytarabine mice/6 tumors, and 5 combination mice/8 tumors. Rabusertib (MedChemExpress) at a concentration of 50 mg/kg were delivered by oral (po) dosing (5 µl/g) in a vehicle solution consisting of 0.5% methyl cellulose w/15% DMSO (Sigma Aldrich), and cytarabine (Accord Healthcare AB) at a concentration of 50 mg/kg were given by Intraperitoneal (ip) injection (10 µl/g) in a 0.9% NaCl solution (Fresenius Kabi). The combination of rabusertib and cytarabine was given at the same concentrations and volumes as the mono-treatments, while the control mice were left untreated. All drugs were given on a 7-day treatment schedule consisting of 5 consecutive days of dosing followed by 2 days without treatment. The treatment schedule was repeated 3 times, for a total of 14 treatments (the last dosing cycle was 4 days long). Tumor volumes were followed twice weekly by measuring tumor diameter with a caliper. The tumor volume was calculated by the formula 0.5 × length × width2. Treatment toxicity was monitored by daily weight measurements and body conditioning assessments. Human endpoints set for the experiments were body weight loss of 10%, development of ulcerating tumors, or if the tumor exceeded 1.4 cm in diameter. The experiment ended 2–6 h after administration of the final treatment, and the mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation. Tumors were subsequently extracted from the mice and divided into two pieces, one snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and the other conserved in 4% Formaldehyde (Sarstedt). Tumor growth curves and body weight curves are presented as relative tumor volume/body weight ± SEM were the tumor volume/body weight at the first day of treatment is set as 1.

Strategies to reduce confounders

Animals were allocated to cages such that each cage contained representatives from all treatment groups. The order of treatment administration was randomized to minimize systematic bias. Tumor measurements were conducted in a randomized order across treatment groups to reduce order-related measurement bias. All animals were housed under identical environmental conditions and handled according to standardized procedures.

The experiments were conducted without blinding. This was not considered necessary because treatment effects were evaluated by standardized caliper measurements of tumor size, which provide objective quantitative data and limit the potential for investigator bias.

Tumors excluded by treatment group

In the untreated group, mouse #23, the left flank tumor failed to establish. In the rabusertib (50 mpk) group, there were no exclusions. In the cytarabine (50 mpk) group, mouse #4, both left and right flank tumors did not develop measurable tumor growth and were excluded; mouse #22, the left flank tumor did not meet inclusion criteria at baseline and was excluded; mouse #24, the right flank tumor did not meet inclusion criteria at baseline and was excluded, and mouse #42, the left flank tumor was identified as two small tumors and was therefore excluded. Finally, in the combination group, mouse #13, the left flank tumor did not develop measurable growth and was excluded, and mouse #35, the left flank tumor failed to establish.

Results

Assembly of the drug library and determining the active range of the drugs

To identify drug-drug interactions, we systematically screened pairwise combinations of 61 drugs at four different concentrations in a full matrix format against a panel of 23 melanoma cell lines and two immortalized melanocyte cell lines (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Data 1). We used CellTiter-Glo as a read-out, which measures relative ATP levels in cell lysates and which is commonly used to monitor cell survival/proliferation34. We started by creating a drug library that included compounds that target a wide range of well-known cancer signaling pathways, including compounds that are directly relevant for melanoma, such as Raf and MEK inhibitors, but also more general anticancer compounds, including PI3K and mTOR inhibitors, TKIs, and several chemotherapeutics (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Data 2). Analysis of single-drug screen responses showed that the drugs were in the active range for most of the cell lines (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. S1, and Supplementary Data 9). Although we initially set out with a library consisting of 64 compounds, we discarded three compounds due to problems with solubility at higher concentrations, i.e., idasanutlin, G007-LK, and MK-8617. Furthermore, we noticed that the GSK3 inhibitor CHIR-98014 often produced inconsistent results, particularly at higher concentrations (Supplementary Fig. S1). This may be due to interference with the CellTiter-Glo assay, because GSK3 is known to negatively affect ATP synthesis45; therefore, treatment of cells with CHIR-98014 may result in changes in cellular ATP levels and thereby interfere with the read-out of the screen. We decided to remove this compound from the dataset.

Having curated the drug library, we performed Hill equation curve fitting to identify active ranges of all the drugs (Supplementary Fig. S2). We noticed that some drugs had little or no effect on cell viability even at high concentrations, such as the glucocorticoid dexamethasone and the LSD1 inhibitor GSK2879552, making it impossible to perform curve fitting and determine IC50 values (Supplementary Fig. S1 and S2). Other compounds reduced relative cell survival to a degree that was similar for most of the cell lines, resulting in a relatively comparable range of IC50 values for each compound. For instance, the IC50 values of DJ34 ranged between 5.4 and 13.3 µM, and for the BTK inhibitor Ibrutinib it ranged between 14.3 and 45.3 µM (Supplementary Fig. S1 and S2 and Supplementary Data 9). However, the majority of drugs exerted effects that ranged substantially between the different cell lines (Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2 and Supplementary Data 9). One example relevant for melanoma is vemurafenib, which had no effect on the immortalized melanocyte cell lines Hermes 3C and Hermes 4C even at the highest tested concentration of 12 µM, whereas it strongly reduced relative cell viability of the BRAF-mutant cell line WM35, resulting in an IC50 value of 120 nM (Supplementary Data 10). Another example is methotrexate, which had no effect at all on several cell lines but strongly inhibited several other cell lines with an IC50 as low as 4 nM (Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2 and Supplementary Data 10). Some compounds even increased proliferation, such as dexamethasone in WM852 cells (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Data 11). These findings clearly demonstrate that screening fixed single-drug concentrations against a drug library (such as the commonly used “anchored” approach) is likely to yield confounding results. For instance, if the fixed single-drug concentration is too high, the drug by itself may kill almost all of the cells, obscuring potential effects of drug combinations. Conversely, if the fixed single-drug concentration is too low, it may be too far outside the active range for drug-drug interactions to be observed. In both cases, there is a substantial risk of generating a large number of type II errors (i.e., false-negative results). To avoid these problems, we decided to use a full-matrix drug combination screening approach.

Systematic identification of drug-drug interactions

To systematically identify drug-drug interactions, we used six single-drug concentrations and four active-range drug combinations in full-matrix format. Using our optimized ex vivo drug sensitivity screening approach for reducing experimental noise34,35, we screened 23 melanoma cell lines and two immortalized melanocytes in a 1536-well plate format (see Supplementary Data 1 for an overview of cell lines). In total, we collected 30,378 datapoints per cell line (Supplementary Data 12) and used data from negative and positive controls, i.e., DMSO and BzCl treatment, respectively, to calculate Z’-factor scores (Supplementary Data 13) to obtain an indication of the overall quality of the screen. Most of the Z’-factor scores were well above 0.5, with only a few plates at 0.3, which is a value commonly used in high-content drug combination screens21,24. Screens for two cell lines had Z’-factor scores below 0.3 (Supplementary Fig. S3A), and therefore, these cell lines were removed from the dataset. The resulting median Z’-factor score of the entire screen was 0.757 ± 0.0772 (Supplementary Fig. S3A), reflecting excellent statistical effect size. Importantly, statistical analysis of three biological drug screening replicates performed on the same cell line demonstrated that the drug screens were highly consistent (Supplementary Fig. S3B).

We processed, analyzed, and visualized the data using screenwerk and bayesynergy to calculate summary measures of drug interaction and efficacy35,39, yielding a large, quality-controlled drug interaction dataset for in-depth analysis.

The vast majority of observed drug-drug interactions are additive

Drug interactions were classified based on a two-step criterion: First, a Bayes factor threshold was used to determine the presence of an interaction effect in the experiment; second, effects exceeding a predefined effect size cutoff were classified accordingly. We arbitrarily defined synergistic and antagonistic interactions as those with a posterior median of volume under the surface (VUS) with an absolute value between 10 and 15 as weak drug interactions; a VUS value between 15 and 20 as moderate interactions; and values over 20 as strong interactions. Antagonistic drug interactions

Systematic identification of antagonistic drug interactions is important because it may reduce attrition in the process of treatment development and avoid testing of harmful drug combinations in clinical studies46. We identified multiple antagonistic drug interactions in our drug screen. In addition to the aforementioned compounds, methotrexate and WP1130, which broadly reduced the effect of many other drugs, we observed particularly strong antagonistic interactions between the CDK4/6 inhibitor abemaciclib and either cladribine or cytarabine, which are nucleoside analogs that interfere with DNA synthesis (Supplementary Fig. S6A). Given the strength of this antagonistic effect of abemaciclib, as well as the relevance of CDK4/6 inhibitor combination therapy for other types of cancer, such as breast cancer47, we decided to explore this antagonistic relationship in more detail. The antagonistic effect of abemaciclib on cytarabine was confirmed by live cell microscopy in which cell proliferation was monitored over time (Supplementary Fig. S6B). A similar antagonistic effect was observed between abemaciclib and methotrexate (Figs. 2A and 3A, 3F, G), which interferes with DNA synthesis by inhibiting de novo thymidine synthesis48. A possible explanation for these antagonistic interactions could be that abemaciclib reduces entry into S phase, which is the phase of the cell cycle where these compounds exert their main cytotoxic effect. Indeed, whereas cytarabine single-drug treatment resulted in accumulation of WM1366 cells in S and G2/M phase, a large proportion of cells remained in G1 phase after treatment with a combination of cytarabine and abemaciclib (Supplementary Fig. S6C, indicated by arrow).

Alternate treatment sequence can switch the antagonistic effect of CDK inhibitors into synergistic drug interactions

Although abemaciclib was originally developed as an inhibitor of CDK4/6, it also strongly inhibits CDK1-cyclin B complexes49. We and others have previously shown that CDK1 activity is important for efficient DNA repair, for S phase checkpoint control, and for recovery from DNA damage50–57. Therefore, we hypothesized that treating cells first with cladribine (to induce DNA damage in S phase) followed by release into abemaciclib to inhibit CDK1 might lead to reduced cell survival.

Synergistic drug interactions

Synergistic drug interactions may be clinically relevant to obtain more durable responses, and, therefore, we explored these interactions in the drug combination screen in more detail. We noticed that synergistic interactions were overrepresented between drugs that either target similar mechanisms or the same pathway, whereas antagonistic drug interactions were underrepresented between drugs that target similar mechanisms or the same pathway (Supplementary Fig. S7A, B). This is reminiscent of large-scale genetic interaction screens in the model organism Saccharomyces cerevisiae58, where negative genetic interactions (equivalent to drug synergy in our drug combination screen) are overrepresented between genes that code for proteins that physically interact or that have co-annotated Gene Ontology terms, whereas positive genetic interactions (equivalent to antagonism) for essential genes tend to have an underrepresentation of gene product pairs that have physical interactions.

One example of an intra-pathway synergistic drug interaction is the combination of everolimus and torin2, which is among the strongest synergistic interactions detected for several cell lines, including FEMX1, WM35, WM266.4, and WM983b (Supplementary Fig. S7C). This synergistic effect may be surprising, given that these compounds both target the kinase mTOR. However, Torin2 is an ATP competitor that binds the ATP pocket of mTOR kinase to inhibit kinase activity, whereas the rapamycin derivative everolimus inhibits mTOR in a non-ATP competitive manner. These non-competing mechanisms of mTOR inhibition may explain the non-additive effect on cell viability; similar synergistic effects have also been reported for non-competing EGFR inhibitors59. In-depth analysis of such synergistic drug interactions will be the focus of follow-up studies.

Another strong synergistic effect was observed between LY2603618 (rabusertib) and either cladribine or cytarabine (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S6A). Rabusertib is an inhibitor selective for the DNA damage checkpoint kinase Chk160. Interestingly, although additivity or synergy between rabusertib and either cladribine or cytarabine was observed in 18 cell lines, synergistic interactions were particularly strong in the cell lines SKMEL28 and A375 (Fig. 5A), which have mutations in TP53 or CDKN2A, respectively (Fig. 3A). Strong synergy was also observed in WM1366 and WM45.1 cells (Fig. 5A), which carry mutations in both TP53 and CDKN2A. CDKN2A encodes the proteins p14ARF and p16INK4a, which function as an indirect activator of p53 and an inhibitor of CDK4, respectively (Fig. 5B)61. Of note, the combination of the Chk1 inhibitor with nucleoside analogs mostly resulted in additivity in cells that do not harbor mutations in TP53 or CDKN2A, such as FEMXI cells (see below). To our initial surprise, the Chk1 inhibitor also synergized with nucleoside analogs in the non-cancerous cell lines Hermes 3C and Hermes 4C (Supplementary Fig. S8). However, closer inspection of these cell lines revealed that they were created by immortalization with TERT and human papillomavirus type 16 proteins E6 and E762. E6 induces p53 degradation63, whereas E7 disrupts the interaction between Rb and E2F64, thus rendering cells refractory to checkpoint control and explaining the sensitivity of these cell lines to combinations of the Chk1 inhibitor with nucleoside analogs.

We validated the synergy between DNA damaging agents and Chk1 inhibitors using a wider drug concentration range matrix and structurally unrelated Chk1 inhibitors, i.e. LY2606368/prexasertib (which also inhibits Chk2) and SCH900776/MK-8776, using FEMXI cells (wild-type TP53 and CDKN2A, Supplementary Fig. S9A–I); A375 cells (mutant CDKN2A but wild-type TP53, Supplementary Fig. S9J–R); and WM45.1, WM1366 and Melmet5 cells (mutations in both TP53 and CDKN2A, Supplementary Figs. S10A–R and S11A–I). These experiments yielded comparable results as observed in the drug combination screen. Together, these data suggest that cells with defects in TP53 and CDKN2A activity rely on Chk1to maintain viability during DNA damage (Fig. 5B), such that the combination of DNA damaging agents with Chk1 inhibitors may induce a synthetic lethal effect.

We used IncuCyte® live cell microscopy and flow cytometry to monitor the effect on cell viability of combining rabusertib with nucleoside analogs (see Fig. 5C for a schematic treatment scheme). Single-drug treatment of cells with rabusertib had a minor effect on cell viability compared to the DMSO control (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, while a substantial number of cladribine-treated cells showed elevated levels of γH2Ax compared to DMSO treatment (Supplementary Fig. S12B, compare panels b and f), this did not result in increased levels of cell death, indicating that cells mounted a prosurvival DNA damage response. In stark contrast, continuous treatment with a combination of cladribine and rabusertib resulted in a strong increase in cell death (Fig. 5E). These cells appeared to be mostly in very early S phase (Supplementary Fig. S12B, panel g), with γH2Ax levels that were considerably higher compared to cladribine single-drug treatment (Supplementary Fig. S12B, compare panels f and h). We also a observed a small population of cells with sub-G1 DNA content, which is indicative of activation of apoptosis65. These findings indicate that cells strongly rely on the Chk1 pathway to maintain cell viability in the presence of low concentrations of cladribine.

We also tested the effect of sequential treatments on cell viability, and found that treating cells first with rabusertib followed by treatment with cladribine did not increase cell death compared to either single-drug treatments (Fig. 5E), and γH2Ax levels were slightly lower than in cells treated continuously with the combination of these compounds (Supplementary Fig. S12, compare panels h and j). However, treating cells first with cladribine followed by treatment with rabusertib resulted in a rate of cell death (as indicated by the slope of the curve) that was comparable to continuous treatment with the drug combination (Fig. 5E). This treatment resulted in a mostly S-phase cell population with elevated γH2Ax levels compared to cladribine single-drug treatment (Supplementary Fig. S12B, compare panels f and l), as well as a substantial number of cells with sub-G1 DNA content (Supplementary Fig. S12B, panel k).

We performed a similar set of experiments with the combination of rabusertib and cytarabine. The effects of this drug combination on cell survival were comparable to those observed with the combination of cladribine and rabusertib (Fig. 5D), although we did notice that the effect of cytarabine appeared to occur later during S phase than cladribine, which was particularly clear for cells continuously treated with a combination of cytarabine and rabusertib (Supplementary Fig. S12B, panel o). This indicates that even though cladribine and cytarabine are both nucleoside analogs (Supplementary Fig. S12C), their mechanism of action may not be identical.

Taken together, the combination of Chk1 inhibitors with nucleoside analogs induces a synergistic effect in melanoma cells, particularly in cells with a compromised DNA damage response pathway. These data also further underscore the importance of careful design of sequential drug treatment algorithms.

Synergy between cytarabine and Chk1 inhibitors in a xenograft model of melanoma

Finally, we used a mouse xenograft model to test whether the combination of DNA damaging agents with Chk1 inhibitors is also synergistic in vivo. We used Melmet5 cells, which are derived from lymph node-metastasized melanoma, harboring mutations in TP53 and CDKN2A31. The rationale for choosing these cells is that (i) they carry mutations in TP53; (ii) they readily form tumors in mice; and (iii) the combination of DNA damaging agents with Chk1 inhibitors is moderately synergistic in these cells (Supplementary Fig. S11), which may avoid biased interpretation of results that might occur when a cell line was used in which this drug combination is highly synergistic. For combination treatment, we decided to use cytarabine and rabusertib. The rationale for choosing cytarabine is that this compound has been used in the past to treat melanoma patients, although response rates were modest66; this reflects the findings of our study that indicate that cytarabine needs to be combined with a Chk1 inhibitor to increase its potency, and that only a subset of patients may be responsive to these drugs depending on TP53/CDKN2A status. We selected rabusertib because it has reduced risk of cardiac toxicity compared to earlier Chk1 inhibitors60.

After formation of tumors, mice were either mock treated or treated with very low concentrations of either rabusertib or cytarabine alone, or with a combination of these two drugs (see Fig. 6A for treatment scheme). Toxicity tests showed that all treatment regimens were well tolerated with no toxicity to the mice (Supplementary Fig. S13). More importantly, whereas treatment with either single compound had no effect at all on tumor size, treatment of mice with the drug combination resulted in a statistically significant reduction in tumor growth (Fig. 6B). Treating the mice with rabusertib or cytarabine alone did not significantly alter this growth rate (log-scale estimates of 0.003 (p = 0.39) and −0.002 (p = 0.65) for rabusertib and cytarabine, respectively). Discussion

In this study, we used active-range drug concentrations to perform a full-matrix drug combination screen on a panel of melanoma cells. Quality control analysis of the data revealed an average Z’ score of 0.73 and a median Z’ score of 0.76, and biological replicates showed that the screening data were highly reproducible. Therefore, we expect that this dataset will be useful not only for melanoma researchers but also for scientists interested in the development of statistical methods to analyze drug combination data. Our study shows that strong synergistic interactions between drugs are rare and that most compounds have additive effects, mirroring findings of recent large-scale drug combination studies21.

Hierarchical clustering of combination response profiles did not yield robust segregation by common driver alterations, such as BRAF, NRAS, and PTEN. One reason for this could be the limited number of lines per genotype and within-genotype heterogeneity, which may reduce power to resolve discrete mutation-defined clusters. However, it is also possible that drug-drug interaction phenotypes are not caused by single mutations alone, but rather the consequence of multiple factors that include mutations, baseline pathway activity, cell-state programs, cell-cycle checkpoint integrity, and DNA damage response capacity. Stratification by pathways or cell states, such as MAPK/PI3K activity scores, DNA damage response, transcriptional programs, or proteomic/phosphoproteomic signatures, may better capture the biology underlying combination sensitivity than mutational status alone. Indeed, we have recently shown in Acute Myeloid Leukemia that ex vivo drug sensitivity signatures and phosphoproteomics better capture drug responses and patient outcome than mutational status of the cancer cells67,68.

Patients with advanced stages of melanoma are typically treated with immunotherapy, including ipilimumab or pembrolizumab, or with targeted therapy, such as the Raf inhibitors dabrafenib and vemurafenib or the MEK inhibitors trametinib and cobimetinib. While checkpoint inhibitor therapy has revolutionized melanoma treatment69, survival of patients who have progressed on immunotherapy, or with advanced or unresectable melanoma, is poor, and new treatment options are needed70. The fact that we identified several highly synergistic combinations of nucleoside analogs with Chk1 inhibitors may be relevant for this hard-to-treat patient population. Synergy was particularly strong in cell lines with mutations in genes involved in the DNA damage response, such as TP53, as well as mutations in CDKN2A. Given that survival of DNA damage involves activation of p53 via the Chk2 branch of the DNA damage response, treating TP53-mutant cell lines with Chk1 inhibitors may result in synthetic lethality when cells are confronted with exogenous DNA damage (Fig. 5B). TP53 and CDKN2A mutations are associated with poor prognosis and shorter survival of melanoma patients, which is at least partially due to increased resistance to chemotherapy of TP53- and CDKN2A-mutated tumors71.

Our study provides a rationale for co-treatment with Chk1 inhibitors to sensitize these tumors to chemotherapy. The concentrations that we used for validation experiments (rabusertib 2 µM with cytarabine 0.03 µM; rabusertib 2 µM with cladribine 0.1 µM) are within or below published systemic exposure ranges for the individual agents. For instance, for rabusertib, the recommended phase II dose is 230 mg, which achieves geometric mean Cmax values of ~3170–3410 ng/mL (approximately 7.3–7.8 µM)72,73. Cytarabine dosing achieves steady-state plasma concentrations generally in the ~0.1–0.5 µM range, with substantially higher transient peaks reported for high-dose schedules74. Thus, the cytarabine concentration at which we observed synergistic effects (0.03 µM) is conservative relative to typical therapeutic exposures. For cladribine, plasma concentrations in leukemia patients have been reported to exceed 0.2 µM, whereas intracellular concentrations of cladribine triphosphate (the active metabolite) can be orders of magnitude higher75. Thus, treatment with these synergistic drug combinations should be clinically feasible, although formal PK/PD-linked translational modelling will be essential to refine clinically actionable dosing schedules for these combinations and to identify potential side effects.

Another important finding of our study is the significance of designing drug combination treatment algorithms that maximize synergy. For example, simultaneous treatment with CDK inhibitors and nucleoside analogs resulted in a strong reduction in the cytotoxicity of the nucleosides. Pretreatment with CDK inhibitors followed by treatment with nucleoside analogs also had little effect on the cells. However, sequential treatment with nucleoside analogs followed by CDK inhibitors resulted in strong synergistic cell killing. Similar observations have been made in pancreatic adenocarcinoma cells that were sequentially treated with taxanes and CDK4/6 inhibitors76. These findings may have clinical relevance, because several clinical trials are testing combinations of CDK4/6 inhibitors with chemotherapy77. For most of these studies, the rationale for combining a CDK4/6 inhibitor (most notably trilaciclib) with chemotherapy is that it can serve as a myelopreservative that protects the hematopoietic system against the detrimental effects of chemotherapy78. However, some studies also analyzed the effect of these drug combinations on overall survival. Phase II clinical trials found little or no effect of this combination on patient survival, although most of them were not randomized or placebo-controlled and only included small numbers of patients. In contrast, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase III trial was recently conducted that evaluated the effect of trilaciclib on anti-tumor efficacy when administered prior to a regimen consisting of chemotherapeutic drugs with bevacizumab in patients with colorectal cancer (PRESERVE 1, NCT04607668; registered on 20-10-2020 at ClinicalTrials.gov). Importantly, this trial was terminated when it was found that patients who received trilaciclib prior to chemotherapy fared worse than patients who received placebo prior to chemotherapy79. This reflects the finding by us and others that CDK4/6 inhibitors can antagonize the efficacy of chemotherapy76. We believe that careful preclinical optimization of drug combination treatment algorithms can help optimize the design of clinical studies that test drug combinations to thereby improve the efficacy of drug combination treatment. Indeed, it has been argued previously that careful analysis of antagonistic drug interactions may avoid cross-resistance during cancer treatment12.

Our study has a number of limitations. For instance, combinations with immunotherapeutic drugs, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, could not be tested in our cell line model. Given that immunotherapy is an important form of therapy for melanoma patients, future drug combination studies should take into account the effect of the immune system. Furthermore, we were interested in modeling drug interactions between compounds that target a wide array of cancer pathways, but this broad approach likely includes a number of compounds that ultimately may prove not to be clinically relevant for melanoma. Another potential limitation is that the screen was performed by adding cell suspensions to pre-printed drug plates, and we cannot exclude the possibility that some of the compounds exerted their effect by interfering with cell adhesion rather than cell proliferation/survival. Furthermore, we only assessed drug effects at a single 72 h endpoint, whereas time-course assays might better capture delayed toxicity, adaptation, or recovery phenomena that may influence the interpretation of synergy versus antagonism. Finally, while several of the melanoma cell lines were obtained from patients diagnosed at Oslo University Hospital, we were not able to validate our findings on primary patient material. The reason for this is that biopsies are only taken at our hospital after initiation of therapy (typically immunotherapy); consequently, the number of viable cancer cells in these biopsies is insufficient to allow for drug combination testing ex vivo. Despite these drawbacks, we believe our study provides multiple starting points for follow-up studies, not only for melanoma but also for other cancer types, and we are currently in the process of expanding our drug combination studies to include breast cancer, ovarian cancer, and hematological cancers, including acute myeloid leukemia.
