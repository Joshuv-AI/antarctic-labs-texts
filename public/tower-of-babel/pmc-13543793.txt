MAPK-driven MYC/p21 axis controls senescence bypass and BCL-XL dependence in vincristine-resistant rhabdomyosarcoma
Authors: Yadong Wang, Andrea D. Largent, Dorothy Lee, Paulomi Modi, Long Wang, Anshu Bandhlish, Myron Ignatius, Eleanor Y. Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTherapy resistance remains the primary cause of treatment failure in rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS), the most common pediatric soft tissue sarcoma, yet underlying mechanisms are poorly understood. Using vincristine-resistant fusion-negative RMS models, RNA sequencing, genetic perturbations, and a syngeneic zebrafish tumor model, we demonstrate that resistance is driven by enhanced MAPK signaling, validated in post-chemotherapy patient specimens. MEK inhibition induced senescence through an MYC-p21 axis; MYC represses CDKN1A transcription, and its overexpression bypasses senescence. Resistant cells exhibited BCL-XL dependence, and combined MEK/BCL-XL inhibition eradicated resistant tumors in vitro and in vivo. These findings reveal that enhanced MAPK signaling and MYC-mediated senescence bypass are candidate resistance-associated mechanisms in fusion-negative RMS with implications across cancer types, and identify targetable vulnerabilities (MEK and BCL-XL), providing preclinical rationale for treatment-refractory RMS.

Introduction

Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is a malignant soft tissue tumor arising from impaired muscle differentiation. It is the most common pediatric soft tissue sarcoma, with approximately 300 cases annually in the United States.1,2 RMS is classified into two major molecular subtypes: fusion-negative (FN) RMS and fusion-positive (FP) RMS. FN RMS, the most prevalent subtype, is driven by mutations in the receptor tyrosine kinase/RAS/PIK3CA signaling axis in over 90% of cases.3 In contrast, FP RMS is defined by a translocation involving PAX3 or PAX7 fused to FOXO1.4,5

Despite aggressive treatment with surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation, outcomes remain poor for patients with relapsed or metastatic disease, with 3-year survival rates of less than 30%.6 Moreover, standard therapies are associated with significant long-term morbidity and secondary malignancies. No new effective treatments have emerged for relapsed RMS over the past three decades, underscoring the urgent need to elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying therapy resistance to develop more effective and less toxic treatment options.

However, the current understanding of chemotherapy resistance in RMS remains limited. Prior studies have shown that RMS cells treated with vincristine or doxorubicin retain an MYOD1-positive population,7 which may contribute to disease persistence. MYOD1, a master regulator of skeletal muscle differentiation, is aberrantly regulated in RMS, leading to repression of myogenic genes and promotion of proliferation.8,9 Other studies have identified elevated anti-apoptotic proteins and activation of survival pathways, such as GP130/STAT and Hedgehog, in therapy-resistant RMS.10,11 These data suggest that therapy resistance is accompanied by dynamic shifts in transcriptional and signaling programs, but the mechanisms enabling the long-term survival of therapy-resistant RMS cells remain incompletely characterized.

We have previously shown that vincristine treatment induces a primitive stem-like phenotype in RMS cells.12 Building on these findings, we investigated the underlying biological mechanisms essential for maintaining long-term survival of therapy-resistant FN RMS cells. Our study identified upregulated MAPK signaling as a key driver of senescence bypass in vincristine-resistant FN RMS. We further demonstrated that the MEK/MYC/p21 axis and its dependence on BCL-xL-mediated survival represent targetable vulnerabilities. These findings suggest that co-targeting MAPK signaling and senescence-survival pathways is a promising therapeutic strategy for treatment-refractory FN RMS.

Results

Increased activity of the MAPK pathway in vincristine-resistant FN RMS cells

To investigate the underlying biological features of vincristine-resistant FN RMS cells, we generated two therapy-resistant FN RMS lines (RD and SMS-CTR) through gradual vincristine dose escalation over 9–12 months, achieving at least a 100-fold increase in IC50 (see schematic in Figure 1A; dose-response curves in Figures S1A and S1B). Vincristine-resistant RD cells were collected for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Pathway and oncogenic signature analyses revealed upregulation of MAPK signaling components, including RAS and RAF (Figure 1B). To validate MAPK pathway activation, we performed immunofluorescence analysis, which showed elevated phosphorylated ERK (pERK) expression in both vincristine-resistant RD and SMS-CTR cells compared with the parental lines (Figure 1C). Similarly, in archival formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor samples from 10 FN RMS patients, including 8 with matched pretreatment samples, residual post-chemotherapy tumors were enriched for pERK-positive cells in 7 of 8 matched pairs (Figures 1D and 1E). Next-generation sequencing data were available for three primary tumors with increased pERK positivity. Two harbored NRAS mutations, and one had wild-type RAS but carried NF1 and PTEN deletions. These findings suggest that MAPK signaling plays a key role in driving therapy resistance in FN RMS.

Therapeutic efficacy of MEK inhibition as monotherapy and in combination with vincristine

Given the activation of MAPK signaling, we assessed whether MEK inhibition could suppress the growth of vincristine-resistant FN RMS cells. Treatment of vincristine-resistant RD and SMS-CTR cells with trametinib, a MEK inhibitor, significantly reduced cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 2A). In contrast, vincristine-resistant Rh30 (FP RMS) cells showed lower sensitivity to trametinib (∼3- to 5-fold increase in IC50) (Figure S1B). Similarly, MEK1 knockdown using two independent shRNAs (Figure 2B) impaired the growth of resistant RD and SMS-CTR cells (Figure 2C). To evaluate whether MEK inhibition enhanced vincristine efficacy in vivo, we used a KRAS (G12D)-induced zebrafish FN RMS model. Primary RMS cells expressing the mylz:mCherry reporter were transplanted into syngeneic CG1 zebrafish, followed by treatment with vehicle (DMSO), trametinib (10 mg/kg), vincristine (0.1 mg/kg), or both drugs in combination for 7 days. The drug doses were titrated to IC50 values in vivo. Combination treatment significantly reduced tumor growth compared with either monotherapy (n = 4–7 per group, Figures 2D and 2E). Apoptosis, assessed via cleaved Caspase-3 immunohistochemistry (IHC), was significantly increased in the combination group compared with that in the single-agent arm (Figures 2F and 2G), supporting enhanced tumor cell death.

To test whether this regimen also prevented relapse, we monitored post-treatment tumor regrowth. By day 17, zebrafish receiving combined vincristine and trametinib showed a significantly lower relapse frequency and slower growth of recurrent tumors than those treated with either drug alone (p < 0.05, Figure 2H). These results indicate that dual targeting of MEK and vincristine may improve the treatment durability in FN RMS.

Next, we evaluated apoptosis using live-cell imaging with NucView 488 Caspase-3 substrate 6 days after shRNA transduction or 3 days of trametinib treatment. Trametinib did not significantly increase apoptosis in either parental or vincristine-resistant RD or SMS-CTR cells compared with DMSO controls (Figures S2A and S2B). While MEK1 shRNA induced a statistically significant increase in apoptosis in vincristine-resistant RD cells compared with parental cells, the effect was modest (mean 1.2% vs. 0.3%, Figures S2C and S2D), suggesting that apoptosis contributes minimally to the reduced cell viability.

Trametinib has previously been shown to induce terminal differentiation of FN RMS cells.13 In agreement with previous findings, trametinib-treated parental RD and SMS-CTR cells exhibited increased numbers of MF20-positive differentiated muscle cells (Figures 3C and 3D). However, trametinib-treated vincristine-resistant RD and SMS-CTR cells failed to undergo terminal differentiation to the same extent, as shown by the reduced MF20 staining (Figures 3C and 3D). Additionally, MYOD1 protein expression decreased in resistant cells following trametinib treatment (Figures 3E and 3F), and quantitative RT-PCR confirmed attenuated induction of MYOD1 and MYOG (20- to 30-fold lower) compared with parental cells (Figure 3G).

Given that trametinib alters the cell cycle without inducing apoptosis or robust differentiation, we investigated whether it also promotes senescence in vincristine-resistant cells. Indeed, trametinib-treated vincristine-resistant RD and SMS-CTR cells showed a significant increase in β-gal-positive senescent cells compared with DMSO-treated controls (Figures 4B, 4C, and S3A). Parental cells did not exhibit this increase, supporting a resistance-associated senescent phenotype.

Next, we investigated whether p21 and p16, which inhibit cell cycle progression, contribute to senescence-associated cell-cycle arrest. Western blotting and immunofluorescence analysis showed that trametinib-treated vincristine-resistant RD and SMS-CTR cells had increased p21 expression but reduced p16 levels (Figures 4D–4H and S3B–S3D). These findings suggest that p21, rather than p16, is the primary driver of trametinib-induced senescence in vincristine-resistant FN RMS cells.

Senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) is associated with senescent cells secreting pro-inflammatory factors that promote cancer relapse and therapy resistance.16,17 Because SASP is highly context-dependent and heterogeneous across cell types and senescence inducers, we evaluated a focused panel of well-characterized SASP markers including CCL2, MMP2, and IL6. We observed downregulation of CCL2 and MMP2, but no significant change in IL6 expression. The attenuated and variable SASP response we observed is consistent with TIS, which frequently exhibits reduced SASP secretion compared with replicative or oncogene-induced senescence (OIS).18 Importantly, these findings indicate that the senescence phenotype induced by MEK inhibition is driven primarily by cell-cycle arrest and sustained growth suppression rather than by a secretory program. A comprehensive SASP profiling including additional time points and a broader range of secreted factors could provide deeper mechanistic insights in future investigations.

MYC represses p21 and suppresses trametinib-induced senescence in FN RMS

MYC is a known downstream effector of MAPK signaling and is a critical regulator of proliferation. We previously showed that MYC promotes the survival of vincristine-resistant FN RMS cells. Based on our observation that trametinib induces senescence and increases p21, we hypothesized that MYC suppresses p21 expression to block senescence.

We first confirmed by Western blotting that trametinib treatment reduced MYC protein levels in RD and SMS-CTR cells (Figures 4D and S3B). MYC protein stability is tightly regulated by phosphorylation at two critical sites: phosphorylation at Ser62 suppresses MYC degradation and stabilizes the protein for transcriptional activity, while phosphorylation at Thr58 triggers ubiquitin-mediated degradation.19,20,21,22,23,24 To determine whether trametinib treatment affects MYC protein stability, we performed cycloheximide chase experiments, which demonstrated similar MYC degradation kinetics between DMSO-treated control and trametinib-treated RD cells over 90 min (Figures S4A and S4B). Furthermore, trametinib treatment did not alter the phosphorylation status of MYC at Ser62 or Thr58 relative to total MYC levels in both RD and SMS-CTR cell lines (Figure S4C). These experiments, demonstrating stable MYC degradation kinetics and unchanged MYC phosphorylation status, rule out post-translational mechanisms as the primary driver of MYC downregulation. Collectively, these findings establish that trametinib-mediated reduction in MYC protein levels occurs through transcriptional suppression rather than altered protein stability or post-translational modification.

Next, we disrupted MYC using lentiviral CRISPR-Cas9 with validated dual gRNA constructs, which we have previously shown to effectively deplete MYC protein in RMS cells.12 MYC disruption led to upregulation of CDKN1A (p21) mRNA, while CDKN2A (p16) levels remained unchanged (Figure 4I), supporting a specific MYC-p21 axis.

To test whether MYC regulates senescence in this context, we overexpressed MYC in RD cells and found that MYC overexpression alleviated trametinib-induced senescence, as measured by the reduced β-gal-positive cell frequency (Figures 4J and 4K). Similarly, MYC overexpression partially abrogated increased expression of p21 in response to trametinib treatment (Figure 4L). MYC has been shown to repress gene expression by interacting with Sp1/Sp3 proteins, which bind to GC-rich sites, including the GGGCGG motif, in the promoter.25 To determine whether MYC suppresses p21 expression in vincristine-resistant FN RMS cells, we performed quantitative CUT&RUN quantitative PCR and showed MYC binding to the promoter region of CDKN1A (encoding p21), and this binding was abrogated by trametinib treatment (Figure 4M). These findings demonstrate that MYC suppresses p21-mediated senescence through transcriptional repression and is required for resistance to trametinib-induced cell-cycle arrest in FN RMS.

Discussion

The MAPK signaling paradox in cancer senescence

The relationship between oncogenic MAPK signaling and cellular senescence is fundamentally paradoxical. In normal human fibroblasts, MEK/MAPK activation induces both p53 and p16, which are required for RAS-induced senescence as a protective mechanism against transformation.27,28,29 Similarly, constitutive MAPK activation induces oncogene-dependent senescence in cancer cells, a well-established tumor suppressive mechanism.17 Yet paradoxically, inhibition of oncogenic MAPK signaling also induces senescence in KRAS-mutant cancers, including lung cancer, pancreatic cancer, and pilocytic astrocytoma.30,31,32 This contradiction suggests that MAPK signaling operates in a dosage-dependent manner: both excessive activation and targeted inhibition can trigger senescence, yet the mechanistic basis for this bidirectional senescence-inducing capacity in therapy-resistant cancers remains poorly understood.

To address this gap in therapy-resistant RMS, we demonstrated using a syngeneic zebrafish tumor model that MEK inhibition induces senescence in vincristine-resistant fusion-negative RMS cells. Critically, this senescence-inducing effect was independent of the specific upstream RAS mutation, as validated by two converging lines of evidence: (1) enhanced pERK was detected in 7 of 8 matched post-chemotherapy patient tumors regardless of RAS genotype and (2) human FN RMS cell lines with distinct genetic backgrounds (NRAS-mutant RD and HRAS-mutant SMS-CTR) both responded to MEK inhibition. These findings establish that enhanced MAPK signaling itself, rather than any single upstream driver, is the critical mechanism promoting vincristine resistance. Notably, the phenotypic response to MEK inhibition diverged dramatically between parental and resistant cells: treatment-naive parental cells underwent myogenic differentiation,13 whereas vincristine-resistant cells exhibited elevated MAPK activity and did not differentiate but instead underwent senescence-associated cell-cycle arrest. This striking divergence reveals that the elevated MAPK activity in resistant cells creates a senescence-prone state in which MEK inhibition paradoxically reinforces growth arrest. Our findings support a dosage-dependent model in which oncogene addiction (parental cells) permits lineage differentiation, whereas enhanced but intermediate MAPK elevation (resistant cells) creates a tipping point favoring senescence over proliferation or differentiation.

The MYC-p21 axis as a gatekeeper of senescence bypass

MYC has a multifaceted relationship with senescence, as it regulates the expression of numerous senescence-associated genes and exerts distinct context-dependent roles in senescence regulation depending on cell type. In normal cells, MYC overexpression can paradoxically trigger OIS through induction of DNA damage and activation of stress response pathways.17,33,34 Conversely, in established tumors, MYC primarily functions as an anti-senescence oncogene by suppressing senescence checkpoints. For instance, in mouse lung tumors, MYC cooperates with mutant BRAF V600E to promote growth specifically through senescence suppression.35 Similarly, in KRAS-mutant pancreatic cancers, targeted ERK inhibition induces therapeutic senescence through MYC degradation,31 linking MYC downregulation to treatment-induced senescence. Notably, how MYC sustains senescence bypass specifically in therapy-resistant cancers remains incompletely understood. In our study, we addressed this mechanistic gap by identifying the MYC-p21 axis as the critical effector mechanism driving senescence bypass in resistant cells. MEK inhibition suppressed MYC expression and elevated the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p21 (CDKN1A), triggering irreversible cell-cycle arrest. Mechanistically, MYC directly repressed CDKN1A transcription by binding to regulatory elements within the CDKN1A promoter, as demonstrated by CUT&RUN chromatin analysis. Loss-of-function studies using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated MYC disruption confirmed that MYC is necessary for senescence bypass, as MYC depletion restored p21 expression and enforced growth arrest upon MAPK inhibition. Complementary gain-of-function studies demonstrated sufficiency: forced overexpression of MYC in trametinib-treated cells abrogated senescence state and p21 induction, providing direct evidence that MYC is sufficient to maintain proliferative capacity despite active MAPK inhibition. These complementary gain-of-function and loss-of-function studies establish MYC as both necessary and sufficient for senescence bypass in vincristine-resistant cells. In addition, the multi-layered control with transcriptional suppression of p21 by MYC, complemented by post-translational antagonism via BCL-XL explains the redundancy of the senescence evasion program and justifies dual targeting of MAPK and anti-apoptotic pathways in therapy-resistant disease.

BCL-XL dependence emerges as a vulnerability in senescence-resistant cells

Beyond the MAPK-MYC-p21 axis, vincristine-resistant cells exploit anti-apoptotic pathways to evade TIS. We demonstrated that resistant cells exhibit significantly increased dependence on the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-XL compared with treatment-naïve parental cells. BCL-XL antagonizes p21-mediated growth arrest by suppressing apoptosis,26 thereby promoting escape from senescence-associated cell-cycle arrest. This heightened BCL-XL dependence reveals that resistant cells employ multiple convergent mechanisms to bypass senescence: elevated MYC suppresses p21 transcription, while elevated BCL-XL antagonizes p21 function. Combined MEK/BCL-XL inhibition demonstrated synergistic suppression of resistant tumor growth both in vitro and in our syngeneic zebrafish model, with combined treatment significantly outperforming either agent as monotherapy. These findings establish that dual targeting of the MAPK pathway and anti-apoptotic mechanisms is necessary to overcome the redundant pro-survival adaptations in treatment-refractory RMS.

Clinical translational considerations and next-generation approaches

The combination of trametinib (FDA-approved MEK inhibitor) and BCL-XL inhibitors represents a promising treatment strategy for treatment-refractory RMS. However, BCL-2/BCL-XL inhibitors have shown treatment-related adverse events in clinical trials, with thrombocytopenia (low platelet count) being the most common.36 Dose optimization, particularly using lower concentrations of BCL-XL inhibitor in combination with trametinib, may reduce toxicity while maintaining efficacy. Additionally, next-generation PROTAC-based BCL-2 and BCL-XL degraders offer promising alternatives by providing improved target selectivity and potentially reduced systemic toxicity through tumor-microenvironment-preferential degradation.37,38

An integrated model of therapy resistance and senescence bypass

Our data support a multi-hit model in which vincristine-resistant FN RMS cells achieve therapy resistance through three coordinated mechanisms: (1) enhanced MAPK signaling drives proliferation and suppresses differentiation, (2) elevated MYC activity suppresses p21 (CDKN1A) transcription, and (3) increased BCL-XL antagonizes p21 function. This explains why monotherapy fails—each addresses only one mechanism while the others sustain resistance. Dual MEK/BCL-XL inhibition is synergistic because it simultaneously disrupts both transcriptional (MYC-CDKN1A) and post-translational (BCL-XL-p21) suppression of the senescence checkpoint, thereby overcoming the redundancy of the multi-hit resistance program.

In conclusion, our findings establish the MEK-MYC-p21-BCL-XL axis as the central regulatory network driving therapy resistance in vincristine-resistant FN RMS. By identifying senescence bypass as a dominant resistance mechanism and defining the molecular vulnerabilities that enable it, we propose a rational therapeutic strategy: simultaneous targeting of MAPK and anti-apoptotic pathways using FDA-approved agents (trametinib and BCL-XL inhibitors) or next-generation degraders. This approach addresses the underlying biology of resistance by restoring senescence-dependent tumor suppression. Given the poor prognosis of treatment-refractory RMS and the lack of effective salvage therapies, these findings have immediate clinical relevance and warrant promising translation into clinical trials for this pediatric malignancy.

Limitations of the study

Single-agent versus combination chemotherapy

Standard RMS therapy combines vincristine with actinomycin D and cyclophosphamide, but our study used single-agent vincristine to isolate drug-specific resistance mechanisms without confounding contributions from multiple agents. This approach enabled precise dissection of vincristine-driven pathways but does not capture the complexity of multi-agent therapy. Future studies examining vincristine combinations will clarify whether the identified MAPK-MYC-p21-BCL-XL axis remains relevant in standard-of-care multi-agent chemotherapy.

Association of MAPK activation with resistance

Our study demonstrates that enhanced MAPK signaling is required to maintain the vincristine-resistant phenotype in established resistant fusion-negative RMS cells (via loss-of-function with MEK inhibition). However, whether elevated MAPK signaling is sufficient to initiate de novo resistance in treatment-naïve parental cells remains to be determined. Gain-of-function studies, such as constitutive MAPK pathway activation in parental cells combined with vincristine treatment, would directly test whether elevated MAPK signaling is sufficient to establish or accelerate the development of therapy resistance de novo.

Lack of comprehensive senescence/SASP profiling in resistant fusion-negative RMS cells

Our characterization of senescence in resistant fusion-negative RMS cells employed a comprehensive panel of senescence markers (SA-β-gal staining, cell-cycle arrest, elevated p21/CDKN1A activity) and selective SASP component profiling (MMP2 and CCL2). This focused multi-parameter approach supported senescence induction because (1) functional senescence is defined primarily by irreversible growth arrest, SA-β-gal positivity, and p21/p16-mediated CDK inhibition; (2) senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) is highly heterogeneous and context-dependent, varying substantially based on senescence trigger (replicative, oncogene-induced, and therapy-induced), cell type, and temporal kinetics.14,39,40,41 Importantly, TIS—the senescence phenotype in our vincristine-resistant RMS model—frequently exhibits attenuated SASP compared with replicative or OIS. Our detection of downregulated MMP2 and CCL2 is consistent with this attenuated SASP pattern and validates our senescence characterization. Nevertheless, comprehensive longitudinal profiling of a broader SASP panel (including additional cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, extracellular proteases, and inflammatory mediators) at multiple time points post-MEK inhibition would provide deeper mechanistic insights into the full senescence phenotype and potentially reveal additional drivers of resistance or disease adaptation in this model.

Fusion status generalizability

Our study focused on fusion-negative RMS. Preliminary observations in one fusion-positive RMS line showed similar MAPK upregulation but reduced MEK inhibitor sensitivity. Additional fusion-positive RMS cell lines are needed to determine whether the mechanistic findings extend across RMS subtypes.

In vivo model limitations

Our study demonstrated that control (vehicle-treated) tumors reached endpoint at approximately 7 days, whereas trametinib combined with vincristine extended survival to day 17 (Figure 2H), representing a >2-fold extension in survival in this rapid-growth model. This accelerated tumor kinetics—approximately 2.5× faster than mammalian xenografts—permits rapid proof-of-principle testing but limits our ability to assess truly long-term sustained responses or directly extrapolate human post-relapse survival timelines. Mammalian xenograft studies would provide additional validation in a more physiologically relevant system. However, consistent results across in vitro, zebrafish, and patient samples support mechanistic conclusions.

Patient sample size

Enhanced phosphorylated ERK was validated in 7 of 8 post-chemotherapy patient pairs. While substantial, this small sample size limits statistical power and generalizability. Validation in a larger independent cohort is warranted.

Clinical drug availability

While trametinib is FDA-approved, the BCL-XL inhibitor used (A-1155463) is not clinically available. FDA-approved BCL-2 selective inhibitors, e.g., venetoclax, have shown toxicity in trials, particularly thrombocytopenia. Next-generation PROTAC-based BCL-XL degraders offer promise but remain in development, delaying clinical translation of the proposed combination therapy.

Experimental models and study participant details

Cell lines

RD cells (female, obtained from ATCC) and SMS-CTR cells (male, obtained from ATCC) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. All cell lines were authenticated by STR profiling and tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Zebrafish

Zebrafish experiments were conducted using the Danio rerio CG1 strain, an isogenic clonal line that enables syngeneic tumor cell transplantation without immunological rejection.42 All zebrafish were housed in the University of Washington Comparative Medicine facility under Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee protocol #4330-01, following standard protocols for temperature (28.5°C), light-dark cycle (14-h light:10-h dark), and tank maintenance. For all experiments, adult CG1 zebrafish aged 4–5 months with approximately equal male-to-female ratios were used. Sex was not a significant variable across experiments.

Method details

Generation of vincristine-resistant RMS cell lines

RD and SMS-CTR FN RMS cell lines were initially treated with vincristine at a dose of 1–1.5 nM which killed approximately 80–90% of the cells. The remaining cells were maintained in long-term culture with weekly replacement of media [10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)] until they grew to >50% confluency, were split to allow regrowth, and then restarted on vincristine at a 10–20% increase in dosage. Incremental increases in drug dosage were continued over the course of 9 months to a year to reach at least a 100-fold increase in the IC50 (half-maximal inhibitory concentration). Vincristine-resistant lines were maintained in 25–35 nM vincristine prior to plating.

CRISPR/Cas9 and shRNA gene targeting in human RMS cell lines

CRISPR/Cas9 targeting was accomplished by transducing RMS cells with lentiviruses expressing safe harbor control8 or gene-specific double guide RNAs (gRNAs) against MYC12 and Cas9. Gene-specific short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Table S1, shRNA sequences). Cells transduced with lentivirus were plated for cell counts and cell-based assays following antibiotic selection six days post transduction. Cloning of the Cas9 and gRNA expression vectors was performed as described previously.8

RNA sequencing

RNA was extracted from parental and vincristine-resistant RD cells using the Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit (catalog number: 74104, Qiagen, Germantown, Maryland), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA sequencing was performed by Azenta Life Sciences (South Plainfield, NJ, USA). RNA quantity and integrity were assessed using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies) and an Agilent TapeStation 4200 (Agilent Technologies). Poly(A)-selected libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, mRNA was enriched with oligo(dT) beads, fragmented at 94 °C for 15 min, converted to double-stranded cDNA, end-repaired, adenylated, ligated to adapters, and PCR-amplified with indexed primers. Libraries were validated, quantified by qPCR, and sequenced (2 × 150 bp) on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 (or equivalent). FASTQ files were generated and demultiplexed using bcl2fastq v2.17, and reads were trimmed for adapter sequences and quality. Clean reads were aligned to the ENSEMBL reference genome using STAR v2.5.2b, and gene-level counts were obtained with featureCounts (Subread v1.5.2) from uniquely mapped exonic reads. Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 using the Wald test, and genes with adjusted p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1 were considered significant. Gene ontology enrichment was assessed with GeneSCF using the MGI GO database, and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed in DESeq2 (plotPCA) using the 500 most variable genes.

Cell viability assays

RMS cell growth was quantified by cell counting or the ATP-based CellTiter-Glo cell viability assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Cells were plated in 96-well plates at 1000–2000 cells per well, and luminescence was read using a microplate reader at days 1 and 6 post-plating (BioTek Synergy H1, Winooski, VT). Drug treatments were introduced 1 day after plating.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF)

Archival paraffin-embedded tissue blocks of human RMS tumor samples were retrieved through the Northwest Biospecimen Service at the University of Washington Medical Center using an approved IRB protocol (#9565). De-identified tissue sections of 4-micron thickness were subjected to IHC. Briefly, following deparaffinization and antigen retrieval with Tris-EDTA buffer for cleaved Caspase 3 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) slides and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for the phospho-ERK antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) slides for 15 min while boiling, the slides were stained with appropriate antibodies using a standard IHC protocol. DAB (3,3-diaminobenzidine) HRP substrate (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) was used for signal detection. IHC slides for determining % positive cells across each tumor section were reviewed by two independent board-certified pathologists (EC and AB) and quantified using QuPath version 0.5.0. Immunofluorescence staining was performed as previously described.51 The antibodies used for IHC and IF are listed in table S1.

Real-time apoptosis detection in cultured RMS cells

To detect apoptosis in cultured cells, adherent RMS cells were incubated with NucView 488 Caspase 3 substrate (Biotium, Fremont, CA) diluted to 2 μM in 500 μL medium for 30 min prior to imaging by microscopy.

Western blots

Human cell lysates were prepared in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX) and 2x Laemmli buffer supplemented with β-mercaptoethanol (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA), electrophoresed on a 4–15% gradient sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA), and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane using TurboTrans-Blot (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Blots were blocked in 5% milk (or 5% bovine serum albumin for the phospho-ERK antibody) in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) and probed with antibodies, as listed in Table S1.

Flow cytometry for cell cycle analysis

One million human RMS cells were placed in Eppendorf tubes and resuspended in 100 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) or FACS buffer [PBS (Gibco), 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and 0.5 mM EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 0.5M)] containing flow antibodies for 20 min at 4°C. Cells were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 20 min at 4°C before staining with DAPI (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) for 30 min at room temperature.

β-Gal staining

β-Gal staining was performed as previously described.52 Imaging was performed using a BioTek Cytation 7 Cell Imaging Multimode Reader (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Quantitative RT-PCR (qPCR)

Human or zebrafish cells were lysed in TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and RNA was isolated according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Approximately 1 μg of RNA was used for cDNA synthesis using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). SYBR Green-based quantitative PCR was subsequently performed using gene-specific primers (Table S1) in a CFX Connect Real-time PCR Detection System Light Cycler (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

CUT&RUN quantitative PCR

Cells (∼100,000 to 200,000) were harvested, snap-frozen and processed with the reagents in the EpiNext CUT&RUN Fast Kit per the manufacturer’s protocol (Farmingdale, NY, USA). MYC antibody and non-immune IgG (provided in the kit as a negative control) were used (diluted 1:50). SYBR Green-based quantitative PCR was performed using primers against the CDKN1A (p21) promoter (STAR Methods) in a CFX Connect Real-time PCR Detection System Light Cycler (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Drug treatment studies in the zebrafish RMS model

Liquid nitrogen-preserved primary tumors with rag2:KRASG12D co-expressing mCherry were expanded by transplantation into 3–4 syngeneic adult CG1 fish. Tumor cells were harvested by dissection and dissociation and transplanted subcutaneously into the peritoneal cavity at 25,000 cells per fish. A drug treatment study was performed on engrafted tumor-bearing fish ∼10 days post-transplantation for 7 days. Drugs reconstituted in DMSO [vincristine (0.1 mg/kg, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and trametinib (10 mg/kg, Selleck Chemicals LLC, Houston, TX)] and vehicle [dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), Thermo Fisher Scientific] were delivered on days 0 and 4 in a 5-mL volume using a Hamilton syringe needle via the intraperitoneal route. Imaging of tumor-bearing fish was performed on days 0 and 7 using a Nikon fluorescent dissecting microscope (Nikon Instruments Inc., Melville, NY, USA). The change in tumor volume was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) as previously described.51

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates unless otherwise specified. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons with Tukey’s multiple comparisons correction. For experiments with two independent variables (e.g., treatment and cell line), two-way ANOVA was performed with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test as appropriate. Specific statistical tests used for each experiment are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

For in vivo zebrafish studies, sample sizes were determined based on prior experience with the model to achieve adequate statistical power while minimizing animal use. Tumor-bearing fish were randomly assigned to treatment groups. Tumor volume measurements were analyzed using one-way or two-way ANOVA as appropriate, with multiple comparisons correction.

For dose-response curves, IC50 values were calculated using nonlinear regression with a variable slope (four-parameter) model in GraphPad Prism. Comparisons of IC50 values between groups were performed using the extra sum-of-squares F test.

For drug combination studies, synergy was assessed using the Bliss independence model. Bliss synergy scores and contour plots were generated using SynergyFinder (version 2.0, www.synergyfinder.org).

For immunohistochemistry quantification, the percentage of positive cells was determined by analyzing whole tumor cross-sections using QuPath software (version 0.3.2). For matched patient samples, paired t test was used to compare pre-treatment and post-treatment specimens. For immunofluorescence quantification, at least three fields per condition were analyzed, and mean fluorescence intensity or percentage of positive cells was quantified using ImageJ software.
