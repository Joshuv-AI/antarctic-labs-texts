Performance and ammonium migration in a three-chamber microbial desalination cell under high ammonium loading
Authors: Xiaoning Ma, Guang Li, Lianhong Li, Xinrui Han, Lulu Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Treating high-ammonia wastewater remains a significant challenge, primarily due to high energy consumption, risks of secondary pollution, and insufficient operational stability. Microbial Desalination Cells (MDCs), a bioelectrochemical technology, offer potential for simultaneous pollutant removal and energy recovery. However, a systematic understanding of their performance under high ammonia loads (e.g., > 1000 mg/L NH4–N) and the coupled effects of key operational parameters is lacking. This study constructed a three-chamber MDC to evaluate the effects of inter-electrode spacing, initial desalination-chamber NH4-N concentration, and anodic substrate type on electrochemical output and apparent NH4-N migration-related performance under controlled batch conditions. In addition, desalination-chamber NH4-N concentration profiles were descriptively compared under different anodic COD levels. Results showed that decreases in NH4-N concentration in the desalination chamber were consistent with transmembrane ion transport under the tested batch conditions, as evaluated using the membrane-area-normalized apparent migration flux (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}_{\mathrm{app}}$$\end{document}). These results identify operating-condition-dependent changes in apparent NH4-N migration-related performance and reveal their trade-off with power output and COD-removal-based coulombic efficiency (CE). Introduction

The global availability of freshwater resources is under sustained pressure from the combined impacts of population growth, economic activities, and pollution1,2. Among various pollutants, ammonia nitrogen (NH₃/NH₄⁺) presents a critical challenge in water environment management due to its widespread sources, broad concentration range, and ecotoxicity. High ammonia nitrogen discharge not only induces eutrophication and accelerates dissolved oxygen depletion in water bodies but can also generate secondary pollutants such as nitrite/nitrate during transformation, posing persistent environmental risks3,4. Consequently, the development of treatment technologies for high-strength ammoniacal wastewater that achieve both efficient removal and low-carbon operation is of significant engineering demand and research value5.

Currently, the treatment of high-concentration ammonia nitrogen wastewater primarily relies on physicochemical processes such as stripping, ion exchange, breakpoint chlorination, and chemical precipitation, as well as biological processes like A/O, A²/O, and SBR3–5. Physicochemical methods typically offer high removal efficiencies but often come with substantial energy and chemical consumption, sludge or byproduct disposal challenges, and risks of secondary pollution. Biological methods, on the other hand, are prone to inhibition under high ammonia nitrogen conditions, and stable operation usually depends on complex pretreatment and process control. Under the synergistic constraints of “water scarcity—pollution control—low-carbon transition”, innovative water treatment technologies capable of recovering energy or achieving energy self-sufficiency while removing pollutants are emerging as research hotspots6–8.

Bioelectrochemical systems (BES) present a novel technological avenue for addressing these challenges9–11. By coupling microbial oxidation of organic matter at the anode with electron consumption at the cathode, BES offer a unique approach to pollutant removal and sustainable operation. Among BES, three-chamber microbial desalination cells (MDCs), consisting of a cathode chamber, a desalination chamber, and an anode chamber separated by ion-exchange membranes, are particularly representative. In this setup, an anodic biofilm oxidizes substrates, generating electrons that are transferred externally to the cathode. To maintain charge balance, ion transport across the ion-exchange membranes is driven by the combined effects of the electric field and concentration gradients. Under these driving forces, NH4+, as a target cation in ammonium-containing wastewater, can also migrate across the membrane and become enriched in the receiving chamber. Consequently, when applied to saline ammonium-containing wastewaters, MDCs exhibit a coupled behavior of power generation, ion migration, and ammonium reduction/enrichment12–21. It is crucial to note that nitrogen reduction in MDCs and related BES is often not solely a result of biological transformation. Its contribution typically arises from the synergistic action of electro-migration/diffusion-driven NH4 migration and enrichment, potentially coupled with co-existing biological conversion processes. The proportion of these contributions is significantly influenced by the chamber configuration, membrane type, and operating conditions15,22, which in turn dictates the system’s positioning and optimization pathway between “removal” and “recovery.” In recent years, research focused on nitrogen (especially NH4+) removal/recovery has spurred the development of MDC derivatives. Examples include microbial electrolysis desalination-related devices that couple MDCs with microbial electrolysis, bipolar membranes, or acid-base generation processes. These systems enhance ion migration while providing more direct conditions for subsequent ammonia recovery. For instance, Ye et al. demonstrated the feasibility of simultaneous desalination and ammonia recovery in a microbial electro-deionization chemical production cell (MEDCC)23, suggesting that a “migration-enrichment-recovery” route holds promise as a key direction for pre-treating high-salinity, high-ammonia side streams. In line with this trend, three-chamber systems exemplified by bio-electroconcentration have been employed to concentrate NH4-N/nutrients from high-nitrogen side streams such as source-separated urine or sludge dewatering refluent into a concentrate. Optimization of operating parameters has achieved NH4-N/nutrient recovery efficiencies of up to 69.6% in hydrolyzed urine systems24 and 75.5 ± 4.6% in reject-water bio-electroconcentration systems, with a specific energy consumption of 6.1 ± 1.1 kWh kgN− 1 reported for real reject water25. However, studies also indicate that such migration-enrichment processes are constrained by basic water quality conditions, such as wastewater conductivity, presenting clear operational boundaries and scale-up challenges26. Therefore, with recovery-oriented applications becoming increasingly relevant, it is necessary to clarify how operating conditions influence apparent NH4-N migration-related performance, electrochemical output, and electron-recovery-related metrics in three-chamber MDCs under high-ammonium conditions.

Although previous studies have reported the effects of concentration, membrane configuration, and operating parameters on ion transport and electrochemical performance, systematic evidence remains limited regarding how inter-electrode spacing, initial NH4-N concentration, and anodic substrate type jointly influence apparent NH4-N migration-related responses and COD-removal-based coulombic efficiency within a single three-chamber MDC platform. Accordingly, this study employed a laboratory-scale three-chamber MDC operated under controlled batch conditions to evaluate these parameter-dependent responses. The objective was to identify operating-condition-dependent performance changes and multi-indicator trade-offs, rather than to demonstrate selective NH4+ transport, migration dominance, direct ammonia recovery, or engineering-scale operation.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

This study employed a parametric matrix design (one-factor-at-a-time within a matrix framework), establishing four experimental series: S1 electrode spacing d = 3.5, 4.5, 5.5 cm27,28;. S2 initial desalination chamber NH4–N = 1500, 3000, 6000 mg/L29,30; S3 anode COD = 200, 300, 500 mg/L31,32; S4 substrate type = sodium acetate, glucose33–35. Except for the varying factor, all other conditions were fixed at the reference condition (RC): d = 4.5 cm, NH4–N = 3000 mg/L, anode COD = 300 mg/L, and substrate = sodium acetate. Triplicate parallel reactors (n = 3) were established for each treatment group. Reactor configuration and sampling points are detailed in Fig. 1; the composition of solutions and initial pH for each chamber are provided in Sect. 2.4; detection items and calculation methods are outlined in Sect. 2.5. The present reactor configuration and operating conditions were designed for laboratory-scale parametric comparison under controlled batch operation, with the aim of isolating the effects of electrode spacing, NH4–N loading, anode COD, and substrate type on migration-related performance, rather than directly simulating a scalable continuous-flow system. Among these experimental series, the anodic-COD series was used for descriptive comparison of desalination-chamber NH4-N concentration profiles. Because a complete set of electrochemical output, COD-removal-based CE, and apparent migration-flux endpoints was not reported for this series, no independent mechanistic conclusion was derived from the anodic-COD comparison.

Construction and materials of the three-chamber microbial desalination cell (MDC)

The three-chamber microbial desalination cell (MDC) was fabricated from polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA; wall thickness, 1 cm). As shown in Fig. 1, the desalination chamber was separated from the cathode chamber by a cation exchange membrane (CEM, CMI-7000) and from the anode chamber by an anion exchange membrane (AEM, AMI-7001). The effective area of each ion-exchange membrane (IEM) was 40 cm² (0.004 m²). Carbon felt was used as both the cathode and anode electrode, with an effective projected area of 72 cm² (0.0072 m²) per electrode. Titanium wires were used as current collectors and connected the electrodes to the external circuit. The working volumes of the cathode, desalination, and anode chambers were 0.08 L, 0.08 L, and 0.12 L, respectively. The inter-electrode spacing (d) was set at 3.5, 4.5, or 5.5 cm according to the experimental series. During routine operation, an external resistance (Rext) of 1000 Ω was applied, and the output voltage was recorded at intervals of Δt = 360 s (every 6 min).

Pretreatment of Ion-exchange membranes, electrode inoculation, and operation

Prior to reactor assembly, the ion-exchange membranes were immersed in a 35 g/L saline solution for 48 h and then thoroughly rinsed with deionized water. No drying step was applied, and the rinsed membranes were maintained in a hydrated state before installation in the aqueous MDC system. Carbon felt electrodes were cleaned with 1 M HCl for 48 h and subsequently rinsed with deionized water until neutral before use. The anode inoculum was anaerobic sludge collected from the anaerobic tank of Dongnan Wastewater Treatment Plant, Changchun, Jilin Province, China. Before reactor operation, the sludge was mixed with the anolyte and introduced to the carbon-felt anode for acclimation and biofilm formation over 3–6 d. The anolyte was purged with N₂ for 5 min to reduce the initial dissolved oxygen content, after which the anode chamber was sealed. The MDC was operated in batch mode at 25 ± 1 °C, and no anodic recirculation reservoir was used during an individual operating cycle. Because dissolved-oxygen transfer through the ion-exchange membranes was not directly quantified, the N₂ purging and sealed-anode operation are interpreted as measures to minimize oxygen interference rather than evidence of strictly anaerobic conditions throughout each operating cycle.

Composition of solutions in each chamber, pH control, and operating mode

Batch operation and solution handling. The MDC was operated in batch mode. At the beginning of each cycle, freshly prepared catholyte, desalination solution, and anolyte were added independently to their respective chambers. Continuous feeding and recirculation were not applied during a single operational cycle. For the experimental cycles used to calculate NH4-N removal rate and apparent migration flux, sampled liquid was not replenished during the quantified interval. The sampled volume was recorded and was sufficiently small relative to the desalination-chamber working volume to avoid materially affecting the concentration-based comparison.

Anolyte composition. The anolyte contained sodium acetate or glucose as the carbon source, with the dosage adjusted according to the target chemical oxygen demand (COD) of 200, 300, or 500 mg/L and verified using the potassium dichromate method. The basal anolyte composition included CaCl₂·2 H₂O (0.1 g/L), NH₄Cl (0.5 g/L), K₂HPO₄·3 H₂O (3.4 g/L), MgCl₂·6 H₂O (0.1 g/L), and KH₂PO₄ (4.4 g/L). A mineral supplement solution was added to the anolyte at 10 mL/L to support anodic biofilm operation.

Mineral supplement solution composition. The mineral supplement solution contained the following components per litre: NH₄Cl, 0.31 g/L; KCl, 0.13 g/L; CaCl₂, 0.01 g/L; MgSO₄, 1.2 g/L; NaCl, 2 × 10⁻³ g/L; MnSO₄, 7.6 × 10⁻⁴ g/L; AlCl₃, 5 × 10⁻⁴ g/L; Na₂MoO₄·2 H₂O, 3 × 10⁻³ g/L; H₃BO₃, 1 × 10⁻³ g/L; and ZnCl₂, 1 × 10⁻³ g/L. This mineral supplement solution was used only in the anolyte and was not added to either the desalination solution or the catholyte.

Desalination solution composition and pH. The desalination solution was prepared as a defined synthetic high-ammonium matrix containing K₂HPO₄·3 H₂O (3.4 g/L), KH₂PO₄ (4.4 g/L), CaCl₂·2 H₂O (0.1 g/L), and MgCl₂·6 H₂O (0.1 g/L). No mineral supplement solution or anodic carbon source was added to the desalination solution. NH₄Cl was added at 5.73, 11.46, and 22.92 g/L to establish initial NH₄–N concentrations of 1500, 3000, and 6000 mg/L, respectively. The initial pH of the desalination solution was adjusted to 6.5 using HCl or NaOH and monitored using a pH meter (AZ8685A). Accordingly, NH₄⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Cl⁻, and phosphate species were present in the defined desalination matrix, whereas Na⁺ was not included as a fixed basal component and could only be introduced when NaOH was used for pH adjustment. Removal time and cycle-termination criteria. A single operating cycle was defined based on the voltage profile. Cycle termination was identified when the output voltage entered a sustained decay and failed to recover to ≥ 90% of the pre-decay stable plateau for at least 60 min, which was defined as an irreversible voltage drop. Here, the cycle-termination criterion was used as an experimental endpoint definition for batch-mode comparison, rather than as an intended operational strategy for scaled or continuous systems. For the substrate-comparison tests (Fig. 7) only, the removal time was defined as the time required for NH4–N in the desalination chamber to decrease to < 50 mg/L, and the corresponding removal times (223 h and 299 h) were calculated accordingly. For the S2 initial-concentration series (1500, 3000, and 6000 mg/L NH4-N), the desalination chamber was sampled at the predefined time points used to generate the NH4-N and conductivity profiles shown in Fig. 5. Each batch cycle was terminated when the irreversible voltage-drop criterion was reached. These time-series measurements were used to compare desalination-chamber NH4-N reduction and conductivity changes among the tested initial concentrations. Because matched time-resolved NH4-N profiles on both sides of the CEM were not obtained, these data were not used for quantitative partitioning of diffusion- and electromigration-related contributions.

Electrochemical performance testing, water quality detection, and calculations

Voltage, current, power, and internal resistance

Electrochemical performance testing, water quality analysis, and the associated calculations were conducted according to commonly used methods reported in the literature10,36. All data were processed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Output voltage was continuously recorded by a smart data acquisition card at a sampling frequency of once every 6 min. Polarization and power density curves were generated using the variable external resistance method10,36: external resistance was incrementally changed within the range of 10–9999.9 Ω. After stabilization for 20–30 min under each resistance condition, the voltage was recorded. The peak power density was defined as Pmax, and the internal resistance (Rint) was determined from the slope of the linearly descending voltage segment of the polarization curve.

System performance characterization during startup

During the startup phase, the Microbial desalination cell (MDC) rapidly establishes and sustains reproducible power generation. The voltage curves exhibit characteristic “stable-decay” cyclical behavior, indicative of progressive anode biofilm maturation and the formation of a stable electron transfer pathway. Concurrently, the conductivity of the desalination chamber and the NH4-N concentration decrease synchronously over time, showing changes consistent with ion transport associated with NH4-N reduction in the desalination chamber. Detailed voltage profiles, polarization/internal resistance parameters, and the variations in NH4-N and COD within a typical cycle during the startup period are presented in the Supplementary Materials (Figs. S1-S3).

Results

Effect of inter-electrode spacing on power output and COD-removal-based coulombic efficiency

Inter-electrode spacing affected both electrochemical output and apparent NH4-N migration-related performance in the three-chamber MDC. As the spacing decreased from 5.5 to 3.5 cm, the polarization and power-density responses were enhanced, resulting in an approximately 1.8-fold increase in maximum volumetric power density. In contrast, COD-removal-based coulombic efficiency (CE) decreased from 4.62% to 2.33%, indicating a trade-off between output intensity and the fraction of COD-removal-associated electron equivalents recovered as external-circuit charge (Fig. 2a-c). Discussion

Technological positioning, advantages, and current bottlenecks of the three-chamber MDC for high-ammonium wastewater treatment

Compared with recovery-oriented bioelectrochemical system derivatives, such as microbial electrolysis desalination and chemical-production cells (MEDCCs) and bio-electroconcentration systems, the three-chamber microbial desalination cell (MDC) examined in this study is more appropriately positioned as a pretreatment platform for high-ammonium wastewater rather than as a direct ammonia-recovery unit6,14,23–26. Its principal advantage lies in coupling anodic organic-matter oxidation, external-circuit current generation, and membrane-mediated ion transport without an externally applied voltage. Under the controlled batch conditions used in this study, the MDC enabled the simultaneous assessment of electrochemical output and apparent NH4–N migration-related performance in the desalination chamber. Therefore, its current value is primarily associated with identifying operating-condition-dependent performance responses before integration with a dedicated enrichment or recovery process37–52.

Nevertheless, the present results also reveal important performance bottlenecks. First, enhanced electrochemical output did not correspond to improved COD-removal-based coulombic efficiency (CE). When the inter-electrode spacing was decreased, power output and apparent NH4–N migration-related performance increased, whereas COD-removal-based CE decreased. This trade-off indicates that reactor optimization should not rely solely on maximum power density or NH4–N concentration reduction, because increased output intensity does not necessarily correspond to a greater fraction of COD-removal-associated electron equivalents being recovered as external-circuit charge. Second, the desalination solution was a multi-ion matrix containing NH4+, K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Cl−, and phosphate species. Accordingly, the migration removal rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{\mathrm{mig}}$$\end{document}) and apparent migration flux (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}_{\mathrm{app}}$$\end{document}) reported in this study describe apparent NH4–N migration-related performance, but do not quantify selective NH4+ transport or the fraction of total transferred charge carried by NH4+.

Additional limitations arise from the laboratory-scale operating configuration. The batch-operation procedure and ferricyanide catholyte employed in this study were suitable for controlled comparison among operating conditions, but they do not directly represent a continuous-flow or recovery-oriented engineering system. Moreover, the present work did not demonstrate the formation of a recoverable ammonia product, long-term membrane stability, or fouling behaviour, the influence of dissolved-oxygen crossover through the ion-exchange membranes, or performance in real wastewater matrices. Therefore, the three-chamber MDC evaluated here should be regarded as a laboratory-scale platform for identifying parameter-dependent trade-offs and migration-related NH4–N response boundaries under high-ammonium conditions. Translation toward practical nitrogen recovery will require further validation under continuous operation, realistic cathodic conditions, chamber-resolved ion and nitrogen balances, real-wastewater matrices, and downstream enrichment or recovery coupling.

Interpretation boundaries for apparent NH4-N migration-related performance in a multi-Ion MDC system

The decrease in NH4-N concentration in the desalination chamber should be interpreted as an apparent system response associated with transmembrane ion transport, rather than as a direct quantification of any single transport mechanism. In the defined desalination solution used in this study, NH4+ was the target cation, whereas K+, Ca2+, and Mg2+ were also present as coexisting cations. Cl⁻ and phosphate species were present as coexisting anions. Under closed-circuit operation, the electrical field generated by anodic substrate oxidation may promote the migration of NH4+ and other mobile cations through the CEM toward the cathode chamber, while concentration gradients may also contribute to solute transport. Because multiple mobile ions coexist in the desalination chamber, the external-circuit charge could not be assigned specifically to NH4+ migration without ion-specific transport measurements and chamber-resolved charge-balance data. Therefore, an ammonium-specific migration current was not calculated in the present study. Accordingly, the migration removal rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{\mathrm{mig}}$$\end{document}) and apparent migration flux (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}_{\mathrm{app}}$$\end{document}) reported in this study describe apparent NH4-N migration-related performance, but do not quantify selective NH4+ transport or its fraction of the total transferred charge.

A quantitative partition of diffusion- and electromigration-related contributions was not performed in the present study. Although time-resolved NH4-N concentration profiles in the desalination chamber were available for the S2 series, as shown in Fig. 5, a defensible Fick-law calculation across the CEM requires matched time-resolved concentration profiles on both sides of the membrane and an effective NH4+ transport parameter for the CEM under the present solution matrix. These data were not obtained in the current study. Therefore, subtracting an estimated diffusion flux from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}_{\mathrm{app}}$$\end{document} and assigning the remainder to electromigration would introduce unverified assumptions and could overstate the mechanistic resolution supported by the present dataset.

Within these evidence boundaries, the concurrent changes in desalination-chamber NH4-N concentration, electrical conductivity, and apparent migration-related performance are consistent with a role of transmembrane ion transport in the tested MDC. However, the present results do not demonstrate migration dominance or quantitatively distinguish electromigration, diffusion, and biological or chemical transformation processes. Future work should combine chamber-resolved time-series measurements of NH4-N, total nitrogen, and major ions with membrane-specific NH4+ transport characterization, integrated-current analysis, open-circuit controls, and inactivated controls. Such measurements would enable a quantitative comparison of diffusion-related and current-associated contributions without assuming that NH4+ is the sole charge-carrying ion.

Performance under a defined synthetic high-ammonium matrix and implications for real wastewater application

At an initial desalination-chamber NH4-N concentration of 6000 mg/L, the system achieved 99.1% NH4-N removal and a peak volumetric power density of 5.42 W·m− 3 under the tested batch conditions. Cross-study comparison of power output requires caution because the reported values are influenced by reactor configuration, electrode area, normalization basis, catholyte chemistry, substrate type, external resistance, and wastewater composition.

The use of a defined synthetic matrix enabled the effects of the tested operating variables to be examined with reduced interference from uncontrolled wastewater constituents. However, this controlled condition also limits direct extrapolation to real wastewater. In a real high-ammonium wastewater, mixed biodegradable and slowly biodegradable organic compounds may alter anodic electron recovery and voltage stability relative to the acetate- or glucose-based conditions examined here. Suspended solids, colloids, multivalent ions, competing cations and anions, and inhibitory compounds may additionally influence membrane transport selectivity, internal resistance, local pH conditions, and membrane fouling behaviour. These factors could change both apparent NH4-N migration-related performance and COD-removal-based coulombic efficiency.

Accordingly, the results reported here should be interpreted as performance responses under a defined synthetic matrix rather than as a demonstration of real-wastewater applicability. Validation using representative real high-ammonium wastewater, together with monitoring of organic-matter biodegradability, major-ion transport, membrane fouling, and electrochemical stability, is required before practical operating conditions can be defined.

Inter-electrode spacing: performance trade-offs and implications for future cell geometry

Reducing the inter-electrode spacing shortened the ionic transport path and was associated with lower internal resistance and enhanced electrochemical output in the present MDC. Decreasing the spacing from 5.5 to 3.5 cm reduced internal resistance by more than 30% and increased maximum volumetric power density by approximately 1.8-fold. Apparent NH4-N migration-related performance also increased, as reflected by the increase in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{\mathrm{mig}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}_{\mathrm{app}}$$\end{document}. However, COD-removal-based CE decreased from 4.62% to 2.33%, indicating that the design benefit of reduced spacing was accompanied by a lower fraction of COD-removal-associated electron equivalents recovered as external-circuit charge. Although the decline in COD-removal-based CE may be associated with non-current-producing COD removal pathways, these pathways were not individually quantified in the present study.

These results suggest that future cell geometry should be optimized using a multi-objective criterion rather than maximum power density alone. A shorter inter-electrode distance may be favourable when the design objective is to increase apparent NH4-N migration-related treatment intensity, but its effect on COD-removal-based CE, hydraulic accessibility, membrane/electrode maintenance, and long-term fouling must also be assessed. For scale-up, a modular short-path configuration with controlled hydraulic channels may provide a practical design direction, provided that continuous-flow validation confirms stable voltage output, manageable pressure loss, membrane accessibility, and acceptable long-term fouling behaviour. Accordingly, the spacing identified as favourable in the present laboratory-scale batch tests should be treated as a design hypothesis for subsequent module development rather than as a directly transferable engineering optimum.

Initial NH4-N concentration: endpoint performance response and loading-control implications

Within the tested initial desalination-chamber NH4-N concentrations of 1500, 3000, and 6000 mg/L, increasing NH4-N concentration was accompanied by lower ohmic internal resistance and higher peak volumetric power density. However, because increasing NH4Cl dosage simultaneously increased the ionic strength and conductivity of the desalination solution, the observed electrochemical response cannot be attributed solely to a beneficial effect of NH4-N loading. Conductivity-matched controls are required to distinguish the effect of ammonium loading from conductivity-mediated changes in ohmic resistance.

The endpoint metrics from the S2 series were used to compare performance among the tested initial NH4-N conditions, while the within-cycle profiles in Fig. 5 describe the accompanying concentration and conductivity changes in the desalination chamber. Because the present dataset does not support quantitative separation of diffusion-, electromigration-, and biological-process contributions, the observed responses should not be interpreted as evidence of a defined transport-dominant loading range. From an engineering-design perspective, future MDC systems treating variable-strength high-ammonium streams should combine loading equalization with monitoring of conductivity, pH, voltage response, ion transport, and COD-removal-based coulombic efficiency. Continuous or semi-continuous validation will be required before defining a stable loading range for practical operation.

Substrate-dependent performance under synthetic conditions and boundaries for real-wastewater application

Under the controlled synthetic-medium conditions used in this study, acetate was associated with lower internal resistance, higher power output, higher COD-removal-based coulombic efficiency (CE), and higher apparent NH4-N migration-related performance than glucose. These results indicate that the form of the anodic carbon source affected the electrochemical and apparent migration-related responses of the three-chamber MDC. A plausible interpretation is that acetate can be more directly available for electroactive oxidation than glucose, whereas glucose may require intermediate conversion steps before contributing to external-circuit electron recovery. However, microbial community structure, metabolic intermediates, and competing electron-consumption pathways were not directly measured in the present study. Therefore, this interpretation should be regarded as a mechanism-consistent inference rather than as a verified biological explanation.

The substrate comparison was conducted using defined synthetic carbon sources and should not be directly extrapolated to real wastewater. As discussed in Sect. 4.3, matrix complexity and organic-matter biodegradability in real high-ammonium wastewater may alter anodic electron recovery, ion-transport competition, voltage stability, and membrane behaviour. Therefore, validation using representative real wastewater is required before substrate-management strategies are proposed for practical MDC operation.

Limitations and future perspectives

The present study evaluated electrochemical output, NH4-N concentration reduction, and apparent migration-related responses of a three-chamber MDC under controlled laboratory-scale batch conditions. Although the observed performance changes support a transmembrane ion-transport interpretation, the current dataset does not quantitatively partition the contributions of electromigration, diffusion, and biological processes, nor does it demonstrate engineering-scale or recovery-oriented performance. Accordingly, the following limitations define the evidence boundary of this study and identify priorities for subsequent validation.

(1) Insufficient Decoupling of NH4-N and Ionic Strength: Given that an increase in NH4-N is accompanied by changes in electrical conductivity, it is challenging in this study to differentiate the independent contributions of the “NH4-N loading effect” from the “ionic strength-mediated conductivity enhancement effect.” Future work should incorporate conductivity-matched controls (adjusted via background electrolytes)53,54 and simultaneously calculate free ammonia (FA) based on pH and temperature to more rigorously assess inhibition risks.

(2) Insufficient evidence for quantitative transport-contribution partitioning and nitrogen fate: This study evaluated NH4-N concentration reduction and apparent migration-related performance in the desalination chamber. However, individual-ion transport, chamber-resolved nitrogen fate, and potential biological or chemical transformation pathways were not directly quantified. Because the desalination solution contained NH4+, K⁺, Ca2+, Mg2+, Cl⁻, and phosphate species, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}_{\mathrm{app}}$$\end{document} cannot be interpreted as a selective NH4+ transport flux or as the fraction of electrical charge carried specifically by NH4+. Moreover, the present S2 sampling design and the available membrane-transport information do not support a reliable Fick-law-based partition of diffusion- and electromigration-related contributions. Future work should combine chamber-resolved time-series measurements of NH4-N, total nitrogen, and major ions with membrane-specific NH4+ transport characterization, integrated-current analysis, open-circuit controls, and inactivated controls to quantify transport contributions and estimate the charge fraction potentially associated with NH4+ transport.

(3) Insufficient assessment of long-term stability, membrane processes, and oxygen crossover: Long-term operational stability, membrane fouling evolution, and recovery-side coupling were not evaluated in the present study. In addition, although the anolyte was initially purged with N2 and the anode chamber was sealed during batch operation, possible dissolved-oxygen crossover through the ion-exchange membranes was not quantified. Consequently, its potential influence on anodic electron recovery and biofilm performance cannot be excluded. Future work should incorporate dissolved-oxygen monitoring in the relevant chambers and appropriate oxygen-transfer controls, together with evaluations of membrane fouling, mass-transfer resistance evolution, electrochemical stability, and overall process robustness. Accordingly, the findings of the present study should be interpreted as laboratory-scale evidence for operating-condition-dependent apparent NH4-N migration-related responses and associated multi-indicator trade-offs under controlled batch conditions, rather than as direct evidence of migration dominance, stable recovery-oriented operation, or engineering-scale performance.

Compared with glucose, acetate increased \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{\mathrm{mig}}$$\end{document} from 2.79 to 3.89 mg·L⁻¹·h⁻¹ and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}_{\mathrm{app}}$$\end{document} from 55.8 to 77.8 mg·m⁻²·h⁻¹, consistent with its more favourable electrochemical output under the tested conditions.
