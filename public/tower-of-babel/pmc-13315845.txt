Genomic features of clonal hematopoiesis–associated genes in primary and CAR T-cell–related secondary T-cell malignancies
Authors: Jonathan J. Park, Zachary M. Avigan, Matthew B. Dong, Cuong V. Nguyen, Joan Guitart, Ritesh R. Kotecha, Karlo Perica, Samir Parekh, Adolfo Aleman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Secondary TCLs after CAR T-cell therapy are enriched for genomic alterations in CH–associated genes, particularly TET2 and DNMT3A.•Primary and secondary TCLs have shared genomic alterations, including those in genes associated with CH.

Introduction

Secondary primary malignancies, particularly T-cell leukemias or lymphomas (TCLs), that manifest after chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy have gained considerable attention since the US Food and Drug Administration issued a class-wide black box warning in January 2024.1,2 The direct mechanistic relationship between CAR T-cell therapy and these TCLs remains uncertain, with potential factors including prior exposure to extensive anticancer treatments and the inherent tendency for patients with B-cell malignancies to develop T-cell malignancies.3 The integration of viral vectors carrying the CAR construct into the genome near proto-oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes, leading to insertional mutagenesis and subsequent malignant transformation, is a potential CAR T-cell therapy specific mechanism for TCL development; however, its frequency in CAR T-cell–associated TCLs and its association with different vector constructs is unknown. Recent reports have demonstrated that CARs can indeed integrate into tumor suppressor genes in secondary TCLs.4,5 However, transgene-negative cancers are encountered more frequently than those with detectable CAR integration, suggesting that additional pathways may be involved.2 These secondary malignancies present substantial clinical challenges because of their aggressive behavior, lack of defined standard management, and poor prognosis. As more cases continue to emerge, effective risk stratification, as well as monitoring and management strategies need to be clarified, highlighting an urgent need for further research in this area.

Methods

Curation of published secondary TCL cases

The PubMed/MEDLINE or Google Scholar online databases were systematically queried for publications describing secondary TCLs after CAR T-cell therapy. The search included all available publications until 4 September 2025, and queries included combinations search strings such as CAR T-cell therapy, CAR-T, secondary, and lymphoma. No date restrictions were applied beyond the end-of-search date of 4 September 2025. References from identified publications were also reviewed in detail to further identify other cited cases for comprehensiveness. The results were manually screened for relevance and quality. Inclusion criteria comprised (1) peer-reviewed publications reporting individual cases or case series of secondary TCLs arising after CAR T-cell therapy; and (2) sufficient clinical or molecular data available for analysis (ie, at minimum, information on the CAR T-cell product administered and confirmation of a secondary T-cell lineage malignancy). Exclusion criteria comprised (1) review articles, editorials, or commentaries without original case data; (2) cases with insufficient molecular characterization to permit meaningful analysis of genomic features; and (3) reports describing only secondary malignancies of B cell or myeloid lineage only. Cases were examined in detail, with a focus on molecular features including vector integration sites, somatic mutations, and CAR expression status.

Primary TCL sample data collection

The American Association for Cancer Research (AACR) Project Genomics, Evidence, Neoplasia, Information, Exchange (GENIE) Consortium v18.0-public cohort,6 comprising 250 018 samples from 211 526 patients and available from cBioPortal v6.3.57 was accessed to construct a composite cohort of primary TCLs. Because of overlap in phenotypes for certain malignancies, natural killer (NK) cell neoplasms were also included. Cohort construction followed a 2-stage hierarchical filtering approach applied within the cBioPortal interface. In the first stage, broad cancer type filters were applied. The curated malignancy cohort comprised 2385 samples from 1627 patients. The cancer type filters included mature T-cell and NK cell neoplasms, blood cancer not otherwise specified (NOS), T-lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma, and lymphatic cancer NOS. In the second stage, cancer type detailed filters were applied to the intermediate set to further restrict the cohort. The cancer type detailed filters included T-lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma, peripheral T-cell lymphoma NOS, lymphoid atypical, angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma, mycosis fungoides, lymphoid neoplasm, mature T-cell and NK cell Neoplasms, T-cell large granular lymphocytic leukemia, adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma, T-cell prolymphocytic leukemia, anaplastic large cell lymphoma, anaplastic large-cell lymphoma anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) negative, extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma, nasal type, early T-cell precursor lymphoblastic leukemia, Sézary syndrome, anaplastic large-cell lymphoma ALK positive, nodal peripheral T-cell lymphoma with T follicular helper (TFH) phenotype, primary cutaneous gamma delta T-cell lymphoma, follicular T-cell lymphoma, primary cutaneous CD30+ T-cell lymphoproliferative disorders, monomorphic epitheliotropic intestinal T-cell lymphoma, hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma, primary cutaneous CD8+ aggressive epidermotropic cytotoxic T-cell lymphoma, primary cutaneous CD4+ small/medium T-cell lymphoproliferative disorder, NK cell lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma, lymphomatoid papulosis, enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma, aggressive NK cell leukemia, subcutaneous panniculitis-like T-cell lymphoma, lymphoid, primary cutaneous anaplastic large cell lymphoma, and systemic Epstein-Barr virus–positive T-cell lymphoma of childhood. Cohort-wide mutations, structural variations, and copy number alterations frequency data were obtained directly from the portal. Samples with frequencies <0.1% were marked as 0.01. Variant-level data were obtained for specific genes of interest. The OncoKB8 precision oncology knowledge base or previously defined gene lists9 were used to label the genes as cancer-associated or clonal hematopoiesis (CH)–associated, respectively. Sample data and labels were then processed for further downstream analysis.

Visualization and standard statistical analysis

Custom R (v4.4.1) scripts using packages such as ggplot were used to generate visualizations. Standard statistical calculations such as Wilcoxon ranked-sum test were performed in R with functions such as pairwise.wilcox.test. Descriptions of the methods, significance, P values, and/or tails are included in the figures, figure legends, and the results.

Results

Comparative molecular analyses of secondary TCLs after CAR T-cell therapy

To better understand the pathogenesis of secondary TCLs after CAR T-cell therapy, we conducted a comprehensive review of published cases and performed comparative analyses of their molecular features (Table 1). We identified 17 patients in the literature as of 4 September 2025. Notably, alterations were found in genes associated with CH including TET2 (8 patients), DNMT3A (3 patients), EZH2 (1 patient), and TP53 (1 patient), as well as Janus kinase (JAK)–signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) signaling (5 patients including those with JAK1, JAK3, SH2B3, and SOCS1 alterations). CH is characterized by the clonal expansion of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) following acquisition of somatic mutations, copy number changes, or other somatic events that confer competitive advantage.9, 10, 11 This age associated outgrowth is highly prevalent in the older adult population and has been associated with blood cancers, cardiovascular disease, and overall mortality.11 CH has been found to be particularly prevalent in patients undergoing CAR T-cell therapy, with published cohorts reporting prevalence rates ranging from ∼34% to 56%, depending on the patient population, detection method, and variant allele frequency (VAF) threshold used.12, 13, 14, 15, 16 The most commonly observed somatic CH mutations include TET2, DNMT3A, ASXL1, JAK2, TP53, and SF3B1, which are often considered leukemia-driver genes, as they are also commonly observed in myelodysplastic syndrome, myeloproliferative neoplasms, and acute myeloid leukemia.10 Athough these genes are commonly associated with myeloid neoplasms,17,18 they have also been seen with lymphoid neoplasms such as primary TCLs.19,20 These results suggest that alterations in genes associated with CH and hematologic malignancies, particularly TET2 and DNMT3A, may play a role in the pathogenesis of secondary TCLs after CAR T-cell therapy. We also found alterations in genes associated with the JAK-STAT pathway, which play complex roles in immunity and cancer through interferon signaling.21 The clinical implications of CH in the CAR T-cell therapy setting are multifaceted and continue to be defined. With respect to treatment efficacy, most published cohorts have not demonstrated a significant adverse impact of CH on response rates or overall survival.14, 15, 16 However, results regarding CAR T-cell therapy–related inflammatory toxicities have been mixed. One group reported an association between preexisting CH and an increased risk of severe immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome in patients undergoing axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi-cel) therapy,15 whereas other cohorts did not confirm a significant association between CH status and rates of either immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome or cytokine release syndrome.14,16 Separately, some groups have found that pre–CAR T-cell CH is associated with a higher incidence of severe cytokine release syndrome.22 Multiple groups have also now documented that preexisting CH is associated with an increased risk of hematologic toxicity or secondary myeloid malignancies following CAR T-cell therapy.12,23,24 The combination of CH and elevated inflammatory markers such as baseline ferritin has been identified as highly predictive of prolonged cytopenias after B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)-directed CAR T-cell therapy.12 These findings collectively underscore the clinical importance of CH in CAR T-cell therapy recipients and its potential relevance not only to secondary TCL risk but also to inflammatory complications and secondary myeloid Although TET2 and DNMT3A appeared enriched among secondary TCLs, given their known roles in both CH and malignancy, we examined whether preexisting CH was present in these cases. We found that 7 of the patients had some level of evidence for preexisting CH, most often through identification of TET2 and DNMT3A mutations at lower VAFs in leukapheresis samples obtained before CAR T-cell therapy (Table 1). Notably, this signal was present despite the fact that not all of the patients were evaluated for CH or had mutational profiles of preinfusion samples examined. Moreover, CH may be present at very low allele frequencies that are below the limit of detection yet clinically relevant to secondary TCLs. In the case reported by Kobbe et al,25 the TET2 p.Q644∗ variant was not identified before CAR T-cell treatment on initial analysis, but further analysis with highly sensitive error-corrected duplex sequencing identified presence of the variant prior to treatment albeit at a very low VAFs (0.0012% in the apheresis sample and 0.02% in the final product). This suggests that CH may be underidentified even in patients where molecular testing is performed. Of the cases with preexisting CH, all of the variants identified were in either TET2 and DNMT3A. Although 6 of the cases found the same variants enriched in the secondary TCLs, the case reported by Perica et al found that the preexisting DNMT3A mutation was not detected in the secondary TCL, suggesting it did not directly contribute to oncogenesis.4

Gene- and variant-level analyses of CH- and cancer-associated genes in primary TCLs

Given the potential importance of CH-associated genes and other known genes associated with secondary T-cell lymphomagenesis in setting of CAR T-cell therapy, we hypothesized that these interactions will synergize in driving the development of primary TCLs. To systematically study the molecular drivers of TCL development, we analyzed a cohort of primary TCLs (2385 samples from 1627 patients), including both cutaneous and systemic TCLs, curated from the AACR GENIE Consortium v18.0 public cohort data set (supplemental Methods). Genes were identified as cancer associated through the OncoKB8 precision oncology knowledge base or as CH associated using previously defined gene lists.9 For the purposes of these analyses, CH association label superseded that of cancer association. Mutations in CH-associated genes, including TET2 (n = 469), DNMT3A (n = 293), and TP53 (n = 217) were found to be among the most frequently altered (Figure 1A). Moreover, mutations in CH-associated genes were significantly enriched in TCL samples compared with non-CH cancer mutations and noncancer-associated genomic alterations (Figure 1B). The most frequently altered structural variants, such as JAK2, ALK, and NPM1, were associated with CH or cancer (Figure 2A), although not significantly enriched compared with structural variants in genes not associated with cancer (Figure 2B). The most frequently altered copy number alterations (CNAs), involving CDKN2A, CDKN2B, TP53, and DNMT3A, on the other hand, were associated with CH or cancer (Figure 2C) significantly more than CNAs not associated with cancer (Figure 2D). Most of the alterations in the CH associated genes, particularly in the frequently mutated TET2, DNMT3A, and TP53, were either truncating or missense mutations identified as putative drivers (Figure 3), highlighting their potential functional impact. These results altogether demonstrate that mutations in CH-associated genes are recurrently altered in both primary and secondary TCLs. However, it is important to note that for primary TCLs, where longitudinal premalignant sampling and multilineage clonality data are unavailable, the presence of such mutations does not establish that they represent antecedent CH events. To identify specific mutations associated with primary TCLs, we performed variant-level analyses for selected CH and JAK-STAT signaling associated genes (Figure 4). We found that the selected genes had recurrent variants in primary TCLs, including TET2 and DNMT3A. Notably, some of the most frequent variants identified for the genes of interest (including TET2 p.R544∗, TET2 p. H1380Y, DNMT3A p.R882H, DNMT3A p.R882C, and IDH1 p.S326F) were located at positions that have also been identified as mutation sites in secondary TCLs after CAR T-cell therapy. This overlap in primary and secondary TCL variants may represent potential key drivers of lymphomagenesis. In an example, the amino acid R882 of DNMT3A has been identified as a hotspot mutation site most frequent in acute myeloid leukemia and may act in a dominant-negative fashion to reduce methyltransferase activity as its mechanism of action.17 The secondary CAR T-cell–related TCL case with the DNMT3A p.R882C mutation was both negative for CAR expression and found to have a preexisting heterozygous DNMT3A clone that was also hemizygous for TET2 L1212Vfs∗15, and these mutations were present in myeloid lineages, which indicated an underlying CH with an original clone derived from a HSC or progenitor cell.26 This case suggests that vector transduction is not necessary for malignant transformation. In contrast, another recent secondary TCL case published in 2025 identified both CAR vector integration as well as the mutations in CH-associated genes, including TET2 p.Q916∗ (present in primary TCL variants; supplemental Data) and EZH2 p.W629∗, which were absent in pre–CAR T-cell samples,5 although the possibility that these mutations were present below the limit of detection should be considered. Secondary TCLs by CAR construct type and vector integration status

To further examine the role of the CAR construct in the pathogenesis of secondary TCLs, we performed summary count analyses by CAR construct type and vector integration status (Table 2). Descriptive patterns in the available data suggest potential differences across CAR T-cell products in the frequency of CAR expression detection in secondary TCLs. Notably, secondary TCLs arising after ciltacabtagene autoleucel (cilta-cel) and tisagenlecleucel (tisa-cel) therapy had detectable CAR expression reported in the available cases (7/7 for cilta-cel and 3/3 for tisa-cel). However, whether this expression is functionally relevant to oncogenesis remains uncertain. CAR positivity as a binary variable does not necessarily establish a causal role, and it has been noted that detection of insertion sites in leukemic cells after gene therapy does not always imply a contribution to transformation.27 The level of CAR expression, rather than positivity alone, may be a more relevant parameter, although this has not been systematically quantified across patients. In contrast, secondary TCLs arising from axi-cel therapy did not typically have CAR integration reported (0/3 for axi-cel), although again, definitive mechanistic conclusions cannot be drawn from these small numbers. Any apparent dichotomy between products may point toward differences in CAR construct design, costimulatory domains, or vector integration patterns as candidate variables, but must be considered as hypothesis-generating only. Notably, this analysis is further limited by the fact that CAR vector integration status was not formally evaluated in all patients, and by the relatively low number of samples. Moreover, there was substantial methodological heterogeneity across studies in how CAR expression was assessed, including differences in flow cytometry parameters, immunohistochemical approaches, and molecular detection strategies, which further constrains cross-study comparisons and preclude firm mechanistic conclusions. Discussion

The shared mutational landscape between CH and certain TCL subtypes suggests common pathways of dysregulation. In primary TCL disease, mutations in CH-associated genes may contribute to T-cell transformation through direct effects on T-cell development, altered cytokine signaling, or immune surveillance evasion. In the CAR T-cell therapy and cancer treatment context, preexisting CH clones may exhibit altered sensitivity to prior lines of chemotherapy as well as CAR T-cell–mediated inflammation that promotes clonal expansion and subsequent malignant transformation. Secondary TCLs may also result from the acquisition of genetic lesions with oncogenic potential in CH-associated genes or because of insertional mutagenesis during delivery of the CAR construct. The role of age-related immune dysfunction also cannot be overlooked, as CH prevalence and T-cell malignancy incidence both increase with age-related changes in immunosenescence, DNA repair mechanisms, and stem cell fitness, which may create a permissive environment for both CH expansion and malignant transformation.

TET2 and DNMT3A alterations, in particular, appear to be enriched in both primary and secondary TCLs. TET2 encodes a dioxygenase enzyme responsible for catalyzing the conversion of 5-methylcytosine to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, thereby facilitating DNA demethylation and epigenetic regulation of gene expression.28 Loss-of-function mutations in TET2 disrupt normal DNA methylation patterns and alter the differentiation and self-renewal capacity of HSCs.29 DNMT3A also regulates DNA methylation to influence HSC differentiation and self-renewal programs.30 These genes are the most frequently mutated CH genes in healthy older adults without a known hematologic cancer or other clonal state,31,32 and TET2 and DNMT3A may also synergize to precipitate T-cell oncogenesis.33 Epigenetic dysregulation therefore plays a key role in both CH and lymphomagenesis. It should be noted, however, that the frequent occurrence of TET2, DNMT3A, and other CH-associated gene mutations may potentially develop in context of antecedent CH or represent de novo, lineage-restricted driver events, and to resolve this distinction studies will need to incorporate longitudinal premalignant sampling, multilineage clonality analysis, or both.

An important aspect of CH biology is the nonuniform distribution of somatic mutations across hematopoietic lineages, which has implications for interpreting VAFs measured in bulk specimens and for inferring the putative cell of origin of TCLs. VAFs of CH-associated mutations have been shown to be higher in myeloid and NK cells compared with B-cell and T-cell populations in peripheral blood.34,35 Despite this myeloid bias, detection of CH-associated TET2 and DNMT3A mutations within T-cell lineages, as documented in several secondary TCL cases, is consistent with an HSC origin. In principle, if a mutation is present in an HSC, presumably some lymphocytes will carry the mutation. Indeed, in-depth single-cell DNA sequencing and lineage-tracing analysis in the study by Hamilton et al found DNMT3A and TET2 clones across all cell populations including those of lymphoid and myeloid lineages, suggesting that the founder population was in HSCs, which then propagated into the common lymphoid and myeloid progenitors.26 However, the possibility that CH-associated mutations may be acquired in more committed T-cell progenitors cannot be excluded and merits future investigation. For primary TCLs, the coexistence of CH-associated mutations in the tumor may similarly reflect either a shared multipotent progenitor origin or, alternatively, mutation acquisition at a partially committed lymphoid stage. The extent to which circulating T-cell clones harbor CH-associated mutations in patients with measurable CH remains an underexplored question with direct relevance to TCL pathogenesis and risk stratification.

Recent studies on the clinical associations between CH and cancer have found that immune cells carrying somatic mutations in CH driver genes can infiltrate the tumor microenvironment.36,37 T-cell trafficking to lymph nodes and to tumor sites, in addition to tumorigenic processes, has also been associated with elevated inflammatory markers commonly associated with CH such as interleukin-6.38,39 These data suggest that cells harboring CH mutations may have potential for specific tissue or tumor trafficking and, particularly with additional oncogenic alterations, may develop tissue residency patterns that directly influence the anatomical distribution of resulting secondary malignancies. For instance, in the case reported by Aleman et al, dual TET2 and EZH2 loss-of-function mutations were associated with CCR4 overexpression and concomitant skin involvement.5 Given that skin involvement appears to be common in patients with CAR+ secondary TCLs with aggressive courses,40,41 it remains to be seen if tissue-specific distribution patterns will continue to be observed as new cases emerge and whether tissue-homing characteristics are related to molecular signatures imprinted during preexisting CH.

TET2 and other cancer-associated targets are currently being explored in cell therapy design because of their additional roles in enhancing T-cell responses.42, 43, 44 The study by Jain et al found that TET2 disruption can enhance the efficacy and proliferation of human CAR T-cells, but that the functional enhancement depends on the CAR construct itself.42 They found that CAR designs that promote greater persistence by incorporating 4-1BB signaling or through transcription from the TRAC-locus had a higher chance of establishing a hyperproliferative state with TET2 editing compared with retrovirally transduced CD28-based CARs,42 representing a probabilistic fate in which longer-lived CAR T-cells had a greater likelihood of developing rare hyperproliferative breakthrough clones through the BATF3/MYC axis. Interestingly, the secondary TCLs in our analysis that had evidence of vector integration, tended to arise from cilta-cel or tisa-cel, both of which use 4-1BB costimulatory domains, compared with axi-cel, which is based on CD28 costimulation.45 Although it should be noted that not all secondary TCLs derived from 4-1BB CAR T-cells carried TET2 alterations, this signal suggests that perhaps a similar probabilistic fate plays a role in humans whereby greater persistence CAR T-cells have a higher likelihood of developing genomic profiles that lead to malignancy. Altogether, these data suggest that targets such as TET2 should be rigorously tested for safety and closely monitored in clinical settings.

Several limitations of this study merit discussion. First, secondary TCL cases with identifiable and clinically relevant molecular alterations are more likely to be reported and are subject to publication and ascertainment bias. Second, the AACR GENIE-derived primary TCL cohort was heterogeneous, comprising multiple subtypes contributed from different institutions using diverse targeted sequencing panels and variant-calling pipelines, which introduces differential gene coverage as a potential confounder. Third, cohort-wide mutation frequency data from cBioPortal are reported at the sample level, introducing potential nonindependence that could inflate apparent mutation frequencies. Fourth, product-level observations regarding CAR construct type and vector integration status are based on small case numbers, lack denominator data, and are subject to confounders including differences in patient selection, prior therapy, disease biology, and institutional testing practices. These observations are therefore primarily exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and prospective mechanistic studies and longitudinal clinical data will be required to determine the directional and functional relationships between CH and T-cell lymphomagenesis.

Secondary TCLs after CAR T-cell therapy represent very rare but clinically significant complications. Ongoing surveillance and research are essential to better understand the underlying mechanisms and risk factors. Screening for CH and other potential safety signals, such as those in the JAK-STAT pathway, before and after CAR T-cell treatment may enhance both risk assessment and treatment optimization for patients with TCLs. For example, the US Food and Drug Administration–approved agents include inhibitors for IDH1, IDH2, and JAK proteins, which may represent targeted treatment approaches, and hypomethylating agents have demonstrated increased efficacy against TET2 mutant myeloid malignancies.46 Patients with high CH burden may also benefit from modified conditioning regimens or enhanced posttreatment monitoring for secondary primary malignancies. Monitoring of CH before CAR T-cell therapy may also aid in risk stratification for other complications such as inflammatory cytopenias and myeloid disease.12,23,24 With respect to the modality of CH screening, testing of bulk peripheral blood or apheresis material using targeted sequencing represents the most practical and clinically actionable approach, and has the advantage of providing insight into myeloid-compartment CH dynamics that are relevant to non-TCL complications including cytopenias and secondary myeloid neoplasms. However, given the myeloid bias of many CH mutations, VAFs measured in bulk blood may overestimate the burden of CH within the T-cell compartment. For the specific purpose of characterizing CH clone size and dynamics within T-cells, analysis of lineage-separated or purified T-cell fractions would provide more granular data. Future prospective studies may incorporate compartment-specific profiling to better resolve the distribution and clonal dynamics of CH in CAR T-cell therapy recipients and to clarify whether CH clone burden within the T-cell fraction specifically correlates with secondary TCL risk. Deep molecular phenotyping of secondary TCLs should also be considered, as previous cases have demonstrated clinical improvements with the anti-CCR4 antibody mogamulizumab5 and the JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib4 based on the TCL molecular profile. Ultimately, a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular landscape of primary and secondary TCLs will be essential for developing predictive biomarkers, targeted interventions, and enhanced monitoring protocols necessary for optimizing patient outcomes.
