Delayed-Onset Life-Threatening Takotsubo Cardiomyopathy Following Pulsed-Field Ablation
Authors: Kazuma Iimura, Atsuhiko Yagishita, Aika Iijima, Mari Amino, Yuji Ikari, Koichiro Yoshioka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulsed-field ablation (PFA) is a novel nonthermal modality for atrial fibrillation ablation. Takotsubo cardiomyopathy (TTC) is associated with autonomic dysregulation, but its relationship with PFA remains unclear.Case SummaryA 62-year-old man underwent redo PFA including pulmonary vein, posterior wall, and superior vena cava isolation. Serial Electrocardiographic and Imaging Findings After Pulsed-Field Ablation

PFA achieved successful pulmonary vein, posterior wall, and superior vena cava isolation, as confirmed by the postablation electroanatomical voltage map (day 1). No significant electrocardiographic abnormalities were observed immediately after the procedure. Three hours later, the patient developed QT prolongation and high-degree atrioventricular block, which progressed to ventricular arrhythmia leading to cardiac arrest. Emergent coronary angiography showed no significant stenosis. Left ventriculography revealed apical ballooning with preserved basal contraction (arrowheads), consistent with Takotsubo cardiomyopathy (day 1). Deep T-wave inversions were observed in the precordial leads on day 2, with gradual normalization by day 68. History of Presentation

A 62-year-old man with recurrent persistent atrial fibrillation (AF) was referred for redo catheter ablation given symptomatic recurrence after a prior pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) performed 2 years earlier. The patient reported intermittent palpitations and reduced exercise tolerance despite medical therapy.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of hypertension and no prior coronary artery disease. There was no history of structural heart disease, heart failure, or prior ventricular arrhythmias. He had no known history of thyroid dysfunction or significant psychiatric or emotional stressors.

Differential Diagnosis

In the setting of sudden hemodynamic collapse and ventricular arrhythmia after ablation, the differential diagnosis included pericardial tamponade, acute coronary syndrome or coronary vasospasm, air embolism, electrolyte imbalance or drug-induced QT prolongation, and Takotsubo cardiomyopathy (TTC).

Investigation

Preprocedural transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 62% with no significant structural abnormalities. Baseline electrocardiogram (ECG) showed sinus rhythm without ST-T abnormalities or QT prolongation (Figure 1). Coronary computed tomography revealed no significant coronary artery stenosis, and no left atrial thrombus was identified.

Management

The pulsed-field ablation (PFA) procedure was performed under conscious sedation with uninterrupted oral anticoagulation. Transseptal access was obtained under intracardiac echocardiographic guidance, and anticoagulation was maintained with an activated clotting time of 350 to 400 seconds. Prophylactic atropine (1 mg) was administered before PFA to mitigate vagal reactions.

PFA was performed using a pentaspline catheter (Farawave, Boston Scientific) with a standardized bipolar energy delivery protocol. Given the diagnosis of recurrent persistent atrial fibrillation, the ablation strategy consisted of redo PVI, posterior wall isolation, and superior vena cava (SVC) isolation after confirming the sinoatrial node location; 4 applications were delivered with catheter rotation. The procedure was completed successfully without immediate complications. No ST-segment changes were observed during or immediately after the procedure.

Three hours after the procedure, the patient developed bradycardia with high-grade atrioventricular block and QT prolongation, which progressed to torsades de pointes and refractory ventricular fibrillation with cardiac arrest (Figure 2). After approximately 25 minutes of resuscitation, return of spontaneous circulation was achieved. Emergency evaluation excluded pericardial tamponade. Coronary angiography revealed no obstructive coronary artery disease, and left ventriculography demonstrated apical ballooning with preserved basal contractility, consistent with TTC. Mechanical circulatory support with an Impella CP device (Abiomed) was initiated for cardiogenic shock, resulting in rapid hemodynamic stabilization. Left ventricular function improved on echocardiography by day 2, allowing successful device weaning by day 5. The patient was extubated on day 10 with full neurological recovery. Acute kidney injury requiring renal replacement therapy resolved by day 32.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged home on day 44 without cardiac or neurological sequelae. ECG findings were normal on day 68 (Figure 3).

Discussion

PFA has rapidly emerged as a novel energy modality for catheter ablation of AF, given its myocardial selectivity and favorable safety profile compared with conventional thermal techniques. By inducing irreversible electroporation, PFA enables effective PVI and posterior wall isolation with minimal collateral injury to adjacent structures.1,2 Large-scale registries such as MANIFEST-US1 and clinical trials including ADVANTAGE AF Phase 22 have established the overall safety of PFA. However, recent reports have described delayed-onset life-threatening myocardial ischemia and malignant arrhythmias after PFA, demonstrating that serious adverse events may occur after an initially uneventful procedure and beyond the immediate periprocedural period.3 These observations suggest that delayed-onset catastrophic events after PFA—whether ischemic or nonischemic, as in the present case—may represent a broader and under-recognized clinical entity related to autonomic or vascular perturbation.

TTC is characterized by catecholamine-mediated myocardial dysfunction associated with sympathetic overactivation.4, 5, 6 Although TTC has been reported after catheter ablation in the era of thermal energy sources,7 recent case reports have also described its occurrence after PFA. However, previously reported PFA-related TTC cases were limited to immediate or mild presentations without hemodynamic compromise.8,9 In contrast, the present case demonstrates a delayed-onset and life-threatening presentation, occurring 3 hours after an initially uneventful procedure and complicated by refractory ventricular fibrillation and cardiogenic shock. The 3-hour delay after ablation argues against direct myocardial injury from electroporation and instead supports a neurohumoral mechanism. Although prior studies have demonstrated that PFA confined to PVI exerts minimal effects on cardiac autonomic tone,10 these findings may not be directly generalizable to more extensive ablation strategies. In the present case, PFA was performed beyond PVI, including posterior wall and SVC isolation, which may involve a broader distribution of ganglionated plexi. These intrinsic cardiac autonomic structures are located not only around the pulmonary vein antra but also along the posterior left atrial wall and near the SVC–right atrial junction, forming an interconnected autonomic network.11, 12, 13 This expanded autonomic engagement could potentially amplify neurohumoral responses and contribute to delayed-onset TTC. Furthermore, prior case reports of TTC during PFA limited to PVI have described immediate and hemodynamically mild presentations, whereas the present case was characterized by delayed-onset and hemodynamic collapse, suggesting a possible mechanistic difference related to the extent of ablation.

The occurrence of a life-threatening event 3 hours after an uncomplicated PFA procedure highlights a potential vulnerability window that is not fully addressed in current practice. In the MANIFEST-US study, 72% of patients were discharged on the same day without mandated extended monitoring,1 and the ADVANTAGE AF Phase 2 trial similarly did not define a structured postprocedural surveillance strategy.2 Given that unexplained cardiac arrests in MANIFEST-US occurred predominantly within the first 3 days,1 the present case suggests that continuous ECG monitoring for at least 12 to 24 hours may represent a pragmatic minimum threshold, particularly in patients undergoing extensive ablation beyond PVI. This duration should not be considered definitive, and a longer period of in-hospital monitoring may be warranted in selected high-risk patients, including those undergoing extensive ablation beyond PVI or demonstrating early postprocedural abnormalities such as QT prolongation or bradycardia. An individualized monitoring strategy based on procedural complexity and patient risk profile may therefore be most appropriate. Early initiation of mechanical circulatory support has been associated with improved outcomes in severe TTC,14 and this case underscores the importance of prompt escalation in the setting of refractory ventricular arrhythmia and cardiogenic shock. Vigilance is essential for the timely recognition and management of delayed-onset TTC after PFA, particularly as this technology is increasingly applied to more extensive ablation strategies.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. First, this is a single-case observation, and therefore causal relationships between PFA and the development of TTC cannot be definitively established. Second, the proposed autonomic mechanism remains speculative, as direct measurements of catecholamine levels or autonomic function were not performed. Third, detailed ECG documentation of the ventricular arrhythmia was limited, precluding precise characterization of the arrhythmic sequence. Finally, procedural factors such as conscious sedation and pharmacologic interventions may have influenced autonomic tone and contributed to the observed clinical course. Despite these limitations, the temporal association and comprehensive imaging findings provide important insights into a potentially under-recognized complication of PFA.

Conclusions

We report a case of delayed, life-threatening Takotsubo cardiomyopathy occurring 3 hours after PFA, presenting as bradycardia, torsades de pointes, and refractory ventricular fibrillation requiring mechanical circulatory support. This case indicates that serious delayed complications may occur after an initially uneventful PFA procedure and suggests a potential role of autonomic or neurohumoral mechanisms, particularly in the setting of extensive lesion sets. These findings support the concept that delayed catastrophic events after PFA may represent an under-recognized clinical entity beyond the immediate periprocedural period.
