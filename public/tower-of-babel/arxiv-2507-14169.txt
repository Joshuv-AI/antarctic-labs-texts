Abstract
We propose a novel interference prediction scheme to improve link adaptation (LA) in densely deployed industrial sub-networks (SNs) with high-reliability and low-latency communication (HRLLC) requirements. The proposed method aims to improve the LA framework by predicting and leveraging the heavy-tailed interference probability density function (pdf). Interference is modeled as a latent vector of available channel quality indicator (CQI), using a vector discrete-time state-space model (vDSSM) at the SN controller, where the CQI is subjected to compression, quantization, and delay-induced errors. To robustly estimate interference power values under these impairments, we employ a low-complexity, outlier-robust, sparse Student-t process regression (SPTPR) method. This is integrated into a modified unscented Kalman filter, which recursively refines predicted interference using CQI, enabling accurate estimation and compensating protocol feedback delays—crucial for accurate LA. Numerical results show that the proposed method achieves over 10× lower complexity compared to a similar non-parametric baseline. It also maintains a BLER below the 90th percentile target of 10 − 6 10^{-6} while delivering performance comparable to a state-of-the-art supervised technique using only CQI reports.



I
Introduction

Link adaptation (LA)
is a fundamental feature in wireless communication systems, enabling the selection of
modulation and coding scheme (MCS)
to optimize data rates and ensure reliability under varying channel conditions. The
LA
problem has been effectively managed in services like enhanced mobile broadband (eMBB) with relaxed latency and reliability requirements
[
1
]
. However, this method becomes inadequate in emerging scenarios such as industrial
sub-networks (SNs)
, which require closed-loop control supported by short-range communications under
hyper-reliable, low-latency communication (HRLLC)
traffic with strict latency (0.1–1 ms) and reliability requirements
[
2
]
. To achieve extremely low
block error rate (BLER)
, smaller
outer-loop link adaptation (OLLA)
step sizes are needed. This results in slow convergence
[
3
]
, further exacerbated by significant
signal to interference plus noise ratio (SINR)
fluctuations caused by co-subband interference stemming from limited subbands, dense deployments, and mobility. Moreover, low latency requirements of
SNs
severely limit packet retransmissions, making
acknowledgement (ACK)
/non-
ACK
feedback less practical and further complicating
LA
. To address these limitations, we propose a predictive
LA
scheme tailored for interference-prone
SNs
, enabling single transmissions under stringent latency and reliability constraints by efficiently leveraging the underlying
probability density function (pdf)
of the inter-SN
interference power value (IPV)
.
IPV
prediction has been widely studied, but as this work focuses on
SN controller (SNC)
-side (access point (AP)-side)
LA
enhancement, we limit discussion to studies leveraging predicted
IPV
for
LA

[
4
]
. In
[
5
]
, the authors proposed a
user equipment (UE)
-side method (as
sensor-actuator pair (SA)
in this work) by incorporating historical interference estimates into
SINR
using a first-order low-pass filter. Brighente et al.
[
1
]
proposed low-complexity UE-side prediction with Gaussian kernel methods, though the Gaussian assumption limits generalization due to interference’s heavy-tailed distribution. Wei et al.
[
6
]
combine seasonal and trend decomposition using locally estimated scatterplot smoothing and require a
gated recurrent unit (GRU)
-based prediction. They require three GRU models per
SA
, raising scalability concerns for low-form-factor SNs with multiple
SAs
. Although not directly an
IPV
prediction scheme, the authors in
[
7
]
propose an
access point (AP)
side Wiener filtering based
channel quality indicator (CQI)
prediction for
long term evolution (LTE)
.

In our recent work, we proposed a scalar

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{n}(t)|\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{n}(t))\preceq\bar{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}.

(1)

) at the

stems from inaccurate interference representation due to compression losses in

IV
Dynamic State Space Modeling

We propose a
vDSSM
at the
SNC
to estimate heavy-tailed
IPV

CQI
reports from associated
SAs
. Specifically, the

at the
n^{\text{th}}

SNC
serving
M

is given by:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\iota}_{n}(t):=F(\boldsymbol{\iota}_{n}(t-1),\boldsymbol{\beta}(t)),

(2a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{n}(t):=G(\boldsymbol{\iota}_{n}(t),\mathbf{u}(t)).

(2b)

The process model in (
2a
) describes the evolution of the
IPV
over
Tx

. The measurement model in (
2b
) relates the outdated
CQI
report of the
n
-th
SN

\mathbf{y}_{n}(t):=[y_{m_{1}n}(t),\dotsc,y_{m_{M}n}(t)]^{T}
, to the

\boldsymbol{\iota}_{n}(t):=[\iota_{m_{1}n}(t),\dotsc,\iota_{m_{M}n}(t)]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times M}

represent the measurement and process noise, respectively. The time-varying positions and temporal correlations render closed-form modeling of

[
10
]
. We adopt a data-driven strategy to learn the unknown process and measurement models directly from observed

and

to model

with covariance
\mathbf{C}_{G}
, and

with covariance
\mathbf{C}_{F}
to capture dynamics across

in

[

]

V
Sparse Student-t Process regression

We propose to utilize a Bayesian framework with a non-parametric probabilistic model, i.e.,
Gaussian Process Regression (GPR)
-based technique to learn the process and measurement models
F(\cdot)
and
G(\cdot)
, along with their noise covariances
\mathbf{C}_{F}
and
\mathbf{C}_{G}

[
11
]
.
GPR
faces two key challenges in
IPV
prediction for industrial
SN
: (i)
IPV
often deviates from Gaussian assumption, whose rapidly decaying tails may under-represent rare interference events—becoming more severe under
LA
optimization constraint in
section

III

[
1
,
8
]
; and (ii) learning highly dynamic interference requires more data, making
GPR
computationally prohibitive due to its
\mathcal{O}(L^{3})
complexity from matrix inversion, where
L
is training instances. To address these issues, we adopt sparse Student-t process regression (SPTPR) with a heavy-tailed prior within a Bayesian framework. The Student-t distribution (by dropping subscript
\tau
and
n
for ease of notation) is defined as:

Definition 1
.

The Student-t distribution with
\nu
degrees of freedom and
\theta
scale parameter for
m
-th SA is defined as
[
12
]

p(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{m}|\mathbf{f}_{m})=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)\sqrt{\nu\pi}\theta}\left(1+\frac{(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{m}-\mathbf{f}_{m})^{2}}{\nu\theta^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}},

(3)

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the gamma function,
\mathbf{f}_{m}=[f_{m}({\iota}_{m}(t))]
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{m}=[y_{m}(t)]\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times L}
are the function and corresponding observations over discrete
Tx
cycles
t=1,\dots,L
.

Since the Student-t distribution is consistent under marginalization,
Student-t Process Regression (TPR)
can be defined using Kolmogorov’s consistency theorem as follows
[
13
]
:

Definition 2
.

A function
f_{m}(\cdot)
is a Student-t process with parameter
\nu>2
, mean function
\mathbf{\mu}(\cdot)
, and kernel
k(\cdot)
, the finite collection of function values has a joint multivariate Student-t distribution with scale
\nu
, mean
\mathbf{\mu}(\cdot)
, and covariance
K
, where
K_{t,t^{\prime}}=k({\iota}_{m}(t),{\iota}_{m}(t^{\prime}))
is represented as
\mathbf{f}_{m}\sim\mathcal{TP}(\nu,\mathbf{\mu},K)
.

TPR effectively models heavy-tailed interference and remains robust to outliers, making it suitable for sporadic traffic scenarios
[
12
]
. In the context of interference prediction in ultra-dense deployments,
IPV
may not exhibit inherent sparsity. To ensure computational scalability, we employ
SPTPR
w.r.t. training data, which efficiently approximates the full posterior by selecting
L_{E}\ll L
inducing points from
L
training samples
<\boldsymbol{\delta}_{m},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{m}>
with
L
sequential instances of
\iota_{m}
and
y_{m}
respectively, thereby capturing sufficient statistics for inference. Specifically, we choose
L_{E}\ll L
inducing points, denoted as
\mathbf{Z}_{m}\in R^{1\times L_{E}}
, with corresponding function values
\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}}:=[f_{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{Z}_{m}(e))\mid 1\leq e\leq L_{E}]
. For simplicity, we denote
\mathbf{f}_{m}
instead of
\mathbf{f}_{m}(\cdot)
. The conditional distribution
p(\mathbf{f}_{m}\mid\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}})
is defined as
[
12
]
:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{f}_{m}|\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}})=\mathcal{SPT}\left(\nu+L_{E},\mu,\frac{\nu+\eta-2}{\nu+L_{E}-2}\times\Xi\right),

(4)

where mean
\mu=K(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{m},{\mathbf{Z}_{m}})[K({\mathbf{Z}_{m}},{\mathbf{Z}_{m}})+\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I}]^{-1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}}
,
\eta=\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}}^{T}[K({\mathbf{Z}_{m}},{\mathbf{Z}_{m}})+\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I}]^{-1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}}
and covariance
\Xi=K(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{m},{\mathbf{Z}_{m}})-K(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{m},{\mathbf{Z}_{m}})[K({\mathbf{Z}_{m}},{\mathbf{Z}_{m}})+\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I}]^{-1}K({\mathbf{Z}_{m}},\boldsymbol{\delta}_{m})
. This conditional distribution
p(\mathbf{f}_{m}|\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}})
enables us to introduce the inducing points to compute the posterior distribution as follows:

p(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}}|\mathbf{f}_{m},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{m})=\dfrac{p(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{m}|\mathbf{f}_{m})p(\mathbf{f}_{m}|\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}})p(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}})}{\int p(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{m}|\mathbf{f}_{m})p(\mathbf{f}_{m}|\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}})p(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}})d\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}}}.

(5)

To approximate the intractable posterior distribution
p(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}}|\mathbf{f}_{m},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{m})
, we introduce a variational distribution
g(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}})
with the variational parameter, such that the approximate posterior closely resembles the true posterior. To determine the variational parameters in
g(\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}})
, we minimize the negative
Kullback-Leibler (KL)
divergence between the true and variational posteriors using the reparameterization technique as follows:

\log(p(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{m}))=\overbrace{\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{f}_{m}|{\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}}})\cdot g({\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}}})}[\log p(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{m}|\mathbf{f}_{m})]}^{\text{Expected Likelihood function}}-\\
\underbrace{KL(g({\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}}})||p({\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{Z}_{m}}}))}_{\text{KL Regularization}}.

(6)

Optimal hyperparameters are obtained via supervised training for the measurement model; the dynamic model is omitted for brevity but derived analogously. A trained
SPTPR
approximates both measurement and dynamic models
eq.

7
, reducing complexity from
\mathcal{O}(L^{3})
to
\mathcal{O}(L_{E}^{2}\cdot L)
.

VI
Proposed interference predictor

We propose an interference predictor that integrates heavy-tailed statistics, along with functional and covariance modeling, within a Bayesian framework, providing robustness to sporadic interference through
SPTPR
. Specifically, the dynamic and measurement model is approximated as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\iota}_{n}(t)\approx\mathcal{STP}^{F}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\iota}}_{n}(t-1))+\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(t),

(7a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{n}(t)\approx\mathcal{STP}^{G}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\iota}}_{n}(t))+\hat{\mathbf{u}}(t),

(7b)

where, noise characterized by
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(t)\sim\mathcal{TP}(0,\mathcal{STP}^{F}_{\Sigma}({\boldsymbol{\iota}}_{n}(t-1)),\nu^{F})
and

\hat{\mathbf{u}}(t)\sim\mathcal{TP}(0,\mathcal{STP}^{G}_{\Sigma}({\boldsymbol{\iota}}_{n}(t)),\nu^{G})
. The ideal outcome is for
\mathcal{STP}^{F}_{\mu}
and
\mathcal{STP}^{G}_{\mu}
to approach to
F(\cdot)
and
G(\cdot)
and for
\mathcal{STP}^{F}_{\Sigma}
and
\mathcal{STP}^{G}_{\Sigma}
is approach to
\mathbf{C}_{F}
and
\mathbf{C}_{G}
. For brevity, we define
\mathcal{STP}^{F}_{\mu}=[\mathcal{STP}^{f_{1}}_{\mu},\dotsc,\mathcal{STP}^{f_{M}}_{\mu}]\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times M}
and the diagonally loaded matrix
\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{F}=\text{diag}[\mathcal{STP}^{f_{1}}_{\Sigma},\dotsc,\mathcal{STP}^{f_{M}}_{\Sigma}]\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
, with analogous definitions applied to the measurement model. This covariance modeling allows flexibility to incorporate intra-
SN
interference by updating the off-diagonal elements of the matrix. However, in this work, we assume minimal or negligible intra-
SN
interference, owing to the use of an orthogonal scheduler as discussed in
section

II
. Furthermore, unlike conventional architectures that include a dequantizer to recover
eSNR
values from
CQI
reports, our proposed technique omits it, as the
SPTPR
directly infers interference from
CQI
based on
eq.

7b
. Given the heavy-tailed and nonlinear nature of both
eq.

7a
and
eq.

7b
, we employ the
modified unscented Kalman filter (MUKF)
, which uses the modified unscented transform to accurately propagate mean and covariance through the nonlinear dynamics, rather than relying on linear approximations as in
[
4
]
. To efficiently handle heavy-tailed Student-t estimates, we adopt the sigma-point based on
[
14
]

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathbf{x}_{m}^{i}=\mu_{m},~\omega_{m}^{i,\mu}=\frac{\kappa}{s+\kappa},~\omega_{{m}}^{i,\Sigma}=\frac{\kappa}{s+\kappa}+\left(1-\alpha^{2}+\beta\right),~i=0\\
&\mathbf{x}_{m}^{i}=\mu_{m}+\sqrt{\frac{\nu(s+\kappa)}{\nu-2}}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\mathbf{e}_{i},~\omega_{m}^{i,\mu}=\omega_{{m}}^{i,\Sigma}=\frac{0.5}{s+\kappa}~i=1,\cdots,s\\
&\mathbf{x}_{m}^{i,\Sigma}=\mu_{m}-\sqrt{\frac{\nu(s+\kappa)}{\nu-2}}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\mathbf{e}_{i},~\omega_{m}^{i,\mu}=\omega_{{m}}^{i,\Sigma}=\frac{0.5}{s+\kappa}~i=s+1,\cdots,2s\end{aligned}\right.

where

are the parameters of the

,

is the square-root matrix of covariance matrix, and
\mathbf{e}_{i}
denotes the

th column vector of a unit matrix for each

with
2s
sigma points. The terms
\mu_{m}:=\mathcal{STP}^{f_{m}}_{\mu_{m}}
(dynamic model) and

(measurement model) are defined accordingly. The sigma points

VIII
Conclusions

We proposed SPTPR-MUKF, a semi-supervised interference prediction method for link adaptation in industrial subnetworks, leveraging CQI to infer heavy-tailed interference via a sparse Student-t process within a
vDSSM
. The approach achieves near-optimal performance with over 10× lower complexity, while remaining robust to protocol delays, outliers, and scalable under dense deployments. Future work will explore real-time deployment, incorporating random CQI acquisition delays and generalization across diverse deployment scenarios.
