Abstract
. In this paper we complete Büchi’s proof that there is no decision algorithm for the solubility in integers of arbitrary systems of diagonal quadratic form equations, by proving the assertion that whenever x 1 2 , ⋯ , x 5 2 x_{1}^{2},\cdots,x_{5}^{2} are five squares such that the second differences satisfy x k + 2 2 − 2 ​ x k + 1 2 + x k 2 = 2 x_{k+2}^{2}-2x_{k+1}^{2}+x_{k}^{2}=2 for k = 1 , 2 , 3 k=1,2,3 , then they must be consecutive. This answers a question of J. Richard Büchi.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

There is no algorithm which decides the existence of integer solutions to an arbitrary system of diagonal quadratic form equations.

Theorem
1.1
is a refinement of the negative solution to Hilbert’s tenth problem, proved by the celebrated work of Davis, Putnam, and Robinson and Matiyasevich (see
[
3
]
). In particular, as opposed to an arbitrary system of polynomial equations defined over
{\mathbb{Z}}
, the solubility of even the simplest non-linear systems, namely those defined by a system of diagonal quadratic forms, cannot be decided. This also answers a question of J. L. Britton in
[
1
]
.

J. Richard Büchi proved Theorem
1.1
, provided that the following statement, known as
Büchi’s problem
, holds:

Conjecture 1.2

(Büchi’s problem)
.

There exists a positive integer
N
such that if
x_{1}^{2},\cdots,x_{n}^{2}
is a sequence of increasing squares with constant second difference equal to
2
, then
n\leq N
.

Conjecture
1.2
was proved by P. Vojta
[
8
]
with
N=8
under the assumption of the Bombieri–Lang conjecture. Büchi’s argument was reproduced in
[
8
]
.

Our principal contribution is an unconditional proof of Conjecture
1.2
, with
N=4
:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
x_{1}^{2},\cdots,x_{5}^{2}
be integer squares with constant second difference equal to
2
. Then there exists an integer
x_{0}
such that
x_{j}^{2}=(x_{0}+j)^{2}
for
j=1,2,3,4,5
.

Theorem
1.3
directly answers a question of J. R. Büchi in its original form (see
[
2
]
and
[
4
]
, and
[
5
]
for a more recent account).

Büchi proved that there is no algorithm which decides the existence of integral solutions to
systems of diagonal quadratic form equations
of the shape

(1.1)

\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{ij}x_{j}^{2}=b_{i}

for
i=1,\cdots,M
and
j=1,\cdots,N,a_{ij},b_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, provided that Conjecture
1.2
holds.

Theorem
1.3
is the best possible, since it is known that there exist infinitely many quadruples of squares with second difference equal to
2
, which do not consist of consecutive squares. Geometrically, the homogeneous system of equations

(1.2)

\begin{matrix}x_{3}^{2}&-&2x_{2}^{2}&+&x_{1}^{2}&=&2x_{0}^{2}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
x_{n}^{2}&-&2x_{n-1}^{2}&+&x_{n-2}^{2}&=&2x_{0}^{2}\end{matrix}

defines a projective surface
X_{n}
of general type when
n\geq 6
, but not when
n\leq 5
. Indeed
X_{3}
is simply a quadric surface.
X_{4}
is a del Pezzo surface of degree
4
, and
X_{5}
is a
K3
surface. Thus, it is expected that there may be infinitely many non-trivial solutions when
n\leq 4
. Indeed, infinite parametric families exist. A classical example, due to D. Hensley (unpublished), is given by the parametrization

(1.3)

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=(2t^{3}+12t^{2}+19t+6,2t^{3}+14t^{2}+31t+23,2t^{3}+16t^{2}+41t+32,2t^{3}+18t^{2}+49t+39),t\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

A generalization which produces many other polynomial parametrized families of non-trivial quadruples of four squares with finite difference equal to
2
was produced by X. Vidaux in
[
7
]
.

We note that the proof of Theorem
1.3
shows that the affine variety
\widetilde{X_{5}}
defined by specializing
x_{0}=1
has no integer points outside the closed subset consisting of trivial tuples. Since
X_{5}
is a K3 surface,
\widetilde{X_{5}}
is
log-general type
, so an analogue of Lang’s conjecture, interpreted as a higher dimensional analogue of Siegel’s theorem on integer points on algebraic curves of positive genus, would indicate that
\widetilde{X_{5}}
has no integer points outside of a finite union of curves.

Since
X_{5}
is a K3 surface and not a surface of general type, the Bombieri–Lang conjecture does not apply, and so our argument is necessarily quite disjoint from that of Vojta in
[
8
]
.

We go further than previous authors in the triple and quadruple cases, where we obtain a complete description of all possible tuples. To this end, we define:

Definition 1.4
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. We say an
n
-tuple
(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})
of positive integers is a
Büchi
n
-tuple
if
u_{1}^{2},\cdots,u_{n}^{2}
is an increasing sequence of squares with second difference equal to
2
.

We have the following characterization of Büchi triples.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
be a Büchi triple. Then there exist non-negative integers
s_{1},s_{3},s_{4},s_{6}
and
\beta\in\{1,2\}
satisfying
s_{1}s_{3}-s_{4}s_{6}=\beta
, such that

u_{1}=\frac{s_{1}s_{3}-s_{3}s_{4}+s_{1}s_{6}+s_{4}s_{6}}{2\beta},u_{2}=\frac{s_{3}s_{4}+s_{1}s_{6}}{2\beta},u_{3}=\frac{s_{1}s_{3}+s_{3}s_{4}-s_{1}s_{6}+s_{4}s_{6}}{2\beta}.

Similarly, we obtain the following theorem capturing Büchi quadruples.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3},u_{4})
be a Büchi quadruple. Then there exist non-negative integers
s_{0},s_{1},\cdots,s_{15}
,
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\{1,2\}
, and
\delta\in\{1,3\}
satisfying the relations

(1.4)

\begin{matrix}s_{8}&=&\dfrac{(3/\delta)s_{4}s_{11}-s_{1}s_{14}}{\beta_{1}(2/\beta_{2})s_{7}}&s_{13}&=&\dfrac{s_{4}s_{11}+\delta s_{1}s_{14}}{\beta_{1}(2/\beta_{2})s_{2}}\\
\\
s_{3}&=&\dfrac{(s_{1}s_{14}+(3/\delta)s_{4}s_{11})s_{9}-2s_{4}s_{14}s_{12}}{2s_{2}s_{7}}&s_{6}&=&\dfrac{2s_{1}s_{11}s_{9}-(\delta s_{1}s_{14}-s_{4}s_{11})s_{12}}{2s_{2}s_{7}}\\
\\
s_{0}&=&\dfrac{s_{9}s_{11}-\delta s_{12}s_{14}}{\beta_{1}s_{2}}&s_{5}&=&\dfrac{(3/\delta)s_{9}s_{11}-s_{12}s_{14}}{\beta_{1}s_{7}}\\
\\
s_{10}&=&\dfrac{s_{1}s_{9}+s_{4}s_{12}}{\beta_{1}s_{2}}&s_{15}&=&\dfrac{-s_{1}s_{9}+s_{4}s_{12}}{\beta_{1}s_{7}}\end{matrix}

with

\begin{matrix}x_{1}&=&s_{0}s_{2}s_{4}s_{6}s_{8}s_{10}s_{12}s_{14},&y_{1}&=&s_{1}s_{3}s_{5}s_{7}s_{9}s_{11}s_{13}s_{15},\\
x_{2}&=&s_{0}s_{1}s_{4}s_{5}s_{8}s_{9}s_{12}s_{13},&y_{2}&=&s_{2}s_{3}s_{6}s_{7}s_{10}s_{11}s_{14}s_{15},\\
x_{3}&=&s_{0}s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}s_{8}s_{9}s_{10}s_{11},&y_{3}&=&s_{4}s_{5}s_{6}s_{7}s_{12}s_{13}s_{14}s_{15},\\
x_{4}&=&s_{0}s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}s_{4}s_{5}s_{6}s_{7},&y_{4}&=&s_{8}s_{9}s_{10}s_{11}s_{12}s_{13}s_{14}s_{15}.\end{matrix}

such that

u_{k}=x_{k}-y_{k}

for
k=1,2,3,4
.

Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
are crucial to show the non-existence of non-trivial Büchi quintuples. In particular, the same argument used to prove Theorem
1.6
is also used to prove the so-called
calibration lemmas
, namely Lemmas
6.3
,
6.4
, and
6.5
.

1.1.
Outline of proof of Theorem
1.3

In this subsection we describe our strategy to proving Theorem
1.3
.

We begin by considering a slightly more general problem: in Proposition
2.5
we show that to obtain a non-trivial sequence of squares with finite second difference equal to
2
is tantamount to showing that a certain monic quadratic polynomial takes on square values at consecutive integer inputs. This compels Definition
3.1
, which gives our notion of an
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs
.

Next, we go step-by-step towards our goal by understanding triples and quadruples of Büchi pairs. We introduce a bijection
\sigma_{n}
between
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n}
and
\{0,1,\cdots,2^{n}-1\}
given by (
4.1
). This allows us to introduce the notion of a
parametrizing sequence
of an
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs; see (
4.3
). We note that a
non-trivial

n
-tuple of Büchi pairs must necessarily have a parametrizing sequence consisting of only
positive
entries; see Proposition
4.2
.

We already know that there are infinitely many non-trivial Büchi quadruples thanks to the work of Hensley and more recently Vidaux
[
7
]
, so we know
a priori
to expect plenty of quadruples of Büchi pairs. However, Definition
3.1
provides us with sufficient structure that we can
parametrize
all triples. The statement is given as Proposition
4.4
.

We then use the crucial observation that a quadruple of Büchi pairs is obtained by weaving two triples together, with each triple given by a parametrization supplied by Proposition
4.4
. This allows us to obtain substantial control over the structure of the quadruples. In particular, the parametrizing sequence of a non-trivial quadruple of Büchi pairs must satisfy the so-called
Pfaffian equation
, namely (
5.7
).

Finally, to address the non-existence of non-trivial quintuples of Büchi pairs, it suffices to show that there is no parametrizing sequence of a quintuple of Büchi pairs consisting of only positive entries. Any such sequence would have to satisfy
five
different Pfaffian equations, namely equations (
6.18
), (
6.19
), (
6.28
), (
6.39
), and (
6.48
). This imposes an incredible strain on the potential parametrizing sequence
{\mathbf{s}}
. Indeed, these equations lead to the so-called
structural equations
, given in Proposition
7.1
.

The system given by Proposition
7.1
is a system of ten bilinear equations in the variables
t_{j}=s_{j}s_{31-j},1\leq j\leq 15
. The two sets of variables are

S_{\heartsuit}=\{t_{1},t_{2},t_{4},t_{8},t_{15}\}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{\spadesuit}=\{t_{3},t_{5},t_{6},t_{7},t_{9},t_{10},t_{11},t_{12},t_{13},t_{14}\}.

By treating the variables in
S_{\heartsuit}
as fixed and viewing (
7.1
) as a linear system in
S_{\spadesuit}
, we obtain a
10\times 10
matrix
M
given by (
7.5
). This matrix is always singular, but if it has rank equal to
9
, then the unique (up to scalar multiplication) null-vector will not lead to a non-trivial parametrizing sequence, because some of the entries will be zero. Thus we require the rank to drop below
9
, which necessitates a relation on some of the so-called
structural constants
(see Proposition
7.2
).

Viewing
S_{\spadesuit}
as fixed, then (
7.1
) gives a
10\times 5
linear system, determined by the matrix
{\mathcal{M}}
in (
7.6
). For a non-trivial parametrizing tuple to exist,
{\mathcal{M}}
must not be full rank. This would require every
5\times 5
sub-matrix of
{\mathcal{M}}
to have vanishing determinant.

There are
\binom{10}{5}=252
such sub-determinants, and many of them give seemingly no information. However, miraculously, many of the sub-determinants of
{\mathcal{M}}
factor into linear and bilinear terms in the
t_{j}
’s. Studying a relatively small subset of the sub-determinants, found by ad hoc methods, allows one to deduce that the price to pay for a non-trivial parametrizing sequence for a quintuple of Büchi pairs is too high, and none is allowed to exist. This is carried out in the last three sections of the paper.

2.
Squares in (monic) quadratic progression and Büchi tuples

In this section we give a refined formulation of Büchi’s problem. We will show that every Büchi
n
-tuple corresponds to squares in certain
quadratic progressions
.

Definition 2.1
.

A sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is called a
quadratic progression
if the second difference of the sequence is constant.

A non-trivial Büchi
n
-tuple is then a sequence of
squares
in quadratic progression with constant second difference equal to
2
, which does not consist of consecutive squares.

We have the following basic lemma which characterizes quadratic progressions:

Lemma 2.2
.

A sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a quadratic progression if and only if there exists a quadratic polynomial
f
such that
f(n)=a_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

Recall that a quadratic progression is characterized by the second difference equation

(2.1)

a_{n+2}-2a_{n+1}+a_{n}=2a\quad\text{for some}\quad a.

This defines a non-homogeneous linear recurrence with characteristic polynomial
g(x)=(x-1)^{2}
. A particular solution to this recurrence is given by

a_{n}=an^{2}\quad\text{for all}\quad n\geq 1.

The homogeneous part is given by a linear term of the shape
bn+c
. Thus the general solution is given by

a_{n}=an^{2}+bn+c=f(n),

where the right hand side is a quadratic polynomial in
n
.
∎

By Lemma
2.2
, we may give the following definition:

Definition 2.3
.

We say a quadratic progression
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is
monic
if it is defined by a monic quadratic polynomial, or equivalently, if the constant second difference is equal to
2
. We say that the quadratic progression is
non-singular
if the associated quadratic polynomial has non-zero discriminant. For a non-singular quadratic progression, the
discriminant
of the quadratic progression is the same as that of the associated quadratic polynomial.

We now relate Büchi tuples to quadratic progressions via the following proposition:

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose
u_{1},\cdots,u_{n}
is a Büchi
n
-tuple. Then
u_{1}^{2},\cdots,u_{n}^{2}
is a non-singular monic quadratic progression of positive discriminant divisible by
16
.

Proof.

We write out the Büchi system

(2.2)

\begin{matrix}u_{3}^{2}&-&2u_{2}^{2}&+&u_{1}^{2}&=&2\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
x_{n}^{2}&-&2u_{n-1}^{2}&+&u_{n-2}^{2}&=&2.\end{matrix}

Note that (
2.2
), without the condition that the terms are square integers, defines a linear recurrence sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of order
2
with characteristic polynomial
(t-1)^{2}
. Thus the general solution is given by

a_{n}=n^{2}+\alpha n+\beta

with
\alpha,\beta
determined by initial conditions. Since we demand that
a_{2}=u_{2}^{2},a_{1}=u_{1}^{2}
, we obtain the expression of
a_{k}=u_{k}^{2}
as a quadratic polynomial:

(2.3)

u_{k}^{2}=k(k-1)+k(u_{2}^{2}-u_{1}^{2})+u_{1}^{2}=k^{2}+k(u_{2}^{2}-u_{1}^{2}-1)+u_{1}^{2}.

For fixed
u_{2},u_{1}
, the right hand side is a quadratic polynomial in
k
. It is clear that
u_{2}\equiv u_{1}+1\pmod{2}
, whence
2a=u_{2}^{2}-u_{1}^{2}-1
is even. We can then write the right hand side as

f(k)=k^{2}+2ak+b,

where
b=u_{1}^{2}
. Then completing the square we have

f(k)=(k+a)^{2}+b-a^{2}.

The equation
u_{k}^{2}=f(k)
can then be expressed as

(u_{k}-k-a)(u_{k}+k+a)=b-a^{2}.

In fact, we have that in the relevant case that
b-a^{2}\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. Indeed, recall that from (
2.3
) that
b=u_{1}^{2}
and
a=(u_{2}^{2}-u_{1}^{2}-1)/2
. Clearly,
u_{2},u_{1}
have opposite parity. If
u_{1}
is odd and
u_{2}
is even, then
u_{2}^{2}-u_{1}^{2}-1\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, hence
(u_{2}^{2}-u_{1}^{2}-1)/2=a
is odd and hence
b-a^{2}
is divisible by
4
. If
u_{1}
is even and
u_{2}
is odd, then
u_{2}^{2}-u_{1}^{2}-1\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, and we again conclude that
b-a^{2}
is divisible by
4
. ∎

Our main proposition, which implies our main theorems, is the following:

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a monic quadratic progression with positive discriminant divisible by
16
. If

a_{s+1},\cdots,a_{s+k}

are all integer squares, then
k\leq 4
.

The discussion preceding Proposition
2.5
shows that every non-trivial Büchi sequence corresponds to such a quadratic progression. Thus, knowing that there are at most four squares among consecutive terms in such a progression shows that Büchi’s problem has a positive answer.

Remark 2.6
.

We note that in
[
6
]
it is observed that there exist squares in quadratic progression of length up to
8
. This does not violate the conclusion of Proposition
2.5
, since we insist that our quadratic progressions are monic. In fact, there is a qualitative difference between quadratic progressions where the second difference is equal to twice a square, versus those which are not. This roughly corresponds to the fact that there are only finitely many integer solutions to the equation
x^{2}-(ky)^{2}=1
but infinitely many solutions to the Pellian equation
x^{2}-ky^{2}=1
when
k\geq 2
is not a square.

3.
From monic quadratic progressions to tuples of Büchi pairs

We start with the set-up to prove Proposition
2.5
. Since we assumed that the monic quadratic polynomial
f(x)
has discriminant equal to
16D
for some positive integer
D
, it follows that after an integer translation, we may assume that
f
takes the form
f(x)=x^{2}-4D
. If
f(s+1),\cdots,f(s+\ell)
are all squares, say
f(s+j)=u_{j}^{2}
, then we put

(3.1)

u_{k}^{2}=(s+k)^{2}-4D\quad\text{for}\quad k=1,2,\cdots,\ell.

This in turn implies that

4D=(s+k-u_{k})(s+k+u_{k})\quad\text{for}\quad k=1,\cdots,\ell.

Writing
x_{k}=s+k+u_{k}
and
y_{k}=s+k-u_{k}
, we obtain

(3.2)

4D=x_{k}y_{k},\quad x_{k}+y_{k}=x_{k-1}+y_{k-1}+2

for
k=1,\cdots,\ell
.

Note that
x_{k}+y_{k}=2(s+k)
. From (
3.2
) we see that
x_{k}\equiv y_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
. By abuse of notation we replace
x_{k},y_{k}
with
2x_{k},2y_{k}
respectively. Then (
3.2
) becomes

(3.3)

D=x_{k}y_{k},\quad(x_{k}+y_{k})-(x_{k-1}+y_{k-1})=1.

We make the following definition, which drops the dependence on
D
:

Definition 3.1

(
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs)
.

We say that
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}):1\leq i\leq n\}
is an
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs
if

(3.4)

0\leq x_{i}y_{i}=x_{i+1}y_{i+1}\quad\text{and}\quad(x_{i+1}+y_{i+1})-(x_{i}+y_{i})=1

for
i=1,\cdots,n-1
. If
x_{1}y_{1}=0
then we say the tuple is
trivial
, and
non-trivial
otherwise.

We have the following statement characterizing monic quadratic progressions and tuples of Büchi pairs:

Proposition 3.2
.

There is a bijection between monic quadratic progressions of length
n
with discriminant divisible by
16
and
n
-tuples of Büchi pairs.

Proof.

A length
n
monic quadratic progression with discriminant divisible by
16
is given by

u_{j}^{2}=(s+j)^{2}-4D,\quad 1\leq j\leq n

for integers
s,D
. This gives an
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs by the map
u_{j}\mapsto(x_{j},y_{j})
, given as

x_{j}=\frac{s+j+u_{j}}{2},\quad y_{j}=\frac{s+j-u_{j}}{2}\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq j\leq n

Conversely, an
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs
\{(x_{j},y_{j}):1\leq j\leq n\}
gives a monic quadratic progression by

u_{j}=x_{j}-y_{j},\quad s+1=x_{1}+y_{1}.

This gives the required bijective correspondence. ∎

It follows that Proposition
2.5
is implied by:

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}=\{(x_{j},y_{j}):1\leq j\leq n\}
be a non-trivial
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs. Then
n\leq 4
.

The remainder of the paper is dedicated to proving Proposition
3.3
.

3.1.
Transfer relations for non-trivial tuples of Büchi pairs and ordered tuples

Let
\{(x_{i},y_{i}):1\leq i\leq n\}
be a non-trivial
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs. The hypothesis that
x_{i}y_{i}=x_{j}y_{j}
for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
implies that for each
1\leq i,j\leq n
, we may write

(3.5)

\begin{matrix}x_{i}&=&v_{i,j}u_{i,j},&y_{i}&=&w_{i,j}u_{j,i},\\
\\
x_{j}&=&v_{i,j}u_{j,i},&y_{j}&=&w_{i,j}u_{i,j}\end{matrix}

with
u_{i,j},u_{j,i},v_{i,j},w_{i,j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
.

Next we prove that for each
1\leq j\leq n
, we have:

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\{(x_{i},y_{i}):1\leq i\leq n\}
be a non-trivial
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs. Then for each
1\leq j\leq n-1
, we have

x_{j}=\gcd(x_{j},x_{j+1})\gcd(x_{j},y_{j+1})\quad\text{and}\quad y_{j}=\gcd(y_{j},x_{j+1})\gcd(y_{j},y_{j+1}).

Thus,

\begin{matrix}v_{j,j+1}&=&\gcd(x_{j},x_{j+1}),&w_{j,j+1}&=&\gcd(y_{j},y_{j+1}),\\
\\
u_{j,j+1}&=&\gcd(x_{j},y_{j+1}),&u_{j+1,j}&=&\gcd(y_{j},x_{j+1}).\end{matrix}

Proof.

Let
p
be a prime and
k\geq 1
such that
p^{k}
divides
x_{1}y_{1}
exactly. Let
p^{k_{j}},p^{\ell_{j}}
divide
x_{j},y_{j}
exactly, respectively. Since
x_{j}y_{j}=x_{j+1}y_{j+1}=x_{1}y_{1}
, it follows that

k_{j}+\ell_{j}=k_{j+1}+\ell_{j+1}=k.

We claim that
\min\{k_{j},\ell_{j},k_{j+1},\ell_{j+1}\}=0
. Otherwise, we have

p|\gcd(x_{j},y_{j})\quad\text{and}\quad p|\gcd(x_{j+1},y_{j+1}),

hence
p
divides
(x_{j+1}+y_{j+1})-(x_{j}+y_{j})=1
, a contradiction. Now the
p
-adic order of
\gcd(x_{j},x_{j+1}),\gcd(x_{j},y_{j+1})
are respectively equal to
\min\{k_{j},k_{j+1}\},\min\{k_{j},\ell_{j+1}\}
. If
0<k_{j}<k
, then one of
k_{j+1},\ell_{j+1}
must equal
0
and the other equal to
k
, in which case
\min\{k_{j},k_{j+1}\}+\min\{k_{j},\ell_{j+1}\}=k_{j}
. If
k_{j}=0
then there is nothing to prove. If
k_{j}=k
then
\min\{k_{j},k_{j+1}\}+\min\{k_{j},\ell_{j+1}\}=k_{j+1}+\ell_{j+1}=k
, so we are done. The proof for the statement concerning
y_{j}
is identical.
∎

Lemma
3.4
gives

x_{j}=v_{j,j+1}u_{j,j+1}\quad\text{and}\quad y_{j}=w_{j,j+1}u_{j+1,j}

for
j\geq 1
. (
3.3
) then implies

(3.6)

1=v_{j,j+1}(u_{j+1,j}-u_{j,j+1})+w_{j,j+1}(u_{j,j+1}-u_{j+1,j})=(v_{j,j+1}-w_{j,j+1})(u_{j+1,j}-u_{j,j+1}).

From here we see that

(3.7)

u_{j+1,j}=u_{j,j+1}+c_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad v_{j,j+1}=w_{j,j+1}+d_{j}

with
c_{j}d_{j}=1
.

Observe that switching the roles of
x_{j+1},y_{j+1}
while fixing
x_{j},y_{j}
has the effect of switching the pairs
\{v_{j,j+1},w_{j,j+1}\}
and
\{u_{j,j+1},u_{j+1,j}\}
. Thus we may assume, without loss of generality, that
c_{j}=1
and
d_{j}=1
for all
1\leq j\leq n-1
. This compels the following definition:

Definition 3.5
.

An
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs is called
ordered
if
x_{1}\geq y_{1}
and

v_{j,j+1}-w_{j,j+1}=u_{j+1,j}-u_{j,j+1}=1

for
j=1,\cdots,n-1
.

We have the following simple consequence of requiring an
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs to be ordered:

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}):1\leq i\leq n\}
be an ordered
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs. Then we have

x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\cdots\leq x_{n},\quad y_{1}\geq y_{2}\geq\cdots\geq y_{n}.

Proof.

Using the relations

v_{j,j+1}=w_{j,j+1}+1\quad\text{and}\quad u_{j+1,j}=u_{j,j+1}+1

for
1\leq j\leq n-1
, we obtain

\begin{matrix}x_{j}&=&(w_{j,j+1}+1)u_{j,j+1},&&y_{j}&=&w_{j,j+1}(u_{j,j+1}+1),\\
\\
x_{j+1}&=&(w_{j,j+1}+1)(u_{j,j+1}+1),&&y_{j+1}&=&w_{j,j+1}u_{j,j+1}.\end{matrix}

This implies that
x_{j+1}>x_{j}
and
y_{j+1}\leq y_{j}
, as required. ∎

4.
Parametrizing triples of Büchi pairs

We start this section with a bit of generality. We introduce the bijection

(4.1)

\sigma_{n}:{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1,\cdots,2^{n}-1\},\quad(\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{n-1})\mapsto\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\varepsilon_{j}2^{j}.

Write a generic element in
\{0,\cdots,2^{n}-1\}
in binary as

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\varepsilon_{j}2^{j},\quad\varepsilon_{j}\in\{0,1\}.

Define the sets
S_{n}^{(j;0)},S_{n}^{(j;1)}
to be the subsets of
\{0,1,\cdots,2^{n}-1\}
consisting of those elements whose
j
-th bits are equal to
0
and
1
, respectively. Now define the functions
\Pi_{n}^{(j;0)},\Pi_{n}^{(j;1)}:{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}^{2^{n}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
by

(4.2)

\Pi_{n}^{(j;0)}({\mathbf{s}})=\prod_{k\in S_{n}^{(j;0)}}s_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\Pi_{n}^{(j;1)}({\mathbf{s}})=\prod_{k\in S_{n}^{(j;1)}}s_{k}.

Then define the
parametrizing sequence
of a
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs by

Definition 4.1

(Parametrizing sequence of
n
-tuples of Büchi pairs)
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}=\{(x_{j},y_{j}):1\leq j\leq n\}
be an
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs. We say that
{\mathbf{s}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2^{n}}
is a
parametrizing sequence
for
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}
if

(4.3)

x_{j}=\Pi_{n}^{(j-1;0)}({\mathbf{s}})\quad\text{and}\quad y_{j}=\Pi_{n}^{(j-1;1)}({\mathbf{s}})

for
j=1,\cdots,n
. We say that the parametrizing sequence
{\mathbf{s}}
is
non-trivial
if the corresponding
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs is non-trivial.

We now prove that every non-trivial
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs has a non-trivial parametrizing sequence (in particular, all of the terms are positive).

Proposition 4.2

(Existence of positive parametrizing tuples for
n
-tuples of Büchi pairs)
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}=\{(x_{j},y_{j}):1\leq j\leq n\}
be a non-trivial
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs. Then there exists a parametrizing sequence
{\mathbf{s}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}^{2^{n}}
such that (
4.3
) holds.

Proof.

We proceed by induction. For the case
n=2
, the answer is supplied by Lemma
3.4
: we put

s_{0}=\gcd(x_{1},x_{2}),\quad s_{1}=\gcd(y_{1},x_{2}),\quad s_{2}=\gcd(x_{1},y_{2}),\quad s_{3}=\gcd(y_{1},y_{2}).

Suppose, for
n\geq 2
, that every non-trivial
n
-tuple of Büchi pairs has a non-zero parametrizing sequence. Now consider a
(n+1)
-tuple of Büchi pairs, say
{\mathcal{B}}_{n+1}=\{(x_{j},y_{j}):1\leq j\leq n+1\}
. Then
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}=\{(x_{j},y_{j}):1\leq j\leq n\}
satisfies the induction hypothesis, and thus admits a positive parametrizing sequence, say
{\mathbf{r}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}^{2^{n}}
. Define
{\mathbf{s}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2^{n+1}}
by
r_{j}=s_{j}s_{j+2^{n}}
, where
s_{j}
is the divisor of
r_{j}
transferred to
x_{n+1}
, and
s_{j+2^{n}}
the divisor transferred to
y_{n+1}
. Since
x_{n}y_{n}=x_{n+1}y_{n+1}
, it follows that
s_{j},s_{j+2^{n}}
are necessarily complementary divisors of
r_{j}
.

We show that as constructed,
{\mathbf{s}}
is a parametrizing sequence for
{\mathcal{B}}_{n+1}
. Note that, by construction,
{\mathbf{s}}
is positive. Observe that for
1\leq j\leq n
, our assumption that
{\mathbf{r}}
is a positive parametrizing sequence for

implies that

x_{j}=\Pi_{n}^{(j-1;0)}({\mathbf{r}})\quad\text{and}\quad y_{j}=\Pi_{n}^{(j-1;1)}({\mathbf{r}}).

Using our construction, it follows that

x_{j}=\prod_{k\in S_{n}^{(j-1;0)}}s_{k}s_{k+2^{n}}\quad\text{and}\quad y_{j}=\prod_{k\in S_{n}^{(j-1;1)}}s_{k}s_{k+2^{n}}.

By the definition of
S_{n}^{(j;\varepsilon)}
, it follows that

(4.4)

x_{j}=\prod_{k\in S_{n+1}^{(j-1;0)}}s_{k}=\Pi_{n+1}^{(j-1;0)}({\mathbf{s}})\quad\text{and}\quad y_{j}=\prod_{k\in S_{n+1}^{(j-1;1)}}s_{k}=\Pi_{n+1}^{(j-1;1)}({\mathbf{s}})

for
j=1,\cdots,n
. For
j=n+1
, we note that by construction, for each
0\leq\ell\leq 2^{n}-1
, we have
s_{\ell}
is the component of
r_{\ell}
which goes into
x_{n+1}
and
s_{\ell+2^{n}}
is the component of
r_{\ell}
which goes into
y_{n+1}
. It follows from the fact that
x_{n}y_{n}=x_{n+1}y_{n+1}
that

(4.5)

\begin{matrix}x_{n+1}&=&\prod_{0\leq k\leq 2^{n}-1}s_{k}&&y_{n+1}&=&\prod_{2^{n}\leq k\leq 2^{n+1}-1}s_{k}\\
\\
&=&\prod_{k\in S_{n+1}^{(n;0)}}s_{k}&&&=&\prod_{k\in S_{n+1}^{(n;1)}}s_{k}\\
\\
&=&\Pi_{n+1}^{(n;0)}({\mathbf{s}}),&&&=&\Pi_{n+1}^{(n;1)}({\mathbf{s}}).\end{matrix}

This shows that
{\mathbf{s}}
is a positive parametrizing sequence for
{\mathcal{B}}_{n+1}
, completing the induction. ∎
