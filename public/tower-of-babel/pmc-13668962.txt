Exploring the landscape of flagellate erythema as a rare cutaneous symptom of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy: a case series-based review
Authors: Aleksandra Opinc-Rosiak, Milena Kaszubska, Aleksandra Nadel, Aleksander Sztajnert, Joanna Makowska, Anna Lewandowska-Polak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Flagellate erythema is an exceptionally rare cutaneous manifestation of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) with poorly understood clinical significance. We report two patients who developed zebra-like flagellate erythema: one with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) and one with anti-Mi-2-positive cancer-associated myositis (CAM). To place these observations in broader context, we performed a thorough literature search across PubMed, Scopus and Embase and identified 34 previously published cases of IIM-associated flagellate erythema. The most frequent underlying diagnosis was dermatomyositis (n = 14, 41.2%), followed by CAM (n = 9, 26.5%), amyopathic/clinically amyopathic dermatomyositis (n = 6, 17.6%), and juvenile dermatomyositis (n = 5, 14.7%). Anti-TIF1-γ antibodies were identified in 6 of 34 patients (17.6%) and anti-MDA5 antibodies in 7 of 34 (20.6%); malignancy was reported in 9 of 34 (26.5%), while a substantial proportion of cases were seronegative or lacked antibody testing. Muscle weakness or myalgia was present in most of the combined cohort, and a fatal outcome was reported in approximately one-fifth of cases. Our cases expand the clinical and serological spectrum of myositis-associated flagellate erythema, including the first report of this presentation in IMNM. While flagellate erythema has most often been reported with CAM or anti-TIF1-γ antibodies, the literature remains inconsistent, and current case-report-based evidence does not support a definitive association with any single serological or clinical subgroup. In our patients, flagellate erythema coincided with disease exacerbation; although this should be interpreted cautiously given the limited evidence, its occurrence may warrant careful evaluation for IIM flare and, where appropriate, malignancy screening.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00296-026-06328-5.

Introduction

Cutaneous lesions are among the most frequent symptoms of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM). Although the clinical presentation is usually consistent, including pathognomonic lesions such as heliotrope rash or Gottron’s sign, rare skin manifestations can also occur. Flagellate erythema is one of the rarest manifestations and its clinical significance remains undefined. We present two cases of patients who developed zebra-like erythema—one with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) and one with cancer-associated myositis (CAM). Moreover, we conducted a literature review and identified 34 descriptions of this rare symptom, further exploring in detail the clinical and serological spectrum associated with this manifestation as well as treatment options and outcomes.

The study was conducted in accordance with Helsinki declaration. Due to the retrospective and non-interventional nature of the study, approval of the bioethical committee was not required. Both patients gave informed written consent for publication.

Patient 1

A 36-year-old male was referred to the rheumatology department due to progressive muscle weakness accompanied by muscle pain of the pelvic and shoulder girdles, burning sensation in the limbs, arthralgia involving the knees and wrists, chronic fatigue, and significant weight loss. Physical examination revealed proximal muscle weakness, with no skin lesions typical of dermatomyositis (DM). Laboratory investigations revealed elevated muscle enzyme levels (creatine kinase [CK] 737 U/l, alanine aminotransferase [ALT] 45.7 U/l, aspartate aminotransferase [AST] 42.8 U/l, myoglobin 156 µg/L) and positive antinuclear antibodies (ANA) at a titre of 1:320 (granular pattern). Myositis panel demonstrated strongly positive (+++) anti-HMGCR antibodies, as well as moderately positive anti-nRNP/Sm, borderline anti-Sm, anti-SAE1, and anti-fibrillarin antibodies. The patient had no previous exposure to statins. A muscle biopsy was obtained from the left vastus medialis muscle for histopathological examination, which confirmed the diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy. Malignancy screening was performed and showed no signs of neoplasm. Based on chest HRCT, interstitial lung disease was excluded. Treatment with intravenous methylprednisolone pulses with subsequent oral prednisone at a dose of 40 mg/day with gradual tapering and azathioprine at 200 mg/day was initiated.

Despite ongoing treatment, the patient’s condition deteriorated. During a follow-up examination, he reported progressive weakness of the thigh muscles, myalgia involving the upper limb muscles, and the presence of burning skin lesions persisting for approximately one month. On physical examination, significant muscle weakness was noted; moreover numerous linear, erythematous lesions resembling whip marks were observed over the skin of the neckline, nape of the neck and shoulders, consistent with flagellate erythema (Fig. 1). Due to persistent myalgia and progressive muscle weakness, pharmacotherapy was escalated to mycophenolate mofetil and further intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) infusions, leading to rapid resolution of erythema dermatitis, but with no significant clinical improvement in the muscular domain (Fig. 2).

After 6 months of IVIG therapy, no significant improvement in muscle strength was noted. Furthermore, myalgia and skin tenderness involving the neck, nape and shoulders worsened. Due to progression of muscle weakness and insufficient response to treatment, IVIG was discontinued and the patient was qualified for rituximab therapy. He received 2 courses of rituximab (RTX), which led to noticeable improvement of muscle strength and mobility.

After 6 months of disease stabilization, a flare-up occurred. The patients complained of severe muscle pain, a decrease in muscle strength and weight loss. Whip marks on the back reappeared but resolved spontaneously. Malignancy screening was repeated and excluded underlying malignancy. IVIG treatment was re-introduced and cyclophosphamide infusions were administered with gradual improvement in muscle strength and myalgia. So far flagellate erythema has not reappeared.

Patient 2

A 54-year-old female was admitted to the rheumatology department with a 2 months history of proximal muscle weakness and myalgia affecting both upper and lower limbs. At symptom onset, erythematous skin lesions were observed over the neckline, hips and periorbital area, consistent with typical DM lesions including the V sign, holster sign, heliotrope and malar rash (Fig. 3). Notably, past medical history revealed exposure to a glyphosate-containing herbicide for approximately five years, with the latest exposure one month prior to the symptom onset. Laboratory findings included elevated levels of inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein (CRP) 19.1 mg/l, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 32 mm/1 h), muscle enzymes (AST 766 U/l, ALT 325.9 U/l, LDH 1569 U/l, CK 22561 U/l, CK-MB mass 481.8 ng/ml, myoglobin 5145 µg/L, troponin T 2405 ng/L), as well as hypoproteinaemia with hypoalbuminemia. Immunological testing revealed the presence of antinuclear antibodies at a titre of 1:2560 (speckled and nucleolar pattern), with moderately positive anti-Mi-2a (++) and anti-Mi-2b (++) antibodies in the myositis panel, supporting the diagnosis of DM. The CA125 level was slightly elevated, but initial malignancy screening upon diagnosis excluded neoplasm, including ovarian cancer. The patient received intravenous pulses of metyloprednisolon, followed by oral prednisone and azathioprine as a steroid sparing agent. As only partial improvement was noted, the treatment was escalated to intravenous immunoglobulins, with a good clinical response.

Approximately 10 months after DM diagnosis, a steady increase in CA125 was noted on follow-up, and the patient was referred for CT of the abdominal cavity and pelvis, which demonstrated no lesions. A gynaecological consultation was obtained, and the patient was qualified for bilateral oophorectomy. Histopathological examination revealed diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) of the ovary and therefore confirming paraneoplastic background of DM. Chemotherapy with the R-CHOEP regimen was administered, leading to temporal remission of also DM symptoms. Approximately 5 months after chemotherapy was initiated, IVIG treatment was reintroduced to manage residual myopathic symptoms. Around 16 months later, a PET-CT scan confirmed no evidence of lymphoma recurrence.

Approximately 5 months after this reassessment, the patient reported a relapse of cutaneous symptoms of DM. Physical examination revealed erythema involving the neckline, nape, back, shoulders and face. Additionally, numerous linear, erythematous lesions resembling whip marks were noted over the skin of the neckline and back, consistent with flagellate erythema (Fig. 4). Neither arthritis nor muscle weakness was found on physical examination, but laboratory tests demonstrated elevated muscle enzyme levels. Given the previous remission after rituximab administration as a part of chemotherapy scheme, she was qualified to rituximab infusion due to persistent IIM symptoms. A rapid reduction in erythematous skin lesions was observed. The patient received 2 courses of RTX, which was further discontinued due to stable remission.

Unfortunately, after 6 months, another flare of typical DM skin symptoms occurred, forcing readmission of RTX. The patient is currently receiving RTX infusions every 6 months as maintenance therapy. During subsequent hospitalizations, the linear, erythematous lesions resembling whip marks were no longer observed.

Search strategy and literature review

A literature search was performed in PubMed, Scopus and Embase databases, using the terms “flagellate erythema”, “flagellate dermatitis”, “myositis”, “myopathy”, “dermatomyositis” and “centripetal flagellate erythema,” combined with Boolean operators, leading to identification of 34 descriptions of this rare symptom. The search was restricted to publications in English and Polish and was last updated on 27 September 2026. Due to the rarity of this clinical symptom, records were not restricted to certain years. Eligible publications included case reports and case series describing patients with flagellate erythema in IIM. The review process is presented in Fig. 5. This case-based review was structured in accordance with the CAse-BAsed REview sTandards (CABARET) recommendations [1].

According to the literature, among the 34 previously published cases of flagellate erythema, the most frequent underlying diagnosis was dermatomyositis (DM; n = 14, 41.2%), followed by cancer-associated myositis (CAM; n = 9, 26.5%) and amyopathic/clinically amyopathic dermatomyositis (ADM/CADM; n = 6, 17.6%). Flagellate erythema was also reported in 5 patients with juvenile dermatomyositis (JDM; 14.7%). Among these 34 previously published cases, females were affected more frequently than males (F: M 20:14; 58.8% vs. 41.2%). Anti-TIF1-γ antibodies were identified in 6 of 34 patients (17.6%), and malignancy was reported in 9 of 34 patients (26.5%). Our study describes two novel cases—one with CAM and one with IMNM. To our knowledge, this is the first report of flagellate erythema in IMNM.

Discussion

Flagellate erythema is a rare skin disorder with intriguing clinical presentation. Although initially associated with exposure to high doses of bleomycin, it is now well known to be induced by various triggers such as different anti-tumour medications (e.g. bleomycin, peplomycin, docetaxel, transtuzumab), food (undercooked/raw shiitake mushrooms or boletus mushrooms), infections (e.g. parvovirus B19, chikungunya), intentionally-made lesions, insects bites or cnidarian stings, allergic dermatitis and connective tissue diseases, such as dermatomyositis, adult onset Still’s disease or systemic lupus erythematosus [34].

Flagellate erythema in the course of IIM is observed in approximately 5% of the patients, mostly with dermatomyositis or amyopathic dermatomyositis. The mechanisms underlying this cutaneous symptom in DM are not fully elucidated, yet exposure to sunlight, as well as pruritis and subsequent scratching leading to repeated physical injury and minor traumas are suspected as major causes. Histopathologically, lesions are nonspecific and consistent with interface dermatitis, including mild atrophy with basal cells vacuolization, hyperkeratosis with focal parakeratosis and acanthosis within the epidermis, oedema and vasodilation of the papillary dermis and infiltrates of the lymphocytes in the upper dermis. Compared with bleomycin-induced lesions, which result from increased density of melanosomes in keratinocytes, flagellate erythema in the course of DM is less hyperpigmented and more reddish, indicating a prominent inflammatory process [2]. In our patients, skin biopsies were not performed, as lesions emerged in patients with an already confirmed diagnosis of IIM.

Flagellate erythema has been frequently reported in patients positive for anti-TIF1-γ antibodies, though the available evidence is limited to case reports and small cohorts, precluding firm conclusions about prevalence or causality. Frequent association with both anti-TIF1γ and malignancy raises a question if flagellate erythema could serve as a sign of paraneoplastic process or rather is associated with a certain serologic profile. Cohort data are conflicting: one Japanese study found flagellate erythema in over 85% of anti-TIF1-γ-positive patients versus 20% with other antibodies [35], and a second Japanese cohort similarly reported higher prevalence with anti-TIF1-γ, with skin lesions occurring independently of malignancy status—suggesting a link to the antibody itself rather than to the neoplastic process [36]. In an Italian DM cohort, no cancer-associated patients presented with flagellate erythema [37]. However, flagellate erythema was also documented in patients with other serological profiles. According to the literature review, in 7 patients (19.4%) anti-MDA5 antibodies were identified. Notably, 33.3% of the described patients were reported to be seronegative and in other 22.2% serological profile was not provided. The vast majority of cases were published when many of the newly identified antibodies were probably not available for routine assessment at the time of publication, leading to falsely negative results. Our patients demonstrated distinct serological profiles—patient 1 was positive for anti-HMGCR antibodies, which to our knowledge had never been associated with flagellate erythema, while patient 2 was positive for anti-Mi2 antibodies. Taken together, these findings indicate that flagellate erythema is not specific to a single serological profile, and that any relationship with anti-TIF1-γ antibodies or malignancy should be interpreted cautiously pending further study. Our Patient 2, who was diagnosed with DLBCL-associated DM, had a history of approximately five years of exposure to a glyphosate-containing herbicide. Glyphosate exposure has been suggested as a risk factor for non-Hodgkin lymphoma, with the most consistent subtype-specific association reported for DLBCL [38]. A pooled analysis of agricultural cohorts from France, Norway and the United States similarly reported an association between glyphosate use and DLBCL [39].

In both of our patients, flagellate erythema appeared shortly before disease exacerbation, consistent with prior reports suggesting a possible temporal association with disease activity [23]; however, this should be regarded as a possible association rather than a definitive clinical warning sign, particularly given the small number of cases described. Flagellate erythema usually appears simultaneously with other skin lesions typical for IIM, indicating general inflammatory response within the skin, however cases of isolated symptom were also reported. Zebra-like lesions typically occur on the back and limbs, corresponding to the area most prone to itching and scratching. This was also true for both of our cases.

According to the literature and our cases, zebra-like lesions usually respond well to immunosuppressive treatment or even diminish spontaneously. Typically, high doses of steroids were used, in some of the patients along with other immunosuppressants, most often with cyclophosphamide, MTX or HCQ. As many of the cases were published long time ago, approach to IIM treatment and therapeutic options have evolved. Our patients received glucocorticosteroids with RTX and IVIG, with good clinical response as for the skin involvement, however, given the concurrent use of multiple agents in our patients and the recognized potential for spontaneous regression, the specific treatment responsible for this improvement cannot be determined with certainty. Seven (19.4%) fatal cases of patients with IIM and flagellate erythema have been reported in the literature, notably many of those patients had ILD, which is in general associated with poor prognosis in IIM.

Study limitations

The search was restricted to publications in English and Polish, potentially introducing language bias. As the review relies predominantly on case reports and case series, it is inherently susceptible to publication bias, and reported frequencies should not be interpreted as true population prevalence. Furthermore, a considerable proportion of the included cases predate the routine clinical availability of myositis-specific antibody testing, resulting in a high rate of seronegative or unclassified serological profiles among historical reports; this may have led to an underestimation of true associations between specific autoantibodies and flagellate erythema. Additionally, biopsies of the flagellate lesions were not performed in either of our patients, as the diagnosis of IIM was already established; this limits direct clinicopathological correlation with the histopathological features reported in the literature.

Conclusions

Flagellate erythema is a non-specific and exceptionally rare presentation of IIM. Our manuscript describes two patients with clinical and serological features that have been never reported previously - zebra-like lesions in individuals with IMNM and anti-Mi2-positive CAM, adding to the evidence on this intriguing cutaneous symptom. Clinical and serological landscape, treatment options and outcomes in patients with IIM-associated flagellate erythema were explored in detail. As this rare skin manifestation may heralds a flare, its occurrence should be considered as a red flag, prompting detailed assessment of IIM exacerbation.
