Improved Approximate EFX Guarantees for Multigraphs
Alireza Kaviani, Alireza Keshavarz, Masoud Seddighin, AmirMohammad Shahrezaei

Abstract
In recent years, a new line of work in fair allocation has focused on 𝖤𝖥𝖷 \mathsf{EFX} allocations for ( p , q ) (p,q) -bounded valuations, where each good is relevant to at most p p agents, and any pair of agents share at most q q relevant goods. For the case p = 2 p=2 and q = ∞ q=\infty , such instances can be equivalently represented as multigraphs whose vertices are the agents and whose edges represent goods, each edge incident to exactly the one or two agents for whom the good is relevant. A recent result of Amanatidis et al., 2024 shows that for additive ( 2 , ∞ ) (2,\infty) bounded valuations, a ( 2 / 3 ) (\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}) - 𝖤𝖥𝖷 \mathsf{EFX} allocation always exists. In this paper, we improve this bound by proving the existence of a ( 1 / 2 ) (\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{2}}}) - 𝖤𝖥𝖷 \mathsf{EFX} allocation for additive ( 2 , ∞ ) (2,\infty) -bounded valuations.



1
Introduction

Fair division is a fundamental problem with deep connections to mathematics, economics, and computer science. The central objective is to allocate a resource among a set of agents so that each perceives their share as fair, according to some well-defined notion of fairness. A canonical example is the cake-cutting problem, where the resource is modeled as a divisible, heterogeneous good.

The history of cake cutting includes many interesting and insightful results. One of the most classic result is the existence of envy-free divisions, where no agent prefers another’s share over their own. Such divisions are guaranteed to exist under mild assumptions on the valuation functions.

A key feature that makes the cake-cutting problem tractable is the divisibility of the resource—it can be cut at any point. However, when moving from divisible resources to indivisible goods—items that cannot be split—the complexity of fair allocation increases significantly. In this setting, the goal is to distribute a set of goods
M
among
n
agents, where each agent
i
has a valuation function
v_{i}:2^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
that assigns a non-negative value to each subset of goods. Unlike in the divisible case, envy-free allocations are not guaranteed to exist; for example, consider a single valuable good and two agents.

For indivisible goods, several relaxations of envy-freeness have been proposed to make the concept more applicable. These relaxations typically allow an agent to envy another only if the envy can be eliminated by removing a single item from the envied bundle. The removed item may be the most valuable one (
EF1
), the least valuable one (
\mathsf{EFX}
), a randomly chosen item (
EFR
), one less preferred item (
EFL
), or defined in some other way. Among these,
\mathsf{EFX}
is widely regarded as the strongest and most compelling relaxation.

Envy-Freeness up to Any Good
(
\mathsf{EFX}
) was introduced by
Caragiannis et al., 2016
. Formally, consider an allocation
X=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})
, where
X_{j}
denotes the bundle of goods allocated to agent
j
. The allocation
X
is
\mathsf{EFX}
if, for every pair of agents
i
and
j
, and for every good
g\in X_{j}
, it holds that

v_{i}(X_{i})\geq v_{i}(X_{j}\setminus\{g\}).

In other words, after removing any single good from another agent’s bundle, agent
i
values their own allocation at least as much as the adjusted bundle.

Perhaps
\mathsf{EFX}
is the strongest fairness guarantee one can hope for when allocating indivisible goods.
The existence of
\mathsf{EFX}
allocations remains a major open problem, and it is still unclear whether
\mathsf{EFX}
allocations exist for every instance involving indivisible goods. However, researchers have made progress in specific cases. For example,
\mathsf{EFX}
allocations are known to exist for two agents with monotone valuations
(
Plaut and Roughgarden, 2020
)
and for three agents, where two have monotone valuations and one has an
MMS
-feasible valuation
(
Akrami et al., 2023
)
. Moreover, in certain settings, relaxations of
\mathsf{EFX}
can be guaranteed, such as a
0.618
-approximate
\mathsf{EFX}
allocation for additive valuations
(
Amanatidis et al., 2020
)
and an
\mathsf{EFX}
allocation that allows discarding up to
n-1
goods under monotone valuations
(
Chaudhury et al., 2021b
)
. Nonetheless, finding a general method for complete
\mathsf{EFX}
allocations remains open.

In this paper, we push the boundaries of the results for
\mathsf{EFX}
by proving the existence of a
(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{2}}})
-
\mathsf{EFX}
allocation for additive
(2,\infty)
-bounded instances. The concept of
(p,q)
-bounded valuations, recently introduced by
Christodoulou et al., 2023
, refers to scenarios where each good has a nonzero value (is relevant) for at most
p
agents, and any pair of agents shares at most
q
common relevant goods.
Christodoulou et al., 2023
first demonstrated that
(2,1)
-bounded instances always admit an
\mathsf{EFX}
allocation.
Amanatidis et al., 2024
later extended this result to
(2,\infty)
-bounded instances, achieving a
(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})
-
\mathsf{EFX}
allocation.
Kaviani et al., 2024
subsequently showed that complete
\mathsf{EFX}
allocations exist for
(2,\infty)
-bounded instances when the valuation functions are restricted additive — where each good
g
is valued at either
0
or
v_{g}
by each agent. Additionally,
Afshinmehr et al., 2024
provided complete
\mathsf{EFX}
allocations for the case where the underlying relevance graph is a bipartite multigraph.
Here, we extend these results by establishing a
(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{2}}})
-
\mathsf{EFX}
allocation for
(2,\infty)
-bounded valuations.

2
Preliminaries

A fair allocation instance involves three elements: agents, goods, and valuations. The agents are represented by
N=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, and the goods by
M
. Agent
i
has a function
v_{i}:2^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
that assigns a non-negative value to every subset of goods. These functions are monotone, meaning that for any subsets
S
and
T
of
M
, if
S\subseteq T
, then
v_{i}(S)\leq v_{i}(T)
. In addition, we assume that valuations are additive; that is, for every pair of disjoint subsets
S
and
T
, we have
v_{i}(S\cup T)=v_{i}(S)+v_{i}(T)
.

We say that a good
g
is
irrelevant
to agent
i
if
v_{i}(\{g\})=0
; otherwise, we call
g

relevant
to
i
.
A family of valuation functions is
(p,q)
-bounded
if each good is relevant to at most
p
agents, and each pair of agents shares at most
q
relevant goods. In this paper, we focus on the special case where
p=2
and
q=1
.
For any subset
S\subseteq M
, let
S{\scriptstyle{\langle i\rangle}}
denote the set of goods in
S
that are relevant to agent
i
, and let
S{\scriptstyle{\langle i,j\rangle}}
denote the set of goods in
S
that are relevant to both
i
and
j
. Note that
S{\scriptstyle{\langle i,i\rangle}}
refers to the set of goods that are relevant
only
to agent
i
, and is therefore different from
S{\scriptstyle{\langle i\rangle}}
.

An
allocation

X
is a distribution of goods (not necessarily all) into
n
bundles
(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})
, where
X_{i}
denotes the bundle allocated to agent
i
. In this paper, we use
\mathcal{P}
to represent the set of unallocated goods, referred to as the
pool
. An allocation is
complete
if
\mathcal{P}=\emptyset
, and
partial
otherwise.

Let
\beta\in[1,+\infty]
be a constant. An agent
i

\beta
-envies agent
j
if
\beta v_{i}(X_{i})<v_{i}(X_{j})
. Furthermore, an agent
i

\beta
-strongly envies agent
j
if there exists a good
g\in X_{j}
such that
\beta v_{i}(X_{i})<v_{i}(X_{j}\setminus\{g\})
. For
\alpha\in[0,1]
, an allocation is
\alpha
-
\mathsf{EFX}
if no agent
(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}})
-strongly envies another.

Our algorithm relies on the concept of the
weighted envy graph
, first introduced by
Farhadi et al., 2021
. For an allocation
X
, the weighted envy graph
G_{X}
is a complete weighted directed graph where each vertex represents an agent. For any pair of agents
i
and
j
, there is a directed edge from
i
to
j
with weight
w_{X}(i,j)=\nicefrac{{v_{i}(X_{j})}}{{v_{i}(X_{i})}}
. Since agents and vertices correspond one-to-one, we use the terms interchangeably and may refer to a vertex by its associated agent.
For any
\alpha\in[0,\infty]
, we define
G_{\alpha,X}
as the subgraph of
G_{X}
that includes only edges with a weight greater than
\alpha
. For example,
G_{1,X}
represents the envy graph where an edge from agent
i
to agent
j
indicates that agent
i
envies agent
j
, while
G_{0,X}
includes an edge if agent
j
’s bundle has a non-zero value for agent
i
.

In this paper, we classify the edges of
G_{X}
into two categories: an edge
(i,j)
is called
heavy
if
w_{X}(i,j)>\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{2}}}
, and
light
otherwise.

Throughout the algorithm, we maintain the pairs

\langle N^{*},X^{*}\rangle

\tilde{N}
and
N^{*}

\tilde{X}

X^{*}

\mathcal{P}
form a partition of the goods: each good is either allocated to exactly one agent in
\tilde{N}

, or remains in
\mathcal{P}
.
We begin with
\tilde{N}=N
,

X^{*}

. By a result of

\tilde{X}
assigns exactly one good to each agent in
\tilde{N}
, and the remaining
m-n
goods are in
\mathcal{P}
.
The algorithm ends when
\tilde{N}=\emptyset
and
N^{*}=N
. At that point,
\tilde{X}
and
\mathcal{P}

X^{*}
gives the final allocation.

Overview of the Algorithm and Ideas

We prove the existence of a complete
(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{2}}})
-
\mathsf{EFX}
allocation for cases where valuation functions are additive and
(2,\infty)
-bounded.
Our algorithm consists of five updating rules followed by a final step. At each step, we apply the first applicable rule in sequence. Thus, if the
k
-th rule is applied at a given step, it implies that the first
k-1
rules were not applicable at that step. Each rule modifies
\tilde{X}
for some agents in
\tilde{N}
, possibly finalizing their bundles and transferring them to
N^{*}
. Once an agent is finalized, her allocation in
X^{*}
remains unchanged for the rest of the process.
The rules are carefully designed to maintain five key properties throughout the algorithm:

(
i
)

Allocation
X^{*}
is
(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{2}}})
-
\mathsf{EFX}
for the agents in
N^{*}
.

(
ii
)

For every agent
i\in N^{*}
, she does not
\sqrt{2}
-envy the union of the bundles of the remaining agents and the pool. In other words,
v_{i}(X^{*}_{i})\geq v_{i}\left(\bigcup_{j\in\tilde{N}}\tilde{X}_{j}\cup\mathcal{P}\right)/\sqrt{2}.

(
iii
)

Allocation
\tilde{X}
is
\mathsf{EFX}
for the agents in
\tilde{N}
.

(
iv
)

For every agent
i\in\tilde{N}
, she does not strongly envy any agent
j\in N^{*}
. In other words, for every

g\in X^{*}_{j}
we have
v_{i}(\tilde{X}_{i})\geq v_{i}\left(X^{*}_{j}\setminus\{g\}\right).

(
v
)

For each remaining agent

, there exists an agent
j

Rule 4.

Rule 4 applies to a homogeneous cycle
C

4.2
Final Step

Recall that in the first update rule, the social welfare of the agents strictly increases, and in each subsequent rule, the bundle of at least one agent is finalized. Therefore, after a finite number of updates, we reach a state where none of the rules are applicable, meaning all agents’ bundles are finalized. However, when all bundles have been finalized, some goods may still remain unallocated in the pool. In the final step, these remaining goods are allocated to the last agent whose bundle was finalized.
