Abstract
. A graph is even-degenerate if one can iteratively remove a vertex of even degree at each step until at most one edge remains. Recently, Janzer and Yip showed that the Erdős–Renyi random graph G ⁡ ( n , 1 / 2 ) G(n,1/2) is even-degenerate with high probability, and asked whether an analogous result holds for any general G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) . In this paper, we answer this question for any constant p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1) in affirmation by proving that G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) is even-degenerate with high probability.



1.
Introduction

For a graph
G
on
n
vertices, we say that
G
is
even-degenerate
if there exists an ordering
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
of its vertices such that for every
1\leq i\leq n-2
,
v_{i}
has an even number of neighbors in the set
\{v_{i+1},\dots,v_{n}\}
. In other words,
G
is even-degenerate if one can sequentially remove its vertices such that every step removes an even number of edges, until at most two vertices remain.

Note that the parity of the number of remaining edges in the graph never changes during this removal. This means that when the edge number
e(G)
of
G
is even, one can legally remove every vertex of the graph; but when
e(G)
is odd, there must be a leftover edge that is not legally removable. Therefore the property of being even-degenerate does not require
e(G)
to be even.

In the case of
e(G)
being even, an even-degenerate graph is in fact
even-decomposable
, a graph property previously studied by Versteegen
[
4
]
and Janzer and Yip
[
3
]
due to motivations coming from the study of graph codes initiated by Alon et al
[
2
,
1
]
. A graph
G
is
even-decomposable
if there exists a sequence of vertex subsets
V(G)=V_{1}\supseteq V_{2}\supseteq\dots\supseteq V_{k}=\varnothing
such that for every
1\leq i\leq k-1
,
G[V_{i}]
has an even number of edges and
V_{i}\setminus V_{i+1}
is an independent set in
G
.

Motivated by applications in estimating the sizes of linear graph codes, Versteegen showed that for
G\sim G(n,1/2)
, the probability that
H
is not even-decomposable is at most
e^{-\Omega(\sqrt{n})}
. The bounds are later improved by Janzer and Yip
[
3
]
to
e^{-\Omega(n^{2})}
. Janzer and Yip also determined the threshold
p(n)
for which
G\sim G(n,p(n))
is very likely to be even-decomposable. In particular, they showed the following.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
3
, Theorem 1.10 and Proposition 1.11]
)
.

There exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for any
p\leq 1-\frac{c_{1}}{n}
and
G\sim G(n,p)
,

\mathbb{P}[G\text{ is not even-decomposable}]=o(1).

For any
p\geq 1-\frac{c_{2}}{n}
and
G\sim G(n,p)
,

\mathbb{P}[G\text{ is even-decomposable}]=o(1).

In the same work, Janzer and Yip initiated the study of the probability of a random graph being even-degenerate, as even-degeneracy is a stronger and more natural property than even-decomposability.
They were able to determine the correct asymptotics for the probability of a random graph
G\sim G(n,1/2)
being even degenerate.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
3
, Theorem 1.13]
)
.

If
G\sim G(n,1/2)
, then
\mathbb{P}[\text{$G$ is not even-degenerate}]=e^{-\Theta(n)}
.

The lower bound side in
Theorem

1.2
is straightforward, because with probability
\geq(1/2)^{2n}
either every vertex in
G\sim G(n,1/2)
has odd degree (when
n
is even), or
G\sim G(n,1/2)
has all odd-degree vertices except for an isolated vertex (when
n
is odd). The upper bound side is more involved, and no analogue of
Theorem

1.2
is known for any other values of
p
aside from
p=1/2
. This is because the proof of
Theorem

1.2
employs an inductive strategy, which critically uses the fact that
p
is exactly
1/2
. More specifically, the proof relies on the observation that given every vertex subset
S\subseteq V
, the random graph
G[V\setminus S]
conditioned on the parities of
\deg_{G}(v)
for all vertices
v\in V
is identically distributed as
G(V\setminus S,1/2)
. Thus, the proof strategy in
[
3
]
cannot be directly generalized to
G(n,p)
for any
p\neq 1/2
.

In our work, we make substantial progress towards proving an analogous result to
Theorem

1.2
for any constant
p\in(0,1)
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For any constant
p\in(0,1)
and any
\alpha>0
,

\mathbb{P}_{G\sim G(n,p)}[G\text{ is not even-degenerate}]=e^{-\Omega(n^{1/2-\alpha})}.

In particular,
Theorem

1.3
shows that for any constant
p\in(0,1)
, even-degenerate graphs are always dominant in
G(n,p)
. Using the same lower bound construction as in
Theorem

1.2
, one can show a lower bound of
e^{-O(n)}
on the above probability. Thus determining the correct asymptotics remains an interesting question.

2.
Proof strategy

To see why it is nontrivial to generalize the proof strategy in
[
3
]
beyond
p\neq 1/2
, let us first briefly discuss how Janzer and Yip obtained the preliminary upper bound

\mathbb{P}_{G\sim G(n,1/2)}[G\text{ is not even-degenerate}]=o(1)

which is an intermediate step in their proof of
Theorem

1.2
.
For
p=1/2
, we have the nice property that given
G\sim G(n,1/2)
conditioned on the parities of
\deg_{G}(x)
for all
x\in V
, the graph
G\setminus\{v\}
for any
v\in V
is identically distributed as
G(n-1,1/2)
. So if one defines
f(n)
as the probability of
G\sim G(n,1/2)
being not even-degenerate, it is easy to obtain a recursive relation between
f(n)
and
f(n-1)
by exposing the parity of the degree of every vertex in
G
and analyzing the correlation between removals of different vertices of even degree.

For other constants
p\in(0,1)
(especially for
p
close to
0
or
1
), the above property is far from being true. If we expose the degree parities of every vertex in
G\sim G(n,p)
, then once we remove a vertex
v
from
G
, the distribution of
G\setminus\{v\}
is different from
G(n-1,p)
.

Despite that such a recursion does not hold, the random graph
G(n,p)
with
0<p<1
still has some nice properties that we can utilize. For example, for each vertex
v\in V
, the probability that
v
has even degree is

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i\leq n\\
i\text{ is even}\end{subarray}}\binom{n}{i}p^{i}(1-p)^{n-i}=1/2\pm o(1).

This guarantees that we can almost always remove one vertex from
G
when
n
is large enough.

The hope is that as long as the remaining graph has enough randomness, we can find a vertex with even degree and remove it. To ensure this, we need to develop a careful removal procedure, which motivates the “
(U,W)
-removal” procedure defined in our proof.
We now move on to give a simplified overview of the
(U,W)
-removal; the full details can be found in
Definition

4.1
.

2.1.
(U,W)
-removal

For
G\sim G(n,p)
, the
(U,W)
-removal on
G
roughly proceeds as follows: we fix a partition of the vertex set
V(G)=B\sqcup C
where the sizes of
B
and
C
are almost the same. We assign a fixed ordering
B=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{|B|}\}
to the vertices in
B
, and we partition
C
into
s+1=\Theta(n^{1/2-\alpha})
sets
C_{1},\dots,C_{s},C_{\#}
of equal sizes where
\alpha>0
is small. Our goal is to remove all the vertices in
B
in the assigned ordering, although we might also remove some vertices in
C
along the way.

Observe that when we try to remove
b_{i}\in B
, as long as, say, a constant fraction of the vertices in
C
are still present,
b_{i}
has even degree in the current graph with probability
1/2\pm o(1)
. If the degree of
b_{i}
in the current graph is even, we can just remove
b_{i}
; if the degree is odd, we will try to find a vertex
u\in C
so that we can remove both. Namely, we wish to find a vertex
u\in C
such that

(1)

b_{i}u
is an edge,

(2)

u
has even degree in the current graph.

After finding such
u
, we can first remove
u
and then remove
b_{i}
.

For every
b_{i}\in B
, if we happen to require such a vertex
u\in C
, we will only look for one in one part
C_{j}
of the partition, and we loop through the parts
C_{1},\dots,C_{s}
every time we need such a vertex. The advantage of this is that every time we get back to looking for a vertex in
C_{j}
, we have already removed at least
s
new vertices from
G
so that the parities (2) we query are again close to being uniformly random.

Once we succeed in the
(U,W)
-removal (i.e., have successfully removed all vertices in
B
), we will recurse and apply the procedure on the remaining graph. Note that after the removal, it is likely that roughly
\left\lvert B\right\rvert/2
vertices in
C
are removed. Thus, if
\left\lvert B\right\rvert=\left\lvert C\right\rvert=n/2
, then there will be roughly
n/4
vertices left. Let
f(n)
be the probability of
G(n,p)
not being even-degenerate. This suggests that
f(n)\leq f(n/4)+o(1)
, assuming that the
(U,W)
-removal succeeds with high probability.

If we apply
(U,W)
-removal only sequentially, we may end up with a constant number of vertices remaining, so we cannot get an upper bound on
f(n)
better than
f(K)
for some constant
K
. To solve this problem, we also consider the same procedure where the roles of
B
and
C
are interchanged. If at least one of the two removal processes succeeds, then we can recurse on the remaining graph. Note that in the two removal processes, the sets of remaining vertices are disjoint, and thus we may expect that the remaining subgraphs are close to being independent. This suggests that
f(n)\leq f(n/4)^{2}+o(1)
which is more amenable to proving a bound of the form
f(n)=o(1)
.

However, it is clear that after the removal procedure, what remains is not exactly a
p
-random graph. What we are left with is a subgraph with some partial information on the parities of certain edge subsets. We will show that these partial information do not affect the further removals too much.
The main technical difficulties of our proof arise from rigorously analyzing the information we expose at each step and showing that the sets we query have almost uniform parities.

We define the quantity we want to bound recursively as follows.

Definition 2.1

(
\alpha
-partially revealed
p
-graph)
.

For
0<\alpha<1/2
and
p\in(0,1)
, we say that a random graph
G=(V,E)
is an
\alpha
-partially revealed
p
-graph
if there exists a set
A\subseteq V
of size at most
n^{1-2\alpha}
, such that
G
follows the distribution
G(n,p)
on vertex set
V
conditioned on the following information:

(1)

the subgraph
G[A]
,

(2)

the parities of
\deg_{G}(a)
for all
a\in A
, and

(3)

the parity of
|E|
.

We call
A
the
revealed part
of
G
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
f_{\alpha}(n)
be the maximum of the probability that
G
is not even-degenerate over all
\alpha
-partially revealed
p
-graphs
G
with
n
vertices.

We will show the following recursive upper bound on
f_{\alpha}(n)
. Solving the recurrence in
Theorem

2.3
directly gives
Theorem

1.3
and we will deduce
Theorem

1.3
in
Section

6
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
0<\alpha<1/2
and
p\in(0,1)
be constants. There exists a positive integer
N_{0}
depending on
p
and
\alpha
such that the following holds. For any
n\geq N_{0}
, we have

1-f_{\alpha}(n)\geq(1-e^{-\Theta(n^{1/2-\alpha})})\left(1-\max_{n^{\prime}\in[n/4-o(n),n/4+o(n)]}f_{\alpha}(n^{\prime})^{2}\right).

3.
Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce some definitions that will be useful in the following proofs. We first recall the definition of the support of a random variable.

Definition 3.1

(Support)
.

Let
X\in\Omega
be a random variable. The
support of
X
is defined as
\textup{supp}(X):=\{x\in\Omega\mid\mathbb{P}[X=x]>0\}
.
Similarly, we define
\textup{supp}(X\mid\mathcal{E}):=\{x\in\Omega\mid\mathbb{P}[X=x\mid\mathcal{E}]>0\}
if
\mathcal{E}
is an event.

Now we introduce the notion of
\varepsilon
-closeness to describe two random variables that are close to each other in distribution.

Definition 3.2

(
\varepsilon
-close)
.

Let
X
and
X^{\prime}
be two random variables and
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
be two events. We say that
X
is
\varepsilon
-close
to
X^{\prime}
if
\textup{supp}(X)=\textup{supp}(X^{\prime})
and for every
x\in\textup{supp}(X)
, we have

1-\varepsilon\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}[X^{\prime}=x]}{\mathbb{P}[X=x]}\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}.

Similarly, we say that
(X\mid\mathcal{E})
is
\varepsilon
-close
to
(X\mid\mathcal{E}^{\prime})
if
\textup{supp}(X\mid\mathcal{E})=\textup{supp}(X^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{E}^{\prime})
and for every
x\in\textup{supp}(X\mid\mathcal{E})
, we have

1-\varepsilon\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}[X^{\prime}=x\mid\mathcal{E}^{\prime}]}{\mathbb{P}[X=x\mid\mathcal{E}]}\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}.

If
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
are two other random variables, we say that
(X\mid Y)
is
\varepsilon
-close
to
(X^{\prime}\mid Y^{\prime})
if
(X\mid Y=y)
is
\varepsilon
-close to
(X^{\prime}\mid Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime})
for all
(y,y^{\prime})\in\textup{supp}(Y,Y^{\prime})
. We write
(X\mid Y)=(X^{\prime}\mid Y^{\prime})
if
(X\mid Y)
is
0
-close to
(X^{\prime}\mid Y^{\prime})
.

For convenience, we say that a random variable
X
is
\varepsilon
-affected by an event or another random variable if conditioning on the latter only changes the distribution of
X
by a multiplicative
(1-\varepsilon)
-factor.

Definition 3.3

(
\varepsilon
-affected)
.

Let
X
be a random variable and
\mathcal{E}
be an event. We say that
X
is
\varepsilon
-affected
by
\mathcal{E}
if
(X\mid\mathcal{E})
is
\varepsilon
-close to
X
. Similarly, for a random variable
Y
, we say that
X
is
\varepsilon
-affected
by
Y
if
(X\mid Y)
is
\varepsilon
-close to
X
.

We now define the notion of
\varepsilon
-uniformity for a random variable over
\{0,1\}^{r}
.

Definition 3.4

(
\varepsilon
-uniform)
.

Let
\vec{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{r})
be a random variable over
\{0,1\}^{r}
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
, then we say that
\vec{X}
is
\varepsilon
-uniform
if for all
\vec{s}\in\{0,1\}^{r}
,

\mathbb{P}[\vec{X}=\vec{s}]\in\left[(1-\varepsilon)\frac{1}{2^{r}},\,(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}\frac{1}{2^{r}}\right].

For a random variable
Y
, we say that
(\vec{X}\mid Y)
is
\varepsilon
-uniform if
(\vec{X}\mid Y=y)
is
\varepsilon
-uniform for all
y\in\textup{supp}(Y)
.

Definition 3.5

(fix-parity uniform)
.

Let
\vec{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{r})
be a random variable over
\{0,1\}^{r}
. We say that
\vec{X}
is
fix-parity uniform
if there exists
s\in\{0,1\}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\vec{X}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{r})]=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2^{r-1}}&\text{ if $s_{1}+\dots+s_{r}=s$,}\\
0&\text{ if $s_{1}+\dots+s_{r}=1-s$.}\end{cases}

Definition 3.6

(fix-parity
\varepsilon
-uniform)
.

Let
\vec{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{r})
be a random variable over
\{0,1\}^{r}
.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
. We say that
\vec{X}
is
fix-parity
\varepsilon
-uniform
if
\vec{X}
is
\varepsilon
-close to a fix-parity uniform distribution over
\{0,1\}^{r}
.
Similarly, if
Y
is another random variable, we say that
(\vec{X}\mid Y)
is fix-parity
\varepsilon
-uniform if
(\vec{X}\mid Y=y)
is fix-parity
\varepsilon
-uniform for all
y\in\textup{supp}(Y)
.

Now we introduce some useful probabilistic lemmas about
\{0,1\}
-valued random variables.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
and let
X_{1},\dots,X_{\eta}
be independent Bernoulli random variables with probability
p
. Let
p^{*}=\min\{p,1-p\}\in(0,1/2]
. Let
Y=\sum_{i=1}^{\eta}X_{i}\pmod{2}
be the parity of the sum of
X_{1},\dots,X_{\eta}
. Suppose
\eta
is sufficiently large such that
e^{-2\eta p^{*}}<1
. Then
Y
is
e^{-2\eta p^{*}}
-uniform.

Proof.

Since
X_{1},\dots,X_{\eta}
are independent random variables, we have

\left\lvert\mathbb{E}[(-1)^{Y}]\right\rvert=\left|\mathbb{E}\left[(-1)^{\sum_{i=1}^{\eta}X_{i}}\right]\right|=\left|\prod_{i=1}^{\eta}\mathbb{E}\left[(-1)^{X_{i}}\right]\right|=(1-2p^{*})^{\eta}\leq e^{-2\eta p^{*}}.

Since
Y
takes value in
\{0,1\}
, we have

\mathbb{P}[Y=0],\,\mathbb{P}[Y=1]\in\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(1-e^{-2\eta p^{*}}\right),\,\frac{1}{2}\left(1+e^{-2\eta p^{*}}\right)\right].

Since
1+e^{-2\eta p^{*}}\leq(1-e^{-2\eta p^{*}})^{-1}
, we can conclude that
Y
is
e^{-2\eta p^{*}}
-uniform.
∎

We will use the following definition of
\eta
-layered set sequences to characterize the sequence of information we revealed during our removal process.

Definition 3.8

(
\eta
-layered set sequence)
.

Let
(A_{1},\dots,A_{r})
be an ordered sequence of sets, and
\eta\geq 0
. We say that
(A_{1},\dots,A_{r})
is
\eta
-layered
if for all
1\leq j\leq r-1
, we have

\left\lvert A_{j}\setminus(A_{j+1}\cup\dots\cup A_{r})\right\rvert\geq\eta.

The following lemma formalizes the main property of
\eta
-layered set sequences we will use, which is that the parities of these sets (more formally, the parities of the sums of these sets of iid Bernoulli variables) are very close to being uniformly distributed.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
and
X_{1},\dots,X_{t}
be independent Bernoulli random variables with probability
p
. Let
p^{*}=\min\{p,1-p\}\in(0,1/2]
.
Let
(A_{1},\dots,A_{r})
be an
\eta
-layered set sequence, with
A_{j}\subseteq[t]
for every
j
. For every
1\leq j\leq r
, define the random variable

Y_{j}=\sum_{i\in A_{j}}X_{i}\pmod{2},

i.e., the parity of
\sum_{i\in A_{j}}X_{i}
. Suppose
re^{-2\eta p^{*}}<1
. Then
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{r})
is
re^{-2\eta p^{*}}
-uniform.

Proof.

We prove by induction on
r
.
For
r=1
, since
|A_{1}|\geq\eta
, the statement follows from
Lemma

3.7
. Inductively, suppose
r\geq 2
and the lemma is true for all
1\leq r^{\prime}\leq r-1
. Let
(A_{1},\dots,A_{r})
be an
\eta
-layered sequence with
A_{j}\subseteq[t]
for every
j
. Consider any
s_{1},\dots,s_{r}\in\{0,1\}
, and let
\mathcal{E}
denote the event that
Y_{j}=s_{j}
for all
j\in\{2,\dots,r\}
. Since
(A_{2},\dots,A_{r})
is
\eta
-layered, we know from the inductive hypothesis that

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}]\in\left[\left(1-(r-1)e^{-2\eta p^{*}}\right)\frac{1}{2^{r-1}},\,\left(1-(r-1)e^{-2\eta p^{*}}\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{2^{r-1}}\right].

Thus, with
T:=A_{2}\cup\dots\cup A_{r}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[Y_{1}=s_{1},\dots,Y_{r}=s_{r}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[Y_{1}=s_{1},\,\mathcal{E}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[Y_{1}=s_{1}\mid\mathcal{E}\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{i\in A_{1}\setminus T}X_{i}=\sum_{i\in A_{1}\cap T}X_{i}+s_{1}\pmod{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{E}\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1-e^{-2\eta p^{*}}\right)\left(1-(r-1)e^{-2\eta p^{*}}\right)\frac{1}{2^{r-1}}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-re^{-2\eta p^{*}}\right)\frac{1}{2^{r}}.

The second to last inequality follows from
Lemma

3.7
, as
\{X_{i}:i\in A_{1}\setminus T\}
is independent from
\mathcal{E}
and is a set of at least
\eta
iid Bernoulli variables with probability
p
. By the same argument, we also know that
\mathbb{P}\left[Y_{1}=s_{1},\dots,Y_{r}=s_{r}\right]\leq\left(1-re^{-2\eta p^{*}}\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{2^{r}}
. Thus
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{r})
is
re^{-2\eta p^{*}}
-uniform.
∎

When we apply
Lemma

3.9
in our problem, each of
X_{1},\dots,X_{t}
will be an indicator whether the edge
uv
is present in
G
for a pair of vertices
u,v
; the sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}
will be sets of vertex pairs in
G
.

Sometimes we wish to show a result like
Lemma

3.9
where the ordered set sequence
(A^{\prime}_{1},\dots,A^{\prime}_{r})
is not
\eta
-layered at the beginning. The following observation shows that we may first apply an invertible linear transformation
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
to the sets by viewing each of them as a vector in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t}
, and apply
Lemma

3.9
to the resulting collection of sets.

Observation 3.10
.

Suppose
X_{1},\dots,X_{t}\in\{0,1\}
.
Let
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}\subseteq[t]
. For every
1\leq j\leq r
, let

Y_{j}=\sum_{i\in A_{j}}X_{i}\pmod{2}.

Let
T:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
be an invertible linear transformation and let
A^{\prime}_{1},\dots,A^{\prime}_{r}
be the sets given by

(\mathbbm{1}_{A^{\prime}_{1}}(i),\dots,\mathbbm{1}_{A^{\prime}_{r}}(i))=T(\mathbbm{1}_{A_{1}}(i),\dots,\mathbbm{1}_{A_{r}}(i))

for all
i\in[t]
. For every
1\leq j\leq r
, let

Y^{\prime}_{j}=\sum_{i\in A^{\prime}_{j}}X_{i}\pmod{2}.

Then for all
s_{1},\dots,s_{r}\in\{0,1\}
, the event
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{r})=(s_{1},\dots,s_{r})
is the same as the event
(Y^{\prime}_{1},\dots,Y^{\prime}_{r})=T(s_{1},\dots,s_{r})
.

Motivated by this observation, we have the following definition and corollary.

Definition 3.11

(
\eta
-transformed set sequence)
.

We say that an ordered set sequence
(A^{\prime}_{1},\dots,A^{\prime}_{r})
is
\eta
-transformed if there exists an
\eta
-layered set sequence
(A_{1},\dots,A_{r})
and invertible linear transformation
T:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
such that

(\mathbbm{1}_{A^{\prime}_{1}}(i),\dots,\mathbbm{1}_{A^{\prime}_{r}}(i))=T(\mathbbm{1}_{A_{1}}(i),\dots,\mathbbm{1}_{A_{r}}(i))

holds for all
i
.

Corollary 3.12
.

Under the same setting as in
Lemma

3.9
, if
(A_{1},\dots,A_{r})
is
\eta
-transformed instead of
\eta
-layered, we can still conclude that
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{r})
is
re^{-2\eta p^{*}}
-uniform.

The following three lemmas will be crucially used to prove a number of results in
Sections

4
and
5
. These results imply that, after revealing some partial information in the removal procedure, we still have enough randomness in the remaining graph.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{t}
be Bernoulli random variables (not necessarily independent or having the same law). Let
1\leq k<t
and
T:=\{k+1,\dots,t\}
.
Suppose the random variables
\vec{X}:=(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})
and
\vec{X}^{\prime}:=(X_{k+1},\dots,X_{t})
are independent.

For subsets
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}\subseteq[t]
, define the random variables
\vec{Y}=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{r})\in\{0,1\}^{r}
and
\vec{Z}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{r})\in\{0,1\}^{r}
by

Y_{j}=\sum_{i\in A_{j}}X_{i}\pmod{2},\quad Z_{j}=\sum_{i\in A_{j}\cap T}X_{j}\pmod{2}\qquad\text{for $j=1,\dots,r$.}

If
\vec{Z}
is
\varepsilon
-uniform, then we have the following:

(1)

\vec{Y}
is
\varepsilon
-uniform.

(2)

If
\varepsilon<1/2
, then
\vec{X}
is
2\varepsilon
-affected by
\vec{Y}
.

Proof.

Since
\vec{Z}
is a function of
\vec{X}^{\prime}
, and
\vec{X}
and
\vec{X}^{\prime}
are independent, we know that
\vec{X}
and
\vec{Z}
are independent. Fix
\vec{x}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
and
\vec{y}\in\{0,1\}^{r}
. Observe that given
\vec{X}=\vec{x}
, the statement that
\vec{Y}=\vec{y}
is equivalent to the statement that
\vec{Z}=\vec{z}
, where
\vec{z}:=\vec{z}(\vec{x},\vec{y})\in\{0,1\}^{r}
is the vector defined by

z_{j}=y_{j}-\sum_{i\in A_{j}\cap[k]}x_{i}\pmod{2}\qquad\forall j\in[r].

Thus for every
\vec{y}\in\{0,1\}^{r}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\vec{Y}=\vec{y}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{x}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}\mathbb{P}[\vec{Y}=\vec{y}\mid\vec{X}=\vec{x}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[\vec{X}=\vec{x}]=\sum_{\vec{x}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}\mathbb{P}[\vec{Z}=\vec{z}\mid\vec{X}=\vec{x}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[\vec{X}=\vec{x}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{x}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}\mathbb{P}[\vec{Z}=\vec{z}]\mathbb{P}[\vec{X}=\vec{x}]\geq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{1}{2^{r}}\sum_{\vec{x}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}\mathbb{P}[\vec{X}=\vec{x}]=(1-\varepsilon)\frac{1}{2^{r}},

where in the second to last step we used the assumption that
\vec{Z}
is
\varepsilon
-uniform. By the same argument, we have the upper bound
\mathbb{P}[\vec{Y}=\vec{y}]\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}\frac{1}{2^{r}}
as well. Since this holds for all
\vec{y}\in\{0,1\}^{r}
, we get that
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{r}
are
\varepsilon
-uniform. This proves (1).

We now prove (2). For every
\vec{x}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
and
\vec{y}\in\{0,1\}^{r}
, with
\vec{z}:=\vec{z}(\vec{x},\vec{y})
defined above, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\vec{X}=\vec{x}\mid\vec{Y}=\vec{y}]=\frac{\mathbb{P}[\vec{X}=\vec{x},\vec{Y}=\vec{y}]}{\mathbb{P}[\vec{Y}=\vec{y}]}=\frac{\mathbb{P}[\vec{X}=\vec{x},\vec{Z}=\vec{z}]}{\mathbb{P}[\vec{Y}=\vec{y}]}=\frac{\mathbb{P}[\vec{X}=\vec{x}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[\vec{Z}=\vec{z}]}{\mathbb{P}[\vec{Y}=\vec{y}]}.

By (1),
\vec{Y}
is
\varepsilon
-uniform, so we can conclude that

\frac{\mathbb{P}[\vec{X}=\vec{x}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[\vec{Z}=\vec{z}]}{\mathbb{P}[\vec{Y}=\vec{y}]}\geq\frac{(1-\varepsilon)2^{-r}}{(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}2^{-r}}\mathbb{P}[\vec{X}=\vec{x}]\geq(1-2\varepsilon)\mathbb{P}[\vec{X}=\vec{x}].

The upper bound follows symmetrically.
∎

We also need the following variant of
Lemma

3.13
for fix-parity distributions.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{t}
be Bernoulli random variables (not necessarily independent or having the same law).
Let
1\leq k<t
and
T:=\{k+1,\dots,t\}
.
Suppose the random variables
\vec{X}:=(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})
and
\vec{X}^{\prime}:=(X_{k+1},\dots,X_{t})
are independent.

For subsets
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}\subseteq[t]
, define the random variables
\vec{Y}=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{r})\in\{0,1\}^{r}
and
\vec{Z}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{r})\in\{0,1\}^{r}
by

Y_{j}=\sum_{i\in A_{j}}X_{i}\pmod{2},\quad Z_{j}=\sum_{i\in A_{j}\cap T}X_{j}\pmod{2}\qquad\text{for $j=1,\dots,r$.}

Finally, let
W=\sum_{j=1}^{r}Y_{j}\pmod{2}
.
Suppose
\vec{Z}
is fix-parity
\varepsilon
-uniform, with
s=\sum_{j=1}^{r}Z_{j}

and
\varepsilon<1/2
. Then
(\vec{X}\mid\vec{Y})
is
2\varepsilon
-close to
(\vec{X}\mid W)
.

Proof.

Note that

W=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sum_{i\in A_{j}\setminus T}X_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{r}Z_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sum_{i\in A_{j}\setminus T}X_{i}+s.

Thus,
W
is independent from
\vec{X}^{\prime}
, and hence
(\vec{X}\mid W)
is independent from
\vec{X}^{\prime}
.

We
apply
Lemma

3.13
with
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{r-1})
in place of
\vec{Y}
,
(\vec{X}\mid W)
in place of
\vec{X}
, and
\vec{X}^{\prime}
as it is. We already showed that
(\vec{X}\mid W)
and
\vec{X}^{\prime}
are independent. Furthermore, since
\vec{Z}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{r})
is fix-parity
\varepsilon
-uniform, it follows that

(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{r-1})

is
\varepsilon
-uniform.
Thus, we may conclude from
Lemma

3.13
that
(\vec{X}\mid W)
is
2\varepsilon
-affected by
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{r-1})
. This shows that
(\vec{X}\mid\vec{Y})=(\vec{X}\mid(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{r-1}),W)
is
2\varepsilon
-close to
(\vec{X}\mid W)
.
∎

For convenience, we define the following notion for describing sets with almost equal sizes.

Definition 3.15

(Balanced sets)
.

Let
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}
be finite sets. We say that
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}
are balanced if
\left\lvert A_{i}\right\rvert,\left\lvert A_{j}\right\rvert
differ by at most
1
for all
i,j\in[r]
.

Finally, we have the following lemma that shows the degree parities of vertices in a
p
-random bipartite graph is close to being fix-parity uniform.

Lemma 3.16
.

Fix
p\in(0,1)
and set
p^{*}=\min(p,1-p)
. There exists
\eta_{0}=\eta_{0}(p)
such that the following holds. For any sufficiently large integer
\eta>\eta_{0}
, let
G=(V,E)
be a random bipartite graph with bipartition
V=A\sqcup B
such that
\eta\leq|A|,|B|\leq e^{\eta(p^{*})^{2}/10}
, and every edge between
A,B
is included independently with probability
p
. For
v\in V
and
C\subseteq V
, let
\textup{par}(v,C)\in\{0,1\}
denote the parity of the number of edges of
G
between
v
and
C
. Then
((\textup{par}(a,B))_{a\in A},(\textup{par}(b,A))_{b\in B})\in\{0,1\}^{|A|+|B|}
is fix-parity
e^{-\eta(p^{*})^{2}/30}
-uniform.

To prove
Lemma

3.16
, first note that
\sum_{a\in A}\textup{par}(a,B)+\sum_{b\in B}\textup{par}(b,A)
is always even, so it suffices to check that with
c:=(p^{*})^{2}/30
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left[((\textup{par}(a,B))_{a\in A},(\textup{par}(b,A))_{b\in B})=s\right]\in\left[(1-e^{-c\eta})\cdot\frac{1}{2^{\left\lvert A\right\rvert+\left\lvert B\right\rvert-1}},(1-e^{-c\eta})^{-1}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{\left\lvert A\right\rvert+\left\lvert B\right\rvert-1}}\right]

for all
s\in\{0,1\}^{|A|+|B|}
with
\sum_{a\in A}s_{a}+\sum_{b\in B}s_{b}=0\pmod{2}
.

We first prove the case where the sizes of
A,B
are unbalanced.

Lemma 3.17
.

Assume the setup of
Lemma

3.16
. Suppose we have
5|B|/p^{*}\leq\left\lvert A\right\rvert\leq e^{\eta(p^{*})^{2}/10}
and
\left\lvert B\right\rvert\geq\eta p^{*}/10
. Then
((\textup{par}(a,B))_{a\in A},(\textup{par}(b,A))_{b\in B})
is fix-parity
e^{-\eta(p^{*})^{2}/20}
-uniform.

Proof.

Suppose
\eta
is sufficiently large so that
m:=|B|\geq\eta p^{*}/10\geq 2
. In the following, we will also assume
\eta
is large enough so that some inequalities hold. Observe that, since the sets
a\times B
for all
a\in A
are pairwise disjoint, by
Lemma

3.9
,
(\textup{par}(a,B))_{a\in A}
is
|A|e^{-2mp^{*}}
-uniform. Let
\mathcal{P}_{A}:=(\textup{par}(a,B))_{a\in A}
and
\mathcal{P}_{B}:=(\textup{par}(b,A))_{b\in B}
.

Fix any ordering
b_{1},\dots,b_{m}
of the vertices in
B
.
For convenience, let
P_{i}:=\textup{par}(b_{i},A)
for every
i\in[m]
and let
N_{S}(v)
denote the neighbors of
v
in
S
.
Consider a balanced partition
A=A_{0}\sqcup A_{1}
. For every
i\in[m-1]
, let
\mathcal{E}_{i}
denote the event that
N_{A_{j}}(b_{i})\neq N_{A_{j}}(b_{i+1})
where
j=i\pmod{2}
; let
\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{i}
denote its complement event
N_{A_{j}}(b_{i})=N_{A_{j}}(b_{i+1})
(that is,
b_{i}
and
b_{i+1}
have identical neighborhoods in
A_{j}
). We first prove that it is unlikely for any
\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{i}
to occur.

Claim 3.18
.

\mathbb{P}[\exists i\in[m-1]\text{ such that }\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\mid\mathcal{P}_{A}]\leq 2me^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/3}.

Proof.

We first show that for every
i\in[m-1]
, whether
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is true is
2|A|e^{-2(m-2)p^{*}}
-affected by
\mathcal{P}_{A}
.
Again, since the sets
a\times(B\setminus\{b_{i},b_{i+1}\})
for all
a\in A
are pairwise disjoint, we know from
Lemma

3.9
that
(\textup{par}(a,B\setminus\{b_{i},b_{i+1}\})_{a\in A}
is
|A|e^{-2(m-2)p^{*}}
-uniform.
Thus we can apply
Lemma

3.13
where

(1)

\vec{X}
is the subgraph of
G
on
A\times\{b_{i},b_{i+1}\}
,

(2)

\vec{X}^{\prime}
is the subgraph of
G
on
A\times(B\setminus\{b_{i},b_{i+1}\})
,

(3)

the sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}
are
a\times B
for all
a\in A
, such that
\vec{Y}=(\textup{par}(a,B))_{a\in A}=\mathcal{P}_{A}
and
\vec{Z}=(\textup{par}(a,B\setminus\{b_{i},b_{i+1}\}))_{a\in A}
.

By
Lemma

3.13
, the subgraph of
G
on
A\times\{b_{i},b_{i+1}\}
is
2|A|e^{-2(m-2)p^{*}}
-affected by
\mathcal{P}_{A}
. Since whether
\mathcal{E}_{i}
holds is completely determined by this subgraph, it is
2|A|e^{-2(m-2)p^{*}}
-affected by
\mathcal{P}_{A}
as well.
Thus for all
i\in[m-1]
and
j=i\pmod{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\mid\mathcal{P}_{A}]

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-2|A|e^{-2(m-2)p^{*}}\right)^{-1}\mathbb{P}[\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{i}].

Since

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{i}]=\mathbb{P}[N_{A_{j}}(b_{i})=N_{A_{j}}(b_{i+1})]

\displaystyle\leq\max_{0\leq x\leq|A_{j}|}p^{x}(1-p)^{|A_{j}|-x}=(1-p^{*})^{|A_{j}|}\leq e^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/3},

we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\mid\mathcal{P}_{A}]

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-2|A|e^{-2(m-2)p^{*}}\right)^{-1}e^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/3}\leq 2e^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/3}.

Here we used the fact that
\left\lvert A\right\rvert\leq e^{\eta(p^{*})^{2}/10}\leq e^{2(m-2)p^{*}}/4
, as
\eta
is large enough. A union bound over all
i\in[m-1]
finishes the proof.
∎

Next, we will show that, conditioning on
\mathcal{P}_{A}
and
\mathcal{E}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{m-1}
, the parities
(P_{i})_{i\in|B|}
are fix-parity uniform.

Claim 3.19
.

For any
s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\in\{0,1\}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{m}s_{i}=\sum_{a\in A}\textup{par}(a,B)
,

\mathbb{P}[P_{i}=s_{i}\,\,\forall i\in[m]\mid\mathcal{E}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{m-1},\mathcal{P}_{A}]=\frac{1}{2^{m-1}}.

Proof.

It suffices to show that for each
i\in[m-1]
, there is a bijection
f_{i}
between bipartite graphs on
A\sqcup B
satisfying
\mathcal{E}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{m-1}
such that the following three conditions hold:

(1)

f_{i}
preserves the total number of edges;

(2)

f_{i}
preserves
\mathcal{P}_{A}=(\textup{par}(a,B))_{a\in A}
and
(\textup{par}(b,A))_{b\in B\setminus\{b_{i},b_{i+1}\}}
;

(3)

f_{i}
flips both
\textup{par}(b_{i},A)
and
\textup{par}(b_{i+1},A)
.

We define the bijection
f_{i}
as follows. Let
j=i\pmod{2}
and fix an ordering
A_{j}=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{\left\lvert A_{j}\right\rvert}\}
. Assume
H
is a bipartite graph on
A\sqcup B
satisfying
\mathcal{E}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{m-1}
.
Since
H
satisfies
\mathcal{E}_{i}
, we can pick a vertex
a_{k}
such that exactly one of
b_{i}a_{k}
and
b_{i+1}a_{k}
is an edge in
H
. We pick
k_{H}
to be the smallest such
k
.
Define
f_{i}(H)
to be the graph obtained from
H
by switching
b_{i}a_{k}
and
b_{i+1}a_{k}
. It is easy to verify that
f_{i}
satisfies the above three conditions. In particular,
f_{i}
is a bijection since
f_{i}(f_{i}(H))=H
for all
H
, which follows from the fact that
k_{H}=k_{f_{i}(H)}
.
∎

Now we proceed to proving
Lemma

3.17
. For any

s\in\{0,1\}^{|A|+|B|}
with
\sum_{a\in A}s_{a}+\sum_{b\in B}s_{b}=0\pmod{2}
, letting
s_{A}=(s_{a})_{a\in A}
and
s_{B}=(s_{b})_{b\in B}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{P}_{A}=s_{A},\,\mathcal{P}_{B}=s_{B}]=\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{P}_{B}=s_{B}\mid\mathcal{P}_{A}=s_{A}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{P}_{A}=s_{A}].

Since
\mathcal{P}_{A}
is
\left\lvert A\right\rvert e^{-2mp^{*}}
-uniform, we have

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{P}_{A}=s_{A}]\in\left[(1-\left\lvert A\right\rvert e^{-2mp^{*}})\frac{1}{2^{\left\lvert A\right\rvert}},(1-\left\lvert A\right\rvert e^{-2mp^{*}})^{-1}\frac{1}{2^{\left\lvert A\right\rvert}}\right].

Also by
3.18
and
3.19
, the probability
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{P}_{B}=s_{B}\mid\mathcal{P}_{A}=s_{A}]
is at least

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{P}_{B}=s_{B},\mathcal{E}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{m-1}\mid\mathcal{P}_{A}=s_{A}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{P}_{B}=s_{B}\mid\mathcal{E}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{m-1},\mathcal{P}_{A}=s_{A}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{m-1}\mid\mathcal{P}_{A}=s_{A}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{m-1}}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{m-1}\mid\mathcal{P}_{A}=s_{A}]\geq(1-2me^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/3})\frac{1}{2^{m-1}}

and is at most

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{P}_{B}=s_{B},\mathcal{E}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{m-1}\mid\mathcal{P}_{A}=s_{A}]+\mathbb{P}[\exists i\in[m-1]\text{ such that }\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\mid\mathcal{P}_{A}=s_{A}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{m-1}}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{m-1}\mid\mathcal{P}_{A}=s_{A}]+(1-\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{E}_{m-1}\mid\mathcal{P}_{A}=s_{A}])

\displaystyle\leq(1-2me^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/3})\frac{1}{2^{m-1}}+2me^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/3}.

Since
m=\left\lvert B\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/5
, we know that when
m
is large enough, we have

(1-2me^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/3})\frac{1}{2^{m-1}}\geq(1-e^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/6})\frac{1}{2^{m-1}}

and

\displaystyle(1-2me^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/3})\frac{1}{2^{m-1}}+2me^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/3}

\displaystyle=(1-2me^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/3}+2me^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/3+(m-1)\ln 2})\frac{1}{2^{m-1}}

\displaystyle\leq(1-e^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/6})^{-1}\frac{1}{2^{m-1}}.

Thus, we showed that
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{P}_{A}=s_{A},\,\mathcal{P}_{B}=s_{B}]
is in

\left[(1-e^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/6})(1-\left\lvert A\right\rvert e^{-2mp^{*}})\frac{1}{2^{\left\lvert A\right\rvert+m-1}},(1-e^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/6})^{-1}(1-\left\lvert A\right\rvert e^{-2mp^{*}})^{-1}\frac{1}{2^{\left\lvert A\right\rvert+m-1}}\right].

Since
(1-e^{-\left\lvert A\right\rvert p^{*}/6})(1-\left\lvert A\right\rvert e^{-2mp^{*}})\geq 1-e^{-\eta(p^{*})^{2}/20}
when
\eta
is large enough, we conclude that
(\textup{par}(a,B),\textup{par}(b,A))_{a\in A,b\in B}
is fix-parity
e^{-\eta(p^{*})^{2}/20}
-uniform.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

3.16
.

Now, we may prove the general case for
Lemma

3.16
. We may assume without loss of generality that
\eta\leq\left\lvert B\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert A\right\rvert\leq e^{\eta(p^{*})^{2}/10}
. Partition
B
into
k=\lfloor 6/p^{*}\rfloor
balanced sets
B_{1},\dots,B_{k}
. Then we may apply
Lemma

3.17
to the bipartite graph between
A
and
B_{i}
for each
i\in[k]
and conclude that
((\textup{par}(a,B_{i})_{a\in B},(\textup{par}(b,A))_{b\in B_{i}})
is fix-parity
e^{-\eta(p^{*})^{2}/20}
-uniform. Since the subgraphs of
G
restricted on
A\times B_{1},\dots,A\times B_{k}
are independent, we know that for all
s^{(1)}_{A},\dots,s^{(k)}_{A}\in\{0,1\}^{|A|}
and
s_{B_{1}}\in\{0,1\}^{|B_{1}|},\dots,s_{B_{k}}\in\{0,1\}^{|B_{k}|}
such that every
s^{(i)}_{A}
and
s_{B_{i}}
have the same parity sum, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[(\textup{par}(a,B_{i})_{a\in B}=s^{(i)}_{A},\,(\textup{par}(b,A))_{b\in B_{i}}=s_{B_{i}}\text{ for all }i\in[k]]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}[(\textup{par}(a,B_{i})_{a\in B}=s^{(i)}_{A},\,(\textup{par}(b,A))_{b\in B_{i}}=s_{B_{i}}]

\displaystyle\in\left[(1-e^{-\eta(p^{*})^{2}/20})^{k}\frac{1}{2^{k|A|+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|B_{i}|-k}},(1-e^{-\eta(p^{*})^{2}/20})^{-k}\frac{1}{2^{k|A|+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|B_{i}|-k}}\right].

Observe that for all
s_{A}\in\{0,1\}^{|A|}
and
s_{B}=(s_{B_{i}})_{i\in[k]}\in\{0,1\}^{|B|}
with the same parity sum, there are
2^{(|A|-1)(k-1)}
ways to choose
s_{A}^{(1)},\dots,s_{A}^{(k)}\in\{0,1\}^{|A|}
such that
s_{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{A}^{(i)}
, and every
s_{A}^{(i)}
and
s_{B_{i}}
have the same parity sum. Indeed, one can pick
s_{A}^{(1)},\dots,s_{A}^{(k-1)}
arbitrarily as long as the parity condition holds, and these uniquely fix
s_{A}^{(k)}=s_{A}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}s_{A}^{(i)}
.

Thus, we get that for every
s_{A}
and
s_{B}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[(\textup{par}(a,B)_{a\in B}=s_{A},\,(\textup{par}(b,A))_{b\in B}=s_{B}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(s_{A}^{(i)})_{i\in[k]}}\mathbb{P}[(\textup{par}(a,B_{i})_{a\in B}=s^{(i)}_{A},\,(\textup{par}(b,A))_{b\in B_{i}}=s_{B_{i}}\text{ for all }i\in[k]]

\displaystyle\in\left[2^{(|A|-1)(k-1)}(1-e^{-\eta(p^{*})^{2}/20})^{k}\frac{1}{2^{k|A|+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|B_{i}|-k}},2^{(|A|-1)(k-1)}(1-e^{-\eta(p^{*})^{2}/20})^{-k}\frac{1}{2^{k|A|+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|B_{i}|-k}}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[(1-e^{-\eta(p^{*})^{2}/20})^{k}\frac{1}{2^{|A|+|B|-1}},(1-e^{-\eta(p^{*})^{2}/20})^{-k}\frac{1}{2^{|A|+|B|-1}}\right].

Thus, we get that
((\textup{par}(a,B))_{a\in A},(\textup{par}(b,A))_{b\in B})
is fix-parity
e^{-\eta(p^{*})^{2}/30}
-uniform, as

(1-e^{-\eta(p^{*})^{2}/20})^{k}\geq 1-ke^{-\eta(p^{*})^{2}/20}\geq 1-e^{-\eta(p^{*})^{2}/30},

when
\eta
is large enough.
∎

4.
(U,W)
-removal

The following
Section

4
and
Section

5
will be devoted to proving
Theorem

2.3
. To prove
Theorem

2.3
, we show that given any
\alpha
-partially revealed
p
-graph
G
, with high probability we can sequentially remove
m:=3n/4\pm o(n)
vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
from the graph
G
so that an even number of edges is removed every time a vertex is removed.
Furthermore, the procedure used to choose these vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
does not reveal too much information about
G
. This means that the remaining graph after the removal is close to an
\alpha
-partially revealed
p
-graph on which we can apply induction.

As mentioned in the
Section

2
, the above process is what we will call the
(U,W)
-removal. We remark that to get the
f_{\alpha}(n)\leq e^{-\Theta(n^{1/2-\alpha})}
upper bound, we do need a quadratic term
\max_{n^{\prime}\in[n/4-o(n),n/4+o(n)]}f_{\alpha}(n^{\prime})^{2}
on the right hand side of
Theorem

2.3
. This in practice means that during the induction, we need to perform
(U,W)
-removal on
G
twice so that the probability that neither of them succeeds is very low.

4.1.
Notations for random graphs

We first introduce the notations we will use in
Sections

5
and
4
.
From now on, we fix

For a set of potential edges

4.2.
(U,W)
-removal

We define the process of
(U,W)
-removal as follows.

Definition 4.1

(
(U,W)
-removal)
.

Input
:
a partially revealed graph
G=(V,E)
with revealed part
A=\{a_{1}.\dots,a_{\left\lvert A\right\rvert}\}
, and a partition
V\setminus A=U\sqcup W
, where
U
is equipped with an ordering
U=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{|U|}\}
, and
W
is equipped with a partition
W=W_{\#}\sqcup W_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup W_{s}

Output
: If the process succeeds, then it outputs a subset
V_{W}\subseteq W
and an ordered sequence
R=(v_{1},\dots,v_{|V\setminus V_{W}|})
of
V\setminus V_{W}
such that for every
1\leq i\leq|V\setminus V_{W}|
,
\textup{par}(v_{i},V\setminus\{v_{1},\dots,v_{i-1}\})=0
. Otherwise the process fails and outputs nothing.

Maintained sets/pointers:

We use pointers
\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}
to keep track of the number of vertices removed from
A\cup U
and
W
respectively. We let
R^{(\mathfrak{p})}
denote the set of vertices removed in the first
\mathfrak{p}
rounds (i.e., after
\mathfrak{p}
vertices are removed from
A\cup U
). We use
\mathcal{I}_{A}^{(\mathfrak{p})}
,
\mathcal{I}_{U}^{(\mathfrak{p})}
,
\mathcal{I}_{W}^{(\mathfrak{p})}
to denote the collections of potential edge sets whose parities are revealed and
\mathcal{I}_{e}^{(\mathfrak{p})}
to denote the potential edge sets revealed in the first
\mathfrak{p}
rounds.

Procedure
:

Concatenate
A
and
U
to form a sequence
(\overline{u}_{1},\dots,\overline{u}_{\left\lvert A\right\rvert+\left\lvert U\right\rvert}):=(a_{1},\dots,a_{\left\lvert A\right\rvert},u_{1},\dots,u_{\left\lvert U\right\rvert})
.

Initialize
\mathfrak{p}=1
,
\mathfrak{q}=1
,
R^{(0)}=\varnothing
, and
\mathcal{I}_{A}^{(0)},\mathcal{I}_{U}^{(0)},\mathcal{I}_{W}^{(0)},\mathcal{I}_{e}^{(0)}=\varnothing
.

For
\mathfrak{p}=1,\dots,\left\lvert A\right\rvert+\left\lvert U\right\rvert
:

(1)

Reveal
\textup{par}(\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}},V\setminus R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)})
. If
\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}}\in A
, set
\mathcal{I}_{A}^{(\mathfrak{p})}=\mathcal{I}_{A}^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}\cup\{S(\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}},V\setminus R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)})\}
and
\mathcal{I}_{U}^{(\mathfrak{p})}=\mathcal{I}_{U}^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}
. If
\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}}\in U
, set
\mathcal{I}_{U}^{(\mathfrak{p})}=\mathcal{I}_{U}^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}\cup\{S(\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}},V\setminus R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)})\}
and
\mathcal{I}_{A}^{(\mathfrak{p})}=\mathcal{I}_{A}^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}
.

(2)

Given the value of
\textup{par}(\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}},V\setminus R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)})
revealed in step (1):

•

If
\textup{par}(\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}},V\setminus R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)})=0
: set
R^{(\mathfrak{p})}:=R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}\cup\{\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}}\}
and set
v_{|R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}|+1}:=\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Set
\mathcal{I}^{(\mathfrak{p})}_{e}=\mathcal{I}^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}_{e}
and
\mathcal{I}^{(\mathfrak{p})}_{W}=\mathcal{I}^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}_{W}
.

•

If
\textup{par}(\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}},V\setminus R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)})=1
: let
j=\mathfrak{q}\pmod{s}
(recall that we use the convention
s\pmod{s}=s
), reveal
G[\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}},W_{j}\setminus R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}]
and set
\mathcal{I}^{(\mathfrak{p})}_{e}=\mathcal{I}^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}_{e}\cup\{S(\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}},W_{j}\setminus R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)})\}
. Reveal
\textup{par}(w,V\setminus R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)})
and set
\mathcal{I}^{(\mathfrak{p})}_{W}=\mathcal{I}^{(\mathfrak{p})}_{W}\cup\{S(w,V\setminus R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}):w\in W_{j}\setminus R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}\}
.

–

If there exists at least one vertex
w\in W_{j}\setminus R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}
such that
\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}}w\in E
and
\textup{par}(w,V\setminus R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)})=0
, then we pick one such
w
and set
R^{(\mathfrak{p})}=R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}\cup\{w,\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}}\}
. Furthermore, we label
v_{|R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}|+1}:=w
,
v_{|R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}|+2}:=\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}}
, and increment
\mathfrak{q}
by
1
.

–

If there is no such
w
, we terminate the process and say the process
fails
.

If the process does not fail, then we say the process
succeeds
and output
R:=R^{(\left\lvert A\right\rvert+\left\lvert U\right\rvert)}
and
V_{W}:=V\setminus R
.

Remark 4.2
.

In the above process, we use the pointers
\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{q}
to keep track of the number of vertices removed from
A\cup U
and
W
respectively. For every
\mathfrak{p}=1,\dots,\left\lvert A\right\rvert+\left\lvert U\right\rvert
, we aim to remove the vertex
\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}}
at round
\mathfrak{p}
, and we use
R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)}
to denote the set of already removed vertices after round
\mathfrak{p}-1
. If
\textup{par}(\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}},V\setminus R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)})=0
, then we can directly remove
\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}}
and be done. If
\textup{par}(\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}},V\setminus R^{(\mathfrak{p}-1)})=1
, then we try to find some
w\in W_{j}
where
j=\mathfrak{q}\pmod{s}
so that we can first remove
w
and then remove
\overline{u}_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Note that we require ourselves to loop through the blocks
W_{1},\dots,W_{s}
when we remove vertices from
W
. In other words, we want the
\mathfrak{q}
-th vertex removed from
W
to lie in the block
W_{\mathfrak{q}\pmod{s}}
.

-removal

-removal succeeds with the number of remaining vertices after the removal is concentrated around its expectation.

\mathfrak{p}

For convenience, we define

\mathcal{I}_{W}=\mathcal{I}^{(\mathfrak{p})}_{W}
,
\mathcal{I}_{e}=\mathcal{I}^{(\mathfrak{p})}_{e}

We emphasis that in the following analysis, all the hidden constants do not depend on
\mathfrak{p}

We know from

\mathfrak{p}
-th round, we revealed
\textup{par}(\mathcal{I}_{A})

\textup{par}(\mathcal{I}_{U})
,
\textup{par}(\mathcal{I}_{W})
,
G[\mathcal{I}_{e}]

\mathcal{J}_{A}:=\{S(a,G)\mid a\in A\}

G[A]
,
\textup{par}(\mathcal{J}_{A})
,
\textup{par}(V)
due to
G
being a partially revealed graph.

Let
w_{i}
denote the
i

W
.
To compute the success probability of the

-removal, we first show the following lemma, which will imply that the parities we queried in the process are close to being uniformly distributed.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\Sigma=\binom{W}{2}\cup S(A\cup U,W_{\#})

\Theta(n^{1/2-\alpha})

We begin by examining what sets in

with

. In other words, we have

\displaystyle m(w)=\begin{cases}\mathfrak{q}^{\prime}&\text{if }w=w_{\mathfrak{q}^{\prime}}\in R,\\
\max\{\mathfrak{q}^{\prime}:\mathfrak{q}^{\prime}\equiv j\pmod{s},\,1\leq\mathfrak{q}^{\prime}\leq|W\cap R|\}&\text{if $w\in W_{j}\setminus R$ and $W_{j}\cap R\neq\varnothing$}\\

\displaystyle S(w,W\setminus\{w_{1},\dots,w_{m(w)-1}\}),\,

\displaystyle S(w,W\setminus\{w_{1},\dots,w_{m(w)-s-1}\}),\dots

\displaystyle\qquad\dots,S\left(w,W\setminus\left\{w_{1},\dots,w_{m(w)-\lfloor\frac{m(w)-1}{s}\rfloor s-1}\right\}\right)

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{W}|_{\Sigma}=\left\{Q_{t}^{\prime}(w):w\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}W_{j},\,m(w)\neq\perp,\,0\leq t\leq\lfloor\frac{m(w)-1}{s}\rfloor\right\}.

\displaystyle\left\{\binom{V}{2}\right\}\bigl|_{\Sigma}=\{\Sigma\}.

Since every potential edge set in

\displaystyle(\mathcal{J}_{A})|_{\Sigma}\cup\mathcal{I}_{U}|_{\Sigma}=(\mathcal{J}_{A})|_{S(A\cup U,W_{\#})}\cup\mathcal{I}_{U}|_{S(A\cup U,W_{\#})}=\{S(u,W_{\#})\mid u\in A\cup U\}.

5.3.

-close to

Proposition 5.5
.

Let

be a partially revealed
p
-graph. Assume the random variables
\Gamma,\Gamma_{0},\mathcal{E},G[V_{B}],G[V_{C}],G_{B},G_{C}
are defined as above so that
\Gamma_{0}\in\textup{supp}(\Gamma)
and

holds when conditioning on

. Then the graph
(G[V_{B}]\cup G[V_{C}]\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0})
is
e^{-\Theta(n^{1/2-\alpha})}
-close to
(G_{B}\cup G_{C}\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0})
.

We will prove

5.5
in

5.4
and
5.5

3
a number of times. We now prove

2.3
assuming

5.5

Proof of
Theorem

2.3
.

Notice that by
Proposition

4.3
and a union bound, we know that the probability
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}]\geq 1-e^{-\Theta(n^{1/2-\alpha})}
.
Furthermore, we have

\mathbb{P}\left[G[V_{B}]\cup G[V_{C}]=H\mid\mathcal{E}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}\left[\mathbb{P}[G[V_{B}]\cup G[V_{C}]=H\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}]\right],

where the expectation is taken over all assignments
\Gamma_{0}
such that
\mathcal{E}
is true. By
Proposition

5.5
, the graph
(G[V_{B}]\cup G[V_{C}]\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0})
is
e^{-\Theta(n^{1/2-\alpha})}
-close to
(G_{B}\cup G_{C}\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0})
.
This implies that for any assignment
H
of potential edges in
\binom{V_{B}}{2}\cup\binom{V_{C}}{2}
, we have

\displaystyle 1-e^{-\Theta(n^{1/2-\alpha})}\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}[G[V_{B}]\cup G[V_{C}]=H\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}]}{\mathbb{P}[G_{B}\cup G_{C}=H\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}]}\leq\left(1-e^{-\Theta(n^{1/2-\alpha})}\right)^{-1},

whenever
H
is in
\textup{supp}(G[V_{B}]\cup G[V_{C}]\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0})=\textup{supp}(G_{B}\cup G_{C}\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0})
. In particular, by an averaging argument, we have

\displaystyle 1-e^{-\Theta(n^{1/2-\alpha})}\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}[G[V_{B}]\text{ or }G[V_{C}]\text{ is even-degenerate}\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}]}{\mathbb{P}[G_{B}\text{ or }G_{C}\text{ is even-degenerate}\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}]}\leq\left(1-e^{-\Theta(n^{1/2-\alpha})}\right)^{-1}.

Now we can lower bound the probability that
G
is even-degenerate as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[G\text{ is even-degenerate}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[G[V_{B}]\text{ or }G[V_{C}]\text{ is even-degenerate}\mid\mathcal{E}]

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-e^{-\Theta(n^{1/2-\alpha})}\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}\left[\mathbb{P}[G[V_{B}]\text{ or }G[V_{C}]\text{ is even-degenerate}\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}]\right]

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-e^{-\Theta(n^{1/2-\alpha})}\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}\left[\mathbb{P}[G_{B}\text{ or }G_{C}\text{ is even-degenerate}\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}]\right]

Since
G_{B}
and
G_{C}
are independent conditioning on
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}\left[\mathbb{P}[G_{B}\text{ or }G_{C}\text{ is even-degenerate}\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma_{0}}\left[1-\mathbb{P}[G_{B}\text{ is not even-degenerate}\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[G_{C}\text{ is not even-degenerate}\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}]\right].

Combining with

\mathbb{P}[G_{B}\text{ is not even-degenerate}\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}]\leq f(\left\lvert V_{B}\right\rvert),

\mathbb{P}[G_{C}\text{ is not even-degenerate}\mid\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}]\leq f(\left\lvert V_{C}\right\rvert),

we get the desired recursion

(5.5)

(G[V_{B}]\mid\textup{par}(\mathcal{Q}_{0}),\textup{par}(\mathcal{Q}_{2}),G[A_{B}],\textup{par}(\mathcal{J}_{A_{B}}),\textup{par}(V_{B}),G[\Sigma_{B}]).

and is thus
e^{-\Theta(n^{1/2-\alpha})}
-close to

(G[V_{B}]\mid G[A_{B}],\textup{par}(\mathcal{J}_{A_{B}}),\textup{par}(V_{B}),G[\Sigma_{B}]).\qed

6.
Upper bound on
f_{\alpha}(n)

In this section we show
Theorem

1.3
. Consider any
p\in(0,1)
and
0<\alpha<1/2
.
Recall that
f(n):=f_{\alpha}(n)
is the maximum of the probability that
G
is not even-degenerate over all
\alpha
-partially revealed
p
-graphs
G
with
n
vertices.

Before proving the exponential upper bound on
f(n)
, we first need to prove that
f(n)
is bounded away from
1
. This is a technical issue due to the fact that we do not know whether
f(n)
is monotone decreasing in
n
. Thus, we first prove
Lemmas

6.1
and
6.2
.

Lemma 6.1
.

There exists constant
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
depending only on
p
and
\alpha
such that the following holds. For all
N>N_{1}
, there exists
\delta_{1}:=\delta_{1}(N)>0
such that for all
N_{1}\leq n\leq N
,
f(n)\leq 1-\delta_{1}
.

Proof.

Pick
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
3n^{1-2\alpha}+4\leq n
holds for all
n\geq N_{1}
.
Suppose
n\geq N_{1}
.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a partially revealed
p
-graph on
n
vertices with revealed part
A
with
|A|\leq n^{1-2\alpha}
. We show that, given any instance
H\subseteq\binom{A}{2}
,
s\in\{0,1\}
, and
(s_{a})_{a\in A}\in\{0,1\}^{\left\lvert A\right\rvert}
, we can construct a specific graph
G_{0}
on
V
that is even-degenerate and
G_{0}[A]=H
,
\left\lvert E(G_{0})\right\rvert\equiv s\pmod{2}
, and
\deg_{G_{0}}(a)\equiv s_{a}\pmod{2}
for all
a\in A
.

By our choice of
N_{1}
, we have
3|A|+4\leq|V|
so that there will be enough vertices in the following construction. We construct the graph
G_{0}
on
V
as follows:

(1)

Start with the prescribed graph
G_{0}[A]=H
and
|V|-|A|
isolated vertices in
V\setminus A
.

(2)

Take any
2|A|+4
vertices from
V\setminus A
and label them as

\bigcup_{a\in A}\{x_{a},y_{a}\}\cup\{w,t_{1},t_{2},b\}.

(3)

For every
a\in A
, if
\deg_{H}(a)\equiv s_{a}\pmod{2}
, add the edges
ax_{a}
,
ay_{a}
; if
\deg_{H}(a)\not\equiv s_{a}\pmod{2}
, only add the edge
ax_{a}
.

(4)

For every
a\in A
, add the edge
wx_{a}
. Add edges
wt_{1},wt_{2},t_{1}t_{2}
.

(5)

If the edge number of the current graph does not agree with the prescribed parity
s
, add the edge
bw
.

Let
G_{0}
be the graph obtained after (1)–(5). It is clear that
G_{0}
agrees with the prescribed information
G_{0}[A]=H
,
\left\lvert E(G_{0})\right\rvert\equiv s\pmod{2}
, and
\deg_{G_{0}}(a)\equiv s_{a}\pmod{2}
for all
a\in A
.

To show that
G_{0}
is even-degenerate, take any ordering
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{|A|}\}
. We sequentially remove
a_{1},\dots,a_{|A|}
from
G_{0}
as follows. For
i=1,\dots,|A|
, let
s
denote the parity of
\deg(a_{i})
in the current graph. If
s=1
, then we first remove
x_{a_{i}}
(as it has exactly two neighbors
a_{i}
,
s
) and then remove
a_{i}
; if
s=0
, then we just remove
a_{i}
.

After this procedure, the remaining edges in the graph form a star centered at
s
(with possible leaves
b
and some
x_{a_{i}}
’s), and a triangle on
s,t_{1},t_{2}
. Now if
s
currently has even degree, we can remove
s
and end at a single edge
t_{1}t_{2}
; if
s
currently has odd degree, we can remove
t_{1}
and then remove
s
, and end at an empty graph.

Finally, since
n\leq N
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{G\sim G(n,p)}[G\text{ is even-degenerate}\mid G[A]=H,\,\left\lvert E\right\rvert\equiv s\text{ (mod 2)},\,\deg_{G}(a)\equiv s_{a}\text{ (mod 2) }\,\forall a\in A]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{G\sim G(n,p)}[G=G_{0}\mid G[A]=H,\,\left\lvert E\right\rvert\equiv s\text{ (mod 2)},\,\deg_{G}(a)\equiv s_{a}\text{ (mod 2) }\,\forall a\in A]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{G\sim G(n,p)}[G=G_{0}]

\displaystyle\geq(\min\{p,1-p\})^{\binom{N}{2}}.

Taking
\delta_{1}:=(\min\{p,1-p\})^{\binom{N}{2}}
gives the result.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

There exist constants
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in(0,1)
, depending only on
p
and
\alpha
, such that
f(n)<1-\delta
for all
n>N_{0}
.

Proof.

We know from
Equation

5.5
that there exist
K>0
,
N_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\delta_{2}<1/4
such that for all
n\geq N_{2}
, we have

(6.1)

\displaystyle 1-f(n)\geq\left(1-e^{-Kn^{1/2-\alpha}}\right)\left(1-\max_{n^{\prime}\in[n/4-\delta_{2}n,n/4+\delta_{2}n]}f(n^{\prime})\right).

Note that we have
f(n^{\prime})
instead of
f(n^{\prime})^{2}
in the last term, which follows from
f(n^{\prime})\leq 1
. We set
N_{0}:=\max\{N_{1},N_{2}\}
(with
N_{1}
as in
Lemma

6.1
) and pick
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

•

\prod_{n=N}^{\infty}(1-e^{-Kn^{1/2-\alpha}})>1/2
;

•

N_{0}<N/4-\delta_{2}N
.

The existence of
N
follows from the fact that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-Kn^{1/2-\alpha}}
converges.
By
Lemma

6.1
, there exists
\delta_{1}=\delta_{1}(N)>0
such that
1-f(n)\geq\delta_{1}
for all
N_{0}\leq n\leq N
.

Define
g:\{N,N+1,\dots\}\to\mathbb{N}
by

g(n)=\text{argmax}_{[n/4-\delta_{2}n,n/4+\delta_{2}n]}f.

For all
n\geq N
, pick the minimum
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
g^{(k)}(n)<N
. Note that we have
g^{(k)}\geq N_{0}
as
g^{(k-1)}\geq N
.
Thus by
Equation

6.1
, we have

\displaystyle 1-f(n)

\displaystyle\geq(1-e^{-Kn^{1/2-\alpha}})(1-f(g(n)))

\displaystyle\geq(1-e^{-Kn^{1/2-\alpha}})(1-e^{-Kg(n)^{1/2-\alpha}})(1-f(g^{(2)}(n)))

\displaystyle\geq\dots

\displaystyle\geq(1-f(g^{(k)}(n)))\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(1-e^{-K(g^{(i)}(n))^{1/2-\alpha}})

\displaystyle\geq\delta_{1}\prod_{n=N}^{\infty}(1-e^{-Kn^{1/2-\alpha}})>\delta_{1}/2.

Here the second last inequality follows from our choice of
N
and the fact that
N_{0}\leq g^{(k)}<N
. Setting
\delta=\delta_{1}/2
gives the result.
∎

We can now prove an exponential upper bound on
f(n)
, which immediately implies
Theorem

1.3
.

Theorem 6.3
.

f(n)\leq e^{-\Omega(n^{1/2-\alpha})}
.

Proof.

With hindsight, we fix a real number
c
such that
0<c<\frac{1}{4}-(\frac{1}{4})^{1/(1-2\alpha)}
. Note that since
1/(1-2\alpha)>1
, we have
\frac{1}{4}>(\frac{1}{4})^{1/(1-2\alpha)}
, and hence
c
exists. Equivalently, we pick
c
such that
\zeta:=2\cdot(\frac{1}{4}-c)^{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}-1>0
.
From
Theorem

2.3
, we get that there exist
K>0
and
N_{3}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N_{3}
, we have

(6.2)

\displaystyle f(n)\leq e^{-Kn^{1/2-\alpha}}+\max_{n^{\prime}\in[n/4-cn,n/4+cn]}f(n^{\prime})^{2}.

We first show that
f(n)=o(1)
.

Claim 6.4
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
M_{\varepsilon}
such that
f(n)<\varepsilon
for all
n>M_{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

Let
x:=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}f(n)
. From
Equation

6.2
, we know that
x\leq 0+x^{2}
. By
Lemma

6.2
, we know that
x<1
. Hence
x=0
.
∎

We can now prove the theorem. With hindsight, pick
\varepsilon>0
such that

\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\zeta}<1.

From
6.4
, there exists
M_{\varepsilon}
such that
f(n)<\varepsilon
for all
n>M_{\varepsilon}
. Now, we pick
M
large enough such that

•

\ln(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})/M^{1/2-\alpha}<K/2
;

•

(1/4-c)M>M_{\varepsilon}
.

Let
K_{0}:=\ln(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})/M^{1/2-\alpha}
, so we have
K_{0}<K/2
and
e^{-K_{0}M^{1/2-\alpha}}=\varepsilon
. We show by induction that

(6.3)

\displaystyle f(n)\leq e^{-K_{0}n^{1/2-\alpha}}\qquad\text{for all $n\geq M_{\varepsilon}$}.

For the base case, if
M_{\varepsilon}\leq n\leq M
, then we have

f(n)<\varepsilon=e^{-K_{0}M^{1/2-\alpha}}\leq e^{-K_{0}n^{1/2-\alpha}}.

Inductively, suppose
n>M
and
Equation

6.3
holds for all
M_{\varepsilon}\leq n^{\prime}<n-1
. From
Equation

6.2
, we have

\displaystyle f(n)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-Kn^{1/2-\alpha}}+\max_{n^{\prime}\in[n/4-cn,n/4+cn]}f(n^{\prime})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-Kn^{1/2-\alpha}}+e^{-2K_{0}(n/4-cn)^{1/2-\alpha}}

\displaystyle=e^{-K_{0}n^{1/2-\alpha}}\left(e^{-(K-K_{0})n^{1/2-\alpha}}+e^{-\left(2(1/4-c)^{1/2-\alpha}-1\right)K_{0}n^{1/2-\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-K_{0}n^{1/2-\alpha}}\left(e^{-(K-K_{0})n^{1/2-\alpha}}+e^{-\zeta K_{0}n^{1/2-\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-K_{0}n^{1/2-\alpha}}\left(e^{-K_{0}M^{1/2-\alpha}}+e^{-\zeta K_{0}M^{1/2-\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-K_{0}n^{1/2-\alpha}}\left(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\zeta}\right)<e^{-K_{0}n^{1/2-\alpha}}.

Here, the second inequality follows from the inductive hypothesis and the last inequality follows from
K_{0}<K/2
. The inductive hypothesis applies because we have
n^{\prime}>n/4-cn\geq(1/4-c)M>M_{\varepsilon}
.
This finishes the proof.
∎
