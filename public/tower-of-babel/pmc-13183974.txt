Sustained effectiveness and safety of esketamine for major depressive disorder: a target trial simulation of real-world data
Authors: Ting-Hui Liu, Hong-Shiang Shen, Jheng-Yan Wu, Po-Yu Huang, Yu-Ju Shih, Chih-Cheng Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the long-term effectiveness and safety of esketamine in adults with Major depressive disorder (MDD), focusing on suicide-related events, all-cause mortality, and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs). This target-trial simulation used the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network. Adults (≥ 18 years) with MDD who received either esketamine or a conventional antidepressant during an inpatient episode were included. After propensity-score matching for demographic and clinical variables, matched pairs were analyzed followed up to 2 years. The primary outcome was a composite of suicide-related events. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were estimated across acute (days 1–14), intermediate (days 15–365), and long-term (days 15–730) periods. After 1:1 matching, 3383 pairs were analyzed. Esketamine use was associated with lower suicide-related events at days 1–14 (0.77% vs 3.78%; HR, 0.19; 95% CI, 0.12–0.29; P < 0.001), days 15–365 (2.13% vs 2.63%; HR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.46–0.86; P = 0.003), and days 15–730 (2.66% vs 2.78%; HR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.55–0.99; P = 0.04). Mortality was lower (6.03% vs 7.63%; HR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.51–0.73; P < 0.001), as was recurrent MDD at 2 years (3.46% vs 4.67%; HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.59–0.98; P = 0.04). MACEs were lower during days 1–14 (2.10% vs 4.97%; HR, 0.39; 95% CI, 0.30–0.51; P < 0.001) and not significantly different thereafter. In conclusion, esketamine treatment was associated with sustained reductions in suicidal ideation and all-cause mortality without increased MACEs risk. The composite suicide-related outcome was primarily driven by reductions in suicidal ideation; differences in suicide attempt and intentional self-harm could not be determined due to low event counts.Subject terms: Bipolar disorder, Depression

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is one of the leading causes of disability worldwide and a major contributor to suicide. Nearly 18–58% of individuals with MDD experience suicidal ideation in observational studies [1]. The rate of completed suicide in MDD is less well quantified but has been estimated in some cohorts to reach 2–8% over longer-term follow-up [2–4]. Despite widespread antidepressant use, approximately 50% of patients fail to remit with first-line therapy, and up to 30% develop treatment-resistant depression (TRD) after multiple adequate antidepressant trials without sufficient response [5–7]. These gaps highlight the urgent need for novel and effective interventions that can rapidly alleviate symptoms and reduce suicide risk.

Esketamine, the S-enantiomer of ketamine, represents one such breakthrough therapy. It is an N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist with a glutamatergic mechanism distinct from traditional monoaminergic antidepressants [8]. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have shown that esketamine produces rapid reductions in depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation [9, 10]. Yet the majority of these data are confined to short-term treatment windows of a few weeks to months [11, 12]. For example, the TRANSFORM-2 trial showed that adjunctive intranasal esketamine significantly improved depressive symptoms over a 4-week induction in adults with TRD, but only limited open-label extension data up to 6 months [11]. In acutely suicidal patients, the ASPIRE I and II trials observed clinically meaningful symptom reductions within 4 h, but follow-up was restricted to 4 weeks [12]. Although the SUSTAIN program extended follow-up up to two to four years and reported no new safety concerns, the trials were industry-sponsored and restricted to highly selected populations [13].

In routine clinical practice, patients with MDD are typically older, present with multiple medical comorbidities, and often require treatment over decades, let alone years. Although post-marketing surveillance has accumulated nearly five years of safety data for esketamine, these reports rely primarily on spontaneous event reporting, lack comparator groups, and do not systematically evaluate key outcomes including suicide-related events, mortality, and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) [13]. Real-world observational studies to date have been restricted to follow-up durations of only 6 to 12 months [14, 15]. To address these gaps, we conducted a target trial simulation using the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network, a federated network of de-identified electronic health records from healthcare organizations across multiple countries. Unlike passive surveillance systems, TriNetX enables the construction of matched comparator cohorts, time-to-event analyses, and evaluation of multiple long-term clinical outcomes. Using this platform, we compared esketamine initiators with antidepressant-treated controls and examined outcome trajectories over a two-year follow-up period. While causal inference is not possible, this approach provides complementary real-world evidence to inform the longer-term safety and outcome patterns associated with esketamine use in routine clinical care.

Methods

Data source

This study utilized de-identified patient-level data from the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network, a federated research platform designed to support real-world evidence generation using electronic health records from healthcare organizations worldwide. The Global Network includes data from over 215 healthcare systems spanning North America, Europe and Asia-Pacific, encompassing both academic medical centers and integrated delivery networks. Participating institutions contribute data in real time through secure connections. All data are de-identified in accordance with Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act guidelines before ingestion into the TriNetX platform. The network includes a wide range of structured data elements, including demographics, diagnoses (ICD-10-CM), procedures (CPT/HCPCS), medications (RxNorm), vital signs, and laboratory results.

Submitted data undergo a standardized preprocessing pipeline, which includes mapping to controlled clinical terminologies, deduplication, temporal alignment, and automated quality checks to ensure internal consistency and completeness. TriNetX also performs routine data refreshes and continuously monitors for data integrity in collaboration with contributing sites.

Data access for this study was obtained through an institutional subscription to the TriNetX platform. No external funding, sponsorship, or influence from TriNetX or related entities was involved in the conduct, analysis, or reporting of this study.

Study design

We employed a target trial design to simulate a randomized controlled trial using observational data (Supplementary Table 1). Participants aged ≥ 18 years with a documented diagnosis of MDD were eligible for inclusion. MDD was identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM codes F32 and F33).

Esketamine group

Patients were included if they had at least one prescription or administration record of esketamine. To ensure esketamine was prescribed in the context of an active depressive episode, patients were required to have an MDD diagnosis within 30 days before or on the same date as the first esketamine record. In addition, patients must have received an antidepressant medication within one month prior to or on the same date as the index esketamine exposure. To restrict the cohort to clinically stable individuals without recent severe episodes, patients were excluded if they had documentation of inpatient encounters, suicidal ideation, suicide attempt, or intentional self-harm more than one month before the index date. Patients with prior diagnoses of schizophrenia spectrum, bipolar disorder, or substance-related disorders were also excluded. In addition, to ensure esketamine initiation occurred in the context of a clinically significant depressive episode requiring higher-level care, we required patients to have documentation of an inpatient encounter within 1 month prior to the first esketamine administration. The index date was defined as the date of the first esketamine record.

Control group

The control cohort included adults (≥ 18 years) with MDD who initiated an antidepressant but had no record of esketamine use at any time. Patients were required to have an MDD diagnosis within 30 days before or on the date of antidepressant initiation. All other inclusion and exclusion criteria were identical to the esketamine group, including restrictions on prior inpatient admissions, suicidal behaviors, and psychiatric comorbidities, as well as the requirement of an inpatient encounter within 1 month prior to index to ensure comparable severity. The index date was the date of first qualifying antidepressant use. A complete list of codes and corresponding definitions is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome was a composite of suicide-related events, identified from ICD-10-CM diagnostic codes in electronic health records. This composite included suicidal ideation (R45.851), suicide attempt (T14.91), and intentional self-harm (X71–X83).

Secondary outcomes included the individual components of the primary outcome suicidal ideation, suicide attempt, all-cause mortality, recurrent MDD (F33), and MACEs. A complete list of codes and definitions is provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Patients were followed from the index date, defined as the first esketamine prescription for the esketamine group or the antidepressant prescription for the control group, until the earliest occurrence of an outcome event, death, or completion of a two-year observation period. To avoid bias from events occurring immediately after treatment initiation, follow-up was divided into three prespecified intervals. Days 1–14 were defined as the acute period, while days 15–365 and 15–730 were analyzed as longer-term intervals. The two-year window (days 15–730) was the primary analysis period, and shorter intervals were used to describe early and one-year risks. This structure distinguishes short-term instability from subsequent outcomes and allows clearer evaluation of long-term effects. Importantly, suicidality may emerge both during the initiation of treatment and after prolonged exposure, and this temporal stratification enables a more clinically relevant assessment of risk dynamics across different phases of treatment.

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed in a 1:1 ratio using a greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm without replacement and a caliper of 0.1 pooled standard deviations, balancing demographics and clinical covariates including demographic factors (age, sex, and race), comorbidities and concomitant medications (Supplementary Table 4) [16–19]. Kaplan–Meier estimators and log-rank tests were used to compare survival distributions, and Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals. We also computed E-values to gauge how unmeasured confounders could affect the stability of our findings for both primary and secondary outcomes [20]. Subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, and race were conducted to evaluate heterogeneity of treatment effects.

Sensitivity analysis

To address potential confounding by healthcare utilization, we conducted a supplementary propensity score matching analysis that included outpatient visit frequency (measured via ambulatory visit records) during the baseline period.

A secondary propensity score model was constructed including borderline personality disorder diagnosis as an additional covariate to assess potential confounding related to psychiatric comorbidity.

To assess the robustness and generalizability of the primary findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis that did not exclude patient with a history of suicidal ideation, suicide attempt, or intentional self-harm prior to the index date. All other inclusion and exclusion criteria remained consistent with the primary cohort.

Results

Patient selection

A total of 186,877,202 individuals were recorded in the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network across 156 healthcare organizations. Of these, 186,651,079 were excluded for meeting at least one of the following criteria: age < 18 years, no diagnosis of MDD, with diagnosis of schizophrenia spectrum disorder, bipolar disorder, or substance use disorder, no prior antidepressant use, no inpatient hospitalization within one month before or on the index date, or presence of hospitalization, suicide-related events more than one month before the index date.

Among the remaining 226,123 eligible patients with MDD, 4856 were newly treated with esketamine and 221,267 received conventional antidepressants without esketamine exposure. After 1:1 PSM on age, sex, race, and clinical covariates, 3383 matched pairs were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Study cohort process.

Demographic characteristics

Before matching, patients treated with esketamine were slightly older than controls (mean age, 57.0 vs 53.2 years) and more often male (39.5% vs 32.7%). Racial distributions also differed, with esketamine users more frequently identified as American Indian or Alaska Native (4.1% vs 0.5%) and less often Black (3.1% vs 8.2%). The esketamine cohort exhibited a higher prevalence of medical comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus (20.4% vs 14.3%), hypertension (36.8% vs 25.8%), hyperlipidemia (24.6% vs 17.0%), chronic kidney disease (10.5% vs 7.1%), liver disease (8.7% vs 3.6%), overweight or obesity (16.1% vs 11.6%), and chronic lower respiratory disease (14.0% vs 10.9%). Psychiatric comorbidities were more common among controls, including recurrent MDD (3.8% vs 7.8%) and suicidal ideation (1.3% vs 8.6%). Esketamine users were more likely to be prescribed concomitant antipsychotics (18.5% vs 13.2%) and less likely to have prior inpatient (39.7% vs 61.2%) or emergency encounters (9.6% vs 24.0%). After PSM, all covariates were well-balanced between the two groups, with standardized differences below 0.1 (Table 1).

Primary outcome

Esketamine use was associated with a significantly lower risk of suicide-related events across all prespecified time intervals. In the acute period (days 1–14), suicide-related events occurred in 0.77% of patients in the esketamine group compared to 3.78% in the control group (hazard ratio [HR], 0.187; 95% CI, 0.123–0.285; p < 0.001; E-value, 10.17) (Table 2). Between days 15 and 365, the incidence was 2.13% in the esketamine group and 2.63% in the control group (HR, 0.627; 95% CI, 0.460–0.855; p = 0.003; E-value, 2.57). From day 15 to 730, the rates were 2.66% and 2.78%, respectively (HR, 0.739; 95% CI, 0.554–0.987; p = 0.040; E-value, 2.04). A similar trend was observed in the Kaplan–Meier analysis (Fig. 2).

Secondary outcomes

Event rates and hazard ratios for secondary outcomes across time intervals are presented in Supplementary Figure 1.

The incidence of suicidal ideation was significantly lower in the esketamine group compared with the control group across all time intervals. During days 1–14, suicidal ideation occurred in 0.44% of the esketamine group and 3.46% of the control group (HR, 0.117; 95% CI, 0.069–0.201; p < 0.001). This difference remained significant during days 15–365 (HR, 0.457; 95% CI, 0.330–0.662; p < 0.001) and days 15–730 (HR, 0.594; 95% CI, 0.434–0.813; p = 0.001). For suicidal attempt, there was no significant difference during days 1–14 (HR, 0.831; 95% CI, 0.415–1.665; p = 0.501). Analyses for subsequent periods and intentional self-harm were not performed due to event counts fewer than 10.

The risk of all-cause mortality was significantly lower in the esketamine group from day 15 onward. During days 15–365, mortality occurred in 5.03% of esketamine users versus 6.47% of controls (HR, 0.601; 95% CI, 0.492–0.734; p < 0.001), and 6.03% vs 7.63% during days 15–730 (HR, 0.608; 95% CI, 0.506–0.731; p < 0.001). No significant difference was observed during days 1–14 (HR, 0.963; 95% CI, 0.740–1.254; p = 0.781).

The incidence of recurrent MDD during days 15–365 was 3.46% in the esketamine group and 4.67% in the control group, showing a significantly lower risk in the esketamine group (HR, 0.762; 95% CI, 0.591–0.982; p = 0.0351). During days 15–730, the incidence was 4.29% versus 3.90%, with no significant difference between groups (HR, 0.869; 95% CI, 0.687–1.100; p = 0.244). Data for days 1–14 were not analyzed due to coding limitations.

The incidence of MACEs was significantly lower in the esketamine group during days 1–14, with 2.10% in the esketamine group and 4.97% in the control group (HR, 0.389; 95% CI, 0.295–0.513; p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in subsequent periods, with incidence rates of 4.26% vs. 3.46% during days 15–365 (HR, 0.967; 95% CI, 0.758–1.235; p = .791) and 4.91% vs. 3.69% during days 15–730 (HR, 1.040; 95% CI, 0.825–1.312; p = .738).

Subgroup analyses of primary outcome (Days 15–730)

In subgroup analyses of suicide-related events during days 15–730, a significantly lower risk was observed in female patients receiving esketamine compared to controls (2.01% vs. 2.40%; HR, 0.637; 95% CI, 0.411–0.987; p =.041). Similarly, among patients aged 18–64 years, esketamine use was associated with a significantly lower incidence of suicide-related events (3.67% vs. 3.86%; HR, 0.684; 95% CI, 0.476–0.983; p = 0.038). No significant differences were detected among male patients (2.82% vs. 2.59%; HR, 0.840; 95% CI, 0.527–1.338; p = 0.462), patients aged ≥65 years (1.23% vs. 1.54%; HR, 0.656; 95% CI, 0.364–1.181; p = 0.156), or White individuals (2.42% vs. 2.57%; HR, 0.755; 95% CI, 0.515–1.107; p = 0.149)(Fig. 3). Estimates were not reported for Black or Asian subgroups due to insufficient event number.

Subgroup analyses of secondary outcomes (Days 15–730)

Esketamine was associated with a significantly lower risk of suicidal ideation among females (1.74% vs. 2.29%; HR, 0.576; 95% CI, 0.364–0.913; p = 0.017), individuals aged 18–64 years (2.77% vs. 3.60%; HR, 0.547; 95% CI, 0.367–0.813; p = 0.002), and White patients (1.76% vs. 2.38%; HR, 0.588; 95% CI, 0.384–0.899; p = 0.013)(Supplemental Figure 2). The difference was not statistically significant in male, or ≥ 65-year-old subgroups.

Esketamine treatment was associated with a significantly lower risk of all-cause mortality among male patients (6.02% vs. 9.67%; HR, 0.471; 95% CI, 0.355–0.624; p < 0.0001), female patients (4.95% vs. 7.57%; HR, 0.493; 95% CI, 0.378–0.642; p < 0.0001), individuals aged ≥65 years (6.96% vs. 11.33%; HR, 0.505; 95% CI, 0.400–0.638; p < 0.0001), and White patients (5.37% vs. 7.84%; HR, 0.541; 95% CI, 0.426–0.687; p < 0.0001)(Supplemental Figure 2). No significant difference in mortality was observed among individuals aged 18–64 years.

No statistically significant differences were observed in the risk of recurrent MDD and MACEs across any subgroups, including males, females, patients aged 18–64 years, patients aged ≥ 65 years, and White individuals (Supplemental Figure 2). Estimates for Black and Asian subgroups were not reported due to insufficient number of events.

Sensitivity analysis

After matching on outpatient visits (N = 3246 per group), the esketamine group continued to demonstrate a significantly lower risk of suicide-related events during the acute period (HR = 0.189, 95% CI: 0.122–0.293, p < 0.0001), and a modestly lower risk during the intermediate period (HR = 0.716, 95% CI: 0.518–0.991, p = 0.0429). In the long-term period (days 15–730), no significant difference was observed between the groups (HR = 0.846, 95% CI: 0.629–1.139, p = 0.2711).

After adjustment for borderline personality disorder in the propensity score matching model (N = 3246 per group), esketamine use remained significantly associated with a reduced risk of suicide-related outcomes. This association was observed during the acute phase (days 1–14; HR = 0.088, 95% CI: 0.049–0.159, p < 0.0001) and persisted during both the intermediate (days 15–365; HR = 0.582, 95% CI: 0.427–0.794) and long-term follow-up periods (days 15–730; HR = 0.675, 95% CI: 0.508–0.896), supporting the robustness of the primary findings.

In the sensitivity analysis including patients with a history of suicidal ideation or behavior prior to treatment initiation (N = 6756 per group), we observed similar trends to the primary analysis. The esketamine group showed a significantly lower risk of suicide-related events during the acute phase (HR = 0.331, 95% CI: 0.237–0.463, p < 0.0001), and a modest but statistically significant reduction during the intermediate phase (HR = 0.784, 95% CI: 0.621–0.991, p = 0.0415). No significant difference was observed over the full two-year period (HR = 0.869, 95% CI: 0.704–1.073, p = 0.1928).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale study to evaluate the impact of esketamine on suicide-related outcomes across both short- and long-term follow-up in a real-world clinical population. Esketamine use was significantly associated with a lower risk of suicide-related events during the acute (days 1–14), intermediate (days 15–365), and long-term (days 15–730) periods, with the most pronounced reduction observed within the first two weeks and a sustained protective association over two years. The observed reduction in the composite suicide-related outcome was primarily attributable to reductions in suicidal ideation, rather than suicide attempt or intentional self-harm. The secondary analysis revealed that suicidal ideation showed consistent and statistically significant reductions across all time periods (days 1–14, 15–365, and 15–730). In contrast, suicide attempt showed no significant difference during the acute period, and analyses for subsequent periods could not be performed due to event counts fewer than 10. Similarly, intentional self-harm data were insufficient for analysis. These findings indicate that while esketamine demonstrates a robust signal for reducing documented suicidal thoughts, our study cannot draw conclusions about its effects on actual suicide attempts or self-harm behaviors due to limited events in this real-world cohort.

Prior RCTs, including ASPIRE I, II and TRANSFORM studies, demonstrated significant improvements in depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation within 24 h of esketamine initiation [21–23]. However, the between-group differences in suicidality severity, as measured by the Clinical Global Impression–Severity of Suicidality–Revised, were not statistically significant, and those studies did not demonstrate reductions in actual suicide-related events. This limitation may be attributable to the short follow-up periods, small sample sizes, and exclusion of higher-risk individuals. In contrast, our study directly examined suicide-related event rates using a target trial simulation framework and longitudinal real-world data drawn from a broader, clinically heterogeneous patient population. Our findings align with prior observational research, a 5-year analysis based on U.S. Global Medical Safety (US-GMS) data reported suicide rates lower than expected background rates among patients treated with esketamine for TRD, and a nationwide cohort study observed sustained reductions in suicidal ideation up to 270 days after ketamine exposure compared with conventional antidepressants [24, 25]. Although the study focused on ketamine, its results support the hypothesis that rapid-acting modulation of glutamatergic and other neurochemical systems may contribute to sustained antisuicidal effects. Nonetheless, contrasting findings have also been reported. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Fountoulakis et al concluded that esketamine’s antidepressant effects were modest and comparable to other augmentation strategies, and that it did not demonstrate significant benefits in reducing suicidality [26]. The authors also raised safety concerns related to potential increases in suicidality and mortality during clinical trials. These findings highlight the ongoing uncertainty surrounding esketamine’s risk-benefit profile, particularly in high-risk populations. In contrast to trials included in the meta-analysis, which often focused on short-term ideation scales and excluded individuals with recent suicidal behavior, our study examined real-world, clinically documented suicide-related events in a broader and more heterogeneous population over a two-year period. As such, our findings offer complementary evidence that may help clarify the long-term impact of esketamine on suicide-related outcomes in clinical practice.

The sustained reduction in suicide-related events - primarily driven by reductions in suicidal ideation - associated with esketamine may stem from its capacity to induce enduring neurobiological changes that stabilize mood regulation circuits. Esketamine’s antagonism of NMDA receptors disinhibits glutamatergic signaling, triggering downstream activation of α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors and subsequent engagement of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and brain-derived neurotrophic factor–tropomyosin receptor kinase B (BDNF–TrkB) pathways [27–29]. These cascades support synaptic plasticity, dendritic remodeling, and network reorganization in brain regions regulating mood, impulse control, and stress response [29, 30]. Clinical and preclinical data suggest that ketamine elevates BDNF expression and enhances synaptic connectivity, which may stabilize circuits implicated in suicidal ideation. Inflammatory modulation may also contribute; reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-6, TNF-α) has been observed in ketamine studies, which could mitigate neuroinflammation linked to suicide risk [30].

The second main finding was a significantly lower risk of all-cause mortality among esketamine users compared with matched controls receiving conventional antidepressants, emerging after the acute treatment phase and persisting through two years. While randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the short-term efficacy of esketamine, they were not designed or powered to evaluate mortality as a primary outcome [21–23]. Long-term open-label data from the SUSTAIN-3 study extended follow-up to four years and reported no new safety signals; two deaths were observed, neither considered related to esketamine [31]. However, the absence of a comparator arm limited assessment of relative survival benefits. Consistent with our findings, a large real-world analysis from the U.S. Global Medical Safety (US-GMS) database also observed low mortality among esketamine-treated patients, with an estimated rate of approximately 0.6 per 100 patient-years, substantially lower than that reported in treatment-resistant depression cohorts (0.79–4.6 per 100 patient-years) [25, 32, 33]. Collectively, these results suggest that the sustained antidepressant effects and clinical stabilization associated with esketamine may contribute to improved long-term survival in patients with major depressive disorder.

The third main finding is that esketamine use was associated with a significantly lower incidence of MACEs during the acute period (days 1–14), though this difference did not persist during the intermediate (days 15–365) or long-term (days 15–730) intervals. Previous clinical trials investigating esketamine have largely concentrated on short-term hemodynamic responses, including transient increases in blood pressure and heart rate [21–23]. Extended follow-up data from SUSTAIN-2 and SUSTAIN-3 have described an overall favorable safety profile [31, 34]. In SUSTAIN-2, routine electrocardiographic monitoring was conducted, and although transient increases in blood pressure were observed following esketamine administration, these changes were generally self-limited and not associated with long-term adverse cardiovascular outcomes [34]. A recent post-marketing analysis based on Janssen US-GMS database identified 193 cases of cardiac arrhythmias among 58,483 esketamine-treated patients, of which 140 were classified as serious [25]. However, the lack of a comparator group limits interpretation. In the current study, the early difference in MACEs risk may reflect clinical stabilization following hospitalization, intensified monitoring during the induction phase, or underlying differences in patient selection, as individuals with unstable cardiovascular disease are less likely to be considered for esketamine treatment under current prescribing guidelines [35–38]. This likely introduces selection bias, potentially underestimating the true cardiovascular risk among esketamine-treated patients. Despite the lack of sustained cardioprotective effects, the absence of increased MACEs risk over a two-year period provides clinically relevant reassurance regarding the long-term cardiovascular safety of esketamine in real-world practice.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, as an observational target trial simulation using electronic health records, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. We addressed this by adopting a new-user, active-comparator design with 1:1 PSM and E-value analysis to assess robustness against unmeasured confounders [20, 39]. Some E-values were modest, indicating that unmeasured confounding of moderate strength could potentially attenuate or explain certain observed associations. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted cautiously as hypothesis-generating rather than causal. In addition, multiple statistical comparisons were conducted across predefined time intervals and outcomes, and no formal correction for multiple testing was applied; therefore, the possibility of inflated Type I error should be considered when interpreting individual statistically significant findings. Secondly, exposure and outcomes were identified through diagnostic and prescription codes, which may lead to misclassification, especially for suicide-related events that are often underreported or coded inconsistently across institutions. To reduce this risk, we constructed a composite endpoint encompassing suicidal ideation, attempts, and intentional self-harm. This broader outcome definition increases sensitivity and minimizes the impact of variable documentation practices while capturing the full clinical spectrum of suicide-related behaviors. Thirdly, detailed clinical data such as symptom scales, medication adherence, and dosing were unavailable; however, we restricted inclusion to recently hospitalized patients with active depressive episodes and stratified follow-up to approximate clinical treatment phases. Fourthly, although limiting the control group to patients newly initiated on antidepressants raises the possibility of enrolling individuals with less treatment resistance, we required both cohorts to have experienced an inpatient encounter within the month prior to index. This inclusion criterion served as a proxy for clinical severity and helped align the baseline acuity between groups. In addition, propensity score matching incorporated clinical and demographic variables to further minimize baseline differences in severity and comorbidity burden. Finally, events outside participating systems might be undercaptured, yet the multicenter real-world design enhances external validity and extends trial findings to broader clinical populations.

Conclusion

This real-world target trial simulation suggests that esketamine treatment in patients with MDD may be associated with sustained reductions in suicide-related events and all-cause mortality without an increased risk of MACEs over long-term follow-up. The composite suicide-related outcome was primarily driven by reductions in suicidal ideation; however, conclusions regarding suicide attempt and intentional self-harm could not be drawn due to insufficient event counts. These findings extend evidence from RCTs to broader clinical populations, supporting the long-term effectiveness, safety, and potential survival benefit of esketamine in routine psychiatric practice.
