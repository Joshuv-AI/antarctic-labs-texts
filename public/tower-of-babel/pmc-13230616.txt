Increasing viability of pathological liver grafts from brain dead donors via intestinal parasympathetic nerve stimulation, or exogenous serotonin
Authors: Francisco Sanus, Cristina Maroto-Serrat, Gloria de la Rosa, Baltasar Pérez-Saborido, Carolina Almohalla, Jordi Gracia-Sancho, Albert Caballeria-Casals, Marc Micó-Carnero, Fátima del Carmen Navarro-Martínez, Alan Omar González-Hernández, Araní Casillas-Ramírez, Carmen Peralta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLivers from donors after brain death (DBDs) with alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) or steatosis, which undergo prolonged cold ischemia are often not considered for transplantation. We evaluate whether norepinephrine and acetylcholine (ACh) deficiencies impair intestinal serotonin (5-HT) delivery to the liver in extended-criteria DBDs liver transplantation (LT). A DBD rat model with ALD or severe steatosis was used. Norepinephrine, ACh and 5-HT levels were evaluated in liver and intestine. Pharmacological (ACh or 5-HT administration) and electrical stimulation of intestinal parasympathetic nervous system (IPNS) were applied to evaluate their effects on ACh, 5-HT, liver and intestinal damage. Our results indicate that norepinephrine levels were maintained in DBDs, whereas ACh and 5-HT were depleted in both intestine and liver. IPNS stimulation or ACh infusion via mesenteric artery restored intestinal ACh, generating 5-HT and intestinal protection. Platelets potentially transported this 5-HT, with restoration of hepatic 5-HT and protection. Intravenous 5-HT (not intravenous ACh) produced similar benefits, which persisted after 24 h cold ischemia and reperfusion, improving survival. Thus, IPNS electrical stimulation or ACh administration via mesenteric artery (which reversed 5-HT deficiencies in intestine and liver) or 5-HT administration can make steatotic or ALD grafts from extended-criteria DBDs viable after prolonged ischemia, improving LT outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47831-w.Subject terms: Diseases, Gastroenterology, Medical research, Neuroscience

Introduction

Using livers from extended-criteria donors might reduce liver transplantation (LT) waiting lists1. Over 70% of donors are brain dead (BD)2,3, and over 25% of grafts are discarded due to alcoholic liver disease (ALD) or severe steatosis, especially if subjected to prolonged (16–24 h) cold ischemia (CI), which increases graft dysfunction risk and reduces post-LT survival3–5.

Circulating norepinephrine levels, the main sympathetic nervous system (SNS) neurotransmitter, decrease after BD6. Even administering norepinephrine intravenously to donors after BD (DBDs) to resolve hemodynamic instability7,8, intestinal and liver injury remain9, suggesting inadequate tissue levels.

Norepinephrine promotes intestinal serotonin (5-HT) synthesis through activation of enterochromaffin cells (ECs)10. The gut produces about 95% of 5-HT; with the remaining 5% synthesized by brain11. When released into circulation, 5-HT is sequestered by platelets and released at damaged tissues11. While the liver is unlikely to generate 5-HT12,13, the parasympathetic efferent nerves where acetylcholine (ACh) is the main neurotransmitter, stimulate 5-HT release from intestinal ECs10,14.

In the present study, using different pharmacological or electrical interventions in DBD, we explore whether decreased norepinephrine or ACh, and impaired intestine–liver neural signaling reduces hepatic 5-HT delivery, contributing to damage and poor regeneration in DBD LT with ALD or severely steatotic grafts. Outcomes will be evaluated before DBD graft retrieval, and after CI and reperfusion in an ex vivo model. Survival benefits will then be evaluated in an in vivo LT model. These strategies may enable high-risk graft use and thus help shorten LT waiting lists.

Results

Throughout, all statistical comparisons were performed only between experimental groups corresponding to the same animal model (Control, severe steatosis, or ALD).

DBD with severe steatosis or ALD evaluated prior to liver retrieval

Effects of BD on damage and potential SNS neurotransmitter deficiencies in livers with severe steatosis or ALD

Increased hepatic damage parameters (AST, ALT, bilirubin, caspase-3) reflected BD-induced damage in all livers before retrieval (BD versus Sham groups; Fig. S1C). These findings were associated with regeneration failure, as reflected in lower PCNA, HGF, BDNF and CNTF levels and higher TGF-β levels in BD compared with Sham groups (Fig. S1D). In contrast, hepatic norepinephrine (NE), the principal SNS neurotransmitter, remained unchanged in BD versus Sham groups (Fig. S1B), indicating that the mentioned hepatic damage and regenerative failure in DBD cannot be explained by altered hepatic norepinephrine levels.

Relevance of the Intestine–liver neural relay in DBD grafts in severe steatosis and ALD models

Intestinal norepinephrine levels were similar in BD versus Sham groups, whereas intestinal 5-HT decreased in all DBDs (Fig. 1B). Reduced intestinal 5-HT aligned with intestinal inflammation and injury: BD groups showed increased oxidative stress and neutrophil accumulation versus Sham, associated with increased pro-inflammatory TNF-α and reduced anti-inflammatory IL-10 levels (Fig. 1C). So, as BD did not alter intestinal norepinephrine levels, then reduced 5-HT and intestinal inflammation appeared unrelated to sympathetic efferent fibers.

Next, we evaluated if the intestinal parasympathetic nervous system (IPNS) (with Ach as its main neurotransmitter) affects intestinal 5-HT production. Intestinal ACh and 5-HT levels were reduced in BD versus Sham groups. IPNS electrical stimulation post-BD (BD + IPNS stimulation group) increased intestinal ACh comparable to Sham group levels (Fig. 1D). This also increased intestinal 5-HT and protection, as reflected by reduced intestinal inflammation and damage in BD + IPNS stimulation versus BD groups (Fig. 1D). Mesenteric artery ACh administration [BD + ACh(ma) group], to reach the intestine directly, mimicked IPNS electrical stimulation (Fig. 1D).

As in intestine, BD reduced ACh and 5-HT in liver grafts (BD versus Sham groups; Fig. 2B). Increases in intestinal ACh induced by IPNS electrical stimulation or mesenteric artery ACh administration were not associated with augmented hepatic ACh : hepatic levels were similar in BD + IPNS stimulation, BD + ACh(ma) and BD groups (Fig. 2B). However, intestinal 5-HT generated by these interventions reached the liver, as hepatic 5-HT increased in BD + IPNS stimulation and BD + ACh(ma) versus BD groups (Fig. 2B). This rise in hepatic 5-HT protected against injury, apoptosis and regenerative failure compared with BD groups (Fig. 2C and D).

These experiments indicate that intestinal ACh generated by IPNS electrical stimulation or delivered locally via mesenteric artery infusion did not reach the liver. Therefore, systemic administration of ACh was also investigated. ACh administered intravenously reached the liver but not intestine (BD + ACh(iv) versus BD groups; Figs. 3B and 4B); and no intestinal protection was observed (Fig. 3C). Although intravenous ACh administration increased hepatic ACh, it reduced only one hepatic injury marker (Figs. 4C and D), indicating limited hepatic protection. Interestingly, ACh administered intravenously [ACh(iv)] did not alter hepatic 5-HT levels (Fig. 4B), indicating that mesenteric artery supplementation of ACh induced intestinal 5-HT production, which was potentially transported to the liver and effectively protected it, whereas intravenous administration did not.

Following this, we investigated whether exogenous administration of 5-HT into systemic circulation was also beneficial. Intravenous exogenous 5-HT had similar effects to endogenous 5-HT induced by IPNS electrical stimulation or mesenteric artery (ma) ACh administration: it counteracted hepatic and intestinal deficits (BD + 5-HT versus BD groups; Fig. 3B and 4B), protecting against damage, inflammation, apoptosis and regenerative failure (BD + 5-HT versus BD groups; Figs. 3C, 4C and D). Platelets facilitated 5-HT transport to liver, where it exerted beneficial effects 10,11. When platelets were specifically depleted using an anti-CD41 antibody, 5-HT was not delivered to the liver and its protective effects were completely abolished (BD + anti-CD41 + 5-HT versus BD + 5-HT groups; Fig. 4B–D).

BD + CI/R groups showed increased transaminase levels, higher damage scores, elevated oxidative stress, and impaired liver regenerative failurecompared with Sham + CI/R group (Fig. 5C).

Liver grafts from the BD + IPNS stimulation + CI/R and BD + ACh(ma) + CI/R groups exhibited ACh levels comparable to those in the BD + CI/R group. However, the BD + IPNS stimulation + CI/R, BD + ACh(ma) + CI/R and BD + 5HT + CI/R groups all showed high hepatic 5-HT, which were associated with reduced liver damage and oxidative stress, and improved regenerative parameters, compared with the BD + CI/R group (Fig. 5B and C). Thus, application of these DBD interventions improves graft viability prior to retrieval and enhances postoperative outcomes after 24h of CI followed by normothermic reperfusion. Similar to the profile observed in DBD grafts before retrieval, livers from the BD + ACh(iv) + CI/R group showed increased ACh but unmodified 5-HT levels, along with limited liver protection compared with the BD + CI/R group (Fig. 5B and C).

In vivo LT of DBD grafts with severe steatosis or ALD after 24 h of CI

Fourteen-day post-LT survival rates in recipients of non-pathological, severe steatosis, or ALD grafts from DBDs without any intervention (BD + LT groups) were 25%, 12%, and 12%, respectively (Fig. 6B). Fourteen-day survival improved considerably in the BD + IPNS stimulation + LT, BD + ACh(ma) + LT, and BD + 5HT + LT groups, regardless of graft pathology. All three therapeutic interventions raised survival to 75% in non-pathological grafts, and to 62% in severe steatosis and ALD grafts. The BD + ACh(iv) + LT group showed only limited survival improvement.

Regarding the causes of death, recipient mortality was observed predominantly within the first 48 h after LT. This early mortality in recipients of untreated liver grafts seems to be associated with severe 24 h cold ischemia–reperfusion injury3,8,15. Animals that died exhibited poor postoperative condition, characterized by delayed recovery from anesthesia, abnormal posture, and markedly reduced spontaneous activity. In contrast, recipients with treated liver grafts exhibited more favorable postoperative course.

Discussion

An intriguing finding of this study is that intravenous ACh restores hepatic ACh levels but fails to restore hepatic 5-HT or improve tissue viability, whereas mesenteric administration is effective. Although systemic or direct cholinergic stimulation is widely considered anti-inflammatory, its effects may be context-dependent and do not consistently confer hepatoprotection. For instance, intravenous ACh administration (as performed in the current study) has been shown to exert adverse effects under conditions of hepatic warm ischemia, promoting hepatic inflammation, damage and regenerative failure16. Moreover, in the BD setting, the effects of systemic ACh suplemmentation might vary depending on factors as the duration of BD, the degree of steatosis, and overall injury severity. In previous experimental studies, ACh conferred hepatoprotection in rats subjected to BD for 6 h and presenting moderate of steatosis7. These conditions differ substantially from those of the present study, which involved rats subjected to BD for 8 h in the presence of severe steatosis. Under these latter conditions, as explained before, ACh appears to require 5-HT to confer hepatic protection. It is established that pathological mechanism differs markedly in DBD setting depending on BD duration6,17,18. Therefore, the effects of systemic or direct cholinergic stimulation on the liver may vary according to the surgical and pathological context19,20, as has also been reported for other pharmacological interventions21,22. In this context, our results suggest that direct cholinergic replenishment via intravenous ACh administration is insufficient to confer hepatoprotection, and that restoration of hepatic 5-HT, potentially derived from intestinal production and platelet-mediated transport, appears to be a necessary downstream event for effective hepatoprotection. Importantly, mesenteric ACh administration did not increase hepatic ACh but induced intestinal 5-HT production and 5-HT may reach the liver to confer protection. In contrast, intravenous ACh bypassed the intestine, failed to stimulate intestinal 5-HT synthesis, and therefore did not restore hepatic 5-HT availability, which is required to confer hepatic protection. All of this is in line with our results indicating that the 5-HT administered intravenously reached and protected DBD liver, similarly to IPNS electrical stimulation or ACh via mesenteric artery. These interventions (mesenteric ACh administration, IPNS stimulation or 5-HT administration) increased intestinal 5-HT levels, which was potentially transported to the liver, where it confers protection (Fig. S2). Once in circulation, most 5-HT is sequestered in platelets which transport it10,11. In line with this, concomitant 5-HT and anti-CD41 administration abolished the benefits of exogenous 5-HT, suggesting that platelets could facilitate tissue repair by releasing 5-HT at liver damage. These findings suggest that, under conditions of BD and liver vulnerability, the protective axis is not solely dependent on direct cholinergic signaling but rather on an intact intestine–liver neurochemical relay, in which 5-HT acts as an essential effector of ACh. Thus, the serotoninergic pathway does not negate cholinergic anti-inflammatory signaling, but appears to be required for its full protective translation in this specific pathological setting, identifying serotonergic signaling as a critical downstream mechanism of ACh-induced hepatic protection in this model.

Improving graft viability before retrieval should improve transplant outcomes, because the effects of the most protective strategies (IPNS electrical stimulation, mesenteric artery ACh, exogenous 5-HT) on DBD grafts before retrieval, remained after 24 h CI and 2 h reperfusion, and were reflected in improved recipient survival. Our results also indicated that hepatic 5-HT levels after ex vivo normothermic reperfusion, resembled those in DBD livers prior to retrieval. It is highly encouraging, from a therapeutic standpoint, that the effects of our donor treatment prior to graft retrieval and the ACh–5-HT pathway are preserved after 24 h liver storage and ex vivo reperfusion, which is not so for other signaling pathways or drugs23.

Our results showing 25% 14-day survival after receiving non-pathological DBD grafts (BD + LT) agree with previous reports8,24; with survival lower for pathological livers. The injurious effects of BD6,25 and prolonged (24 h) CI3,8,15 are well known and responsible for the low survival observed in non-pathological grafts. Clinically, prolonged CI corresponds with increased LT graft dysfunctions: just 10 h is sometimes used as a viability cutoff3,15. Fourteen-day survival improved considerably in the BD + IPNS stimulation + LT, BD + ACh(ma) + LT, and BD + 5HT + LT groups, regardless of graft pathology (75% in non-pathological grafts, and to 62% in severe steatosis and ALD grafts). The BD + ACh(iv) + LT group showed only limited survival improvement.

In living donor LT (32 recipients), lower serotonin levels within 5 h after reperfusion were associated with poor outcomes26. In another study involving 44 recipients of DBD LT (including donors with a history of alcohol abuse), higher donor intra-platelet 5-HT per platelet (IP 5-HT PP) were associated with lower postoperative hepatic damage, fewer postoperative complications of recipients, and serum 5-HT was decreased even 5 days after LT27. Donor IP 5-HT PP seem to be lower in donor-recipient pairs that developed early graft dysfunction after LT27. Our findings, together with clinical data, suggest 5-HT is a potential therapeutic target in LT from extended-criteria donors.

These recent advances in LT26,27 reinforce the role of 5-HT-immune interactions in early allograft dysfunction (EAD). Different innate immune cells are principal drivers of early inflammatory injury culminating in EAD after LT28,29. Kupffer cells, M1-type cytokines, and liver-resident NK cells recognize DAMPs via Toll-like receptors and release ROS and proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), promoting neutrophil recruitment28. Dendritic cells (DCs) upregulate MHC-II, CD80/CD86, and IL-12, amplifying innate-adaptive crosstalk, while recruited monocyte-derived macrophages produce IL-12, TNF-α, and other mediators that exacerbate injury. Although direct mechanistic evidence in LT is unavailable, data from other contexts indicate that 5-HT modulates innate immune cells involved in EAD30,31. Thus, 5-HT may influence NK cells30,31 but its role in EAD remains unclear because both high and low NK levels associate with EAD32,33; additionally, 5-HT promotes monocyte differentiation and macrophage polarization toward M2-like anti-inflammatory states30,31 and modulates DC differentiation by regulating IL-12, TNF-α, IL-10, CCL22, and CXCL10, favoring a tolerogenic immune phenotype30.

Although this study was not designed to identify the specific 5-HT receptor subtypes, mediating protection, existing evidence suggests that 5-HT7, together with selected 5-HT1 and 5-HT2 receptors, are the most plausible candidates. Other receptors (i.e. 5-HT3) have either unclear roles or have been associated with detrimental effects. Hepatic cells express several 5-HT receptors, including 5-HT1, 5-HT2A, 5-HT2B, and 5-HT734 which activate signaling pathways such as MAPK/ERK and cAMP/PKA that regulate inflammation, cell survival, stress responses, regeneration and apoptosis11,34–37. Evidence suggests that activation of 5-HT1 receptors may attenuate warm I/R reperfusion injury by limiting oxidative stress, inflammation, and tissue damage36, whereas 5-HT2A/2B receptors are central to protection against hepatic regenerative failure11,37. Several reports identify 5-HT7 signaling as a key pathway modulating inflammation and tissue injury in both liver and intestine. Thus, the 5-HT2 receptor family may also participate in intestinal repair, although its role appears less dominant than the 5-HT7 centered immunomodulatory axis38. In addition, 5-HT1 receptors, have been linked to reduced intestinal inflammatory and oxidative damage39. Collectively, these findings suggest that serotonergic signaling, likely through coordinated activation of 5-HT7, 5-HT1, or 5-HT2 receptors, may limit liver and intestinal injury and promote organ functional recovery under DBD conditions.

Several clinically available drugs target 5-HT receptors, underscoring the translational relevance of findings from the present study. The 5-HT1 family represents the most immediate opportunity for clinical application. Buspirone, an FDA-approved partial 5-HT1A agonist, has a favorable safety profile, improves gastrointestinal symptoms and has been pharmacokinetically evaluated in hepatic impairment40. Overall, 5-HT1A agonists are well tolerated, and engage immunomodulatory and cytoprotective pathways compatible with attenuating inflammatory and I/R injury39,41, making them suitable repurposing candidates. Agonists targeting 5-HT1B/1D receptors, such as triptans, reduce intestinal injury and inflammation in preclinical I/R models, but their vasoconstrictive effects and contraindications in patients with cardiovascular disease and hepatic impairment limit translation39,42. Direct targeting of 5-HT2 receptors is constrained by safety concerns39,42,43. In contrast, 5-HT7 signaling emerges as a key upstream pathway regulating inflammation and tissue injury in liver and intestine39; however, selective 5-HT7 agonists are not yet clinically available, highlighting a critical translational gap. Progress toward clinical application will require precise identification of the relevant receptor subtypes, proof-of-concept studies with available 5-HT1A agonists, and the development of safe agonists targeting 5-HT2 and 5-HT7 receptors, thereby defining clear priorities for future research.

The 6–24 h from BD to organ retrieval6,7,17 is a crucial window for cytoprotective intervention to counteract injurious BD effects and improve graft quality (possibly targeting the ACh–5-HT signaling pathway), especially from extended-criteria donors. DBDs usually have intra-arterial, and central venous catheters placed in the ICU. Invasive monitoring often requires these or pulmonary artery catheters, and large-bore peripheral intravenous cannulas may also be inserted44. Selective mesenteric arterial catheterization has been shown to be technically feasible in humans through minimally invasive radiological approaches via the femoral or axillary artery and has been used for targeted drug infusion for targeted drug infusion to evaluate intestinal effects or assess hepatic metabolism45,46. Although mesenteric arterial drug administration is not routinely performed during brain-dead donor management, it is common practice in many centers to anatomically identify the mesenteric artery in anticipation of potential pharmacological interventions by this route. Taken together, the available published evidence and real clinical practice during brain-dead donor management support that mesenteric artery administration of acetylcholine would be technically feasible for clinical evaluation in DBD. Nevertheless, intravenous 5-HT administration to DBDs might be more practical and clinically applicable. While ACh induces 5-HT synthesis, targeting downstream effectors (5-HT) rather than upstream mediators (ACh) directly activates the final biological responses and may represent a more effective strategy. This approach enables a faster therapeutic response and avoids potential limitations associated with impaired downstream acetylcholine signaling in the clinical setting. The advantage of targeting the final effector of a signaling pathway has been previously described47.

Similarly to ACh, 5-HT dosing was selected by previous studies7,48 as well as on our own preliminary data. The experimental design aimed to normalize the BD-induced reduction of both mediators and restore the physiological levels observed in Sham animals, thereby allowing assessment of their pathophysiological relevance in BD injury. Lower doses were insufficient to achieve physiological concentrations, whereas higher doses resulted in supraphysiological levels (data not shown). Importantly, this 5-HT dosing strategy also addresses potential concerns regarding systemic effects of intravenous 5-HT in a brain-dead donor setting. Supraphysiological or pharmacological elevations of 5-HT have been associated with changes in heart rate and blood pressure, potentially resulting in undesirable cardiovascular effects49. However, restoration of 5-HT to the physiological range (as performed in the current study), rather than pharmacological stimulation, was intended to re-establish its normal regulatory functions and physiological 5-HT levels are not associated with clinically relevant hemodynamic disturbances50,51. Importantly, in the context of organ donation, systemic hemodynamics are strictly controlled through donor management protocols, in which vascular tone and cardiac output are largely determined by exogenous interventions52,53. This highly regulated environment would allow timely interventions to mitigate any potential systemic effects following intravenous therapeutic strategies applied clinically in DBDs.

Future experimental studies (beyond the scope of the present study) using long-term NMP (20–24 h) are of scientific and clinical interest, since prolonged NMP may reduce time pressures when managing high-risk liver grafts54. Using NMP for 20–24 h results in less liver injury compared to an equivalent period of cold storage. However, compared with cold storage, maintaining long-term NMP might require additional considerations, such as complex logistical demands55. Several studies in rats and pigs have investigated prolonged NMP (up to 24 h) of healthy livers; whereas clinically relevant questions concerning fibrosis and steatosis remain only partially addressed56. Preclinical models offer distinct advantages, independently of the species used. For instance, pig livers more closely resemble human anatomy and physiology57, while preclinical models of BD, ALD, severe steatosis and fibrosis are more reliably established in rats. Inducing severe steatosis and fibrosis in pigs is challenging, possibly due to species-specific metabolic differences; for instance, the liver is not the primary site of de novo lipogenesis in pigs26,27,55,58. It is scientifically and clinically useful to know the conditions in which the interventions proposed in the current study might be useful in clinical practice, to translate these protective preclinical strategies to clinical practice. This will also depend on the economic, logistical, ethical, and cultural views of each country or center.

In conclusion, our results indicate deficiencies in the intestinal neurotransmitters ACh and 5-HT, which consequently can affect their levels in the liver and lead to damage, inflammation, and impaired hepatic regeneration in extended criteria liver grafts from DBDs subjected to prolonged ischemia. We therefore propose interventions aimed at correcting intestinal and hepatic ACh and 5-HT deficiencies, protecting both intestine and liver (Figure S3), as IPNS electrical stimulation and mesenteric artery ACh administration. In contrast, ACh administered intravenously does not replicate these benefits, as it fails to reach intestine or induce 5-HT synthesis; thus, despite correcting hepatic ACh deficiency, 5-HT is not available to protect liver. Exogenously administered 5-HT, however, reaches and protects both intestine and liver. Importantly, these benefits observed in pathological DBD grafts prior to retrieval remained after 24 h of CI and following ex vivo isolated perfusion or transplantation into recipients. These findings may be of clinical relevance, as they suggest potential therapeutic strategies in LT from non-decased donors to improve outcomes, expand the use of extended-criteria donors, and reduce waiting-lists. Enhancing liver graft viability may also help avoid re-transplantation and re-hospitalization.

Material and methods

Experimental animals

Control animals were male Sprague–Dawley (SD) (Charles River, París, Francia) rats (200–250 g) fed a standard diet (Teklad Global, 14% Protein Rodent Maintenance Diet; Envigo, UK)59 showing no steatosis or fibrosis. For the severe steatosis model, male SD rats were fed a high-fat diet (D12451, 45% kcal from fat; Research Diets, USA)23 for 12 weeks, producing marked macrovesicular and microvesicular hepatocyte fatty infiltration (60–70% steatosis) and fibrosis. For the ALD model, male SD rats were fed a standard diet59 with chronic ethanol exposure (12% v/v in water, ad libitum) for 6 weeks23, inducing mild (< 30%) steatosis and fibrosis (Fig. S4). All procedures were approved by the Barcelona University Ethical Committee for Animal Experimentation. The study was carried out in accordance with the Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes and followed the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, and the study is reported in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Animals were anesthetized with isoflurane and subsequently euthanized by exsanguination. Animals were randomly assigned (using the random number table method) to experimental models (control, severe steatosis, or ALD) and further randomized into experimental groups. All biochemical and histological analyses were blinded: investigators were unaware of group assignments.

Experimental models

Control, Severe steatosis and ALD rats underwent the following surgical procedures to form the experimental groups.

A.Sham: Under anesthesia, rats were ventilated, subjected to laparotomy and maintained normotensive for 8 h; then plasma, intestine and liver samples were collected.

Sham: Under anesthesia, rats were ventilated, subjected to laparotomy and maintained normotensive for 8 h; then plasma, intestine and liver samples were collected.

B.BD: Under anesthesia, rats were ventilated, and a balloon catheter was introduced into the extradural space. Rapid brain injury and immediate BD were induced by inflating the balloon for 1 min; confirmed by the absence of corneal reflexes and apnea7. Normotension achieved by iv colloid infusion containing norepinephrine 0.01 mg/ml. Eight hours post-BD, plasma, intestine and liver grafts were extracted. Clinically, liver donors are often kept in an ICU for 8 h post-BD; inflammatory alterations during this period negatively influence organ quality7,8.

Livers warmed to 22 °C for 30 min and connected via portal vein to a recirculating perfusion system for 2 h at 37 °C (isolated from influences of other organs or neural-hormonal effects). After 2 h ex vivo reperfusion, perfusate and liver samples were collected.

Biochemical determinations

Levels of norepinephrine (MBS760375, MyBioSource Inc., USA), ACh (E-EL-0081, Elabscience, USA) and 5-HT (CSB-E08364r, Cusabio, USA) were measured in liver and intestine using immunoassay kits. Hepatic damage was biochemically assessed via alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (GN41125) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (GN40125, Gernon, Spain) plasma levels using standard procedures; and bilirubin (MBS730053) by immunoassay. Interleukin 10 (IL-10) (E-EL-R0016, MyBioSource Inc., USA) and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) (ab100785, Abcam, UK) levels in intestine determined by immunoassays indicated an inflammatory response. Myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels marked neutrophil accumulation and lipid peroxidation7 respectively, in intestine and liver. Caspase-3 (ab39401, Abcam, USA) indicated hepatic apoptosis; while regeneration was evaluated by quantifying proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) (MBS2515480), hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) (MBS177216), transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) (MBS260302), and mediators involved in hepatocyte proliferation improvement and damaged neuronal tissue repair60: brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) (MBS2019439, MyBioSource Inc., USA) and ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNTF) (CSB-E04528r, Cusabio, USA) using immunoassays.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Hepatic sections were stained with hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) and scored to evaluate sinusoidal dilatation, sinusoidal congestion, atrophy, perisinusoidal fibrosis, portal fibrosis, and portal inflammation23. Steatosis was evaluated using Oil Red O staining and fibrosis was assessed with Sirius Red staining to visualize collagen I fibers. Apoptotic cells in liver tissue sections were identified using a Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) (ab206386, Abcam, UK) assay kit to detect DNA fragmentation. Liver sections were examined under an Olympus BX51 Microscope.

Statistics

All statistical analysis and graphical representations were performed with GraphPad Prism 10.0.2 for Windows (GraphPad Software; USA). Results are expressed as mean ± SEM. Before assessing whether means were different between groups, we first assessed normality and variance homogeneity of the sample distribution in order to confirm that parametric tests could be applied. Normality was analysed with the Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variance with the Brown-Forsythe test. Comparisons among multiple groups were made using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. In all cases, differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. Statistical analysis of group comparisons (p-values) related to Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and Fig. S1 are shown in Tables S1, S2, S3, S4, S5 and S6, respectively. Probability of survival was reported using survival rate curves with 95% confidence intervals. The time origin was defined as the end of surgery. The event of interest was animal death before the planned study endpoint (14 days). Animals that survived until day 14 were censored at the time of scheduled sacrifice. Sample size was calculated using the GRANMO software (https://www.datarus.eu/en/applications/granmo/) to determine the minimal number of rats to achieve statistical significance, with a power of 90% and alpha level of 0,05.
