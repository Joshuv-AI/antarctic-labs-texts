A high-resolution historical long-term gridded daily meteorological data set for agricultural climate change analysis in Japan
Authors: Yasushi Ishigooka, Tsuneo Kuwagata, Mayumi Yoshimoto, Toshihiro Hasegawa, Motoki Nishimori, Takahiro Takimoto, Hitomi Wakatsuki, Yusuke Toda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We created a high-resolution (approximately 1 km x 1 km) historical (from 1978) gridded daily meteorological dataset, called the Historical Gridded Meteorological Dataset in Japan (HGMD-Japan), as basic information infrastructure for analyzing the impact of climate change on agricultural productivity in Japan. By adding wind speed and relative humidity to a previously developed dataset, a comprehensive dataset that contains the input meteorological data needed for existing major agricultural impact assessment models was developed. To maintain the time-series homogeneity of each variable, possible sources of time-series heterogeneities unrelated to climate change, such as changes in statistical methods and instrument types, were eliminated as far as possible. Additionally, some specific agro-meteorological variables that are related strongly to crop development environments, and seasonal or yearly indices that can indicate potential crop productivity (mainly for paddy rice) or risk of damage due to meteorological extreme events, such as the heat stress index, the water temperature of paddy fields, and the panicle temperature, are provided.

Background

To properly assess the impact of projected future climate change on agriculture, as well as the effectiveness of adaptation measures, it is important to have an accurate understanding of the actual impacts on crop production and the effectiveness of measures that have been implemented in the past in response to climate change. For this purpose, it is essential to have a meteorological dataset that is spatially and temporally homogeneous over a long period of time and that has a sufficient variety of meteorological variables. On the basis of a previously developed high-resolution (approximately 1 km x 1 km) gridded daily meteorological dataset [1], we created a long-term high-resolution gridded meteorological dataset, called the Historical Gridded Meteorological Dataset in Japan (HGMD-Japan), that is temporally and spatially homogeneous. To the variables contained in the previous dataset, relative humidity and wind speed; some daily agro-meteorological variables that are related strongly to crop development environments; and seasonal or yearly indices that can indicate potential crop productivity or risk of damage due to meteorological extreme events have been added. Efforts were made to eliminate temporal discontinuities in the data that were not related to climate change, such as changes in statistical methods and observation conditions.

Data Description

HGMD-Japan is a gridded meteorological dataset that, apart from some small islands, covers all the land area of Japan with a spatial resolution of 30′′ in latitude by 45′′ in longitude (approximately 1 km × 1 km) within the ranges of 24°N and 46°N latitude and 122°E and 146°E longitude, having a total of 378,040 grid cells (Fig. 1). It covers the period from 1978 to the latest day and is continuously updated each day. This dataset contains daily general meteorological variables that are spatially interpolated data routinely observed at the observation stations (Fig. 1); daily (hourly) agro-meteorological values that are estimated by using the general variables; and seasonal or annual agro-climatic indices that are created by aggregating the above data. HGMD-Japan consists of a total of 142 files per year, including 13 daily files, 122 hourly files and 7 seasonal or annual files, divided into each folder. The daily file contains the data of one year (365 or 366 days), the hourly file contains the data of one day (24 hours), and the seasonal or yearly file contains one data. All data files are stored in binary format and consist of the location information (latitude and longitude coordinates) and data values for each grid cell. More detailed information, such as the directory structure and record format of the data files, is described in the documentation of data repository as metadata (https://www.scidb.cn/en/detail?dataSetId=0a094405c6d24f8e88f21f438145c2c2).

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

General meteorological variables

The algorithm used to create the gridded data for the basic variables in HGMD-Japan was basically according to that of Seino [1]. In this algorithm, the values at each target grid cell were estimated by using the following procedure: 1) several observation stations around the target grid cell are selected (generally four or five stations); 2) the differences (or ratios) between the observed values and the normal value (30-year average) at the selected stations are estimated; 3) the average of the differences (or ratios) at the selected stations is calculated, weighted by the inverse of the distance between the center of the target grid cell and each of the selected stations; and 4) the averaged difference (or ratios) is added to (or multiplied by) the daily normal value of the grid cell. This procedure is presented by the following formulas.(1)Ye=d0+A0d0=∑j=1n{(Yj−Aj)(Wj)}/∑j=1n(Wj)(2)Ye=r0×A0r0=∑j=1n{(Yj/Aj)(Wj)}/∑j=1n(Wj)where Ye is the estimated value at the target grid cell, d0 is the difference to the normal value, r0 is the ratio to the normal value, and A0 is the normal value at the target grid cell. Yj and Aj are the observed and normal values, respectively, at the jth observation station. Here, the process using differences (Eq. (1)) is used for temperatures, whereas the process using ratio (Eq. (2)) is used for the other elements. Wj is the weighting factor related to the distance between the target cell and the jth observation station (Rj), which is given as 1/Rj for temperature, precipitation, solar radiation, and sunshine duration and given as exp{–(Rj/Ro)(Rj/Ro)} for humidity and wind speed (Ro is set as 20 for wind speed and 40 for humidity).

The observed values were obtained from the Automated Meteorological Data Acquisition System (AMeDAS) stations, and partly from the surface synoptic stations for estimation of sunshine duration, solar radiation and relative humidity. And the daily normal values of the grid cells were obtained from the Mesh Climatic Data 2000 (30-year average of monthly gridded normal data with a spatial resolution of 45″ longitude and 30″ latitude; JMA, 2002) [2] in which the monthly values were converted to daily values by using the procedure of harmonic analysis.

Observed values were obtained principally from 1300 Automated Meteorological Data Acquisition System (AMeDAS) stations and partly from 150 surface synoptic stations. The daily normal values of the grid cells were obtained from the Mesh Climatic Data 2000 (30-year average of monthly gridded normal data with a spatial resolution of 45″ longitude and 30″ latitude [2]); the monthly values were converted to daily values by using the harmonic analysis procedure [1].

Mean, maximum, and minimum temperatures

Mean, maximum, and minimum temperatures were estimated according to the algorithm of Seino [1], described above, by using the differences between the observed and normal values. Note that the sampling interval of temperature observation at AMeDAS stations for deriving daily maximum and minimum temperatures was changed in 2003 (from 1 h to 10 min; by all stations at the same time) and again in 2008 (from 10 min to 10 s; sequentially from some stations), in accordance with the Weather Observation Statistics Guidelines [3]. To prevent substantial bias due to differences in the sampling intervals used to estimate maximum and minimum temperatures, the data were derived from hourly values after the change in 2003 and in 2008, in the same manner as before 2003.

Sunshine duration and solar radiation

Sunshine duration and solar radiation were estimated by using the algorithm of Seino [1] and the ratio of the observed to normal values. As solar radiation is not observed at AMeDAS stations, the solar radiation at a station (Sd) was estimated by using sunshine duration (N) and the following cubic regression formula:(3)SdS0=b0+b1NN0+b2(NN0)2+b3(NN0)3where S0 is the solar radiation at the top of the atmosphere, N0 is the day length, and b0, b1, b2, and b3 are regression coefficients derived for each month and each prefecture; these are published as additional information in the Supplementary Material.

The instruments used to observe sunshine duration were changed several times after observations began. The different types of instruments have different characteristics in terms of sensitivity to sunshine, and this causes bias in the observed values. To prevent temporal discontinuity, the values observed by different instruments (such as solar cell–type sunshine recorders) were corrected to correspond to those observed by a rotating mirror sunshine recorder (which is currently the standard instrument in main use for observing sunshine) in accordance with the process presented in the statistical guidelines [3]. Most of the AMeDAS stations had stopped their in-situ measurements of sunshine duration by 2021 and had begun to provide estimated values based mainly on satellite data. As there was no information on the bias in these estimated values, no correction was made for them.

Precipitation

Although the existing gridded precipitation data were created simply by interpolating the values observed at surrounding AMeDAS stations, without using the gridded normal data in the original algorithm of Seino [1], the gridded precipitation in this dataset was estimated using the gridded normal data and the ratio of the observed to the normal values, as for other elements, in accordance with procedures 1) to 4) described above. As there were no major changes in the precipitation observations that could have caused temporal discontinuities, no correction of values was performed.

Wind speed

As wind speed was not included in the original dataset of Seino [1], additional daily gridded windspeed data were created by using procedures 1) to 4), described above.

In the AMeDAS observations, the heights of the anemometers differed among stations, and they were also changed frequently during the observation period, even at the same station, thus causing spatial and temporal discontinuities in the observed values. To achieve the objective of creating temporally and spatially homogeneous data, these observed values needed to be corrected to a uniform height. Such an attempt has been made in an agro-meteorological database called MeteoCrop DB [4], in which wind speed values were corrected to a uniform height of 2.5 m above the ground above a rice paddy for application to research into the impact of climate change on rice productivity. The same correction was made for wind speed in HGMD-Japan. As in MeteoCrop DB, wind speed values measured at the specific height of each station were corrected as follows [4]:(4)u(2.5m)=f(2.5m)×uobswhere u(2.5m) is the daily mean wind speed at 2.5 m above the ground and uobs is the value observed at each station. The coefficient is given as:(5)f(2.5m)=ln(zi/z0i)ln(100/z0i)·ln(100/z0)ln(zobs/z0)where zi is the corrected height (= 2.5 m), zobs is the height of the wind speed observation at each station, z0 is the aerodynamic roughness length (m) at each station, and z0i is that at the assumed paddy field (= 0.05 m). Here, z0 is a site-specific value that can be obtained by using the empirical formula based on gridded land-use data [4,5].

As gridded normal values for wind-speed data are not created by the Japan Meteorological Agency, we obtained data created by Masatsuka et al [6]. These data were created in the process of developing a wind energy–distribution map as part of the Green Energy Program being implemented from 1978 to 1987 by the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry, and Fisheries [7]. For some islands where values were missing, the data were filled in by using the CRU-TS2.0 dataset [8]. As the reference height of wind speed in the HGMD-Japan was set as 2.5 m, the obtained normal gridded data for which the reference height was 10 m were converted to 2.5 m height by multiplying the correction factor by the normal gridded data obtained. The correction factor was calculated as the averaged ratio of the observed normal values that were corrected to 2.5 m height at AMeDAS stations (derived from MeteoCrop DB [4]) and the values of the gridded normal data obtained at the corresponding grid cells.

Relative humidity

Unlike other meteorological elements which are calculated by using procedures 1) to 4) describe above, gridded relative humidity data is calculated directly using observed relative humidity around target grid. In Japan, relative humidity observations were conducted exclusively at about 150 surface synoptic stations, but since 2021, they have also been implemented at AMeDAS stations. However, to maintain temporal and spatial homogeneity over the entire period of the data, HGMD-Japan does not use these data observed at AMeDAS. Therefore, only observations made at the surface synoptic stations were used to create gridded humidity data.

Evaluation of estimations of basic variables

The accuracy of the estimations of the gridded values for each basic variable in HGMD-Japan was determined by using a leave-one-out cross-validation procedure. At the stations selected for validation, values were estimated from the values observed at surrounding stations by using the same algorithm described above for each variable. For mean, maximum, and minimum temperatures, precipitation, sunshine duration, and wind speed, from among the AMeDAS stations we selected 215 stations where the location had not moved throughout the period from 1978 to 2023 were selected. For solar radiation and relative humidity, which are not observed at AMeDAS stations, validation stations were selected from among the surface synoptic stations: 64 stations for solar radiation and 153 stations for relative humidity. The root mean square errors (RMSEs) were calculated by using the estimated and observed values at these stations.

The seasonal variations in the calculated RMSEs for all selected stations were plotted (Fig. 3). Although these results indicate that the gridded values for each variable were estimated with some accuracy, whether the accuracy is sufficient for users is highly dependent on the purpose for which these data will be used. As Seino [1] pointed out, such estimation errors can be reduced by averaging the estimated daily values over a period of time. The relationship between the number of averaging days and the RMSE were examined for each variable (Fig. 4). Temporal homogeneity of data

Homogeneity tests were conducted to verify the appropriateness of adjustments applied to sunshine duration (also used for estimating solar radiation) and wind speed for homogeneity in time series. The Standard Normal Homogeneity Test (SNHT [9]) was conducted to yearly data from 312 stations for sunshine duration and those from 328 stations for wind speed, all of which covered entire period from 1981 to 2020 without any missing values. As a result, implementing the adjustments described above improved homogeneity for sunshine duration at 280 stations (90% of the total) and for wind speed at 210 stations (64% of the total), which indicates that the adjustment methods used in this study were effective in improving the temporal homogeneity of the data.

Agro-meteorological variables

Agro-meteorological variables in HGMD-Japan are calculated by using the basic variables described above, along with an existing micro-meteorological model, in accordance with the procedure used to develop MeteoCrop DB [4], as described below.

Downward longwave radiation, potential and reference evapotranspiration

Potential evapotranspiration adopted here is defined as the evapotranspiration from a saturated surface [10]. It is regarded as the upper limit of evapotranspiration and is calculated from daily meteorological variables (mean temperature, air pressure, water vapor pressure, solar and downward longwave radiation, and wind speed at 1 m height above the ground). Air pressure at the target grid (Pa: hPa) is estimated by using a simple hypsometric equation as:(11)Pa=P0×exp[−(g·zRd·Tk)]where P0 is the standard air pressure (= 1013.25 hPa), g is the acceleration due to gravity (= 9.807 m s–2), Rd is the gas constant (= 287.0 J kg–1 K–1), and z and Tk are, respectively, the elevation (m) at, and the mean temperature (K) of, the target grid cell. Daily mean downward longwave radiation is estimated by using an empirical formula [11,12], where the turbidity coefficient is assumed to be 0.1 and the surface albedo to be 0.2 [4]. Wind speed at 1 m height is calculated in the same manner as for u(2.5m), except with zi = 1 m and z0i = 0.005 m in Eq. (5) [4].

Reference evapotranspiration is the potential evapotranspiration from the reference surface, which was assumed to be covered by a grass crop 0.12 m high and with adequate water, as defined in Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56 [13]. This is calculated by using the FAO Penman–Monteith formula, modified so that the existing variables in HGMD-Japan can be applied [13,14]. Wind speed at 2 m height is calculated in the same manner as u(2.5m), except with zi = 2 m in Eq. (5) [4]. Note that the downward longwave radiation used here is the same as that used to calculate the potential and reference evapotranspiration.

Paddy water temperature

Paddy water temperature is estimated theoretically from a model that can calculate the water temperature of a paddy field by using the daily mean meteorological variables presented above and solving the vertical heat balance of the paddy field under flooded conditions [15]. Two kinds of daily mean paddy water temperature are provided in HGMD-Japan, namely the temperature under unvegetated conditions (leaf area index (LAI) = 0) and the temperature when the paddy is covered completely with vegetation (LAI = ∞). Note that paddy water temperature under specific conditions of rice crop growth can be evaluated from the two estimated kinds of paddy water temperature, along with the solar radiation and wind speed with LAI, under those conditions [15].

Rice panicle temperature

Rice panicle temperature is estimated in an hourly time step by using a micrometeorological model (IM2PACT: Integrated Micrometeorology Model for PAnicle and Canopy Temperature [16]). IM2PACT can estimate the panicle temperature from hourly general meteorological values by solving the heat balance of the panicle and the rice canopy [16]. The hourly values of temperature, solar radiation, and wind speed are calculated by using an empirical function [17], and the daily values are used for downward longwave radiation (see above), vapor pressure, and air pressure as constant values throughout the day. The parameters required to calculate panicle temperature by using IM2PACT are given as follows: difference vegetation index = 2.0; LAI = 4.0. The rice panicle temperature is provided as hourly values in HGMD-Japan.

Agro-climatic indices

Some agro-climatic indices relevant to assessments of the impact of heat stress on crop productivity [18] are estimated by aggregating the daily or hourly variables presented above during specific periods with some statistical data related to crop growth and geographical data, as described below.

Some kinds of heat dose (HD), defined as the cumulative temperature exceeding a certain threshold temperature (HD = ∑(T – Tb), where T is the daily observed temperature and Tb is the base temperature), were adopted as indicators of heat stress intensity. Annual cumulative daily maximum temperature above the threshold value of 35°C (HD_x35), daily minimum temperature above the threshold value of 25°C (HD_n25), and daily mean temperature above the threshold value of 26°C (HD_m26), are included in HGMD-Japan as respective indices of daytime, nighttime, and all-day heat stress throughout the year.

Mean air temperature and heat dose of daily mean temperature above 26°C during the 20 days after the heading date was adopted as an indicator of rice quality degradation risk due to high temperature. Furthermore, mean panicle temperature in the daytime from 10:00 to 15:00 during the 5 or 7 days around the heading date was adopted as an indicator of rice yield decrease risk due to the increase in spikelet sterility caused by high temperature. These indices are estimated only at those grid cells that have a paddy field percentage larger than 1%. Land-use information for each grid was obtained from “Land Use Mesh 2006” National Land Numerical Information [19]. Yearly information on rice heading date is obtained from government crop statistics provided by the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry, and Fisheries and the data for each administrative unit is assigned to each grid cell in accordance with the process described previously [18].

Annual distribution maps and their explanatory articles for these agro-climatic indices are available on the websites of NIAES (the National Institute for Agro-Environmental Sciences) and NARO (the National Agriculture and Food Research Organization) and as research materials titled “Agricultural meteorology in summer (indicators for high temperature)” [20].

Limitations

Environmental changes such as urbanization or tree growth around observation stations are significant factors that affect the temporal homogeneity of meteorological variables. However, these factors were not taken into account when this dataset was developed. Consequently, there is a possibility of temporal heterogeneity in meteorological variables, particularly in temperature and wind speed.
