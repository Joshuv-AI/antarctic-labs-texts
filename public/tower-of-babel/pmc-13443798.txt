An Earth Observation-based Tier 3 framework for monitoring greenhouse gas emissions from forest fires in Italy
Authors: Chiara Aquino, Valentina Bacciu, Maria Vincenza Chiriacò, Alessandro D’Anca, Jishnu Jeevan, Sergio Noce, Adriana Torelli, Manuela Balzarolo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from forest fires are increasingly contributing to atmospheric concentrations of climate-altering gases and represent a growing and poorly constrained component of global carbon budgets, particularly under more frequent droughts and heatwaves. Closing this gap is critical for achieving the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 13, specifically indicator 13.2.2, “Total GHG emissions per year”, urging accurate quantification and monitoring for timely mitigation strategies. Here we introduce FIRE-TRACE, an Earth Observation-based framework integrating satellite data with field-based biomass measurements to improve estimates of forest fuel load and fire emissions. Unlike existing national inventories and global satellite products, FIRE-TRACE resolves spatial variation in burned area and fire severity at the scale of individual fires, enabling emission disaggregation across ecological and administrative units. This work advances SDG 13 by providing a standardised approach, demonstrated for Italy over the period 2018–2023 yet transferable to other countries and time periods, thereby strengthening national reporting capacities and advancing global climate monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55318-x.

Introduction

Biomass combustion from open vegetation fires – the burning of live or dead plant biomass across forests, savannas, crop residues, and peatlands1- is a critical driver of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions2,3. This trend is increasingly exacerbated by prolonged droughts and extreme heatwaves that elevate both the frequency and severity of fire events4–10. These fires release substantial quantities of CO₂, CH₄, aerosols, and their precursors, thereby altering atmospheric composition11–13 and accelerating the transfer of carbon from terrestrial reservoirs to the atmosphere14,15. Although the total global burned area has declined16, fire activity is becoming increasingly concentrated in carbon-rich forest ecosystems17,18. Forest fires, while comprising only ~ 5% of burned areas, contribute approximately 20% of global CO and 18% of CO2 from fires17. Unlike grassland and savanna fires, forest fires release more CO2 per unit area, with post-fire recovery often slow, incomplete, or absent in cases of degradation and deforestation19. Between 2001 and 2023, global forest fire carbon emissions surged by 60%, driven by more extreme climatic conditions and dry fuel accumulation5. As fires events expand across forested landscapes, the diminished regenerative capacity of these systems impairs their ability to reabsorb atmospheric carbon, delaying recovering and reducing their role as effective carbon sinks5,8,20–22.

Achieving the Paris Agreement’s goal of limiting global warming to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels requires drastic reductions in anthropogenic GHG emissions, with residual emissions neutralised by sustained carbon removals23. Recognising the urgent need for climate action, the United Nations has introduced Sustainable Development Goal 13 (SDG 13: Climate Action) as part of its 2030 Agenda. One key indicator, SDG 13.2.2 “Total GHG emissions per year”, explicitly highlights the need of accurately quantifying and monitoring GHG emissions to inform timely climate mitigation policies (https://sdg.data.gov/13-2-2/). Despite advances in fire science, significant knowledge gaps persist regarding the spatial and interannual variability in burned area and GHG emissions, as well as their implications for achieving net-zero targets24–26. Under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), Annex I countries are required to submit annual National Inventory Reports (NIRs) detailing anthropogenic GHG emissions and removals, including CO₂ and non-CO₂ emissions from biomass burning, primarily within the Land Use, Land Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) sector. These estimates rely on fire activity data at the national scale - such as burned area, fuel load, and combustion completeness – integrated with emission factors, as outlined by the 2006 and 2019 IPCC Guidelines for National Greenhouse Gas Inventories (NGHGI)27,28. The Tier 1 IPCC approach uses default values for activity data and emission factors derived from national statistics or international datasets, which are globally or regionally averaged. Tier 2 and 3 IPCC methods rely on country-specific data and increasingly sophisticated models or measurement systems, respectively, resulting in improved accuracy and better representation of national circumstances. In practice, most countries - particularly those in fire-prone southern Europe - still use lower-tier methods (Tier 1 or 2), leading to higher levels of uncertainty29. For example, Italy’s Tier 1 for CO2 emissions assumes complete and instantaneous oxidation of forest biomass during fires, with all carbon content released immediately to the atmosphere30. This can significantly overestimate emissions, particularly in low-intensity fires where biomass combustion is incomplete and fire-induced mortality is limited29. Although the 2006 and 2019 IPCC Guidelines encourage the adoption of higher-tier methodologies to improve the accuracy of GHG estimates, limited progress has been made in improving forest fire emission reporting in national inventories. Indeed, improving the representativeness of activity data (e.g. burned area and fuel load) requires extensive in situ measurements, often gathered through field surveys31,32. These data are essential to capture the variability in fuels and fire behaviour but can significantly increase reporting complexity, time, and uncertainty33,34.

Satellite-based monitoring provides continuous, wall-to-wall observation of biomass burning, overcoming the limitations and high costs associated with field-based collection of fire activity data. Thermal anomalies detected by spaceborne sensors such as the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) and the Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) are capable of identifying fire fronts in near-real time at moderate spatial resolution35. These data underpin operational systems such as the European Forest Fire Information System (EFFIS), which delivers spatially explicit products including burned area delineation and post-fire severity assessments36. Fire behaviour is further characterised through proxies like Fire Radiative Power (FRP), a direct measure of the thermal energy released by active fires37,38. Beyond serving as an indicator of fire intensity, FRP is a critical parameter for activity-based emission estimation approaches39, most notably the Global Fire Assimilation System (GFAS), which infer fire emissions at 0.1° spatial resolution without requiring explicit fuel load and burn area estimates40. Other activity-based approaches39, including the Global Fire Emissions Database (GFED)13,41 and the Fire INventory from NCAR (FINN)42,43, derive emissions at 0.25° and 1 km spatial resolution, respectively, by integrating burned area maps with biome-averaged fuel loads, combustion completeness factors, and emission factors. Across both methods, land cover classification remains a major source of errors, as coarse land cover classes and inconsistencies between land cover datasets fail to accurately represent combustion dynamics and fuel loads44, limiting the potential for spatial granularity that high-resolution satellite data can provide45. Given the global scale and relatively coarse spatial resolution, these datasets also struggle to detect small fires or capture fine-scale variations in fuel loads46–48. Finally, these global inventories require non-trivial processing to extract country-specific emission estimates, which makes it challenging to easily integrate data into NIRs and track progress towards SDG targets.

Accurately estimating forest fire emissions requires bridging satellite observations with inventory-based datasets. National forest inventories (NFIs) provide country-specific aggregate statistics of forest biomass by forest functional type, based on field data collection, useful to derive estimates of CO2 fluxes. Indeed, a critical component of fire emission modelling is quantifying available fuel (i.e., live biomass, ground litter, dead wood)32,49,50 and combustion completeness – sometimes referred to as burning efficiency (BE) – as these factors directly influence the magnitude and composition of emissions13,51. Combustion completeness describes the fraction of biomass that is oxidised during fire, whereas burning efficiency typically refers to the fraction of biomass consumed or lost. In this study, we use burning efficiency (BE) as an empirical proxy for combustion completeness, as it can be directly estimated from observed biomass loss derived from field and remote sensing data. BE is closely linked to fire severity, which varies within burn areas depending on fire behaviour52–54. An incorrect estimation of the combusted fraction can introduce substantial errors, ranging from 23 to 46%, in the quantification of emissions from fires55. Therefore, accurately characterising both the extent of fire damage (severity) and the pre-fire fuel load is crucial for improving emission estimates56.

This study introduces FIRE-TRACE (Forest fIRe framEwork for Tier3 Reporting and Accounting of Country-level Emissions), a satellite-driven model designed for Tier 3-level GHG emissions calculation from forest fires and their integration into NIRs. This framework is demonstrated for Italy, a Southern European hotspot where forest fire activity has intensified markedly in recent years. By coupling satellite observations with ground NFI data, FIRE-TRACE computes a spatially and temporally dynamic combustion factor, improving the tracking of LULUCF carbon fluxes. Moreover, FIRE-TRACE provides fire-level resolution emission estimates, allowing to increase the granularity of reporting in the NIRs at national and subnational scales. The outputs are integrated into the FIRE-TRACE platform, which offers an interactive interface for visualising fire events, analysing emission trends and exporting statistical or georeferenced data for use in NIRs or by national statistical offices. Ultimately, this work aims to: (1) provide a consistent framework to operationalise the integration of remote sensing within the IPCC model; (2) advance emissions estimation from Tier 1/2 to Tier 3, particularly through the development of a spatially and temporally resolved combustion model; and (3) facilitate access to the data for statistical officers and policymakers, while maintaining a flexible, modular framework that supports transferability to other countries and integration of new datasets as they become available.

Burned area and fuel load

Burned areas were derived from the European Forest Fire Information System (EFFIS) Burnt Areas Database (https://forest-fire.emergency.copernicus.eu), which produces bi-daily fire perimeters across Europe, the Middle East and North Africa, allowing standardised measurements and timeliness36,60.

From 2018 onwards, EFFIS incorporated Sentinel-2 MSI data, enhancing spatial granularity to detect fires ≥ 5 ha with ~ 95% classification accuracy61. Within mapped fire scars, internal combustion heterogeneity was addressed using the EFFIS fire severity product, which classifies fire severity into five classes, from unburned/regrowth to high severity. These were used to quantify effective combustion area and fire patchiness following Slocum et al.62, with severity interpreted as a proxy for combustion completeness54.

Fuel type and loadings were estimated by selecting forest classes from CORINE Land Cover 201863 (CLC18) and combining these with publicly available regional forest inventory data from the Italian National Forest Census (INFC)64,65. CLC18 provides a standardised, pan-European land cover classification at a spatial resolution of 100 m. Its hierarchical system ranges from Level I (broad categories) to Level IV (highly detailed classes); we utilised Level IV to identify forest types and matched these to the corresponding INFC categories using the fuel classification framework proposed by Ascoli et al.32. This classification harmonises over 600 quantitative fuel samples collected over the past decade in alpine, temperate and Mediterranean ecosystems, encompassing duff, litter, herbaceous, shrub, and downed woody fuel loadings by size classes (1 h, 10 h, 100 h). We adopted the IPCC27 classification of carbon pools, including aboveground live biomass, and dead organic matter (e.g., standing deadwood, coarse woody debris, and litter). Soil organic carbon was excluded from the analysis, given its limited combustion under most fire scenarios66. The INFC provides regionally disaggregated carbon stock estimates (Mg ha⁻¹) for multiple biomass components - namely, live trees, dead standing trees, woody debris ≥ 9.5 cm in diameter, stumps, and litter - across Italy’s 20 NUTS-2 regions64,65. For vegetation types not covered by national classifications, such as the “high maquis,” we applied an empirical estimate of aboveground biomass (65 Mg ha⁻¹) derived from field measurements of Mediterranean sclerophyllous vegetation by Costa and La Mantia67. Fuel load values for the 19 vegetation types were obtained by spatially intersecting CLC18 land cover classes with INFC forest typologies. Each fuel stratum was assigned a corresponding dry biomass value, calculated by applying a standard carbon content factor (CC) of 0.5. These biomass estimates were mapped at 100 m resolution and aggregated by ecoregion using the RESOLVE global ecoregions dataset, which classifies terrestrial ecosystems based on ecological and biogeographic characteristic68. This aggregation allows accounting for spatial variability in vegetation types and ecological conditions relevant to fire emissions. Emission factors were drawn from the IPCC (2006) and EMEP/EEA (2023) protocols for trace gases and PM2.5, respectively.

Burning efficiency model

The combustion factor (C in Eq. 1 and Eq. 2) describes the fraction of pre-fire aboveground biomass that is consumed during fire events. Combustion completeness formally refers to the fraction of biomass oxidised during combustion. In this study, combustion completeness is estimated using burning efficiency (BE), derived from observed biomass loss, and BE is used as an empirical proxy to parameterise C. We developed a country-specific model of BE following the approach of Balde et al.69, using empirical field data from the two most recent INFC (2005 and 2015). The INFC employs a three-phase, stratified sampling framework over a 1 km × 1 km UTM grid65. Phase I, aimed at identifying points belonging to forest-related land cover classes, involves a preliminary land use and land use cover classification through photo-interpretation of orthophotos at over 301,000 randomly distributed sampling points across the grid. Phase II consists of a terrestrial survey conducted on a stratified subset of forested sites to collect qualitative data on forest structure, management, and ownership. Phase III involves quantitative sampling on concentric circular plots (4 m and 13 m radii), optimized to reduce edge effects. This study utilised the larger (530 m²) plots from Phase III, which include measurements of tree diameter at breast height (DBH), total height, increment cores, deadwood, and stumps. Consistent field protocols between the 2005 and 2015 inventories enabled temporal comparisons, however, it is important to note the associated uncertainty introduced by the fact that field surveys were not conducted precisely within the reference years, but rather during broader periods. Plot coordinates correspond to the theoretical centre of the sampling unit targeted during field campaigns. Tree-level AGB is provided at the plot level as derived by species-specific allometric equations based on tree diameter and height. Plot-level AGB is retrieved from aggregating biomass estimates across all callipered trees. These data are publicly accessible via the online spatial database at inventarioforestale.org70. Following previous findings71, the uncertainty associated with the allometric model predictions was deemed negligible and therefore excluded from further error propagation in this analysis72. Fire-affected plots were identified by intersecting INFC plots with the ESA FireCCI51 dataset (250 m resolution, 2001–2019). Only plots burned between 2006 and 2014 and surveyed ≥ 3 years post-fire were retained to account for the uncertainty of the measurement period. An additional 30 m buffer was applied to each plot to guarantee complete inclusion within the fire perimeter. Plots were classified by dominant forest type (coniferous or broadleaved) and stratified by fire severity. Biomass burning efficiency (BE) was calculated as the proportion of live AGB relative to total biomass (live + dead + stumps). A correction factor was applied to avoid overestimation in low-biomass plots:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{LBE}_{adj}=\:LBE\:\times\:\:\left(\frac{LBE}{(LBE+0.5)}\right)$$\end{document}

Plots with LBE ≤ 0 or dNBR < 0.1 were excluded. Visual validation was performed via high-resolution Google Earth imagery to eliminate outliers and confirm fire impact. After filtering, 15 plots from the initial 6667 georeferenced plots met the necessary criteria and were retained for regression analysis. Fire severity was quantified using the differenced Normalised Burn Ratio (dNBR), computed from pre- and post-fire Landsat surface reflectance. NBR is computed from near-infrared (NIR) and shortwave infrared (SWIR) bands using the Tier 1 Surface Reflectance product as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:NBR\:=\frac{NIR-SWIR}{NIR+SWIR},\:\:dNBR={NBR}_{pre\:\:}-\:{NBR}_{post\:\:}$$\end{document}

The positive dNBR values (between 0.1 and 1.30) are produced by the degree of impact of the fire on the vegetation and the soil, which can be categorized into different classes of severity52. Maximum dNBR within each plot was extracted using an 8-month time window to mitigate seasonal artefacts. Values were classified into severity categories per USGS standards: low (0.1–0.27), moderate-low (0.27–0.44), moderate-high (0.44–0.66), and high severity (> 0.66)52. To quantify the relationship between fire severity and BE, we fitted linear regressions using dNBR as the predictor and proportional BE as the response. Separate models were built for coniferous and broadleaved plots to account for differing combustion dynamics73. Model performance was assessed using Pearson’s R². To generalise combustion completeness across severity gradients, BE was summarised by dNBR class (mean, median, SD, IQR), and statistical differences were tested using one-way ANOVA with post hoc comparisons. BE values for each forest type and fire severity class were derived from the empirical models relating plot-level biomass consumption to dNBR. For each EFFIS-defined severity class, we sampled 10 evenly spaced dNBR values across the corresponding range, computed the associated BE values and uncertainties, and then calculated the mean and standard deviation for each class. Uncertainty in BE was estimated by propagating the standard errors of the slope and intercept of the empirical models for coniferous and broadleaved forests.

Cross-comparison with reference fire emission inventories and an empirical fuel consumption and emission model

To evaluate the performance and consistency of the FIRE-TRACE framework, we conducted a comparative analysis against the Italian National Greenhouse Gas Inventory (IT NIR), three satellite-derived global fire emission inventories (GFEDv4s, GFAS v1.2, FINNv2.5), and the First Order Fire Effects Model (FOFEM v6.7) which explicitly simulates fuel consumption based on meteorological conditions, fuel type, and combustion phase. The comparison was based on two primary metrics: the relative percentage difference (RPD) and the correlation coefficient (r) between annual emission estimates. For each inventory, we used the reported activity data; specifically, burned biomass or dry matter per unit area, and applied a harmonised set of emission factors: IPCC (2006) for trace gases and EMEP/EEA (2023) for PM2.5. Using common emission factors ensured that RPD values capture differences in activity data alone. The only exception to this harmonisation is FOFEM, which uses internally defined emission factors. We further standardised the analysis by consistently using the land cover classes most representative of the dominant forest ecosystems in Italy and by spatially constraining all estimates to the same national boundary. For satellite-based inventories which typically report emission as wildfire fluxes (g C m⁻² month⁻¹), we converted all outputs to dry matter (DM) units (kg DM m⁻² month⁻¹), enabling consistent application of source- and species-specific EFs derived from IPCC (2006) and EMEP/EEA (2023) guidelines.

GFEDv4s

The Global Fire Emissions Database version 4s (GFEDv4s) provides monthly gridded emissions at 0.25° spatial resolution globally and builds upon the GFEDv4 framework41,46 with key enhancements detailed by van der Werf et al.13. Notably, GFEDv4s incorporates emissions from small fires through the inclusion of active fire detections which reduces known underestimations in agricultural and fragmented fire regimes. Fire emissions are stratified by six fire types - savanna, boreal, temperate forest, deforestation, peat, and agricultural waste - based on MODIS MCD12Q1 land cover classification at 500 m resolution. For this study, only emissions from temperate forest were retained to align with the dominant forest classification in Italy. Mediterranean scrublands, which are ecologically significant across much of Italy, are classified within the savanna category in GFED; however, they were excluded from this analysis to avoid capturing emissions from low maquis and non-forest shrublands, likely resulting in a conservative emissions estimate (see Supplementary Fig. 3). FINNv2.5

The Fire INventory from NCAR (FINN v2.5)43 provides daily fire emissions at 1 km resolution, derived from MODIS and VIIRS active fire detections combined with land cover and biomass density maps74,75. We opted to use the individual fire event files rather than the standard gridded datasets, as the former provide higher spatial granularity and include explicit fire characteristics. Each record includes burned area, biomass loading, vegetation type and emissions of both aerosols and gas-phase species. Emissions for each compound were recalculated by multiplying burned area, biomass, and our harmonised EFs consistent with IPCC protocols. Only fire events located within Italy were retained for analysis. While FINNv2.5 distinguishes between temperate forest and temperate evergreen forest classes, we applied a unified EF across both to maintain consistency with national forest typologies. Similarly, emissions from woody savannas and grasslands were processed using a common EF, although their contribution was not included in the final national inventory to avoid capturing emissions from Mediterranean shrublands.

GFAS v1.2

The Global Fire Assimilation System (GFASv1.2) estimates global daily DM combustion at 0.5° resolution by scaling MODIS fire radiative power (FRP) using biome-specific conversion factors40. Similarly to GFED and FINN, the resulting gridded DM flux for individual species was integrated over time and spatially scaled using the grid cell area to compute total emissions. Unlike GFED and FINN, GFAS lacks explicit land cover classification. To constrain emissions to forested regions, we applied a spatial mask based on CLC18, extracting only forest-related classes. Due to its coarse resolution (~ 55 km), GFAS may underestimate emissions in fragmented or heterogeneous forest landscapes.

FOFEM v6.7

The First Order Fire Effects Model (FOFEM v6.7)76 simulates fire-induced biomass consumption and emissions based on fuelbed properties and moisture content. FOFEM employs empirical equations calibrated to experimental and field data, including the National Fire and Fire Surrogate Study and other combustion datasets77–79. The model’s emissions module incorporates default and updated species-specific emission factors (e.g., from Urbanski et al.80) aligned with contemporary air quality modeling frameworks. We customised fuelbeds for each fire event using CLC18 classes and region-specific biomass loadings from Scarpa et al.81 derived from field estimates by Ascoli et al.32. For each CLC class, we assigned mean fuel loads (in Mg ha⁻¹) across standard categories: duff, litter, dead woody fuels (1 h, 10 h, 100 h), and live herbaceous and shrub biomass. Fuel moisture conditions, a critical driver of combustion completeness and emission intensity, were derived from the Copernicus Emergency Management Service (CEMS). Specifically, we extracted the Fine Fuel Moisture Code (FFMC) at the centroid of each fire event as a proxy for the moisture content of litter, mosses, and small twigs (< 1 cm diameter). Moisture categories (wet to extreme) were assigned using regional FFMC percentiles, and associated fuel moisture values were adapted from Scarpa et al.81.

National inventory of Italy (IT NIR)

The Italian National Inventory Report (IT NIR)30 represents Italy’s official submission to the UNFCCC. Within the LULUCF sector, emissions from biomass burning are reported for forests, croplands, and grasslands. Non-CO₂ fire emissions (CH₄, N₂O) are estimated using a semi-empirical model that incorporates fire severity and vegetation type, originally developed by Bovio82 and refined by Chiriacò et al.29 to include scorch height as a fire intensity proxy. While CO₂ emissions are reported indirectly through net carbon stock changes and are not disaggregated by disturbance type (e.g., forest fires), CH₄ and N₂O emissions are explicitly reported as wildfire-related in the Common Reporting Format (CRF) tables. To ensure comparability with other datasets, we isolated wildfire emissions occurring exclusively on forest land. Using the reported CH₄ values in conjunction with associated activity data and our own set of trace gas and aerosol emission factors harmonised with IPCC guidelines, we derived additional species-level emissions (e.g., CO, PM2.5) to enable a comparative analysis with the other inventories.

Results

A satellite and field-based framework of fire emissions

For each reporting year, burned area perimeters are ingested from EFFIS (Fig. 1A), which provides detailed information on fire occurrence date and spatial extent. The availability of high-resolution fire perimeters allows for the classification of fuel stocks based on field-based biomass inventories (Fig. 1B). Fire severity, retrieved from EFFIS severity maps, is used to estimate the amount of combusted biomass through a locally-calibrated burning efficiency model (Fig. 1C). This model generates spatially variable burning efficiency factors that depend on the fire severity within each burned area perimeter. When combined with emission factors for key trace gases (CO₂, CH₄, CO, NOₓ, N₂O) and fine particulate matter (PM2.5), the framework yields estimates of fire emissions for each forest type and fire severity class (Fig. 1D).

Burning efficiency model from earth observation

Statistically significant correlations (p < 0.05) were observed between Landsat-derived differenced Normalised Burn Ratio (dNBR) and burning efficiency (BE) in 15 INFC plots measured before and after fires between 2006 and 2014 (Fig. 2A). For both broadleaved and coniferous forests BE was significantly correlated to fire severity (Fig. 2B), with coniferous forests showing a higher correlation than broadleaved forests (p < 0.05 R2 = 0.79 and p < 0.05, R2 = 0.52, respectively). As expected, burning efficiency increased with fire severity (Fig. 2C). Coniferous forests consistently exhibited higher combustion completeness across all severity classes, with median values ranging from 0.54 (moderate-high) to 0.89 (± 0.03) (high). Broadleaved forests displayed less variation between low and moderate-low severity classes (0.23 (± 0.19) and 0.20 (± 0.17), respectively), but showed a marked increase under high severity conditions (0.69 ± 0.19). While standard deviation in BE was relatively uniform across severity classes at the global level, interquartile range (IQR) was greatest in the moderate-low and high categories, suggesting increased variability in combustion completeness at these extremes (Supplementary Table 1). These trends reflect a fire regime in the study region skewed towards higher severity.

Spatiotemporal patterns of ghg emissions from forest fires

Forest fire-related GHG emissions, expressed in carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e), were estimated as the combined output of CO2, CH4 and N2O. Over the period 2018–2023, forest fires affected a 10,926 ha yr⁻¹ across Italy, resulting in an average annual flux of 1,096 Gg CO2e yr⁻¹ (Supplementary Table 2). Fire activity peaked in 2021, contributing 45% (2,892 Gg) of total emissions during the study period. Large fires (> 50 ha), though comprising only 7% of all fire events, were responsible for 63% of the burned area and contributed disproportionately (67%) to total GHG emissions. Emission intensity exhibited marked variation across vegetation types (Supplementary Tables 3–4). Coniferous forests displayed the highest per-hectare emissions, with Silver fir/Norway spruce stands reaching 0.26 Gg CO₂e ha⁻¹, followed by Larch/Arolla pine (0.23 Gg CO₂e ha⁻¹) and mixed conifers (0.21 Gg CO₂e ha⁻¹). In contrast, sclerophyllous, mesophilous and Mediterranean evergreen oak forests had substantially lower emission intensities (0.05–0.06 Gg CO₂e ha⁻¹). Mediterranean pine forests were the dominant source of GHG emissions, with an average annual emission of 255 Gg CO₂e yr⁻¹ over the 2018–2023 period, with values ranging from 50 Gg CO₂e in 2019 to a peak of 507 Gg CO₂e in 2021. This was followed by mixed coniferous forests (211 Gg CO₂e yr⁻¹) and deciduous oak forests (119 Gg CO₂e yr⁻¹). At the biome level (Fig. 3A), Mediterranean forests, woodlands, and scrublands were the primary contributors, accounting for 92% of national forest fire emissions. Temperate conifer forests and temperate broadleaved/mixed forests contributed 5% and 3%, respectively (Supplementary Tables 5–6). Among ecoregions (Fig. 3B), the Tyrrhenian-Adriatic sclerophyllous forests - which encompass Sardinia, Sicily, and southern coastal zones- emitted 655 Gg CO₂e yr⁻¹. The South Apennine mixed montane forests represented the second most affected ecoregion (136 Gg CO₂e yr⁻¹), with 79% of those emissions concentrated in 2021 (Supplementary Tables 7–8).

Discussion

Forest fire emissions are predominantly determined by four variables: the extent of the burned area, the type and amount of vegetation biomass available for combustion (fuel load), the combustion completeness (or burning efficiency) and the emission factors specific to each fuel type. Among these, the burned area is typically the most reliably quantified. While the field-based perimeter mapping, such as that employed by IT NIR, is theoretically the most accurate approach, its widespread application is hindered by logistical, financial, and accessibility constraints, which delay reporting. Moreover, perimeter delineations are not always accurate, due to GPS survey errors and the challenges of mapping vegetated and inaccessible areas83. In Italy, comparisons between EFFIS-derived burned areas and field data from the Italian Forest Service (CUFAA) reveals agreement in certain NUTS-2 regions and years, while in general, disagreements are very variable in time and space. Here, ‘agreement’ is defined as instances where EFFIS and CUFAA report similar numbers of fire events and burned area (within ± 10%), while ‘disagreement’ refers to larger discrepancies. Between 2007 and 2022, CUFAA recorded 6.5% more fire events and 5.5% more burned area than EFFIS. With the integration of Sentinel-2 data from 2018, the difference in the number of fire events narrowed to just 0.2% less than EFFIS, while CUFAA reported 2.8% more burned area59. Incorporating small fires, once data from CUFAA become available, could retrospectively improve the accuracy of historical emissions estimates derived from EFFIS alone. To avoid the time lag inherent in field data collection, promising remote-sensing approaches using change detection in radar and optical imagery may also complement EFFIS in the detection of small burned areas84.

With available geographical information of burnt areas, pre-fire vegetation can be classified into different fuel types within each fire perimeters, and the corresponding fuel loads can be used to estimate emissions. To reduce uncertainty, direct measurements of fuel components, such as litter, duff, and coarse wood, should be prioritised85. To address this, FIRE-TRACE links field-based measurements of fuel load from the INFC - covering live biomass, deadwood, litter, and fine woody debris - to CLC data (Fig. 1B) to generate a fine-resolution (100 m) spatial fuel map. Global satellite-based inventories aggregate fuel types by coarse biome categories (temperate, tropical, or boreal forests – with the exception of North America in FINNv2.543, which is based on FCCS). In contrast, our study enables the differentiation of 19 vegetation classes across 21 NUTS-2 regions. These include broadleaved and coniferous forests in Alpine, temperate, and Mediterranean regions, following the classification framework of Ascoli et al.32 Importantly, our estimates include fuel components such as dead organic matter and litter (from INFC 2005), thus improving representation of combustion potential across diverse forest strata.

Combustion completeness or burning efficiency (BE) - the fraction of biomass consumed during fire51,55,86- is arguably the most sensitive parameter in fire emission estimation29 together with fuel characterisation87. BE is influenced by multiple factors, including fuel moisture content and fire intensity, which varies with meteorological conditions and fuel type88. Most regional and global emission estimates rely on an average BE (Tier 1/Tier 2 methodology). They either assign it to major vegetation or fuel types or apply it uniformly across the entire burned area, in both cases assuming complete vegetation combustion. However, numerous studies demonstrate significant regional and intra-biome variability55. Ward et al.89 and Sá et al.90 suggested that BE should be treated as a dynamic variable, that varies seasonally rather than remaining constant. In fact, the use of a fixed BE values can introduce significant uncertainties, estimated to range between 23% and 46%55,91–93. A different approach is to estimate BE based on fire severity, which quantifies landscape changes relative to pre-fire conditions52. Fire severity is closely linked to BE, as variations in fire severity not only affect the amount of biomass consumed but also influence an ecosystem’s ability to recover carbon after a fire56.

In this study, we adapted the method proposed by Kasischke et al.94 which associates fire severity classes with different levels of biomass loss. Following Balde et al.69, we expanded their analysis to include both live and dead biomass losses from fire-affected NFI plots, making this the first study of its kind in Italy. Our analysis revealed that most plots experienced moderate-to-high severity fires, in line with known firedynamics in Mediterranean ecosystems95–97. The observed correlations between dNBR and BE were strong (R = 0.89 for conifers; R = 0.72 for broadleaves) suggesting that the dNBR-BE relationship provides a robust method for estimating biomass loss. Our findings by severity class for broadleaved forests (0.22 for low severity and 0.32–0.46 for medium severity) are consistent with De Santis et al.55, who reported values of 0.25 for low severity and 0.40 for medium severity, although our estimate for high severity (0.81) is higher than their reported 0.56. Similarly, Oliva and Chuvieco98 found BE values of 0.25 (low), 0.40 (medium), and 0.57 (high) for broadleaved forests in Spain. These discrepancies may arise because both De Santis et al.55 and Oliva and Chuvieco98 adjusted BE values using the geometrically modified Composite Burn Index (GeoCBI), a qualitative visual measure that accounts for vegetation cover fraction to determine fire severity of the total plot99. Furthermore, the discrepancies in higher-end values may reflect our sample biases toward severe fires. Compared to the more recent study by Balde et al.69, our findings are consistent with their higher BE values for broadleaved forests at moderate-to-high severity (0.64–0.83), while our estimates for lower severity levels align more closely with earlier literature. For conifers, our BE values ranged from 0.44 to 0.96, exceeding values reported by Balde et al.69 (0.43–0.78), likely due to our limited sample size and severity bias. Comparisons with Balde et al.69 suggest our model slightly underestimates BE for broadleaves and overestimates it for conifers by ~ 10.9%. This difference likely stem from the sample composition: our dataset included more broadleaved plots (N = 10) than coniferous ones (N = 5), whereas Balde et al.69 analysed a predominantly coniferous dataset (Nconifers = 49, Nbroadleaved = 11). The limited number of fire-affected NFI plots constrained our assessment to only two vegetation types. This sample size reflects the realities of opportunistic post-fire assessments - being the first of its kind in Italy, where no systematic fire-damage inventories exist. Obtaining larger samples remains challenging due to the rarity of permanent plots surviving wildfires and fire access restrictions. While sufficient to explore dNBR-BE relationships, broader datasets would improve model robustness and generalisability, while also enabling species-specific BE estimates. Future studies should expand the study area and incorporate targeted field campaigns, as NFI plots are not specifically designed for fire assessments. This is particularly important for Mediterranean ecosystems, where shrub-dominated understorey vegetation - often entirely consumed during fires - is underrepresented in inventories that overlook shrublands and assume CO₂ emissions are offset by forest regrowth69,96. To enable replication of the study, especially in other Mediterranean contexts, it is therefore essential to incorporate a broader diversity of species, particularly sclerophyll and fire-resistant ones, into the dataset. Additionally, the 2015 INFC lacked data on dead woody material and litter, limiting temporal assessments of these components. As a result, our model focused on live and coarse dead biomass, which is more readily detectable via remote sensing. Although our estimates are consistent with previous studies, omitting understorey biomass likely leads to underestimations of total fire emissions. Given that the herbaceous layer typically burns almost entirely even under moderate conditions, obtaining reliable estimates of fuel load becomes particularly important to reduce uncertainties in emission estimates. Expanding the study area and implementing targeted field campaigns - particularly prescribed burns with pre- and post-fire measurements - would therefore enhance the characterisation of understorey dynamics, especially for fire-resilient species.

A persistent source of uncertainty in fire emission models stems from the emission factors (EFs) used to convert biomass consumed into atmospheric pollutants80. Tier 1 methods apply uniform EFs across all biomes27 while Tier 2 estimates provide some differentiation based on biome-specific characteristics11. Global emission inventories commonly use biome-averaged arithmetic means derived from laboratory and field measurements, with two primary datasets: Andreae and Merlet11(updated annually) and Akagi et al.1, which form the backbone of GFED, GFAS and FINN. Despite expansions in the suite of gases considered, these datasets remain limited in their ability to capture the variability intrinsic to different fire regimes, vegetation types, and combustion phases100,101. In this study, we refined the EF selection by adopting the methodology of Volkova et al.57. Consistent with IT NIR30, we employed IPCC EFs for CH₄, N₂O, CO, and NOₓ, for the temperate regions while PM2.5 estimates were based on the EMEP/EEA Air Pollutant Emission Inventory Guidebook (2023)102, which explicitly includes Mediterranean forest categories, addressing the region’s lower biomass density and high fire incidence. Crucially, our modular framework allows for future integration of species- or country-specific EFs as new empirical data become available, which can then be linked with their relevant vegetation type.

Given the absence of systematic, field-based measurements of emissions during forest fires, model validation typically relies on cross-inventory comparisons48,81,103. As shown in Fig. 5, despite differences in absolute emission values, all inventories captured the interannual variability and the pronounced emissions peak in 2021 across all gas species. Among the inventories assessed, FIRE-TRACE demonstrated the strongest alignment with the process-based model FOFEM, particularly for CO₂, with a correlation coefficient of 0.99 and a relative percent difference (RPD) of just 1.19%. This high concordance suggests that physically explicit combustion modelling, when combined with harmonised inputs such as burned area, yields robust estimates for more stable species like CO₂, which exhibited the lowest coefficient of variation across inventories (CV = 1.75%)48. However, substantial divergence was observed for more variable species, notably PM2.5 and CH₄. These differences stem in part from emission factor assumptions. For instance, FOFEM’s use of a PM2.5 EF nearly twice that of EMEP/EEA 2023 (17.6 vs. 9 g/kg) and its explicit modelling of combustion phase dynamics - where smouldering, often dominant, is associated with higher EFs - contribute to elevated emission estimates. Residual differences between FIRE-TRACE and FOFEM thus appear largely attributable to differences in EF parameterisation, combustion phase representation, and underlying fuel characterisation. When compared with IT NIR, FIRE-TRACE produced consistently lower emissions, with an average RPD of − 72.72% across all species. This gap is primarily due to substantial differences in reported burned area (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 11). Another contributing factor is the treatment of combustion efficiency: whereas IT NIR applies uniform combustion classes (derived from measurements of scorch height)30, FIRE-TRACE incorporates spatial heterogeneity in fire severity, enabling differentiation between areas of complete, partial, and negligible combustion. This refinement yields lower, but more spatially nuanced, emission estimates. Satellite-derived inventories (GFEDv4.1s, GFASv1.2, FINNv2.5) clustered closely in magnitude but diverged from both IT NIR and FOFEM, placing FIRE-TRACE in an intermediate position between the two groups. This pattern echoes previous findings by Scarpa et al.81, who reported substantial discrepancies between FOFEM and IT NIR for forest emissions – particularly for CH₄ (− 62%) and CO (− 81%) - while differences for PM were comparatively modest. Relative to GFED4s, the authors also found elevated CO₂ emissions from process-based modelling (70%), which is consistent with our observed 41–97% increases over satellite-derived inventories. Disparities among inventories also reflect divergent land cover classifications and input data resolutions. Global products such as GFEDv4.1s, GFASv1.2, and FINNv2.5 typically categorise fires within broad biome classes (e.g., “temperate forest” or “savanna”) which often fail to capture ecologically distinct yet fire-prone systems like Mediterranean maquis. In our analysis, we limited comparisons with global inventories to their temperate forest category, as the “savanna” class in these datasets likely encompasses large extents of low maquis vegetation that we did not classify as forest. Sensitivity tests confirm that including savanna-like vegetation leads to a marked increase in total emissions (Supplementary Fig. 3–4), indicating that land cover representation is a primary driver of divergence between FIRE-TRACE and global satellite-based inventories, especially for CO2, NOx and PM2.5 emissions. Species-specific comparisons further underscore these inconsistencies. While FIRE-TRACE and FOFEM show tight agreement for CO₂, they diverge markedly for CH₄ and N₂O. For instance, CH₄ emissions were nearly 89% higher than those from GFAS, but 54% lower than those from FOFEM. In our dataset, annual CO₂ emissions ranged from 317 to 1,805 Gg yr− 1 across inventories (CV = 62.9%), though limiting the comparison to FIRE-TRACE and satellite products reduced this to 48% (Supplementary Tables 12–13). Such divergence highlights how variations in EF assignment - not just burned area estimates - play a pivotal role in inter-model discrepancies. Of all systems assessed, FOFEM displayed the highest internal consistency (mean r = 0.99), underscoring the relative stability of process-based approaches when benchmarked against remote-sensing proxies. Nonetheless, the applicability of FOFEM beyond its North American origin requires caution. Its EF database and combustion algorithms, derived from U.S.-centric fuelbeds and fire regimes104, may misrepresent fuel consumption patterns in structurally distinct ecosystems such as Mediterranean forests or tropical savannas77. Customisation of fuel properties is possible within FOFEM, but without region-specific data, uncertainty remains elevated.

The limitations of satellite-derived approaches were also evident. Remote-sensing products are subject to spatial and temporal sampling gaps105: MODIS, for instance, detects active fires only during its overpass windows and tend to underestimate emissions from large, continuous events48. FIRE-TRACE’s integration of fine-resolution forest inventory data allowed for improved attribution of emissions to specific vegetation classes across Alpine, temperate, and Mediterranean zones32, which could support future analyses of ecosystem functions in forest environments. However, further refinement is needed, particularly in linking combustion efficiency and EF variability to specific fuelbeds. Scarpa et al.81 previously recommended combining FOFEM with high-resolution biomass metrics (e.g., from LiDAR) to improve post-fire consumption estimates and emission factor calibration.

FIRE-TRACE’s modular design enables incorporation of higher-resolution inputs as they become available, facilitating national-level customisation and interannual consistency. While demonstrated here for Italy, the framework is adaptable to other national contexts. Burned-area data are available across Europe and North Africa through EFFIS, while global coverage can be achieved using FIRE CCI products, albeit at lower spatial resolution106. Land-cover information can be integrated using CLC (Level 3) in Europe, whereas other countries may rely on national datasets, if available, or alternative global products such as ESA CCI Land Cover, which provides five forest classes. Although NFI data provide the most detailed information for calibrating combustion models, differences in reference periods, biomass pools, and spatial scales can limit their comparability across countries. Variations in definitions and methodologies may introduce inconsistencies when integrating biomass data at international scales, or when comparing NFI-based estimates with model outputs107. In such cases, globally consistent datasets, such as ESA CCI Biomass products108 or harmonised forest AGB stock maps107 can be used, while carefully accounting for the uncertainties inherent in remotely sensed biomass estimates109. For example, European-wide fire disturbance data, such as those reported by Senf and Seidl (2021)110, coupled with ESA Biomass, can be used to build a dataset analogous to that employed here, even in the absence of extensive plot networks.

Hence, FIRE-TRACE’s disaggregated outputs - spanning emission hotspots, fuel type contributions, and temporal fire dynamics - offer critical value for forest management111,112, air quality regulation113–115, and GHG accounting116. FIRE-TRACE directly supports Annex I countries’ UNFCCC obligations by providing Tier 3-compliant emission estimates for integration into NIRs, replacing Tier 1 assumptions of complete combustion with dynamic severity-based burning efficiency. For SDG 13.2.2 (“Total GHG emissions per year”), the framework delivers annual, gas-specific forest fire contributions with standardised export formats for national statistical offices, facilitating real-time progress tracking toward Paris Agreement targets. Coupled with vegetation change projections, FIRE-TRACE can support scenario-based planning and long-term resilience strategies81,117, offering actionable insights at the science–policy interface.
