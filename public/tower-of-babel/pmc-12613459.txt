Impact of COVID-19 on pituitary function: a cross-sectional study in southern Iran
Authors: Setare Sheikhi, Farnaz Kamali Haghighi Shirazi, Ali Zamani, Marjan Jeddi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesCOVID-19 is associated with multiorgan dysfunction, including the endocrine system, and severe illness often involves endocrine-metabolic issues. We investigated hormonal axes in hospitalized COVID-19 patients in Iran and their correlation with disease severity, outcomes, and inflammatory biomarkers.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study with 200 hospitalized COVID-19 patients. An initial endocrinology panel assessed thyroid hormones, gonadal hormones, adrenal hormones, and prolactin levels. Disease severity was evaluated using the COVID-19 Severity Index.ResultsOf 183 patients analyzed, most had severe (21.7%) or critical (63.3%) infections. Abnormal hormone levels were common, with 26.8% having non-thyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS), 30.1% suspected of adrenal insufficiency, and no patient with subacute thyroiditis. Additionally, 95.5% of males had hypogonadism, comprising 20.5% with primary and 75% with secondary disease. However, hormonal levels and diagnoses did not significantly correlate with COVID-19 severity (P > 0.05). Deceased patients had lower T3 levels and a higher NTIS diagnosis rate (P = 0.013 and 0.012, respectively). Moreover, C-reactive protein levels were significantly higher among COVID-19 patients with adrenal insufficiency likelihood (P = 0.005) and primary or secondary hypogonadism (P = 0.018). The ESR levels were significantly higher in COVID-19 patients with NTIS (P = 0.009) and significantly lower in patients with primary or secondary hypogonadism (P = 0.033).ConclusionHospitalized COVID-19 patients exhibit hormonal abnormalities, which were correlated with inflammatory biomarkers, but these do not significantly correlate with disease severity or outcome, except for lower T3 levels and a higher NTIS diagnosis rate in deceased patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-024-01790-3.Keywords: COVID-19, Thyroid hormones, Gonadal hormones, Testosterone, Pituitary, Adrenal system, Prolactin, Severity of illness index

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic, caused by the novel coronavirus SARS-CoV-2, has caused a global health crisis, prompting extensive research into its diverse physiological effects [1, 2]. While primarily characterized as a respiratory illness, COVID-19 exhibits systemic manifestations, including the endocrine system. Previous investigations have shown the intricate association between hormonal dysregulation and disease severity among COVID-19 patients [3–5].

A pivotal aspect of this association lies in the dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, a central neuroendocrine system orchestrating stress responses and immune modulation. Studies have documented aberrant cortisol levels and impaired glucocorticoid signaling in severe COVID-19 cases, indicating the role of HPA axis dysfunction in exacerbating inflammatory responses and contributing to adverse clinical outcomes such as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and mortality [6, 7]. In addition, thyroid dysfunction has shown as a significant feature in the context of COVID-19, with reports of thyroid hormone abnormalities, including thyrotoxicosis and hypothyroidism, among affected individuals. Thyroid hormones play crucial roles in immune regulation and metabolic homeostasis, and their dysregulation may potentiate inflammatory cascades and predispose patients to severe COVID-19 complications [8, 9].

Moreover, alterations in gonadal hormones, particularly testosterone and estrogen, have directed researches for their potential implications in COVID-19 pathogenesis and clinical outcomes. Male COVID-19 patients have shown lower testosterone levels, which might contribute to disease severity and adverse prognosis, given the immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties of testosterone [10–12]. Conversely, estrogen, with its immunoregulatory effects, has been postulated to confer protective benefits in female COVID-19 patients, potentially mitigating disease severity and improving clinical outcomes [13, 14].

Despite these advancements, there remains a paucity of research focusing on the Iranian population’s hormonal responses to COVID-19. Iran has confronted a substantial burden of COVID-19 cases, characterized by diverse demographic profiles and clinical presentations [15], necessitating context-specific investigations to elucidate disease mechanisms and inform clinical management strategies. Therefore, this study was conducted to assess the prevalence and clinical significance of abnormal hormonal levels among hospitalized COVID-19 patients in Teaching Hospitals affiliated with the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (SUMS), southern Iran.

Materials and methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study enrolled two hundred eligible COVID-19 patients (100 males and 100 females) through a convenient sampling method. They were admitted to two major Teaching Hospitals (Faghihi and Namazi), affiliated with the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (SUMS), Shiraz, southern Iran, from the declaration of the pandemic state in Iran (20 February, 2020) to 23 September, 2021. Patients with disorders of the adrenal glands (i.e., Addison’s disease, Cushing syndrome), thyroid glands (i.e., hypo- and hyperthyroidism), and gonads (i.e., hypogonadism in males), or those using thyroid drugs (e.g., levothyroxine and methimazole), long-term steroids, and testosterone were excluded. All samples were obtained before initiation of in-hospital medications such as Remdesivir, Tocilizumab and systemic corticosteroids. Additionally, patients were excluded if hormonal assessments were not performed within the first 48 h of hospital admission. The study was approved by the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences Ethical Committee (code: IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1399.417). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and their data were anonymized, with no information regarding ID, name, or hospital code recorded.

Assessments

Initially, a data collection form was completed for all patients based on their on-arrival medical records. Data included age, gender, COVID-19 confirmation method (reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) or high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT)), contact history, presenting symptoms and duration, smoking history, underlying diseases, drug history, vital signs, and routine laboratory parameters. Lung involvement severity and patterns of COVID-19 pneumonia were assessed based on HRCT reports and independent radiologist re-evaluation. Disease severity was estimated using the COVID-19 Severity Index developed by Huespe et al. in which a narrative review was conducted to generate a list of possible predictors based on clinical signs and symptoms, comorbidities, laboratory and radiographic findings and then patients were divided into four risk categories based on estimated score (0–2: Low clinical risk, 3–5: Moderate clinical risk, 6–7: High clinical risk, and 8 or more: Critical) [16]. Additionally, various disease indices, including simple laboratory inflammatory biomarkers (i.e., C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), absolute lymphocytes count (ALC), and platelets count (PLT)), admission to the ward or ICU, need for mechanical ventilation or oxygen supplementation, administration of antiviral, immunosuppressant, or immunomodulator drugs, in-hospital complications, mortality rate, and hospital length of stay (LoS), were recorded for all patients.

The endocrinology panel was conducted within the first 48 h of hospital admission. Participants fasted for 8 h prior to blood tests. Thyroid function was assessed by measuring TSH, total T4, total T3, and T3RU (reuptake) levels. Basal morning cortisol levels were obtained to assess the HPA axis, and prolactin levels were measured. To evaluate the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis in males, LH, FSH, testosterone, and free testosterone levels were measured. Serum samples were prepared according to standard procedures and stored at 2–8 ℃ if assays were performed within 24 h; otherwise, aliquots were stored deep-frozen (-20 ℃). All assays were conducted according to the kits’ manual using immunoradiometric (IRMA) or radioimmunoassay (RIA) systems (ISOTOP, INSTITUTE OF ISOTOPES Ltd., Budapest), for free testosterone levels ELISA method was used. The reference numbers and normal ranges for assays are provided in supplementary Table 1.

Hypogonadism was defined as free testosterone below age specific kit reference ranges (20-39Y: 8.8 to 27 pg/ml, 40-59Y: 7.2 to 23 pg/ml, 60-80Y: 5.6 to 19 pg/ml) and/or low total testosterones (which we used cut-off 3 µg/L), and further classified as primary or secondary (central) disease based on LH levels. Non-thyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS) was defined as decreased levels of serum T3 and/or T4 without an increase in TSH secretion. Additionally, subacute thyroiditis (SAT) was defined as decreased levels of serum TSH with increased T3 and T4. Moreover, T3/T4 ratio was simply calculated by dividing T3 by T4 levels. Patients with cortisol levels less than 10 µg/dL were considered likely to have adrenal insufficiency and were recommended to undergo an ACTH stimulating test for confirmation. Patients with cortisol levels below 10 µg/dL were considered likely to have adrenal insufficiency (AI) based on criteria established by Mao et al. [17], who identified low cortisol levels in critically ill COVID-19 patients. We did not measure ACTH levels during this study, as it was not a primary objective. However, participants with suspected AI were recommended to undergo ACTH stimulation testing for further evaluation in follow-up. Notably, all hormone samples were collected upon hospital admission, prior to any medical intervention, ensuring that corticosteroid administration during the acute disease phase did not affect the cortisol levels.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics (SPSS Statistics Inc., Chicago, US) version 26.0. Descriptive statistics, including frequency (percentage) and mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range (IQR)], were used to report results. Patients were categorized into different COVID-19 severity groups and hospital outcomes categories. Hormonal assays in these groups were compared using Kruskal-Wallis, Pearson’s correlation coefficient, and Pearson’s Chi-square tests for severity groups and Wilcoxon rank-sum and Fisher’s exact tests for hospital outcomes categories. In addition, the association between inflammatory biomarkers and hormonal assays assessed using Kruskal-Wallis, Pearson’s correlation coefficient and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline demographic, medical and clinical characteristics

A total of 183 patients were included in the study, with 48.1% being male and an average age of 52 years (IQR: 39, 61). Seventeen patients were excluded due to delayed hormonal assessment beyond 48 h of hospital admission. Most patients had severe (21.7%) or critical (63.3%) infections, with the remainder classified as having mild (3.3%) or moderate (11.7%) disease. Patients with severe and critical infections had higher rates of ICU admission, need for mechanical ventilation and oxygen supplementation, receipt of antiviral, immunosuppressant or immunomodulator drugs, in-hospital complications, mortality, and longer hospital stays. Detailed patient information about past medical history, are shown in supplementary Table 2. Among the included patients, hypertension, diabetes mellitus and CVD were the most common comorbidities, present in 47 (26.1%), 32 (17.8%) and 23 (12.8) individuals, respectively. Additionally, the in-hospital outcomes (i.e., ICU admission, hospital LoS, need for oxygen supplement, need for mechanical ventilator, used medications [Remdesivir, systemic corticosteroids, tocilizumab], complications [ARDS, septic shock, AKI, DIC, PE, MI], and end-point status [discharged or deceased]), which are stratified based on the COVID-19 severity index groups, are shown in supplementary Table 3. Furthermore, we assessed the possible effects of most common comorbidities with baseline hospital hormonal assay levels, and no association was observed for almost all of the analyses (Supplementary Table 4).

Baseline hormonal assay levels

The median levels of on-arrival hormonal levels are shown in Table 1. Notably, NTIS was diagnosed in 26.8% of patients. In addition, based on the cutoff value of 10 µg/dL for probable adrenal insufficiency, 30.1% of patients were recommended to undergo an ACTH stimulating test. Furthermore, based on definitions of primary and secondary hypogonadism using total and/or free testosterone and LH levels, 20.5% and 75% of men had primary and secondary hypogonadism, respectively, representing a 95.5% hypogonadism diagnosis among men with severe or critical COVID-19.

Association of baseline hormonal assay levels with COVID-19 severity score

TSH levels were lower in moderate-to-critical COVID-19 patients; however, this was not statistically significant (P = 0.339). Likewise, quantitative levels of these hormonal parameters showed no correlation with COVID-19 severity scores (Table 2).

HPA axis

Cortisol levels showed an increasing pattern with more severe COVID-19 disease, but this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.877). Similarly, the correlation coefficient was not significant (P = 0.582). In addition, the frequency of probable cases of adrenal insufficiency did not differ significantly between various severity groups (P = 0.996) (Table 2).

Prolactin

Prolactin levels were higher among severe and critical patients compared to the mild-to-moderate patients, but this difference was also not statistically significant (P qualitative, quantitative = 0.497, 0.979) (Table 2).

Male gonadal hormones

Male sexual hormones showed no significant difference between COVID-19 severity groups (FSH: P qualitative, quantitative = 0.391, 0.320; LH: P qualitative, quantitative = 0.944, 0.630; Testosterone: P qualitative, quantitative = 0.718, 0.538; Free testosterone: P qualitative, quantitative = 0.480, 0.770). Additionally, hypogonadism diagnosis rates did not significantly differ between different severity groups (P = 0.223). It is worth noting that the association remained insignificant when low and moderate COVID-19 severity groups were aggregated (P = 0.342) (Table 2).

Association of baseline hormonal assay levels with COVID-19 hospital outcome

T3 levels and T3/T4 ratio were significantly lower among deceased patients compared to those who were discharged from the hospital (79.5 [IQR: 57.5, 98.75] vs. 101 [IQR: 80, 126]; P = 0.013 and 11.3 [IQR: 8.7, 15.3] vs. 14.8 [IQR: 11.8, 17.4]; P = 0.027, respectively). In addition, deceased patients had a higher rate of NTIS diagnosis (57.1% vs. 24.2%; P = 0.012). However, there was no statistical difference regarding TSH, T4, T3RU, cortisol, prolactin, FSH, LH, testosterone, and free testosterone levels between deceased and recovered patients (P = 0.413, 0.505, 0.526, 0.084, 0.595, 0.986, 0.320, 0.810, 0.958, respectively). Additionally, hypogonadism diagnosis rates were not statistically significant between recovered and deceased patients (P = 0.153). The association remained insignificant when primary and secondary hypogonadism groups were aggregated (P = 1.000). Moreover, probable adrenal insufficiency diagnosis rates were not statistically significant between recovered and deceased patients (P = 0.234) (Table 3).

Discussion

The current study aimed to assess the prevalence of abnormal hormone levels and their association with disease severity index and hospital outcomes in COVID-19 patients admitted to Shiraz Teaching Hospitals in southern Iran. The key findings of the study are as follows: Abnormalities in male sexual hormones were highly prevalent, with a substantial proportion of patients diagnosed with hypogonadism. In addition, NTIS was prevalent in a significant proportion of patients and SAT was diagnosed in none of the patients. Moreover, cortisol levels were elevated in a minority of patients, with a considerable proportion susceptible to adrenal insufficiency. However, except for the significantly lower T3 levels, lower T3/T4 ratio, and a significantly higher rate of NTIS observed in deceased patients, the remaining biomarkers or diagnoses did not show significant associations with the COVID-19 severity index or hospital outcomes among hospitalized COVID-19 patients. Furthermore, we found statistically significant associations for the diagnoses of adrenal insufficiency, primary or secondary hypogonadism and NTIS in COVID-19 patients with inflammatory biomarkers like ESR and C-reactive protein.

Various thyroid disorders, such as NTIS, SAT, and thyrotoxicosis, have been documented in COVID-19 patients through retrospective observational studies, prospective studies, and case reports published in the last two years [8, 18–22]. Furthermore, there have been reports of thyroid abnormalities occurring during acute SARS-CoV-2 infection or even several weeks after recovery [5]. The findings of present study showed that COVID-19 patients who deceased during their hospital stay had significantly lower T3 levels and T3/T4 ratio, as well as a higher prevalence of NTIS. Chen et al. [9] found a positive correlation between the COVID-19 prognosis and decreasing levels of TSH and fT3. Additionally, Gao et al. [23] showed that lower levels of fT3 were significantly associated with COVID-19 severity and all-cause mortality. Similar results regarding the role of T3 have been reported in other studies [18, 24–27].

Also, NTIS was found to affect 26.8% of patients in our study. The primary hypothesis suggests that NTIS is caused by a direct influence of SARS-CoV-2 on the thyroid gland, and reported as the most typical laboratory finding in mild-to-moderate COVID-19 [28]. NTIS prevalence is reported to range from 1.7 to 66.3% among hospitalized COVID-19 patients [18]. Zou et al. [21] reported that NTIS is an independent risk factor for disease severity. Zheng et al. [29] demonstrated that mild to moderate NTIS and severe NTIS were associated with a higher risk of severe outcomes compared to subclinical hypothyroidism. According to Chen et al. [9], there is a similar association between the severity of COVID-19 patients and the levels of NTIS-dependent serum T3/T4 hormones. Moreover, based on a systematic review by Çabuk et al. [18], according to thyroid function tests of more than 2,000 COVID-19 patients reported in the literature, low serum fT3 and NTIS at admission strongly predict poor outcomes in these patients. It should be noticed that in our study, no patients exhibited the combination of low TSH with high T3 and T4, suggesting that none developed SAT or thyrotoxicosis, which have been occasionally reported in COVID-19 cases.

Furthermore, we found a positive correlation between the diagnosis of NTIS and higher ESR levels. Similarly, Zou et al. [21], found that NTIS patients exhibited significantly higher levels of ESR and CRP. Also, there was a significant inverse correlation between T3 levels and ESR levels (P = 0.010) in our study, which was in accordance with the study conducted by Lui et al. [30] found an inverse relationship between the ESR and the FT3/FT4, suggesting the impact of systemic inflammation on deiodinase activity. However, we could not find any association between CRP and either NTIS or thyroid function test parameters, which was in contrast with the findings reported by Campi et al. [25] or Zou et al. [21].

We found that although the COVID-19 patients who had a more severe illness or those who deceased during hospitalization had the higher levels of cortisol, these differences were not statistically significant. Previous studies have shown a dissociation between ACTH and cortisol in COVID-19 patients, where cortisol production becomes ACTH-independent, likely due to elevated cytokine levels seen in severe cases [31]. Additionally, reduced peripheral cortisol metabolism contributes to higher cortisol levels and subsequent ACTH feedback inhibition [32]. On the other hand, in severe COVID-19 cases, there is a risk of developing critical illness-related corticosteroid insufficiency (CIRCI) [17], characterized by inadequate cortisol secretion to regulate inflammation, similar to what is observed in other critically ill patients [33]. In this respect, we found that 30.1% of patients were susceptible for adrenal insufficiency and recommended to perform ACTH stimulating test to confirm this diagnosis. Additionally, the probable cases of adrenal insufficiency had a significantly higher C-reactive protein levels in our patients. Various research teams have examined the link between cortisol levels and COVID-19 severity, yielding conflicting results [34]. For example, Ahmadi et al. [35], found lower cortisol levels in deceased COVID-19 patients and those with more severe illness, while Tan et al. [7], Güven and Gültekin [36], and Das et al. [37] reported the opposite trend. Overall, the existing body of evidence does not consistently indicate a clear connection between severe COVID-19 and either decreased or increased cortisol levels. During the acute phase of severe COVID-19, there is typically an observed increase in serum cortisol within the first 48 h of admission [38]. Conversely, critical illness can lead to a CIRCI, which results from physiological stress suppressing the HPA axis [39].

We also observed that 95.5% of severe or critical COVID-19 male patients had the evidence of hypogonadism, comprising 20.5% and 75% of primary and secondary hypogonadism, respectively. In accordance, this is shown that COVID-19 males who were diagnosed with hypogonadism had higher odds, up to 2.4, than their counterparts of being hospitalized [11]. In addition, a high proportion of hospitalized COVID-19 males may show low levels of testosterone on arrival to the admitting ward, for example 89% in Dhindsa et al. study [12].

However, we observed that diagnosis of hypogonadism, as well as single levels of testosterone, free testosterone, FSH, and LH did not show a significant association with COVID-19 severity or outcomes. Opinions on whether testosterone improves or worsens COVID-19 severity have varied [40]. Testosterone is involved in regulating ACE2 and TMPRSS2 expression, potentially influencing SARS-CoV-2 internalization. Prior to the pandemic, low testosterone levels have been linked to obesity and advanced age, which both are associated with a higher risk of COVID-19 complications [41, 42]. Kadihasanoglu et al. [43], study found significantly lower testosterone levels in COVID-19 patients compare to patients with non-COVID respiratory disease and age-matched controls, with a majority meeting the criteria for testosterone deficiency, and lower levels were linked to longer hospital stays. In contrast with our result, Abedi et al. [44] showed that deceased COVID-19 patients were older and had significantly lower total testosterone levels. In addition, Cinislioglu et al. [45], reported that severe COVID-19 patients and those who did not survive had significantly lower serum testosterone levels compared to mild-to-moderate COVID-19 patients and survivors, respectively. Similar results were also reported by other studies [46–48].

Notably, we also found that the presence of hypogonadism in COVID-19 patients was significantly associated with higher CRP levels and lower ESR levels. Low testosterone levels have been associated with elevated inflammatory markers, including IL-6, C-reactive protein, IL-1 receptor antagonist, hepatocyte growth factor, and interferon c-inducible protein 10, potentially explaining why severe disease correlates with lower testosterone levels [49].

In general, emerging evidence suggests a potential bidirectional relationship between COVID-19 and pituitary function. Hypopituitarism may increase susceptibility to COVID-19 due to associated metabolic derangements, such as diabetes and obesity, and impaired hormonal regulation. Additionally, SARS-CoV-2 may directly or indirectly damage the pituitary gland, potentially precipitating events like pituitary apoplexy, particularly in patients with pre-existing adenomas. Although our study did not directly assess pituitary dysfunction, the findings of elevated cortisol levels in some patients and significant associations of adrenal insufficiency with inflammatory markers align with previous reports on COVID-19 impacts on endocrine function, warranting further investigation into its effects on the pituitary gland [50].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we used RIA/IRMA for hormonal assays, which might differ in sensitivity and specificity compared to modern assay techniques like CLIA and ECLIA since these methods are improved in accuracy, particularly in the measurement of free testosterone, cortisol, and other key hormones. Therefore, it is possible that the hormone levels in our study might differ slightly from those obtained using more advanced methods, potentially affecting the generalizability of our findings. Second, while we assessed a wide range of hormones, other important endocrine factors, such as parathyroid hormone, insulin, and growth hormone, were not evaluated. Including a broader spectrum of endocrine markers could yield a more comprehensive understanding of the hormonal dysregulation caused by COVID-19. Third, the cross-sectional nature of the study limited our ability to infer causality between hormonal alterations and disease outcomes. Longitudinal studies are needed to better establish the temporal relationship between endocrine dysfunction and COVID-19 prognosis. Finally, the study was conducted in a single center in southern Iran, which might limit the external validity of our results, particularly in populations with different demographics, comorbidities, and healthcare settings. Larger multicenter studies are required to confirm the reproducibility of these findings across diverse patient populations.

Conclusion

The study found that hospitalized COVID-19 patients exhibited various hormonal abnormalities, which were correlated with inflammatory biomarkers; however, these hormonal disturbances did not significantly correlate with COVID-19 severity or outcome, except for lower T3 levels and a higher NTIS diagnosis rate in deceased patients compared to those discharged.
