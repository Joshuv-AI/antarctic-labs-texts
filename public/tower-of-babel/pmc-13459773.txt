Antimicrobial resistance and genomic characteristics of nontyphoidal Salmonella strains from diarrheal patients in Shandong, China
Authors: Ruyue Fan, Peibin Hou, Bin Hu, Ying Yang, Na Sun, Dandi Li, Yuan Gao, Hao Song, Wenjuan Liu, Tingting Miao, Kai Guo, Weiwei Li, Yunchang Guo, Shaofei Yan, Xinpeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNontyphoidal Salmonella (NTS) is a major cause of foodborne illness worldwide. This study aimed to determine the antimicrobial susceptibilities of clinical NTS isolates from Shandong, China, and to characterize the genomic determinants of antimicrobial resistance and virulence genotypes using whole-genome sequencing (WGS).MethodsA total of 125 NTS strains were isolated and identified from diarrhea patients in Shandong, China. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed for all isolates. Antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs), virulence factor genes (VFGs), and plasmid replicons, serotypes, sequence types (STs) were determined through WGS analysis.ResultsThe NTS isolates in this study showed high resistance to ampicillin (AMP, 73.6%), tetracycline (TET, 62.4%), and chloramphenicol (CHL, 56.8%), among them the multidrug resistance (MDR) rate was 59.2%. In total, 28 serotypes, and 30 STs were identified, with ST34 serotype 1,4,[5],12:i:- being the most prevalent. Genomic analysis revealed 63 ARGs and 171 VFGs across all isolates. Type VI secretion system-associated genes were identified in Salmonella Liverpool (S. Liverpool). Plasmid replicons, especially Col(pHAD28), pKPC-CAV1321, and IncHI2, were more frequently present among MDR isolates. Among dominant serotypes, S. Enteritidis carried the highest number of VFGs but relatively few ARGs compared with other dominant serotypes.ConclusionThese findings indicate a substantial MDR burden and diverse genomic features among clinical NTS isolates in Shandong, China. The deep understanding of resistance and virulence characteristics from NTS genome can be quite supportive for continuous surveillance and antimicrobial stewardship.

Introduction

As one of the main causes of foodborne illness and diarrheal disease worldwide, NTS infections are a serious public health concern. According to estimates from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, NTS infections were responsible for 215,000 deaths globally in 2019 [1]. Although salmonellosis is often self-limiting, it can lead to extraintestinal disease, especially in vulnerable populations such as infants, young children, and immunocompromised individuals [2]. In China, NTS is a key pathogen targeted by foodborne disease surveillance and is frequently reported as a leading bacterial cause of human gastrointestinal infection [3, 4]. Previous studies have shown that salmonellosis poses a substantial public health burden in China, with an estimated foodborne salmonellosis disease burden of 245 cases per 100,000 population [5], and a prevalence of 8.19% in northern China [3]. These findings highlight the importance of continuous monitoring of clinical NTS isolates. The genus Salmonella comprises more than 2600 serotypes, among which a subset predominates in clinical settings, and the burden of NTS infection has been increasing [6, 7]. Nationwide surveillance in China indicates that Salmonella Enteritidis (S. Enteritidis), Salmonella Typhimurium (S. Typhimurium), and the monophasic Salmonella serotype 1,4 [5],12:i:- are among the most common causes of NTS infections [8]. The persistence and increasing prevalence of these serotypes underscore the need for sustained surveillance to better understand transmission dynamics and to implement appropriate control measures.

MDR in NTS has become an escalating public health threat, complicating treatment, and increasing the risk of adverse clinical outcomes [9, 10]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has highlighted third-generation cephalosporin-resistant and fluoroquinolone-resistant Salmonella as priority pathogens, and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales are listed as critical priorities [11]. Studies in China reported an overall antimicrobial resistance (AMR) rate of approximately 73% among NTS isolates, with high resistance to tetracycline, penicillins, and quinolones, and lower resistance to carbapenems and macrolides [3].Resistance in NTS is primarily driven by specific genes often found on mobile genetic elements. For example, β-lactamase genes blaCTX−M−15 and blaCTX−M−55, tetracycline resistance gene tet(A), aminoglycoside resistance genes aac(6’)-Iaa and aph(3’)-Ib, fluoroquinolone resistance genes qnr, gyrA, and parC, and the carbapenemase gene blaNDM−5 have been identified in certain strains [8, 12–15].These findings point out the urgent need to better understand the genetic mechanisms underlying antimicrobial resistance.

The epidemiological success of NTS is closely linked to its virulence profile and the mobile genetic elements it carried [16]. NTS harbors numerous VFGs, many of which are located within Salmonella pathogenicity islands (SPIs) that mediate host cell adhesion, invasion, and systemic infection [17]. Type III secretion system (T3SS) encoded by SPI-1 and SPI-2 are central to NTS pathogenesis [18]. Plasmids are essential for the horizontal gene transfer of both ARGs and VFGs, and the genomic architecture of NTS is highly adaptable, making it easier for ARGs and VFGs to be acquired and spread [19]. Several plasmid types, including IncA/C, IncFII(S) and IncHI2, have been identified as significant vectors for resistance, facilitating the dissemination of genes that confer resistance to multiple antibiotic classes [20, 21]. Genomic characterization is therefore essential for tracing the evolution and spread of virulent and resistant NTS clones.

Given the dynamic emergence of MDR NTS and the continual appearance of new clonal complexes with distinct genetic profiles, continuous localized surveillance is required [22]. In this study, we provided a detailed antimicrobial resistance phenotypic and genotypic characterization of NTS isolates from patients with diarrhea in Shandong, China, in 2024. We investigated the prevalence of key serotypes and identified the genetic determinants of ARGs, VFGs, and plasmids in NTS isolates. These data improve understanding of local NTS molecular epidemiology and support public health interventions and clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolates and identification

In this study, a total of 125 NTS strains were recovered in 2024 from fecal specimens of sporadic diarrhea patients presenting with foodborne or suspected foodborne infection in 12 sentinel hospitals across 8 cities in Shandong Province, China (Fig. 1A). All isolates were placed in separate sterile tubes and transported to the laboratory at the Shandong CDC under refrigeration at 4 °C. The isolates were initially identified as Salmonella enterica using the MALDI Biotyper Sirius IVD (Bruker Daltonics GmbH & Co. KG, Germany) and the VITEK® 2 Compact system (BioMérieux, France). Isolates were subsequently classified as nontyphoidal Salmonella based on serotyping results, with exclusion of typhoidal serovars such as S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

AST was conducted using the broth microdilution method, and minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for 10 antibiotics were determined via broth microdilution, including ampicillin (AMP), azithromycin (AZM), ceftriaxone (CTX), chloramphenicol (CHL), ciprofloxacin (CIP), ertapenem (ETP), imipenem (IPM), meropenem (MEM), TET, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (SXT). The antimicrobial susceptibilities of NTS isolates were classified as sensitive, intermediate, or resistant according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, M100-A34) breakpoints. The reference strain for quality control was E. coli strain ATCC 25922.

Whole-genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted with the TaKaRa MiniBEST Bacterial Genomic DNA Extraction Kit. To obtain the draft genomes of NTS isolates, library preparation and whole-genome sequencing were performed at Smartgenomics Technology Co., Ltd., Tianjin, using the Illumina NovaSeq (PE150) platform. SOAPdenovo v2.04, SPAdes v3.15.4 and AbySS v2.1.5 were used for the initial assembly process. Then, GapCloser v1.12 was used to refine the assembly. The species identification was reconfirmed using the SpeciesFinder 2.0 on the Centre for Genomic Epidemiology (CGE) website.

Serotyping

To determine the serotype of each strain, SeqSero2 software (v1.3.1, https://github.com/denglab/SeqSero2) was first used for in silico serotype prediction [23]. Subsequently, traditional serotyping was performed by slide agglutination using commercial Salmonella O and H antisera (SSI Diagnostica, Hillerød, Denmark) according to the Kauffmann-White scheme. The antigenic formula obtained was then interpreted using the Kauffmann-White scheme to assign the serotype.

MLST

MLST was conducted using the MLST database on the public databases for molecular typing and microbial genome diversity (PubMLST) website (https://pubmlst.org/bigsdb?db=pubmlst_Salmonella_seqdef, accessed on 27 May 2024). The analysis was based on seven housekeeping genes: aroC, dnaN, hemD, hisD, purE, sucA, and thrA. A minimum spanning tree of NTS isolates was generated using PHYLOVIZ Online (https://online.phyloviz.net/index, accessed on 27 May 2024).

Screening of ARGs and VFGs

ARGs were identified using ABRicate v1.0.1 with a customized resistance gene database generated by merging the CARD and ResFinder databases, followed by redundancy removal and re-screening to improve annotation comprehensiveness and consistency. VFGs were annotated using the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) through ABRicate. The thresholds for sequence identity and coverage were both set at 80%.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic analysis was performed based on core genome single-nucleotide polymorphisms (cgSNPs). Briefly, draft genomes were first annotated using Prokka (v1.14.6), and the resulting files were used for pan-genome analysis with Roary (v3.13.0) to generate a core gene alignment. The alignment was then processed and cleaned, followed by recombination filtering using Gubbins to remove putative recombinant regions. Core SNP sites were subsequently extracted from the recombination-filtered alignment using snp-sites, generating the final cgSNP alignment for downstream analysis. A maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed from this cgSNP alignment using FastTree (v2.2.0). The phylogenetic relationships of the NTS isolates were visualized using R software (v4.5.0).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (v4.5.0). Statistical differences of the antimicrobial susceptibility, VFGs, and ARGs of isolates from different serotypes were calculated using Fisher’s exact test or the Mann-Whitney U test. A two-sided P value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, the multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) index was calculated for each isolate as a/b, where a is the number of antibiotics to which the isolate was resistant and b is the total number of antibiotics tested.

Results

Antimicrobial resistance profiles

Of the 125 NTS isolates, 97 isolates were resistant to at least one antibiotic, and the MDR rate was 59.2% (74/125). The Sankey plot showed a widespread prevalence and high diversity of NTS during distribution analysis of sampling demographics (patient age and gender), NTS isolate serotype and MDR pattern (Fig. 1B). The drug resistance rate for isolates to AMP (73.6%) has the highest drug resistance rate, followed by TET (62.4%), CHL (56.8%), SXT (45.6%), CTX (28.0%), CIP (26.4%) and AZM (12.8%) (Fig. 1C). None of the isolates were resistant to ETP, IPM, and MEM. One isolate exhibited intermediate susceptibility to ETP. In 97 isolates, 25 types of antibiotic resistance were identified. The resistance type was commonly observed in 26 isolates conferring resistance to four classes of antibiotics (either AMP-CHL-TET-SXT or AMP/CTX-CHL-TET-SXT). Notably, the distribution of MDR and non-MDR strains in S. Enteritidis groups showed significant differences when compared to the other three serotype groups (1,4 [5],12:i:-, S. Typhimurium and S. Goldcoast) (P < 0.05). In the S. Enteritidis group, the number of non-MDR strains was higher than that of MDR strains (Fig. 1B). The MAR index of the 125 NTS isolates ranged from 0.0 to 0.7, with a mean of 0.306 and a median of 0.4. Overall, 59.2% of the isolates had a MAR index greater than 0.2.

Serotyping and antimicrobial sensitivity of NTS isolates

As shown in Fig. 1D, a total of 28 serotypes were identified by WGS prediction and slide agglutination tests in 125 NTS isolates. The dominant serotype was 1,4 [5],12:i:- (32.0%), followed by S. Enteritidis (12.0%), S. Typhimurium (10.4%) and S. Goldcoast (6.4%). There were 3 main serotypes in 74 MDR isolates, including 1,4 [5],12:i:- (44.6%), S. Typhimurium (12.2%) and S. Goldcoast (8.1%). Among the four dominant serotypes (1,4 [5],12:i:-, S. Enteritidis, S. Goldcoast, and S. Typhimurium), the resistance rate of S. Enteritidis to CHL, SXT, and TET was lower than that of the other three serotypes. Additionally, only S. Enteritidis isolates exhibited resistance to AZM. In contrast, the resistance rate of S. Goldcoast to CIP and CTX was higher than that of the other three serotypes, while the resistance rate of S. Typhimurium to AMP was lower than that of the other three serotypes.

Analysis of antibiotic resistance genes

A total of 63 ARGs were identified among NTS isolates. Specifically, 100.0% and 55.2% of the isolates harbored the aminoglycoside resistance gene aac(6’) and floR. The floR and sul2 genes, which confer resistance to phenicols and sulfonamides, respectively, were found in 55.2% and 50.4% of the isolates. Additionally, 50.4% of the isolates were found to possess the blaTEM−1B gene, conferring resistance to β-lactam antibiotics. The harboring rates of the remaining ARGs were below 50.0%. Among them, the harboring rates of quinolone, tetracycline and macrolide resistance genes were the highest for qnrS1 (35.2%), tet(A) (28.8%) and mph(A) (10.4%), respectively (Fig. 2). One isolate exhibited intermediate susceptibility to ETP and carried blaNDM. The carriage of ARGs varied among the different serotype isolates. For example, one isolate sd24S272 belonged to serotype S. Indiana, harbored the maximum number of resistance genes (13) observed in the study., encompassing up to 6 different classes of antibiotics. Isolates carrying 10 and 11 resistance genes were found in S. Montevideo and S. Kedougou serotypes. The prevalence of ARGs differed across dominant serotype isolates, with S. Enteritidis carrying the fewest number of ARGs compared to other serotypes (Fig. 3A). Overall, the distribution of major ARGs was broadly consistent with the observed resistance phenotypes. In particular, the high prevalence of blaTEM−1B, floR, sul2, and tet(A) generally corresponded to the high resistance rates to AMP, CHL, SXT, and TET, respectively, whereas ciprofloxacin resistance appeared to be only partially explained by the presence of qnrS1.

Sequence types

A total of 30 different STs were identified in 125 NTS isolates. The most prevalent STs were ST34 (29.6%), ST11 (12.0%), ST19 (10.4%), and ST358 (6.4%). Additionally, the STs of two isolates remained undetermined, as confirmed through the PubMLST database. Among the NTS isolates, there was a correlation between STs and serotypes, except for the serotypes 1,4 [5],12:i:- and S. Montevideo, which exhibited multiple STs (Fig. 2).

Analysis of virulence factor genes

A total of 171 VFGs were identified among NTS isolates. Among them, 108 VFGs were carried by all isolates and are mainly involved in invasion-related genes, secretion system-related genes, and pili-related genes. The harboring rate of 63 VFGs was 0.8%−99.2%. In the NTS isolates, these genes were classified into 10 VFG classes, including secretion system (16), fimbrial adherence determinants (11), magnesium uptake (9), toxin (8), non-fimbrial adherence determinants (7), serum resistance (5), macrophage inducible gene (2), stress adaptation (2), invasion (2), and polysaccharide synthesis (1) (Fig. 4). Among them, the virulence genes invG (invasion gene), mig-14 (macrophage inducible gene), and the secretion system genes (espO1-1, orgA, orgC) were found to have a high prevalence rate of 99.2%. The VFGs exhibited greater similarity within the same serotype. The tssD2, tssI, tssJ, tssK, tssL and tssM genes (encoding components of the type VI secretion system, T6SS) were predominantly found in S. Agona and S. Liverpool. Similarly, the spv operon genes (spvB, spvC and spvD) and pef operon genes (pefA, pefB, pefC and pefD) were mainly detected in S. Enteritidis and S. Typhimurium. The toxic virulence genes entE and gogB were primarily identified in serotype S. Kentucky and serotype 1,4 [5],12:i:-, predominantly. The prevalence of VFGs differed across dominant serotype isolates, with S. Enteritidis carrying the most number of VFGs (Fig. 3B).

Phylogenetic analysis

The phylogenetic tree based on recombination-filtered core genome single-nucleotide polymorphisms (cgSNPs) demonstrated substantial genetic diversity among the 125 NTS isolates (Fig. 2). A total of 30 branches were formed. Strains from the same serotype and ST were closer in evolutionary branches and showed close genetic relationships. With respect to most serotypes, the 1,4 [5],12:i:- and S. Typhimurium genomes were close to each other and shared a common basal node. On the other side of the tree, S. Enteritidis shared another basal node.

Analysis of plasmids

A total of 38 plasmids were detected in all NTS isolates, including 11 Col plasmids, 24 Inc plasmids, and 3 unique plasmids (p0111, pENTAS02, pKPC-CAV1321) (Fig. 5). Among these plasmids, Col(pHAD28) (39.2%), pKPC-CAV1321 (20.8%), and IncHI2 (20.8%) were the most common. Notably, no plasmids were found in the serotypes S. Cannstatt, S. Muenster, and S. Oranienburg. The highest diversity of plasmid types was observed in strains of serotype 1,4 [5],12:i:-, with 21 different types identified. Notably, MDR isolates carried 32 plasmids, among which Col(pHAD28) (47.3%), pKPC-CAV1321 (35.1%) and IncHI2 (35.1%) were the most prevalent. These plasmids were predominantly present in strains of serotype 1,4 [5],12:i:-, followed by S. Goldcoast.

Discussion

This study provided a comprehensive characterization of antimicrobial resistance of NTS isolates in Shandong, China, demonstrating a high prevalence of MDR isolates. The MAR index analysis further supported the high prevalence of multidrug resistance among these clinical NTS isolates. This is consistent with global trends of increasing antimicrobial resistance in Salmonella, particularly in clinical settings. Resistance to first-line antibiotics (e.g., AMP, TET, CHL) was notably high, reflecting the selective pressure exerted by the overuse and misuse of these antibiotics [23, 24]. The emergence of resistance to third-generation cephalosporin CTX and fluoroquinolone CIP have been observed, a in this study. These antimicrobial agents are frequently considered the last line of defense against severe Salmonella infections. The development of resistance to these antibiotics poses a significant threat to their clinical treatment efficacy [25]. In contrast, no isolates were resistant to carbapenems (ETP, IPM, and MEM) with only one blaNDM-carrying isolate showed intermediate susceptibility to ETP, suggested that carbapenemase-producing NTS are not yet widespread in patients. One of the most common MDR pattern AMP-CHL-TET-SXT is frequently reported worldwide and is often associated with the presence of mobile genetic elements, which can carry multiple resistance genes [26]. Given the documented burden of foodborne salmonellosis in China reported by previous surveillance studies, the genomic and antimicrobial resistance characteristics identified in this study provide valuable evidence for regional public health surveillance and risk assessment [3, 5].

Considerable serotype diversity was found among the NTS isolates.The serotype distribution illustrated that a small number of serotypes account for most human Salmonella illness caused by Salmonella spp. S. Typhimurium and its monophasic variant are widely known for their global prevalence, and are leading causes of invasive NTS illness, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa [27]. Our study also found a higher non-MDR prevalence in the S. Enteritidis group compared to other prevalent serotypes. Given that other studies have identified significant frequencies of MDR in this serotype, it is especially intriguing that this serotype exhibited a higher prevalence of non-MDR strains [28, 29]. This result implies that the resistance profiles of certain Salmonella serotypes may be significantly shaped by the regional epidemiological setting and selective factors.

The genomic analysis further revealed the high genetic diversity of NTS isolates, with different STs identified. The most prevalent STs were ST34, ST11, ST19, and ST358. Particularly noteworthy is the preponderance of ST34, which is commonly associated with serotype 1,4 [5],12:i:- [15, 21, 30]. While ST19 is an important lineage of S. Typhimurium [31], ST11 is a known lineage of S. Enteritidis that has caused a worldwide pandemic of egg-associated infections [32, 33]. The discovery of several STs within the same serotype (e.g., 1,4 [5],12:i:- and S. Montevideo) suggested a complex and polyclonal population structure, rather than a single epidemic clone. Phylogenetic analysis emphasized these findings, where the phylogenetic tree showed strong genetic kinship of strains belonging to the same serotype and ST. Given that the monophasic variation is known to have developed from S. Typhimurium, the close evolutionary link between serotypes 1,4 [5],12:i:-, and S. Typhimurium is biologically significant [34]. The distinct genotypic and phenotypic traits of S. Enteritidis are likely influenced by its separate basal node in the phylogenetic tree, as it is a member of a distinct phylogenetic group. This finding is in line with research on other Salmonella serotypes [35, 36].

The genomic analysis of ARGs provides a molecular basis for the observed phenotypic resistance. The high prevalence of blaTEM, which confers resistance to β-lactam antibiotics, is a direct genetic explanation for the high rate of AMP resistance. Similarly, the high prevalence of floR and sul2 genes and the tetracycline resistance gene tet(A) aligned well with the high phenotypic resistance rates to CHL, SXT, and TET. Previous studies have also highlighted the presence of blaTEM and sul2 in animal-derived Salmonella, which demonstrates the importance of surveillance in all the food production chain [37]. It is noteworthy that 34.7% of the isolates had the plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance gene qnrS1, which can facilitate the acquisition of higher-level quinolone resistance and poses a serious risk to the effectiveness of fluoroquinolone therapies. These results are in line with earlier studies that discovered a varied resistome in isolates of Salmonella [4]. A nationwide study showed on meropenem resistance in serotype 1,4 [5],12:i:- isolates containing multiple resistance genes [8]. In our study, the carbapenem resistance gene blaNDM was detected in one strain of serotype 1,4 [5],12:i:-, which showed intermediate resistance to ETP. Although blaNDM was not yet widespread in patients, it raised some concerns.

The analysis of VFGs revealed a high number of genes related to invasion, secretion systems, and pili. A core set of VFGs were carried by all isolates, including those involved in the Salmonella SPIs-encoded T3SS, which is crucial for host cell invasion [15]. The virulence gene profiles exhibited greater similarity within the same sequence types, further supporting the clonal nature of these isolates and their shared pathogenic characteristics [4, 15, 32]. A key finding is the serotype specific VFG profiles. In our study, the genes encoding the core components of the T6SS were identified in S. Liverpool for the first time. It has been previously reported that the genus Salmonella harbors five T6SSs encoded within pathogenicity islands SPI-6, SPI-19, SPI-20, SPI-21, and SPI-22. These are present in strains of serotypes such as S. Typhimurium, S. Dublin, S. Weltevreden, S. Agona, and S. bongori [38]. Because T6SS gene clusters are differentially distributed among distinct serotypes of Salmonella, the functionality and specific role of the T6SS in S. Liverpool isolates need to be further studied. Moreover, the spv and pef operon genes, which are associated with systemic infections, were predominantly detected in S. Enteritidis and S. Typhimurium. This finding is consistent with the high virulence of these serotypes [31, 39]. Interestingly, this study found that S. Enteritidis carried the most VFGs but the fewest ARGs compared to other dominant serotypes. Interestingly, S. Enteritidis carried the most VFGs but the fewest ARGs compared with other dominant serotypes. This inverse relationship may reflect a trade-off between virulence and resistance or distinct evolutionary pressures acting on these traits across Salmonella lineages, and further investigation is needed.

The analysis of plasmids revealed a high diversity of replicon types. Plasmids are critical mobile genetic elements that facilitate the horizontal transfer of ARGs and VFGs, contributing significantly to the dissemination of resistance and virulence traits [29, 40]. The prevalence of replicon types like IncHI2 and IncHI2A is particularly concerning, as these are known to be large plasmids that often carry a wide array of resistance genes, including those for extended-spectrum β-lactamases [21, 41]. The strong association between MDR strains and the presence of specific plasmid replicons, including pKPC-CAV1321, IncHI2, and IncHI2A, provided a clear molecular link between plasmid carriage and the MDR phenotype. The highest diversity of plasmid types was observed in serotype 1,4 [5],12:i:-, further supporting its emerging and highly adaptable nature [8, 30]. A key observation is that no plasmids were detected in the serotypes S. Cannstatt, S. Muenster, and S. Oranienburg strains; however, the S. Muenster isolate were MDR despite the absence of detectable plasmids, whereas the other two serotypes were susceptible to the tested antimicrobials. This finding suggests that plasmid carriage contributes to antimicrobial resistance in NTS, but MDR can also be mediated by chromosomal determinants and/or non-plasmid mobile genetic elements [42–44].

We acknowledge several limitations of our study. First, the isolates were obtained from sentinel hospitals in only 8 of the 16 prefecture-level cities in Shandong Province, which limited the geographic representativeness of our findings. Second, only 125 isolates collected in 2024 were included, and thus this study provides a cross-sectional overview rather than a long-term epidemiological analysis. A larger multi-year dataset would improve the robustness of trend analysis and detection of less common lineages. Third, plasmid analysis was performed using short-read WGS data only, which limited complete plasmid reconstruction and precise characterization of the genomic context of resistance genes. Future studies using long-read sequencing and hybrid assembly are warranted.

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive genomic characterization of NTS isolates, revealing a diverse population structure with significant levels of antimicrobial resistance. The high MDR rate and the prevalence of specific ARGs and plasmids, such as blaTEM and IncHI2/IncHI2A, are major public health concerns. This study also highlights the serotype-specific differences in both AMR and virulence gene profiles, suggesting distinct evolutionary and epidemiological dynamics for different Salmonella lineages. The strong correlation between genotypic (ARGs, VFGs, plasmids, STs) and phenotypic (antimicrobial resistance, serotype) characteristics underscores the importance of a holistic genomic approach for understanding the epidemiology of NTS.
