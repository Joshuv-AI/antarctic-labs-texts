Proximal junctional kyphosis in distraction-based growth-friendly treatment: Definition, risk factors, and timeline
Authors: Ataberk Beydemir, Rafik Ramazanov, Mehmet Berktas, Mehmet Ayvaz, Halil Gokhan Demirkiran, Muharrem Yazici
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo identify risk factors and characterize the timing and nature of proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK) following dual-rod distraction-based growth-friendly treatment (DB-GFT) in early-onset scoliosis (EOS).MethodsNinety-five EOS patients (37 male, 58 female) treated with dual growing rod systems between 2004 and 2023, with ≥ 2 years of follow-up, were retrospectively reviewed at a single center. Radiographic parameters were assessed preoperatively, postoperatively, and at final follow-up. PJK was defined as a proximal junctional angle(PJA) ≥10° at final follow-up with an increase of ≥10° compared with preoperative measurement. Serial lateral radiographs were evaluated at 3 or 6 month intervals to determine occurrence and timing of PJK. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify risk factors.ResultsThe mean age at index surgery was 6.8 years, with a mean follow-up of 5.8 years. PJK developed in 17 patients (17.9%): 3 immediately postoperatively(mechanical) and 14 gradually (biological), of whom 12 were diagnosed after the first year. Univariate analysis identified UIV at T3 or below and T5–T12 kyphosis correction ≥ 30° as significant risk factors. Each 1° increase in preoperative T5–T12 kyphosis increased PJK risk by 3%. Multivariate analysis confirmed preoperative T2–T12 kyphosis ≥45° and UIV at T3 or below as independent risk factors.ConclusionsWhile extensive kyphosis correction was identified as a significant risk factor on univariate analysis, preoperative T2–T12 kyphosis ≥45° and UIV at T3 or below emerged as independent predictors, underscoring the importance of both preoperative sagittal alignment and UIV selection in surgical planning. Segment-adjusted PJA values may better identify clinically significant cases at risk of progression to proximal junctional failure. Introduction

Early onset scoliosis (EOS) refers to any spinal deformity, regardless of its etiology, that is identified before the age of 10. 1 Given their risk of progression and the possibility of life-threatening outcomes (i.e. thoracic insufficiency syndrome), which can lower quality of life if treatment is not given, they merit special attention.2,3 Although the deformity can be corrected by instrumented fusion procedures carried out prior to lung maturation, such interventions may result in iatrogenic issues by shortening the trunk, particularly in the thoracic spine. 4 This truncal shortening may compromise thoracic growth and chest wall development, thereby contributing to impaired pulmonary function and thoracic insufficiency syndrome. Therefore, the most appropriate type of treatment should be surgery that allows spinal growth while partially correcting the deformity and preventing progression of the deformity. Distraction-based (DB) approaches, which are commonly referred to as growing rod (GR) methods, are among the most commonly used strategies and are effective in controlling deformities and preserving growth. 5 However, they are still controversial due to the need for repeated operations and likelihood of complications.6,7

Proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK) is one of the most common problems with distraction-based growth-friendly treatment (DB-GFT) that has not been completely resolved yet. An increase of more than 10° in sagittal angulation between the uppermost instrumented vertebra and the second vertebra above it between pre and final follow-up X-rays is referred to as PJK. 8 Proximal junctional failure (PJF) refers to circumstances that require revision surgery when PJK develops in the corresponding segment as a result of fractures, implant failure, or other issues.

Despite the fact that numerous studies regarding the causes and incidence of this complication have been published, there is still debate over its definition, etiology, and measures to prevent it. 9 The goals in the present study were to determine the risk factors influencing this complication and the incidence of PJK in patients treated with DB-GFT at the same institution utilizing the same standardized surgical technique by the same surgical team. The timing of PJK’s emergence, the rate of this angle increase turning into proximal junctional failure (PJF), and potential etiological variables were investigated, and possible prevention strategies examined. Therefore, the aim of this study was to identify independent risk factors for PJK and to characterize its timing and pattern in patients with early-onset scoliosis treated with dual-rod DB-GFT.

Methods

This was a single-center, retrospective study carried out after approval by the Institutional Ethics Committee, with informed consent waived due to its retrospective design. Patients with EOS who had been managed with dual growing rod (GR) systems from March 2004 until May 2023 were included. All surgical procedures were performed by the same surgical team using the same standardized technique. Patients who fulfilled the following requirements were included: they had to have been diagnosed with EOS, treated using the dual GR technique, undergone at least two distraction procedures at intervals of 3 or 6 months, and had a minimum of 2 years of clinical and radiological follow-up.

In patients whose chronological age exceeded 10 years at the time of index surgery, skeletal age was confirmed to be below 10 years by Greulich-Pyle atlas assessment, and surgical indication was determined based on skeletal maturity, curve progression, and trunk height rather than chronological age alone. Patients with missing or poor quality preoperative, postoperative, or final follow-up anteroposterior (AP) and lateral radiographs, or those treated with GRs for isolated kyphotic deformity were excluded. A total of 95 patients were included in the final analysis.

Demographic and medical details were gathered from institutional charts, including sex, index surgery age, follow-up interval, and EOS etiology. Surgical parameters included index surgery date, number and date of distractions, implant type (conventional GRs or magnetically controlled GRs), use of transverse connector, type of anchor utilized at the proximal foundation, upper instrumented vertebra (UIV) level, and lower instrumented vertebra (LIV) level, along with the most tilted upper instrumented vertebra level after index surgery (MT-UIV). The patients were further divided into subgroups with respect to UIV level (T2 and proximal vs. T3 and distal) as well as with respect to LIV level (L3 and proximal vs. L4 and distal) in an effort to evaluate their conceivable association with PJK incidence. The complete spine, preoperative, postoperative, and final follow-up examinations were analyzed with respect to utilizing a picture archiving and communication system (PACS). The following parameters were measured: major coronal Cobb angle, T5-12 thoracic kyphosis, T2–T12 thoracic kyphosis, L1–S1 lumbar lordosis, and sacral slope, along with the sagittal vertical axis (SVA). The proximal junctional angle (PJA) was determined using the angle between the caudal endplate of the UIV and the cephalad endplate of the second vertebra cranial to UIV. PJK was defined as a PJA ≥10° at final follow-up with an increase of ≥10° compared with preoperative measurement. 8 The patients were grouped into PJK and non-PJK categories based on these criteria. While this classification was based on final follow-up radiographs, the onset timing of PJK was additionally determined by reviewing serial radiographs obtained following each lengthening procedure in relation to the preoperative PJA, without altering the final classification. Additionally, normative segmental PJA values reported by Pesenti et al. were applied to all patients meeting conventional PJK criteria to assess whether the observed junctional angles exceeded the physiological upper limit for their respective UIV level (defined as mean + 2SD). 10 All radiographic measurements, including PJA, were independently performed by three senior surgeons, and the mean of the three values was used for analysis. In the event of disagreement about whether a patient had developed PJK, the majority decision was used. To assess interobserver reliability, a random subset of 19 patients (20% of the cohort) was selected for interobserver comparison. Within this subset, discrepancies of 3° or more between any two surgeons’ measurements were resolved through joint review and consensus; for all other measurements, the mean of the three values was used, and the same consensus process was applied in cases of disagreement regarding PJK classification. Interobserver reliability was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC: 2,1; two-way random-effects model, absolute agreement, single measures) and was good-to-excellent for preoperative and postoperative measurements across all parameters: PJA 0.857 (95% CI: 0.81–0.90) and 0.939 (95% CI: 0.92–0.96); T5–T12 thoracic kyphosis 0.997 and 0.993; T2–T12 thoracic kyphosis 0.998 and 0.996; major coronal Cobb angle 0.996 and 0.995; and lumbar lordosis 0.999 and 0.998 (all p < 0.001).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM, New York, NY, USA). Continuous variables were reported as mean and standard deviation or median with interquartile range, and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. Normality of continuous variables was assessed, and appropriate parametric or non-parametric tests were used. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis. Group comparisons were conducted using chi-squared, Fisher’s exact, or Fisher-Freeman-Halton tests for categorical variables, and the independent t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables based on distribution. Interobserver reliability for radiographic measurements was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). Cut-off values for continuous predictors were determined using ROC curve analysis and the Youden index. The diagnostic performance of these thresholds, including area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity with 95% confidence intervals, is presented in Table 1. Univariate analysis was performed using chi-square tests and univariate logistic regression to identify potential risk factors for PJK; odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Variables with p-values <0.1 were included in multivariate logistic regression to identify independent predictors. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Ninety-five patients (37 male and 58 female) with EOS who underwent dual GR surgery at the same institution were included in the present retrospective study. The mean follow-up period was 5.83 ± 2.37 years (range: 2.02–11.73 years), and the mean age at the time of the first operation was 6.84 ± 2.16 years (range: 2.83–12.42 years). Congenital (36 patients), neuromuscular (17 patients), syndromic (21 patients), and idiopathic (21 patients) were among the etiologies of EOS. Seventy-nine patients were treated with traditional growing rods (TGRs), and 16 with magnetically controlled growing rods (MCGRs). The incidence of PJK did not differ significantly between patients treated with TGRs and those treated with MCGRs(13/79 vs 4/16, p = 0.645; Table 2).

PJK was observed in 17 out of the 95 patients (17.9%). It occurred immediately after surgery in 3 patients, within the first postoperative year in 2 patients, and after the first year in 12 patients (Figure 1). Among the 17 patients who developed PJK, 3 (17.6%) progressed to proximal junctional failure, all of which were managed with revision surgery performed during a scheduled lengthening procedure. PJF was attributed to mechanical failure at the UIV, including two cases of screw pull-out and one rod fracture at the UIV. At final follow-up, definitive fusion had been performed in all 3 PJF patients and in 8 of the remaining 14 PJK patients, with 6 patients yet to undergo definitive fusion. Among the 11 patients who underwent definitive fusion, 6 had the UIV extended proximally by one level at the time of final fusion (most commonly from T3 to T2), suggesting that PJK influenced the surgical decision-making in these cases. Among all 17 PJK patients, 7 (41.2%) experienced at least one unplanned return to the operating room, with a total of 9 unplanned surgical interventions recorded. Additional complications included rod fracture in 9 patients, anchor failure in 3 patients, deep infection in 3 patients, connector fracture in 3 patients, and implant-related metallosis or magnetic rod mechanism failure in 1 patient. Six patients (35.3%) experienced no complications beyond the radiographic PJK diagnosis.

When normative segmental PJA thresholds were applied to the 17 patients meeting conventional PJK criteria, 16 (94.1%) exceeded the physiological upper limit for their respective UIV level. One patient with a UIV at T2 and a final PJA of 12.5° did not surpass the normative threshold of 19.5° and would therefore not be classified as having true PJK under segment-adjusted criteria. Given that only one patient was reclassified, the primary risk factor findings were not meaningfully affected by the choice of definition.

In the comparison between the patients who developed PJK and those who did not, significant differences were observed in the UIV level (T2 and proximal vs. T3 and distal, p = 0.034; Table 2), the preoperative T5–12 kyphosis (p = 0.005), the preoperative T2–T12 kyphosis (p = 0.006), and the final follow-up T2–T12 kyphosis measurements (p = 0.004; Table 3).

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified subsequent significant risk factors associated with PJK: Patients with a UIV level at T3 or more distal showed a 3.27-fold higher risk of developing PJK in comparison to those with a UIV level at T2 or proximal (OR = 3.27, 95% CI: 1.05-10.19, p=0.041). The probability of developing PJK increases by 3% for each 1° increase in the preoperative T5-T12 thoracic kyphosis angle (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.00-1.06, p = 0.045). In the patients who underwent postoperative correction of thoracic kyphosis between T5 and T12 exceeding 30°, the likelihood of developing PJK was 4.49 times higher compared to those with correction of less than 30° (OR = 4.49, 95% CI: 1.06-18.98, p = 0.041).

Each 1° increase in the preoperative T2-T12 thoracic kyphosis angle correlates with a 3% increase in the likelihood of developing PJK (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.00-1.06, p = 0.022). The probability of developing PJK was 4.8 times higher in patients exhibiting a preoperative T2-T12 thoracic kyphosis angle of 45° or greater, compared to those with an angle less than 45° (OR = 4.8, 95% CI: 1.53-15.08, p = 0.007). Postoperative T2–T12 thoracic kyphosis ≥45° was not significantly associated with PJK (OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 0.63–6.06, p = 0.244) (Table 4).

Discussion

Immobilization of the mobile spinal column with fusion and/or instrumentation causes stress concentration in the transition area between the fixed and mobile segments. Surgically achieved alignment may be compromised by this increased load, especially in the sagittal plane, and further deformities may emerge in the surrounding segment. In this sense, the instrumentation’s proximal region is especially fragile. The condition, also known as PJK, can occur at any age, at any level of the spine, and with any kind of deformity. This issue may arise as a result of a number of factors, including preexisting osteoporosis, implant selection, surgical technique (failure to preserve the musculoligamentous structure at the end of instrumentation) and instrumentation level errors, preoperative kyphosis angle and amount of kyphosis correction, and the patient’s global and lumbopelvic alignment. 9 Postoperative angular changes in the adjacent proximal segment may trigger structural changes such as disc degeneration, vertebral fracture, or spondylolisthesis in some patients, leading to PJF, which necessitates revision surgery. This type of surgery, particularly in the upper thoracic and cervical regions, almost always complicates the treatment process and negatively impacts overall success.

One of the most popular surgical techniques for treating EOS is DB-GFT. This treatment utilizes instrumentation and fusion to stabilize one or two upper thoracic motion segments (proximal foundation), employing longitudinal members (rods) to substantially, if not entirely, restrict spinal motion across an extended thoracolumbar segment. DB-GFT alters sagittal plane alignment, and distractive forces are applied to the end vertebrae and adjacent segments through repeated lengthening due to the nature of the technique. PJK is a common complication of DB-GFT. PJK development rates of between 20%-38% are documented in the literature.11,12 With an incidence of 17.9%, the rate of PJK observed in the present study is lower than those reported in the literature. This variation may stem from differences in follow-up, sample size, etiology, or surgical technique. Much research has been published in recent years to determine risk factors and establish preventive measures in place for PJK after DB-GFT.

Based on this context, we examined which patient-related, radiographic, and surgical factors were associated with PJK in our cohort and compared our findings with those reported in the literature. Although one study reported a higher risk of PJK was reported in syndromic patients, in most others, including the present study, no association was found between etiology and PJK; this supports a stronger relationship with the biomechanical characteristics of the curve.13,14 While El-Hawary et al. linked higher surgical age to increased PJK risk via greater thoracic kyphosis, Wang and Yang et al. associated younger age (<6–7 years) with risk due to soft tissue and bone immaturity.15–17 Our findings showed no significant association between age and PJK, suggesting that age alone may not be an independent risk factor.

Watanabe et al. identified more than four lengthenings as a risk threshold, but our analysis did not show such an association (10.12 vs. 11.06, p = 0.32), suggesting that when distraction intervals are appropriately timed and controlled, the number of lengthenings does not influence PJK development. 18 Helgeson et al. associated pedicle screw use with increased construct rigidity and higher PJK rates; however, anchor type was not significantly related to PJK in this cohort (p = 0.835). 19 In children, preservation of the facet capsule and posterior ligamentous complex may be more protective than anchor design alone.11,15,20 In several studies, larger preoperative Cobb angles and greater correction magnitude were correlated with heightened PJK risk; however, these relationships were not evident in the current analysis. 17 While preoperative T5–T12 kyphosis and a postoperative T5–T12 correction ≥30° were significant in the univariate analysis, they did not persist as independent predictors.16–18 In contrast, preoperative T2–T12 thoracic kyphosis ≥45° emerged as an independent risk factor (OR = 4.44, 95% CI: 1.38–14.22, p = 0.012), consistent with previous reports linking increased preoperative thoracic kyphosis to PJK, although at higher thresholds (≥50–60°).15,18,21 The lower cut-off identified in our cohort was derived from ROC/Youden index analysis and may reflect differences in patient population, instrumentation strategy, or follow-up duration. A distal UIV (T3 or below) emerged as the second independent risk factor in our study (OR = 2.94, p = 0.047). Instrumenting at or below T3 shortens the transitional segment between the cervical spine and thoracic kyphosis, likely increasing mechanical stress in the junctional zone.11,17,21

In patients with preoperative thoracic kyphosis or in whom substantial kyphosis correction is planned, preoperative halo-gravity traction has been described as an adjunct to partially reduce kyphosis prior to index surgery, though this was not part of our routine protocol. Instead, we addressed significant preoperative kyphosis through minimal initial correction at index surgery with gradual sagittal optimization via in situ bending during subsequent lengthening procedures, supplemented by multiple proximal anchor points and, in selected cases, apical anchors to better distribute junctional loading. Our institutional practice has also been to extend the proximal foundation to at least the T2 level, if patients have preoperative kyphosis or require substantial sagittal correction. At this level, transverse process hooks are routinely used, which allow for a smoother transition between the fused and mobile segments while minimizing iatrogenic injury to the posterior ligamentous structures and facet capsules. We believe that extending fixation to T2 rather than stopping at T3 has not resulted in a clinically meaningful difference in motion or growth arrest between the T2–T3 segments. However, UIV selection should not be regarded as a binary decision, as it may be influenced by multiple factors including curve type, sagittal apex location, proximal thoracic deformity, shoulder balance, and severity of kyphosis. A distal UIV may therefore reflect underlying curve morphology or increased junctional stress rather than representing an isolated modifiable risk factor.

In the literature, PJK describes conditions in which the change in kyphotic angle between the lower endplate of the uppermost instrumented vertebra and the superior endplate of the adjacent second upper vertebra is greater than 10° between preoperative and final follow-up radiographs. The normative value of segmental spinal alignment in the relevant segment is not taken into account for this measurement. The same threshold value is used for both the upper thoracic and lower thoracic spines, which exhibit different angulations in a healthy spine. 22 This can lead to inaccurate assessments. In their recent analysis of normative PJAs, Pesenti et al. demonstrated that normative PJA reference values more accurately reflect physiological sagittal alignment, highlighting that uniform thresholds may result in misclassification. When these normative values are applied to our cohort, 16 of 17 patients surpass the expected physiological range, whereas one does not, indicating that this patient would not be considered to have true PJK according to normative segmental PJA. 10 It should be acknowledged that the observed risk of developing PJK may vary depending on the definition used, and that the use of fixed angular thresholds may lead to overestimation of PJK incidence when physiological, level-specific sagittal alignment is not considered.

In the evaluation of complications in adult spinal deformity surgery, in recent years conditions requiring revision (PJF) have become more important than simply radiological measurement differences (PJK), and there is a tendency not to regard asymptomatic PJKs as serious complications. 23 Consistent with this perspective, only a small proportion of patients in our cohort progressed to PJF, suggesting that several radiographically identified PJK cases may have limited clinical relevance in the absence of mechanical failure. However, the EOS literature still considers angular changes greater than 10° to be a serious complication.

Another issue worthy of discussion is the time interval between index surgery and PJK onset. This timeframe varies among patients. In our cohort, 3 of the 17 PJK patients developed PJK in the early postoperative period, while 2 additional cases occurred within the first year. Eight patients developed PJK between 12 and 24 months, and the remaining 4 cases manifested more than 2 years after surgery (Figure 1). Furthermore, measuring the difference between preoperative and follow-up radiographs fails to distinguish between PJK that develops in the early postoperative period due to surgical technical errors such as ligamentous disruption(mechanical), and PJK that develops over time due to stress-concentration differences between fixed and mobile segment junction(biological). Previous studies have demonstrated that early PJK may develop when the contour of the rod—particularly at its proximal end—fails to match the physiological sagittal alignment of the vertebral column. 24 In such cases, the rod imposes excessive corrective forces on the adjacent segments. Beyond ligamentous disruption, this mismatch likely leads to disproportionate stress concentration at the proximal junction, which can be considered a key contributing factor in the development of so-called mechanical PJK.

The conventional PJK definition, based on the difference between final follow-up PJA and preoperative PJA, as described by Glattes et al., inherently incorporates both subtypes into a single measurement, precluding their distinction. 8 We propose that measuring the difference between final follow-up PJA and immediate postoperative PJA may more accurately identify biologically driven junctional deterioration, as it excludes angular changes attributable to the index surgery itself. This alternative measurement approach may represent a more clinically meaningful definition for delayed, progressive PJK. Understanding this distinction is crucial for determining follow-up and radiological examination frequency, as well as for preoperative patient counseling.

The strengths of our study include its single-center design, long-term follow-up, and relatively large cohort. However, several limitations merit consideration. First, the retrospective design and heterogeneity in EOS etiology may limit generalizability. Second, pelvic incidence and other spinopelvic parameters could not be reliably incorporated into the analysis; as this is a historical cohort spanning nearly two decades, adequate visualization of the femoral heads was not consistently available on older radiographs, and including this parameter would have introduced excessive missing data. Third, not all patients were followed up until skeletal maturity, which may have resulted in missed late-onset PJK cases. Fourth, comprehensive patient-reported outcome measures such as the EOSQ were not consistently available across this historical cohort. Finally, the multivariable model was restricted to two independent predictors to minimize overfitting; nonetheless, the resulting events-per-variable ratio of 8.5:1 remains slightly below the conventional 10:1 threshold, and the limited number of PJK events (n=17) constrains the precision of the diagnostic performance estimates reported for the radiographic thresholds in Table 1, as reflected in their wide confidence intervals. As these thresholds were identified within the same dataset, external validation in a larger, independent cohort is warranted before their adoption in clinical decision-making.

In short, it is important to determine the overall risk of developing PJK after DB-GFT in a series as homogeneous as possible (in terms of surgical technique, determining instrumentation limits, applying a standard approach in the lengthening protocol, and the rules applied in sagittal plane restoration). Reviewing the risk factors contributing to the development of this complication in this homogeneous group will significantly contribute to the existing literature. Identifying the differences in PJK between the pre-index surgery and final measurements and between the post-index surgery and final measurements will help distinguish biological and technical causes, thus reducing complications through technical improvements.

Conclusion

In the present study, a focused evaluation is provided of PJK development in EOS patients treated with a standardized DB-GFT protocol, highlighting the interplay between patient-related factors, sagittal alignment changes, and instrumentation strategy. Our findings underscore the importance of interpreting radiographic junctional changes within both their anatomical and clinical context.

Patients who are preoperatively kyphotic and/or undergo extensive kyphosis correction and/or have a UIV located below T3 are at increased risk. Adjusting the PJK according to the normal values of the relevant segment will prevent erroneous assessments in some cases. Predicting the mean time to development of PJK is important for follow-up protocols. Finally, distinguishing between patients who require revision surgery and those who remain asymptomatic, even with angular change, will enable a more realistic assessment of this complication.
