A novel adhesive dual-sensitive hydrogel for sustained release of exosomes derived from M2 macrophages promotes repair of bone defects
Authors: Xiaoying Zhao, Ximiao Chen, Yuxin Deng, Chenyu Wu, Zihang Ruan, Chenchao Li, Yu Chen, Chaoyong Bei, Linyong Zhu, Huachen Yu, Xiaolei Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The repair of bone defects remains a huge clinical challenge. M2 macrophage-derived exosomes (M2-Exos) can act as immunomodulators to promote fracture healing; however, how to retain the sustained release of exosomes to the target area remains a challenge. Here, we report a composite hydrogel loaded with M2-Exos aiming to accelerate bone defect healing. It was verified that the F127/HA-NB hydrogel had a dense network structure, tissue adhesiveness, and dual sensitivity to temperature and light. F127/HA-NB loaded with M2-Exos (M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB) exhibited good biocompatibility and achieved sustained release of exosomes for up to two weeks. The study showed that both M0-Exos and M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB significantly promoted osteogenic differentiation of rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells. The mechanism study implied that M2-Exos activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway to promote osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Finally, we evaluated the osteogenetic effects of M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB in a rat cranial defect model, and the results showed that M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB had superior bone regeneration-promoting effects. This study provides a new strategy for cell-free treatment of bone defects.

Statistical data reveals that the ability of bone healing is generally robust, as approximately 90 % of fractures can successfully undergo normal healing through timely treatment and rest. However, a small proportion, around 5 %–10 % of patients, experience inadequate fracture healing, including in nonunion or delayed healing [1]. The occurrence of nonunion in fractures not only profoundly affects patients' work life and mental well-being but also has adverse consequences for their families and society [2].

The process of fracture healing consists of four sequential stages: hematoma inflammation, cartilage callus formation, bone callus development, and remodeling. Fractures often cause damage to local blood vessels and soft tissues, initiating the formation of hematomas in the early stages, which serve as the initial form of callus production. Following this, the healing process enters the inflammatory phase, wherein immune cells, including neutrophils and macrophages, infiltrate the vicinity of the fracture. Immunocytes play crucial roles in eliminating dead cells and tissue debris, while also recruiting stem cells for further reparative processes during the inflammatory phase [3]. Adequate early-stage inflammation is vital for initiating fracture healing [4], however inappropriate or prolonged inflammation can impede or even obstruct the healing process [[5], [6], [7]]. Research has demonstrated the effectiveness of administering anti-inflammatory therapy after the inflammatory phase in promoting fracture repair.

Macrophages, a type of immune cells, serve as the primary force in the immune response and are present in bone tissue. They are recruited during the initial stages of tissue injury and regulate all phases of the subsequent repair process [8,9]. Macrophages are pivotal components of the intricate regulatory network involved in bone regeneration. They assume a vital role and serve as highly promising targets for expediting the process of bone regeneration [9]. Once they reach the damaged area, macrophages can eliminate pathogens and phagocytose cellular fragments, while also promoting the growth of new granulation tissue. Recent research suggests that macrophages not only engage in phagocytosis but also regulate other bone histiocytes, exhibiting high plasticity [10,11]. In a study by Alexander et al. [12], the depletion of macrophages through genome editing in mice resulted in a significant reduction in type I collagen and bone mineralization at the fracture site, indicating that macrophages play a role in regulating osteogenesis in addition to their phagocytic function [13]. Additionally, Vi et al. [14] discovered that macrophages can stimulate the differentiation of osteoblasts in vivo, further confirming their regulatory role in bone histiocytes.

In complex intercellular and intracellular cascade reactions, the transformation of macrophage phenotypes M1/M2 has attracted special attention [15]. In general, M1 macrophages promote inflammation, while M2 macrophages suppress it [16]. Extensive studies have shown that M2 macrophages contribute to the regulatory mechanisms involved in the osteogenic differentiation of pre-osteoblast somatic cells. By inducing the polarization of M2 macrophages, efficient bone regeneration can be stimulated, accelerating the repair of bone defects. In the subsequent phases of inflammation, anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages have been shown to assist in the healing process of bone abnormalities [[17], [18], [19]]. However, currently, direct cell therapy faces numerous challenges, making it difficult to effectively supply exogenous M2 macrophages to the defect site. Moreover, most studies tend to introduce biomaterials to indirectly promote bone regeneration at the defect site by influencing the function of macrophages [[20], [21], [22]]. Therefore, the focus of this article is to explore whether a method can be found to directly and efficiently utilize the functionality of M2 macrophages at the site of bone defects.

Exosomes are small, uniform vesicular bodies that cells actively release into the extracellular space [23,24]. They contain diverse functional substances, such as proteins, mRNA, and miRNA, and serve as a novel means of cellular communication and regulation of various cellular processes. Research studies have revealed that macrophages are capable of secreting exosomes that exert control over processes such as monocyte differentiation [25]and the release of inflammatory factors in placental tissue [26]. For instance, Wei et al. [27] demonstrated that exosomes secreted by macrophages pretreated with BMP2 effectively enhance osteogenesis, while Xiong et al. [28] showed that exosomes derived from M2-type macrophages (M2-Exos) can stimulate osteogenic differentiation of MSCs. In addition, the latest research suggests that M2 macrophage-derived extracellular vesicles can be used as immune regulators to promote fracture healing [29,30]. These findings highlight the significant role of M2-Exos in bone regeneration. Currently, exosomes are primarily administered through injection. However, due to their liquid nature and rapid degradation in vivo, it becomes challenging to retain them in the affected area. Given the prolonged duration required for bone defect repair, the clinical efficacy of exosomes as a treatment modality falls short of expectations.

Hydrogels, as biocompatible polymer materials, find extensive applications in biomedicine, serving as drug carriers, cell scaffolds, and 3D printing materials [31,32]. Recently, their unique properties have led to the exploration of hydrogels for in vivo exosome delivery [33,34]. Hyaluronic acid (HA), a biodegradable constituent of the extracellular matrix in connective tissues [35], can be modified and incorporated into hydrogels to generate new materials with diverse physical, chemical, and biological properties and functionalities. These materials are commonly employed as scaffolds for bone regeneration, facilitating cell and factor interactions [34,36]. For effective treatment of bone defects, an ideal hydrogel-based exosome carrier should possess specific characteristics: gelation at the defect site, efficient retention and sustained release of extracellular vesicles, and cell scaffold functionality. To address these requirements, we developed a novel hydrogel material, F127/HA-NB, by combining the hydrogel molecule F127 with tissue-adhesive o-nitrobenzyl alcohol-modified hyaluronic acid (HA-NB) [37]. Leveraging F127's temperature sensitivity and high biocompatibility, the resulting hydrogel incorporates HA-NB's tissue adhesion, photosensitivity, and exocrine sustained-release effects, synergistically surpassing the individual components' capabilities to achieve ‘1 + 1>2’, specifically the in-situ sustained release of exosomes.

In this investigation, we aim to assess the therapeutic efficacy of F127/HA-NB hydrogel incorporated with M2-Exos in the context of bone defect healing. Additionally, we endeavor to elucidate the specific pathway through which M2-Exos induces osteogenic differentiation in stem cells. By doing so, we intend to present novel insights for the management of bone defects and offer potential avenues for the clinical utilization of exosomes.

Materials and methods

Cultivation and polarization of macrophages

The RAW264.7 monocytes (Baiye, China), namely primitive macrophages (M0), were cultured at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 in RPMI-1640 Media (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1 % Penicillin/Streptomycin sterile solution (Solarbio, China). Observe the growth of cells every 1–2 days and pass them in a ratio of 1:2 or 1:3 when the cells grow to 70%–80 %. Induced polarization of M0 towards M2 under the action of IL-4 (P00196, Solarbio, China) at different concentrations and times and q-PCR was performed on macrophages induced at different concentrations and times to identify their degree of differentiation.

Extraction and identification of macrophage exosomes

Extraction of exosomes by hypervelocity gradient centrifugation. M0 and M2 culture medium were separately collected and centrifuged at 800 g for 5min and then an additional 10 min at 2000g to remove lifted cells. Then the supernatant was subjected to filtration on a 0.2 μm pore membrane filter to remove large membrane vesicles. The supernatant was ultra-centrifuged at 100,00 g for 90min at 4 °C using a 100Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter), precipitation was the extracted exosomes. Final exosomes were obtained and stored at −80 °C to standby. The protein content, as the quantification of exosomes, was determined using a microBCA protein assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Surface markers of exosomes (CD9, CD81, CD63) were analyzed using Western blot. The morphology of the extracted exosomes was observed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEOL, Japan). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was employed for the assessment of particle size by NanoSight (Malvern, UK).

Extraction and culture of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs)

Selecting 1-week-old SD rats, euthanize the rats with excessive pentobarbital sodium, sterilize the skin with 75 % ethanol, cut off the femurs on both sides under sterile conditions and separate the skin and muscle, soak the femurs in sterile PBS, remove the epiphyseal ends on both sides of the bones in a sterile environment, expose the bone marrow, and use 1 ml disposable sterile syringe to absorb complete culture medium: F12/DMEM culture medium (Gibco, USA), 10 % FBS and 1 % Penicillin/Streptomycin sterile solution. Insert a needle into one end of the bone and slowly inject it to rinse the bone marrow into a culture bottle. Change the culture medium after 48 h of cultivation to remove the cell fragments in the culture dish. Digest and passage with 0.25 % Trypsin-EDTA solution (Solarbio, China) When the cell fusion reaches 80 %–90 %, continue culturing at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 to the third generation obtain the purified BMSCs and standby.

Synthesis and characterizations of M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB hydrogel

Content of HA-NB [38] and Pluronic F-127 (F127) (Sigma, USA) was 20 mg mL−1 with the mass ratio of 1: 1. The M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB hydrogel was obtained by using mixing 1 μg M2-exo with 100 μL F127/HA-NB hydrogel with stirring at 4 °C. The mechanical properties of hydrogels were examined using an oscillatory rheometer (HAAKE MARS 40, ThermoFisher, USA). For the time-sweep test, shear moduli were measured at a frequency of 1 Hz and a strain of 0.5 % at 37 °C and 395 nm LED light (20 mW cm−2) for 10 min. The lap-shear stress of the adhesive hydrogel was measured using a universal test machine (5944, Instron, USA) to investigate the adhesion strength of the hydrogel to the skull tissue of rats. The lap shear stress was calculated by dividing the maximum stress by contact area (10mm × 10 mm) between the hydrogel and tissue [39]. The porous morphology of hydrogels was observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Quanta 250 FEG, FEI). The hydrogel was placed at different temperatures and irradiated with a 395 nm LED light (20 mW cm−2) recording the temperature and time of its gel. Verification of tissue adhesion was conducted using various rat organs, including the heart, liver, kidney, spleen, and skull. On the wet tissue surfaces, about 500 μl of hydrogel solution was evenly distributed and exposed to irradiation. The resulting adhesion state was observed and documented.

The exosome release profile was tested by a microBCA protein assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Briefly, the above prepared M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB were placed in the upper transwell chamber in a 24-well plate, while 100 μL PBS was added in the lower chamber. Then the PBS once every three days and summarized the data. The cytotoxicity of BMSCs on different time points (1–4 days) after co-culture with F127/HA-NB or M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB was assessed by a Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime, China) and TUNEL (Beyotime, China).

Cell transfection of si-RNA

The si-RNA β-catenin (GCACCATGCAGAATACAAA) and si-RNA negative control (NC) vectors were synthesized by Ribo-bioCo., Ltd (Guangzhou, China). According to the instructions of the kit, si-β-catenin and si-NC were mixed with riboFECT™ CP Reagent, and then co-cultured with BMSCs.

BMSCs osteogenic differentiation

Osteogenesis-inducing medium was used to induce osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, which contained 100 μg/mL ascorbic acid, 2 mM β-glycerophosphate, and 10 nM dexamethasone, which needed to change every 3 days. Alizarin Red S Staining Kit for Osteogenesis (Beyotime, China), Alkaline Phosphatase Assay Kit (Beyotime, China), and Calcium Stain Kit (Von Kossa Method) (Solarbio, China) were used at the 7th and 14th day according to the instructions.

Cellular immunofluorescence assay

After 24 h of exos culture, samples were fixed by 4 % paraformaldehyde, permeabilized in 0.5 % Triton X-100 for 10min, and blocked with 10 % goat serum at 37 °C for 1 h, then incubated with primary antibody (anti-β-catenin) at 4 °C overnight. Then the slides were washed and incubated with fluorescein isothiocyanate-conjugated secondary antibody for 1–2 h at 37 °C.At least, visualized cell nuclei by DAPI (Beyotime, China) and viewed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Rat skull defect model

Thirty 10-week-old healthy SD rats weighing 200–250 g were randomly divided into 5 groups. Pentobarbital was used to drill holes in the skull to form a direct 4 mm circular single-layer cortical bone cavity. Saline, 50 μg free M0-Exos, 50 μg free M2-Exos, F127/HA-NB alone, and M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB (containing 50 μg M2-Exos) were filled with the bone defect site. Samples were taken on the 4th and 8th week after surgery to observe the tissues at the skull defect site, and perform imaging analysis and measurement of various parameters of the newly formed bone.

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) analysis

To determine new bone formation in the calcarium. After removing the calvarium, the specimens were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 72 h and then used Micro-CT (Micro CT m100, SCANO Medical, Switzerland). Software CTvox, Data Viewer, and CTAN were used to analyze the 3D reconstructed images, 2D reconstruction, and quantitative analysis.

Histological analysis

The specimens harvested at 4 and 8 weeks, after fixing in 4 % paraformaldehyde, were decalcified in 10 % EDTA solution for 8 weeks. Then the samples were dehydrated by an alcohol gradient and embedded in paraffin blocks. Slides were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or Masson's trichrome. Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining Kit was purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China) and Masson's Trichrome Stain Kit was purchased from Solabio (Beijing, China), used according to the manufacturers' instructions. Photographing the stained sections under a microscope.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently performed at least three times to support reproducibility. All quantitative data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). Using SPSS 22.0 for statistical analyses. One-way ANOVA was used for comparisons of multiple groups. Values of *P＜0.05, **P＜0.01, ***P < 0.001was considered to be significance.

Results

Preparation and characterization of M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB hydrogel

Using the well-established cytokine IL-4 to induce polarization of M2-type macrophages, we selected undifferentiated RAW264.7 cells as M0 and employed q-PCR to identify M2-type macrophage-specific genes. Our results demonstrated that the optimal concentration for IL-4-induced polarization of M2 macrophages was 20 ng/ml (Fig. 1A), with an optimal duration of 24 h (Fig. 1B). To obtain macrophage exosomes, the conditioned medium from M0 and M2 macrophages was separated through hypervelocity gradient centrifugation. We employed Western blot analysis, transmission electron microscopy, and Nanosight measurements to characterize and confirm the purified exosomes. Western blot analysis revealed positive expression of extracellular surface marker proteins (including CD9, CD81, and CD63) in the two types of exosome lysates compared to M0 and M2 macrophages (Fig. 1C). Transmission electron microscopy observations indicated that the exosomes derived from M0 and M2 macrophages exhibited a circular or rounded morphology, with sizes below 100 nm (Fig. 1D). Nanosight measurements further supported these findings (Fig. 1E), with the size distribution of exosomes derived from M0 and M2 macrophages predominantly centered around 60 nm, consistent with both electron microscopy results and previous literature reports [37]. Collectively, these results substantiated the successful acquisition of exosomes derived from M0 and M2 macrophages in this study.

We synthesized F127/HA-NB according to Fig. 2A and characterized it subsequently. To commence, we conducted initial explorations into the gelation conditions of F127/HA-NB, Fig. 2B demonstrated that F127/HA-NB combined the thermosensitive characteristics of F127 with the photosensitive properties of HA-NB [37,40], enabling gel formation upon exposure to light (395 nm, 20 mW cm−2) for 20 s or at 37 °C. The rheological properties of the hydrogels were illustrated in Fig. 2C, indicating that the F127/HA-NB hydrogel could rapidly gel within approximately 10 s upon exposure to light irradiation and at a temperature of 37 °C. The F127/HA-NB hydrogel formed a 3D structure that was more stable than F127. Its storage modulus (G’) is comparable to that of HA-NB, and it also exhibits a shorter gelation time compared to HA-NB. To achieve the in-situ release of exosomes, we evaluated the tissue adhesiveness of hydrogels (Fig. 2D and E). Observation of the distinct three-dimensional porous morphology of the hydrogels (Fig. 2F). These data indicated that F127/HA-NB effectively combined the tissue adhesiveness of HA-NB with the dense structure of F127 [41], and it still maintained a dense structure after the synthesis of M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB. The release profile of exosomes from the hydrogel was depicted in Fig. 2G, demonstrating sustained release for over 12 days after encapsulation of M2-Exos within the hydrogel. Subsequently, the biocompatibility of the material was evaluated using bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). CCK-8 assay and TUNEL staining were employed to assess the impact of F127/HA-NB and M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB on BMSC proliferation and apoptosis (Fig. 2H and I). The results indicated that BMSCs treated with M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB exhibited similar proliferation rates compared to the control group and did not affect cell death. In summary, the M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB hydrogel can be characterized as a novel formulation that possesses several notable properties. These include its porous and dense structure, its ability to adhere to tissue, its responsiveness to dual stimuli, and its capability to sustain the release of exosomes.

M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB hydrogel promotes osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs in vitro

To investigate the in vitro impact of M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB hydrogel material, we evaluated the expression of osteogenic-related genes and proteins, including Runx2, collagen I, and OPN, on the seventh day using qPCR and Western blot analysis. The results showed that compared to M0-Exos, M2-Exos promoted high expression of osteogenic markers. Compared to F127/HA-NB, M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB also promoted osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, and the upregulation of osteogenic markers was comparable to that of M2-Exos (Fig. 3A–C). Interestingly, the upregulation effect of M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB on osteogenic markers is slightly superior compared to the M2-Exos treatment group at the genetic level. This could be attributed to the fact that M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB only released approximately 50 % of exosomes by the seventh day (Fig. 2G), while the osteogenic cycle is more protracted. To further investigate the osteogenic potential, we conducted osteogenic staining experiments. BMSCs were cultured in an osteogenic induction medium, and the calcification was assessed using Alizarin Red S (ARS), ALP, and Von Kossa staining on the seventh and fourteenth days. The results demonstrated that the addition of M2-Exos effectively enhanced the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Moreover, the bone-promoting effect of M2-Exos was superior when incorporated into F127/HA-NB hydrogel compared to M2-Exos alone, as evidenced on the fourteenth day (Fig. 3D and E). However, there was no statistically significant difference in calcification between these two groups on the seventh day.

These results indicate that M2-Exos exerts an osteogenic effect, and the sustained release effect of M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB can enhance the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs to a greater extent over a more extended duration.

The osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs is facilitated by M2-Exos through the activation of the wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

The osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) is a tightly regulated process influenced by various signaling pathways, encompassing Wnt [42,43], TGF-β [44], hedgehog [45], notch [46], and other intricate molecular networks. Notably, the Wnt signaling pathway holds significant prominence in orchestrating the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs [47]. Perturbation or inhibition of key proteins involved in the Wnt pathway has been associated with impaired bone repair kinetics [48]. Therefore, we are focusing our attention on the Wnt signaling pathway, attempting to explore the effects of M2-Exos on this pathway.

To investigate the impact of M2-Exos on BMSCs, western blot analysis was conducted to assess the expression levels of proteins involved in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, namely GSK-3β, pGSK-3β, and β-catenin (Fig. 4A). The findings revealed that the M2-Exos treatment group exhibited significantly higher levels of β-catenin and pGSK-3β protein expression compared to the M0 macrophage exosomes group and the blank control group (Fig. 4B). These results suggest that M2-Exos potentially facilitates the phosphorylation of GSK-3β, thereby activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Additionally, cellular immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that M2-Exos influenced the subcellular distribution of β-catenin protein, facilitating its entry into the nucleus (Fig. 4C). To further investigate the impact of M2-Exos on the intracellular signaling of wnt/β-catenin, we conducted a transfection of rat BMSCs with β-catenin siRNA and subsequently assessed the β-catenin protein levels using western blot analysis. The results exhibited a significant reduction in β-catenin protein expression in the group transfected with β-catenin siRNA (Fig. 4D and E). As depicted in Fig. 4F, when β-catenin was inhibited, the regulatory effect of M2 macrophage exosomes on BMSCs' bone formation was diminished, leading to a notable decrease in the expression levels of collagen Ⅰ, OPN, and Runx2, all of which are indicators associated with bone formation. These expression levels were comparable to those observed in the group without supplementation of M2-Exos. This result indicated that β-catenin was an important factor for M2-Exos to promote bone formation, and knocking out β-catenin will block the bone-promoting effect of M2-Exos.

In summary, M2-Exos promotes the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs by activating the wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and promoting β-catenin nuclear translocation.

M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB promotes bone defect healing in vivo

To validate the osteogenic potential of M2-Exos and M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB in vivo, we established a skull defect model in adult SD rats by creating a 4 mm round cortical bone hole on the skull (Fig. 5A). Micro-CT reconstruction images were employed to assess the bone morphology of different experimental groups at the 4th and 8th weeks of skull defect (Fig. 5B). Quantitative analysis of parameters such as newly formed bone surface area (B.Ar/T.Ar), bone volume (BV/TV), and bone mineral density (BMD) was performed (Fig. 5C). The results demonstrate that M2-Exos exhibited a therapeutic efficacy in promoting bone defect healing compared to Ctrl and the M0-Exos treatment group. It is noteworthy that the M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB treatment group exhibited superior performance in terms of B.Ar/T.Ar, BV/TV and BMD compared to the other four groups at both the 4th and 8th weeks. Accidently, we found the B.Ar/T.Ar in the F127/HA-NB treatment group was higher than the control group and comparable to that of the M2-Exos treatment group. To validate the findings obtained from micro-CT and to conduct a more comprehensive analysis of new bone formation, histological examinations were performed on the skull samples at the 4th and 8th weeks post-surgery. Both H&E staining and Masson's trichrome staining revealed minimal bone formation and even fewer fiber formations in the defect area of the control and M0-Exos treated samples. While there was no bone formation observed in the F127/HA-NB treated samples, a significant increase in fiber formation compared to the control and M0-Exos treated samples was evident. New bone formation was observed in the M2-Exos and M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB groups at week 4, whereas the M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB group exhibited more mature bone formation at week 8 (Fig. 5D and E). In summary, the treatment with M2-Exos promotes bone formation during the bone defect repair process. Additionally, F127/HA-NB can serve as an osteogenesis scaffold to expedite bone defect healing. Furthermore, the combination of M2-Exos and F127/HA-NB (M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB) can enhance healing, provide sustained release of M2-Exos, and facilitate bone maturation during bone defect repair.

In general, M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB effectively promotes new bone regeneration in the body and accelerates the healing of bone defects. This treatment approach demonstrates promising outcomes for the acceleration of bone defect repair and exhibits a favorable effect on bone defect treatment (Fig. 6).

Discussion

In this study, we proportionally mixed F127 with a dense structure and a temperature-sensitive effect, and HA-NB with adhesive photosensitive material. On this basis, we effectively loaded M2-type macrophage exosomes to create a novel hydrogel, M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB, which has a dense structure, adhesion, dual sensitivity to temperature and light, and sustained release of M2-Exos. In vitro studies showed that M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB promoted the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs through the sustained release of M2-Exos, and long-term osteogenic effects were observed due to the absence of encapsulated hydrogel. Additionally, we demonstrated that M2-Exos promoted the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs through the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. In in vivo experiments, M2-Exos and F127/HA-NB hydrogel without loaded exosomes exhibited varying degrees of accelerated bone healing. M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB still demonstrated superior bone regeneration efficacy, as it could adhere to tissues and accelerate bone healing by releasing M2-Exos.

As one of the most versatile organs, the musculoskeletal system controls mineral homeostasis, provides the basic framework for movement, provides other necessities for movement, and fulfills other essential life functions [49]. For proper functioning, the musculoskeletal system must coordinate with other bodily systems. Being a crucial component of the immune system, macrophages exhibit a highly intricate association with bone regeneration [50]. Undifferentiated macrophages (M0) can differentiate into pro-inflammatory macrophages (M1) and anti-inflammatory macrophages (M2), each type serving distinct functions [16]. Previous studies have shown that promoting the preferential transfer of macrophage phenotype M1 to M2 contributes to immune regulation and tissue regeneration in MSCs [[51], [52], [53]], and IL-4-induced M2 macrophages stimulate bone regeneration [[54], [55], [56]]. During the process of repairing bone defects, the M2 subtype of macrophages plays a major role in promoting the repair, but the mechanism remains unclear. Nevertheless, employing live cells as a direct therapeutic approach inevitably entails immunological risks. This is precisely why research into the application of exosomes in bone regeneration has been steadily progressing.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that exosomes derived from adipose stem cells [57], umbilical cord stem cells [58], bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells [59,60], and even M2 macrophages [61] have been proven to promote osteogenic differentiation. These vesicles play a crucial role in the process of bone regeneration. This cell-free therapeutic strategy of administering extracellular vesicles not only avoids the immunological risks associated with cell therapy but also facilitates the repair and regeneration of experimentally defective bone tissue by modulating inflammatory responses and osteogenic differentiation. Currently, injection is the most commonly used method of exosome administration in clinical practice, but because exosomes are difficult to retain in the affected area, rapid clearance is inevitable [62]. For bone defect healing or fracture, the course of the disease is long, and exosomes need to be effectively preserved in the affected area to play their role in a lasting way. To achieve the ideal therapeutic effect, periodic local injection of exosomes is selected clinically, which makes the application process of exosomes complicated and increases the pain of patients immaterially. Therefore, it is essential to find a delivery vehicle that can achieve sustained release of exosomes at the site of injury while preserving their biological activity.

Hydrogel, composed of a three-dimensional hydrophilic polymer, has gained significant attention as an absorbable biodegradable scaffold due to its exceptional biocompatibility. HA is an important component of the extracellular matrix (ECM), plays a key role in many cell and tissue functions, and has been used clinically for decades [[63], [64], [65]]. Biomaterials made from HA have been widely used in bone tissue engineering [[66], [67], [68]] and wound dressing [69]. In this study, we selected a kind of photoinduced imine crosslinking (PIC) hydrogel, in which o-nitrobenzyl alcohol moieties modified hyaluronic acids (HA-NB) generate aldehyde groups under light irradiation and subsequently react with amino groups distributed on other polymers or on tissue surfaces to form a hydrogel in situ and simultaneously realize seamless hydrogel-tissue attachment and integration [37,38]. In addition, the thermal response performance of F127 can be used for a wide range of drug delivery applications, and there have been studies on osteogenesis [70]. We mixed the two components in equal proportions and incorporated them with M2-Exos, resulting in M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB. After verification, this hydrogel has the following advantages: (1) The HA-NB molecule can bind to the amino group of body tissue, so it can adhere to the tissue surface stably and have tissue adhesion. (2) Exosomes are rich in amino acids, and HA-NB can bind to exosomes stably, thus having the slow-release function of exosomes. (3) The F127 molecule is temperature-sensitive and dense-structure, liquid at low temperatures, and can be solidified into hydrogel at 37 °C. HA-NB can be gelled under light irradiation. Therefore, this hydrogel has a dense structure and dual-sensitive characteristics. (4) In vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that the hydrogel has good histocompatibility and is conducive to the application of in vivo biological therapy. In addition, exosomes themselves can also serve as vectors for targeted drug delivery by participating in intercellular transport [71]. M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB can also continue to carry drugs to facilitate further clinical transformation.

To gain further insight into the mechanism of action of exosomes in M2-type macrophages, we conducted in vivo and in vitro experiments to compare the effects of M0-Exos and M2-Exos on bone regeneration. Our findings revealed the bone-promoting effect of M2-Exos, highlighting their ability to activate the wnt signaling pathway through the promotion of GSK-3β phosphorylation. This activation subsequently increased intracellular β-catenin content and its translocation into the nucleus. Notably, GSK-3β is recognized as a pivotal point of convergence for multiple signaling pathways [72]. In addition to participating in the Wnt signaling pathway, GSK-3β is also downstream of Akt [73]. Increased p-GSK-3β leads to the inactivation of GSK-3β, which may activate the Akt signaling pathway as well as the wnt signaling pathway. Its phosphorylation is also regulated by kinases such as MAPK and ERK1/2 [74]. In addition, GSK3 can also regulate the activity of NF-κB [75]. These pathway proteins are involved in various life activities such as inflammation, metabolism, and apoptosis. It should be noted that our study did not investigate the specific subtypes of M2-type macrophages, and it remains unclear which exosomes derived from M2-type macrophages possess greater advantages in osteogenesis. Further research is warranted to obtain specific certification in this regard.

In general, M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB exhibits superior clinical application potential in the treatment of bone defects, thereby enhancing the practical implementation of M2-Exos in the field of regenerative medicine.

Conclusion

We have successfully developed an innovative adhesive and double-sensitive hydrogel to repair bone defects, achieved through the combination of M2-Exos with F127/HA-NB hydrogel. The integration of these components has enabled the hydrogel, M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB, to seamlessly adhere to the site of the defect while facilitating the effective release of exosomes. Moreover, the inclusion of M2-Exos has been found to activate the wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby expediting the process of osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs and promoting the repair of bone defects. The M2-Exos@F127/HA-NB hydrogel represents an innovative cell-free material that holds significant practical value for a wide range of tissue and organ repair applications, demonstrating promising potential for future clinical translation.
