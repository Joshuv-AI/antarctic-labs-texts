WSI-LLaVA: A Multimodal Large Language Model for Whole Slide Image
Yuci Liang, Xinheng Lyu, Wenting Chen, Meidan Ding, Jipeng Zhang, Xiangjian He, Song Wu, Xiaohan Xing, Sen Yang, Xiyue Wang, Linlin Shen

Abstract
Recent advances in computational pathology have introduced whole slide image (WSI)-level multimodal large language models (MLLMs) for automated pathological analysis. However, current WSI-level MLLMs face two critical challenges: limited explainability in their decision-making process and insufficient attention to morphological features crucial for accurate diagnosis. To address these challenges, we first introduce WSI-Bench , a large-scale morphology-aware benchmark containing 180k VQA pairs from 9,850 WSIs across 30 cancer types, specifically designed to evaluate MLLMs’ understanding of morphological characteristics crucial for accurate diagnosis. To the best of our knowledge, WSI-Bench presents the first benchmarking systematically evaluate morphological understanding capabilities in WSI analysis. To enhance the model explainability, we present WSI-LLaVA , an MLLM framework for gigapixel WSI understanding with a three-stage training strategy, which can provide detailed morphological findings to explain its final answer. For more precise model assessment in pathological contexts, we develop two specialized WSI metrics : WSI-Precision and WSI-Relevance , focusing on clinical accuracy. Extensive evaluation on WSI-Bench reveals both the capabilities and limitations of current WSI MLLMs in morphological analysis and various pathology tasks, while demonstrating WSI-LLaVA’s superior performance across all capabilities on both internal and external datasets. Source code and data are released.

The first challenge in current WSI-level MLLMs lies in their explainability: these models typically struggle to provide explanations for their final answers. Existing WSI-level models tend to offer short answers without supporting explanations. Some approaches

Another significant challenge with current pathology MLLMs is their tendency to map pathology images directly to diagnostic outcomes, overlooking crucial morphological features necessary for accurate diagnosis
[
18
]
. Pathologists depend on detailed observation of these morphological characteristics when interpreting pathology images. For example, distinguishing between invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) and invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) requires recognizing subtle differences in tumor cell arrangements
[

]
. While some pathology MLLMs incorporate questions about morphological features, many studies
[
13
,
25
,
18
]
are limited to patch-level analysis—preventing holistic assessment across the entire slide—or are trained on closed-ended VQA data that constrains their ability to produce rich, descriptive responses
[
4
,
5

. As a result, they cannot analyze morphological features comprehensively and systematically with the detailed, nuanced descriptions characteristic of expert pathologists. Moreover, morphological observation capabilities remain unevaluated in current WSI multi-modal benchmarks
[
23
]
, which primarily assess WSI MLLMs on common pathology tasks such as diagnosis, prognosis, and report generation. Therefore, integrating detailed
morphological observations
into WSI MLLMs is essential to better emulate human pathologists and properly evaluate their morphological understanding capabilities.

WSI-Bench
: To address these challenges, we introduce
WSI-Bench
, a morphology-aware gigapixel WSI benchmark that systematically evaluates WSI-level MLLMs on both
morphological understanding
and clinical pathology tasks. The benchmark assesses 4 core capabilities through 11 distinct tasks, comprising over 180k VQA pairs derived from 9,850 WSIs spanning 30 cancer types from 8,368 patients. As illustrated in Fig.
2
, the core capabilities include morphological analysis, diagnosis, treatment planning, and report generation, comprehensively covering 11 specific clinical tasks in pathology. All open-ended tasks incorporate corresponding explanations, particularly for diagnosis tasks where models must provide both conclusions and supporting morphological descriptions. This design ensures interpretability by making the reasoning process transparent, enabling pathologists to understand and validate the model’s decision-making pathway. For morphological analysis, WSI-Bench includes four hierarchical morphology tasks that progress from global to local perspectives, offering a comprehensive evaluation of morphological understanding capabilities. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first and largest morphology-aware WSI benchmark with comprehensive clinical tasks specifically designed for WSI-level MLLM training and evaluation.

WSI-LLaVA
: To enhance
explainability
in pathology tasks, we introduce a novel WSI-level Large Language and Vision Assistant framework (
WSI-LLaVA
) that leverages detailed
morphological observations
to explain diagnostic reasoning. Trained on the WSI-Bench training set, WSI-LLaVA can first provide morphological findings related to the query before presenting the final answer, significantly enhancing model explainability. To mitigate the cross-modal gap between WSI and text, WSI-LLaVA employs a three-stage training approach: WSI-text alignment, feature space alignment, and task-specific instruction tuning. Our framework integrates both patch-level and slide-level encoders to capture local details and global context, providing a comprehensive representation of gigapixel WSIs for advanced pathological analysis and reasoning.

WSI Metrics
: Although natural language understanding (NLU) metrics are commonly used to evaluate medical language tasks, they do not accurately assess performance due to complex and often similar terminology in pathology.
[
17
,
11
]
. To address these limitations, we introduce two specialized WSI metrics:
WSI-Precision
, which verifies the accuracy of each claim derived from the ground truth against the model’s answers, and
WSI-Relevance
, which assesses the alignment of each claim in the model’s responses with the ground truth to ensure their relevance. These metrics are specifically designed to evaluate the quality of morphological reasoning and explanatory content in model outputs, providing a more nuanced assessment and explanation than traditional metrics allow.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We introduce WSI-Bench, the first large-scale morphology-aware benchmark for gigapixel WSI understanding and evaluation, encompassing 180k VQA pairs from 9,850 WSIs across 30 cancer types. This benchmark uniquely evaluates the morphological understanding capability and incorporates morphological descriptions as supporting evidence for each task.

•

To enhance model explainability, we propose WSI-LLaVA, a novel framework for gigapixel WSI analysis and explanation that integrates patch-level and slide-level features through a three-stage training approach, which leverages detailed morphological observations to explain diagnostic reasoning.

•

We develop WSI-specific evaluation metrics that provide a more accurate assessment and explanation of model performance in pathological contexts, which address the limitations of traditional NLU metrics by verifying claim accuracy and response relevance.

•

We conduct a comprehensive evaluation of current WSI MLLMs and other MLLMs, shedding light on their strengths and weaknesses in WSI analysis, and prove the effectiveness of our WSI-LLaVA.

3.1
Pathological Capabilities

Morphological Analysis.

To assess the morphological analysis capability of WSI MLLMs, the four hierarchical pathological tasks are introduced for a comprehensive evaluation, ranging from global to local perspectives. These tasks include global morphology description, key diagnostic description, regional structure description, and specific feature description. Each category is designed to capture distinct pathological aspects, enhancing the dataset’s comprehensiveness while providing clinically relevant insights.

1) Global Morphology Description
evaluates the ability to provide a comprehensive overview of WSIs, establishing a context for global analysis. We construct the open-ended VQA pairs by a question with fixed content and a global morphological description. The question asks for the overall description of the WSI. The global morphological description is extracted from our WSI-report pairs, which should capture the general tissue architecture and overall tumor presentation, ensuring a broad understanding of the slide’s features.

2) Key Diagnostic Description
focuses on the evaluation of crucial morphological features vital for accurate diagnosis, linking specific observations to their clinical implications. Each open-ended VQA pair includes a question about key morphological features for diagnosis and its answer. We construct the answer by extracting content related to the cancer type’s diagnosis from the WSI report.

3) Regional Structure Description
aims to evaluate the ability of broad observations in specific regions, such as
tumor infiltration
,
tissue architecture
, and
cellular morphology
. We construct both open-ended and close-ended (multiple-choice questions, MCQs) VQA pairs, which include a question about a specific region and its answer. The answer is obtained by extracting relevant descriptions for the specific region from the WSI report.

4) Specific Feature Description
evaluates detailed morphological examinations of specific aspects (vascular invasion, gland formation patterns, cellular pleomorphism, etc.). We create both open-ended and close-ended (MCQs) VQA pairs about specific features, with answers indicating their presence in WSI reports.

Diagnosis.
To evaluate diagnostic capabilities, we examine four fundamental tasks:
histological typing
,
grading
,
molecular subtyping
, and
staging
. For interpretable and accurate diagnosis, each result includes both the answer and explanatory morphological analysis.

1) Histological Typing
evaluates the ability of histological type classification. It includes both open-ended and close-ended (MCQs) VQA pairs with a question and its answer, where the answer is obtained from the WSI report.

2) Grading
assesses the ability to classify the grade with both open-ended and close-ended (MCQs) VQA pairs, where each VQA pair includes a question about grading and its answer obtained from the WSI report.

3) Molecular Subtyping
evaluates identification of specific subtypes based on histological typing and immunohistochemical data. We create open-ended VQA pairs with questions containing histological typing and immunohistochemical data, with answers from original data labels.

4) Staging
assesses prediction of Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM)
[
24
]
labels based on clinical scenarios. Both open-ended and close-ended (MCQs) VQA pairs present clinical information translated from TNM classifications, requiring inference of TNM classification from the provided context.

Treatment Planing.

In the context of treatment planning, WSI-Bench includes three pathological tasks, including
prognosis
,
treatment recommendations
, and
report generation
. To imitate real-world settings, we include both WSI and its comprehensive information like diagnostic and immunohistochemical results for treatment planning.

1) Treatment recommendations
evaluate treatment suggestion capabilities. We create open-ended VQA pairs with questions including diagnostic results (histological typing, grading, staging) and immunohistochemical data. GPT-4o generates initial recommendations based on WSI reports, refined by clinical experts.

2) Prognosis
evaluates prediction of disease outcomes (good or bad). We create Yes/No VQA pairs with questions containing diagnostic and immunohistochemical data. GPT-4o generates initial prognosis results, refined by clinical experts.

Report Generation.

Report generation assesses the ability to create reports from input WSIs. Ground-truth reports are extracted from original pathology reports using GPT-4o. We exclude content not observable in WSIs, retaining only morphological descriptions and diagnostic results (histological typing and grading).

3.3
WSI Metrics

Although NLU metrics are widely used to evaluate open-ended language tasks, they cannot accurately assess performance in pathology due to its complex and often similar terminology, and the scores themselves lack interpretability
[
14
,
22
]
. Thus, we introduce two WSI metrics for open-ended questions:
WSI-Precision
and
WSI-Relevance
.The detailed evaluation workflow for these metrics can be found in Appendix Section
Fig.
9

WSI-Precision
measures the accuracy of model responses by comparing them directly to claims derived from the ground-truth (GT) response. It involves three main steps. 1)
Claims Extraction
: We extract distinct claims from the GT response using GPT-4o to break down complex medical information into assessable units. 2)
Individual Score Calculation and Interpretation
: We compare each extracted claim with the generated response using our scoring criterion. We assign Score 1 for complete accuracy with perfect alignment to established facts; Score 0.7 for mostly correct information with minor discrepancies that do not alter clinical significance; Score 0.3 for significant errors in critical content areas affecting the core message; and Score 0 for completely incorrect or unrelated information. Each score is accompanied by a corresponding explanation that justifies the reasoning behind the score. 3)
Final Score Calculation
: We calculate the overall WSI-Precision score by averaging all individual claim scores.

:
Overview of our gigapixel
WSI-LLaVA
framework with a three-stage training approach for cross-modal alignment and pathological tasks learning.

WSI-Relevance
measures the alignment of each claim in the model’s responses with the ground truth to ensure their relevance. This metric uses a similar three-step process. The detailed step-by-step procedures, prompt designs, and implementation specifics for both WSI-Precision and WSI-Relevance metrics are provided in Appendix Section
4
.

Reliability Validation.

To evaluate the reliability of our proposed WSI metrics, we randomly sample 2% of open-ended responses for assessment by five medical experts. Results show strong alignment between automated metrics and human evaluation (Pearson correlation: 0.87 for WSI-Precision, 0.82 for WSI-Relevance). Further details are provided in Appendix Section
4.3
.

4
WSI-LLaVA

To enhance the explainability, we introduce a
WSI-LLaVA
framework with a three-stage training approach for gigapixel WSI analysis (Fig.
4
). Trained on the training set of WSI-Bench, it can first provide morphological findings related to the query before presenting the final answer, significantly enhancing model explainability. To mitigate the cross-modal gap, the three-stage training approach includes a WSI-text alignment, feature space alignment, and task-specific instruction tuning. In the first stage, we integrate a patch-level encoder using Prov-GigaPath
[
32
]
’s tile-level encoder (pre-trained with DINOv2
[
21
]
) to process
256\times 256
pixel patches, and a slide-level encoder based on LongNet
[
8
]
to generate global features. These are aligned with textual features encoded by Bio_ClinicalBERT
[
1
]
through contrastive learning on WSI-report pairs. In the second stage, we combine the pre-trained WSI encoder with a projection layer and LLM, freezing the pre-trained models while training only the projection layer on WSI-report pairs. In the last stage, we freeze WSI encoder while training the projection layer and LLM on all tasks in WSI-Bench. Please see Appendix Section
5
for more details.

5.3
Ablation Studies

We conduct ablation studies to verify our WSI encoder and three-stage training scheme (Appendix Table
2
). Without the WSI encoder, performance drops across all pathological capabilities, with overall average decreasing from 68.0% to 63.2% (-5%), demonstrating its effectiveness for WSI feature extraction. For our training approach, removing the WSI-text alignment stage reduces overall performance by 2.2%, while ablating both this and the feature space alignment stage decreases performance by 3.3%, confirming each stage’s contribution to cross-modal alignment.

5.4
External Validation

To assess WSI-LLaVA’s generalization capability, we performed zero-shot external validation on three datasets: WSI-VQA dataset, SlideBench(BCNB), and CPTAC-NSCLC. In Table
6
, WSI-LLaVA consistently outperforms existing methods across all datasets, showing robust generalization to non-TCGA sources. The performance gap widens significantly on tasks requiring detailed morphological interpretation, with WSI-LLaVA achieving 46.3% vs. second-best 30.0% for histological grading and 29.2% vs. second-best 18.1% for molecular subtyping. These results indicate WSI-LLaVA’s strong morphological understanding enables more accurate zero-shot diagnostic assessments.

5.5
Comparison of NLU Metrics and WSI Metrics

Table
5
compares pathology report generation performance using traditional NLU and proposed WSI metrics. While Hist-Gen and WSI-LLaVA showed similar NLU scores (BLEU-1: 0.531 vs. 0.521, ROUGE-L: 0.536 vs. 0.534), their clinical accuracy differed significantly. Hist-Gen incorrectly reported “no evidence of vascular invasion or necrosis” despite their presence in ground truth. WSI metrics better reflected clinical accuracy, with WSI-LLaVA scoring much higher (WSI-Precision: 0.8) than Hist-Gen (0.233). This reveals NLU metrics’ limitations in prioritizing surface similarity and n-gram matching over factual correctness, emphasizing the need for domain-specific metrics that evaluate clinical accuracy rather than linguistic similarity in computational pathology.

Conclusion

This work introduces WSI-Bench, the first morphology-aware benchmark containing 180k VQA pairs from 9,850 WSIs across 30 cancer types, alongside WSI-LLaVA, an MLLM for WSI analysis. Through three-stage training, WSI-LLaVA bridges the gap between WSIs and text, enabling detailed morphological reasoning and explainability. WSI-LLaVA outperforms existing methods across all pathological tasks on both internal and external datasets, while our WSI metrics provide more clinically relevant assessment than NLU metrics. By improving morphological understanding and explainability, WSI-LLaVA represents significant advancement toward trustworthy AI assistants for computational pathology.

Discussion

From the above results, three key insights have been deduced as follows:

1)
WSI-LLaVA demonstrates superior morphological understanding across all dimensions.
As illustrated in Fig.
5
, our model consistently outperforms existing approaches in critical morphological analysis tasks. WSI-LLaVA achieves notable advantages in specific feature detection (0.76 vs. 0.752 for Quilt-LLaVA), cellular morphology analysis (0.46 vs. 0.404 for WSI-VQA), and infiltration pattern recognition (0.45 vs. 0.40 for Quilt-LLaVA). The performance gap is particularly pronounced when compared with general-purpose models like GPT-4o, which averages only 0.193 across morphological tasks versus WSI-LLaVA’s 0.473. This superiority stems from our model’s dual-encoder architecture that effectively captures both local details and global context, combined with a three-stage training approach that ensures proper alignment between visual and textual representations. Such comprehensive morphological understanding directly translates to improved diagnostic accuracy, as demonstrated in our qualitative analysis in Table
4
.

2)
Morphological understanding enables robust cross-dataset generalization.
External validation results reveal a striking pattern: models with stronger morphological capabilities demonstrate substantially better generalization to unseen datasets. This relationship is most evident in tasks requiring nuanced morphological assessment, where WSI-LLaVA outperforms the next best model by substantial margins on histological grading (46.33% vs. 23.11%) and molecular subtyping (29.2% vs. 18.1%). Unlike previous approaches that may learn dataset-specific correlations, WSI-LLaVA’s focus on fundamental morphological principles provides a more transferable form of knowledge. This finding has significant implications for clinical deployment, suggesting that morphology-aware models can maintain consistent performance across diverse institutional settings and patient populations without requiring extensive retraining or adaptation.

3)
Traditional NLU metrics fail to capture clinical accuracy in pathology language tasks.
Table
5
shows that both Hist-Gen and WSI-LLaVA achieve similar scores on standard NLU metrics (e.g., BLEU-1: 0.531 vs. 0.521, ROUGE-L: 0.536 vs. 0.534), despite evident differences in their clinical accuracy. This is because traditional NLU metrics focus on surface similarity, such as lexical overlap, rather than the correctness and relevance of key pathological findings. For example, Hist-Gen’s response—”There is no evidence of vascular invasion …”—is structurally similar to the ground truth and thus receives high NLU metric scores, but its meaning is completely opposite. In contrast, WSI-LLaVA accurately identifies these features, achieving superior WSI metrics (WSI-P: 0.800, WSI-R: 0.830). This demonstrates the limitations of conventional NLU metrics in evaluating pathology language tasks, where capturing clinically relevant information is essential. Our proposed WSI-Precision and WSI-Relevance metrics address these limitations by focusing on factual correctness and clinical relevance rather than linguistic similarity. These metrics provide a more reliable and clinically meaningful assessment of model performance in pathological contexts.
WSI-LLaVA demonstrates substantially higher clinical precision and relevance (WSI-Precision: 0.800 vs. 0.233, WSI-Relevance: 0.830 vs. 0.520), highlighting the inability of traditional metrics to reflect clinical utility.

This discrepancy stems from three key limitations of conventional metrics:

1)
Surface Similarity
: High word overlap between generated text and references inflates scores without considering clinical accuracy. For example, phrases like ”Microscopic observation reveals poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma” appear in both Hist-Gen and WSI-LLaVA, leading to similar scores despite differences in factual correctness.

2)
n-gram Matching
: Shared terms such as ”nuclear pleomorphism” and ”prominent nucleoli” contribute to high scores, even when these terms are used in incorrect or contradictory contexts.

3)
Semantic Insensitivity
: Conventional metrics fail to penalize clinically significant errors, such as Hist-Gen’s incorrect statement that ”there is no evidence of vascular invasion or necrosis,” which directly contradicts the reference text.

Our proposed WSI-Precision and WSI-Relevance metrics address these limitations by focusing on factual correctness and clinical relevance rather than linguistic similarity. These metrics provide a more reliable and clinically meaningful assessment of model performance in pathological contexts.

3.3
Reverse-Engineer Scheme

To enrich morphological descriptions, we introduce a reverse-engineer scheme to provide a morphological description extension. It uses diagnostic labels to identify the corresponding morphological characteristics according to tumor classification standards like the Nottingham grading system. This extension is included in the WSI reports that are then refined by clinical experts.
This reverse-engineering process is supported by the breadth of medical literature included in GPT-4o’s training data, ensuring a high level of precision that mirrors the expertise of professional pathologists.

Specifically, we take the instance of breast cancer where reports utilize the Nottingham grading system
[
7
]
, a method frequently used in TCGA datasets. The grading system assesses three critical aspects: gland formation, nuclear pleomorphism, and mitotic count, assigning each a score from 1 to 3. Consider a report that assigns a score of 3 for both gland formation and nuclear pleomorphism. This high score indicates minimal gland formation and substantial variation in nuclear size and shape, suggestive of aggressive tumor characteristics. Furthermore, if the pathology report confirms a diagnosis of invasive ductal carcinoma, it characterizes the cancer cells as having breached duct walls and forming invasive clusters. Such diagnostic details are crucial as they provide clear visual markers that can be directly observed in WSIs. We incorporate these specific findings into our dataset to enrich the morphological descriptions, ensuring that they reflect real-world pathological assessments and are tightly aligned with established diagnostic criteria.

Table
6
presents an example of the Nottingham grading system applied to breast cancer, a method derived from the WHO Classification of Tumors
[
7
]
. This system is recognized for its rigor and authority in classifying tumor severity. It systematically scores three critical histological features: gland formation, nuclear pleomorphism, and mitotic count. Each feature is evaluated based on specific morphological characteristics observable in tissue samples. The scores correlate directly with these features, ensuring that the derived descriptions are not only standardized but also clinically meaningful. By adhering to this methodology, the descriptions generated are both accurate reflections of the histopathological findings and relevant for clinical assessment and treatment planning.

To address
hallucinations
, we generate three versions of each report and use another LLM to verify if the content aligns with the original reports. The model performs five iterations of selection, and if one version is selected three or more times, it is deemed accurate. If none achieve this threshold, a pathologist reviews and selects the appropriate content. Furthermore, to avoid the pitfalls of incomplete filtering, especially concerning immunohistochemical data and macroscopic descriptions, we use regular expressions to identify and exclude 30 keywords, with any flagged text revised by a pathologist.

3.5
Expert Review and Quality Control

To ensure medical accuracy, clinical applicability, and educational effectiveness of the generated VQA pairs, we employed a structured expert review process with a proportional sampling strategy due to the extensive dataset size. The expert panel comprised two senior pathologists and five junior physicians with diverse clinical experience.

The detailed quality control workflow was systematically organized as follows:

•

Sampling Strategy:
For open-ended VQA pairs, we randomly selected 10% of the generated pairs for thorough expert review. This sampling aimed to statistically ensure at least 90% accuracy across all pairs. For close-ended VQA pairs, we randomly reviewed 5% to verify their quality.

•

Medical Accuracy Check:
Senior pathologists rigorously validated the sampled pairs by cross-checking the diagnostic accuracy and alignment between answers and visual evidence presented in Whole Slide Images (WSIs), strictly adhering to established clinical guidelines and diagnostic criteria.

•

Clinical Relevance Evaluation:
Both senior and junior reviewers assessed the clinical practicality of sampled VQA pairs, ensuring that they reflected realistic clinical scenarios frequently encountered in pathological diagnosis.

•

Educational Clarity Review:
Junior physicians specifically evaluated whether the VQA pairs were clearly phrased, comprehensible, and effective as educational resources suitable for learners at varying stages of medical training.

If significant inaccuracies or issues were identified during the review, prompts were adjusted accordingly, and affected VQA pairs were regenerated. For close-ended pairs, we randomly reviewed 5% using the same rigorous criteria. This structured sampling and iterative review ensured high-quality, clinically accurate, and educationally valuable VQA pairs across the entire dataset.

WSI-Precision

WSI-Precision measures the accuracy of model responses by comparing them directly to claims derived from the ground-truth response. As shown in Table

Individual Score Calculation.

With the extracted claims, we compare them with the generated response using our scoring criterion, which is defined as follows:

•

Score 1:
The information in the model’s response is completely accurate with respect to the claim. The response perfectly aligns with established facts, correctly addressing all elements of the claim.

•

Score 0.7:
The information is mostly correct and closely aligns with the claim, with minor discrepancies that do not fundamentally alter the truth or clinical significance of the response.

•

Score 0.3:
The claim is acknowledged, but the response contains significant errors in critical content areas, such as the degree of differentiation or malignancy status, affecting the core message.

•

Score 0:
The information in the model’s response is completely incorrect or unrelated to the claim, indicating a fundamental misunderstanding or misrepresentation of the clinical facts.

Following this scoring criterion, we design the prompt to guide GPT-4o to compute the individual score for each claim, as demonstrated in Fig.

Final Score Calculation.

With the individual score for each claim, we calculate the overall WSI-Precision score by averaging all the individual claim scores.

5.1
Network Architecture

Our model’s network architecture is divided into three components: a WSI encoder, a text encoder, a projection layer, and a large language model (LLM). This design enables efficient processing of gigapixel pathology WSI data.

WSI Encoder.
WSI Encoder, is responsible for extracting meaningful features from gigapixel pathology images, consisting of a patch-level encoder and a slide-level encoder. The patch-level encoder employs Prov-GigaPath
[
32
]
’s tile-level encoder, which is specifically designed for pathology data. The tile-level encoder is pre-trained using the DINOv2
[
21
]
framework on 1.3 billion
256\times 256
pathology image tiles derived from over 170,000 WSIs spanning 31 major tissue types. Its task is to encode each
256\times 256
image tile, extracting efficient feature representations at the patch level. The slide-level encoder is based on the LongNet
[
8
]
architecture. LongNet is an improved Transformer that utilizes dilated self-attention mechanisms to reduce the computational complexity of traditional Transformers from O(n
2
) to a more manageable range. This enables the model to efficiently aggregate features from tens of thousands or even hundreds of thousands of tiles, capturing global spatial contexts while minimizing computational costs.

Text Encoder.
We use Bio_ClinicalBERT
[
1
]
as our text encoder which is a BERT
[
6
]
model pre-trained on biomedical and clinical text to optimize its performance in these domains.

Projection Layer.
To achieve cross-modal alignment, a projection layer is designed between the WSI encoder and the LLM. The projection layer consists of a two-layer multi-layer perceptron (MLP) module, which maps visual features into a feature space compatible with the language model.

Large Language Model.
We use Vicuna-7b-v1.5
[
34
]
as the pre-trained model for the large language model, which is a highly efficient and powerful open-source language model tailored for dialogue generation tasks. Through fine-tuning, Vicuna demonstrates exceptional adaptability to various medical scenarios, generating accurate and natural diagnostic reports.

5.2
Training Details

The training process is divided into three stages, i.e. WSI-text alignment, feature space alignment, and task-specific instruction tuning.

Stage 1: WSI-Text Alignment.

The learning rate is set to 0.001, with a batch size of 64. During this stage, only a two-layer MLP projection layer is trained to align WSI and text features, while both the WSI encoder and text encoder remain frozen. The training runs for 50 epochs, with a temperature parameter set to 0.02.

Stage 2: Feature Space Alignment.

In this stage, both the WSI encoder and the LLM are frozen, and only the projection layer is trained. The learning rate is set to 1e-3, with 1 training epoch and a global batch size of 256. The maximum sequence length is set to 2048, weight decay is disabled (set to 0), and the warmup ratio is configured as 0.03 to ensure a smooth start to training.

Stage 3: Task-Specific Instruction Tuning.

In this stage, we freeze the WSI encoder and train the projection layer along with the LLM. The learning rate is set to 2e-5, with 1 training epoch and a global batch size of 128. The maximum sequence length is set to 2,048, weight decay is set to 0, and the warmup ratio is maintained at 0.03. We utilize LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) with a rank of 128 and an alpha value of 256 for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, enabling adaptation to the target dataset with minimal additional parameters. To enhance computational efficiency, we use DeepSpeed with ZeRO-3 optimization for distributed training. Additionally, BF16 precision is adopted, and TensorFloat32 is enabled to accelerate matrix operations while maintaining numerical stability.
