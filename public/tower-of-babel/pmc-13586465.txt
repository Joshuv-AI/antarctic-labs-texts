Undetected Gastric Angiodysplastic Lesion Causing Obscure Gastrointestinal Bleeding
Authors: Donghyoun Lee, Heung Up Kim, Hyun Joo Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obscure gastrointestinal bleeding (OGIB) has traditionally been defined to as bleeding originating from the small intestine that is beyond the reach of esophagogastroduodenoscopy and colonoscopy. However, gastric lesions associated with chronic intermittent bleeding may be undetected if there is no overt bleeding during initial endoscopic examination, resulting in a diagnosis of OGIB and repeated complex follow-up investigations. Vascular lesions, including angiodysplasia, Dieulafoy’s lesion, and small vascular ectasias, are major causes of chronic intermittent gastrointestinal bleeding. Hematemesis is an important clue for identifying the upper gastrointestinal tract as the source of anemia. Even when these vascular lesions are small and undetected, or not recognized as foci of bleeding during the initial endoscopy, confirmation of interval changes in size and morphology on follow-up endoscopy may provide an opportunity for definitive treatment of the lesion. Herein, we report a case of a patient with chronic anemia accompanied by recurrent syncope in whom no bleeding focus was identified on the initial endoscopic examination. After the patient had overt hematemesis, upper gastrointestinal bleeding was strongly suspected. Follow-up end oscopy revealed an angiodysplastic lesion with interval changes in size and morphology, which was recognized as the source of bleeding, and it was successfully treated endoscopically.

INTRODUCTION

Upper gastrointestinal bleeding is a major cause of chronic anemia. When hematemesis or melena is present and a lesion is identified on esophagogastroduodenoscopy, immediate therapeutic intervention can be performed. However, in the absence of overt bleeding or distinct mucosal alterations, such as those seen in ulcerative or polypoid diseases, lesions may be overlooked during endoscopic examination.

Obscure gastrointestinal bleeding (OGIB) includes intermittent bleeding from lesions that may not be visible during endoscopy when not actively bleeding [1]. Although OGIB is generally used to refer to bleeding originating from the small intestine beyond the reach of esophagogastroduodenoscopy and colonoscopy, vascular lesions arising in the stomach—such as angiodysplasia (AD), Dieulafoy’s lesion (DL), and small vascular lesions—are also characterized by intermittent bleeding. These lesions therefore represent a significant diagnostic challenge in identifying the source of upper gastrointestinal bleeding. When such lesions are small or not actively bleeding at the time of the initial endoscopic examination, they may go unrecognized and present clinically in a manner consistent with OGIB [2].

Herein, we report a case of a patient with chronic anemia of unknown origin accompanied by recurrent syncope, in whom hematemesis confirmed upper gastrointestinal bleeding. On repeat endoscopy, a gastric angiodysplastic lesion with marked changes in size and morphology was identified and successfully treated with endoscopic band ligation (EBL) and hemoclipping, resulting in resolution of anemia. We also discuss the presumed temporal morphological changes of DL according to its initial presentation and subsequent recurrent episodes.

CASE REPORT

A 53-year-old woman presented to the gastroenterology outpatient clinic with hematemesis. She reported her most recent episode occurring two days prior and brought a photograph showing bright red blood pooled in the toilet.

Four months earlier, she had visited the emergency department because of vomiting. At that time, her hemoglobin level was 6.2 g/dL. Nasogastric lavage yielded partially black-colored drainage; however, the patient insisted that the color resembled chocolate she had consumed the previous evening. Digital rectal examination revealed an almost empty rectum, making it difficult to determine the presence of melena. She received two units of packed red blood cells. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy and colonoscopy were performed, but no definite bleeding focus was identified (Fig. 1A). As she reported a history of chronic anemia, outpatient follow-up was arranged. One week later, her hemoglobin had increased to 10.8 g/dL. She was prescribed a proton pump inhibitor and oral iron supplementation; however, she was subsequently lost to follow-up.

Six weeks prior to the current visit, she experienced syncope followed by lower back pain and was evaluated at a local neurosurgical clinic. She was diagnosed with a herniated intervertebral disc and received conservative treatment. After her back pain improved and she resumed daily activities, she again experienced syncope four weeks prior to presentation while walking near a general hospital and was transported to its emergency department. Her hemoglobin level was approximately 5 g/dL. She received two units of packed red blood cells and underwent evaluation for iron-deficiency anemia, including endoscopic examination, but no cause was identified. She was referred to our gastroenterology clinic and admitted for further evaluation of suspected upper gastrointestinal bleeding.

On admission, her vital signs were stable. The conjunctivae were slightly pale. Abdominal examination was unremarkable except for a Pfannenstiel incision scar. Her medical history was significant for hypertension and longstanding chronic anemia. She had undergone hysterectomy for uterine myoma four years earlier. She was a homemaker.

Laboratory findings revealed a hemoglobin level of 6.8 g/dL, mean corpuscular volume of 77.3 fL, and mean corpuscular hemoglobin of 22.7 pg. The reticulocyte count was 2.91%. Blood chemistry tests and urinalysis showed no specific abnormalities. Serum iron was 23 μg/dL, total iron-binding capacity 444 μg/dL, ferritin 13.58 ng/mL, and vitamin B12 558.00 pg/mL. Direct antiglobulin test and indirect Coombs test were negative. Plasma hemoglobin was 0.6 mg/dL and haptoglobin 130 mg/dL. Chest radiography showed no active lesions in either lung field. Plain abdominal radiography and electrocardiography were unremarkable, with the latter demonstrating normal sinus rhythm. To exclude the possibility of oropharyngeal bleeding, consultations with the otorhinolaryngology and dental departments were performed, but no bleeding focus was identified.

Colonoscopy revealed no significant findings. On esophagogastroduodenoscopy, a slightly elevated, bright red angiodysplastic lesion measuring approximately 0.7 cm with a central depression resembling a scar was observed on the posterior wall of the lower gastric body (Fig. 1B). Review of the previous endoscopic images showed a small erythematous spot at the center of a slightly elevated area resembling a submucosal vein at the same location (Fig. 1A). At the current examination, the lesion appeared more prominently elevated and distinctly erythematous compared to the prior study, without a peripheral halo (Fig. 1B). Abdominal CT demonstrated a small but clearly enhancing nodular vascular structure in the arterial phase corresponding to the posterior wall of the lower gastric body. The lesion became less distinct during the portal venous phase (Fig. 2).

Although active bleeding was not observed during endoscopy, recent hematemesis had been documented. Given her longstanding history of chronic anemia with recurrent syncope requiring repeated transfusions, and the concordance between the angiodysplastic lesion observed on endoscopy and the nodular enhancing lesion identified on CT, endoscopic treatment of the lesion was planned.

Because a relatively large submucosal vascular lesion was suspected, argon plasma coagulation (APC) or electrocoagulation was considered to have a high likelihood of failure. The lesion appeared slightly elevated and was located in apparently normal surrounding gastric mucosa without significant fibrosis, making EBL a suitable therapeutic option. A single-use air-shooting band ligator, typically used for esophageal variceal ligation, was mounted on the tip of the endoscope. After positioning the endoscope over the lesion and applying sufficient suction to capture the gastric mucosa, a rubber band was deployed (Fig. 1C, upper).

After removal of the ligation device and reinsertion of the endoscope for inspection, severe retching occurred, causing dislodgement of the rubber band and resulting in active bleeding. A second band ligation was promptly performed. Finally, hemoclips were applied to both the proximal and distal sides of the banded polypoid lesion, and the procedure was completed (Fig. 1C, lower).

The patient was subsequently managed with iron supplementation, and her hemoglobin level gradually increased. Follow-up endoscopy performed two months later revealed a linear scar on the posterior wall of the lower gastric body corresponding to the previous intervention site (Fig. 1D). At the 6-month follow-up, her hemoglobin level had increased to 13 g/dL, and she reported no further dizziness or syncope. Iron supplementation was discontinued.

DISCUSSION

Acquired vascular malformations of the gastrointestinal tract include AD, DL, gastric antral vascular ectasia, and radiation-induced vascular ectasia [3]. AD is characterized by abnormally tortuous and dilated small blood vessels located in the mucosal and submucosal layers of the gastrointestinal tract, composed predominantly of endothelial cells with little or no smooth muscle component [4].

The pathogenesis of gastrointestinal AD is believed to involve vascular endothelial growth factor–dependent proliferation in the setting of chronic, low-grade intermittent obstruction of the submucosal veins [5]. Angiogenesis is an essential biological process that increases tissue vascularity in response to hypoxia or ischemia and results from an imbalance between pro-angiogenic and anti-angiogenic factors [6]. The process is thought to begin with dilation of the submucosal veins, followed by capillary ectasia, and ultimately progression to arteriovenous shunting [7]. During this sequence, various angiogenic factors contribute to the development of AD [8,9].

von Willebrand factor (vWF), a well-known hemostatic factor, plays an important role in maintaining vascular integrity and is believed to regulate angiogenesis through multiple pathways [10]. In severe aortic stenosis, shear stress–induced degradation of vWF can lead to a bleeding tendency and the development of gastrointestinal AD, resulting in gastrointestinal bleeding; this condition often improves after surgical correction of the aortic stenosis [11]. Endoscopic therapy remains the most practical treatment option. However, when endoscopic access is technically unfeasible or when endoscopic treatment fails, pharmacologic therapies such as somatostatin analogues, thalidomide, or bevacizumab may be considered [9].

Another important diagnostic consideration in this case is DL. DL is characterized by a caliber-persistent artery located in the submucosal layer in close proximity to the mucosal surface. Even a small mucosal defect may expose the artery, leading to rupture and massive bleeding. DL most commonly occurs within 6 cm of the gastroesophageal junction, particularly along the lesser curvature of the cardia or upper body [12]. The proposed mechanism underlying this vascular abnormality is as follows. While most submucosal arteries of the stomach arise from the submucosal vascular plexus, arteries along the lesser curvature tend to originate directly from the arterial arcade running along the external lesser curvature of the stomach. As a result, a caliber-persistent artery may develop within the submucosal layer [13].

Pathologically, DL is characterized by an abnormally large caliber-persistent artery measuring approximately 1–3 mm in diameter, which follows a tortuous course within the submucosa. It typically protrudes through a small mucosal defect measuring 2–5 mm in diameter, and the base of the defect often demonstrates fibrinoid necrosis [13]. Clinically, DL is more common in elderly men and is frequently associated with the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or warfarin. It often presents as massive gastrointestinal bleeding in patients without a prior history of gastrointestinal disease, and recurrence is possible [13]. Because of its propensity to cause severe bleeding, surgical treatment was previously the standard approach; however, endoscopic mechanical hemostatic techniques are now widely used as first-line therapy.

Endoscopic treatment options for AD include APC, EBL, heater probe therapy, electrocoagulation, laser photocoagulation, and epinephrine injection therapy, among which APC is the most commonly preferred modality [14]. The overall success rate of endoscopic hemostasis is high, approximately 85%; however, the overall rebleeding rate reaches up to 42%, with EBL demonstrating a lower rebleeding rate compared with APC [14]. DL frequently presents with massive bleeding and was therefore historically managed surgically. More recently, however, mechanical endoscopic therapies such as EBL or hemoclipping have been recommended as first-line treatment [15]. Accordingly, when an isolated AD is considered to carry a high risk of bleeding or when the possibility of DL cannot be excluded, mechanical therapeutic modalities may be preferable.

Although numerous cases of AD or DL treated with EBL or hemoclipping have been reported, the key message of the present case is that early-stage lesions or temporarily hemostatic lesions without overt bleeding may be overlooked during endoscopic examination.

In this case, the absence of a characteristic peripheral halo makes the diagnosis of typical AD less convincing. Furthermore, the history of recurrent syncope suggests intermittent episodes of massive bleeding, which clinically favors the possibility of DL. In addition, CT clearly demonstrated a nodular enhancing lesion within the gastric wall corresponding to the location of the vascular lesion identified on endoscopy, a finding that is more suggestive of DL than AD.

However, most reported cases and studies of DL describe endoscopic images obtained after the onset of bleeding or pathological findings following surgical resection, and little is known about the gross morphology of the lesion prior to bleeding. DL has been described as a small mucosal defect with a protruding vessel; however, if one attempts to infer its prebleeding morphology, it is plausible that the lesion initially presents as a small submucosal tumor–like elevation covered by normal-appearing mucosa without erythema or visible vascular changes. After an episode of massive bleeding, the lesion may appear as a small erosion, and with repeated cycles of bleeding and healing, it may gradually evolve into a scar-like appearance.

In the present case, both endoscopic examinations were performed after recent bleeding events. We cautiously suggest the possibility that, following active bleeding from a DL with subsequent spontaneous hemostasis, the small mucosal defect may have appeared as a vascular erythematous lesion resembling AD.

APC or electrocautery is optimal for treating vessels exposed on the mucosal surface or exposed submucosal vessels; however, it is technically challenging to eradicate submucosal vascular tissue that is not externally exposed. Therefore, even if the surface lesion were consistent with AD, EBL may be the most effective modality for en bloc treatment of the underlying submucosal vascular component.

Nevertheless, one limitation of EBL is the potential for band dislodgement due to retching, which was observed in the present case. After a second EBL was performed, the ligated lesion was secured with two hemoclips, resulting in stable and successful treatment thereafter.

Vascular lesions such as AD and DL frequently present with chronic intermittent bleeding, and the resulting anemia may lead to a high likelihood of the lesion being overlooked during the initial endoscopic examination, particularly when the lesion is small. In such cases, a history of hematemesis is the most important clue for recognizing upper gastrointestinal bleeding. Therefore, during follow-up, patients should be educated to seek immediate medical attention and undergo evaluation for active bleeding when symptoms suggestive of bleeding, such as hematemesis or syncope, occur.

Even if no definite bleeding focus is identified on the initial endoscopy, follow-up endoscopic examination is advisable. If interval changes in the size or morphology of a lesion are observed on repeat endoscopy, that site may represent a source of intermittent bleeding.

Because the morphology of DL in the absence of overt bleeding is not well established, when an angiodysplastic lesion is identified, clinicians should consider not only AD but also the possibility of DL at various temporal stages, and plan management accordingly. Furthermore, if CT reveals a submucosal or intramural nodular enhancing vascular lesion, initial treatment with EBL may be a reasonable therapeutic approach.
