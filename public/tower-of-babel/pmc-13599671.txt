Evaluation of malpractice fears and defensive approaches in orthodontists' clinical practice
Authors: Gönül Dinç, Hüseyin Melik Böyük
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the impact of medical malpractice litigation and associated defensive medicine practices on the clinical decision-making of orthodontists.Materials and MethodsThis study was conducted between December 2025 and February 2026. Questionnaires were emailed to 2,526 orthodontists registered in the database of the Turkish Orthodontic Society (TOD), and 252 orthodontists completed the survey. Of the participants, 64.3% (n = 162) were female and 35.7% (n = 90) were male. In addition to demographic questions, participants completed the Fear of Medical Error Scale (FMS), the Defensive Medicine Practice Attitude Scale (DMPAS), and clinical preference questions specific to orthodontic practice. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsOverall, 51.2% (n = 129) of the participants exhibited a high level of fear of medical malpractice. Orthodontists aged over 35 years had significantly lower FMS scores than younger orthodontists. According to the DMPAS, 53.6% of orthodontists exhibited a high level of defensive medicine behavior, while 15.5% exhibited a very high level.ConclusionOrthodontists' clinical decision-making appears to be influenced by concerns about legal repercussions. To mitigate the legal pressure experienced by physicians, education in health law should be strengthened for physicians and other healthcare professionals, and medical malpractice legislation should be revised to provide a more balanced framework that also safeguards physicians' rights.

Introduction

The rapidly increasing number of malpractice claims in healthcare has become an important source of pressure on clinical decision-making (1). The term malpractice, derived from the Latin terms male (bad) and praxis (practice), refers to professional negligence or errors resulting from healthcare professionals' failure to meet the accepted standard of care. The legal and professional pressures associated with medical malpractice may lead clinicians to adopt defensive medicine practices (DMPs), defined as clinical strategies primarily intended to reduce the risk of potential litigation rather than to address patients' actual medical needs (2). In defensive medicine, clinical decisions may therefore be influenced not only by considerations of patient benefit but also by concerns regarding potential legal consequences (3).

Defensive medicine encompasses a range of clinical behaviors, including the use of tests and treatments with limited or no clinical necessity and the avoidance of patients or procedures perceived as high risk (4, 5, 6). In the literature, defensive medicine is generally categorized as positive or negative. Positive defensive medicine involves additional or unnecessary diagnostic tests, consultations, or treatments intended to reduce legal exposure, whereas negative defensive medicine involves avoiding high-risk patients, procedures, or clinical situations (4, 6). Such behaviors may affect not only clinical effectiveness and healthcare costs but also patient safety and the patient–physician relationship (1, 7).

A heightened perception of legal risk may introduce additional uncertainty into clinicians' decision-making processes (8, 9), potentially leading to increased use of diagnostic investigations or consultations and greater avoidance of high-risk cases (9, 10). Hellyer and Radford reported that increased perceptions of risk may influence professional behavior and promote more cautious approaches in high-risk clinical situations (11). Accordingly, defensive medicine has been discussed in both the medical and dental literature in relation to its potential effects on clinical decision-making, healthcare utilization, and costs (5, 6).

Defensive dentistry can be considered the application of defensive medicine principles to dental practice (6). Although defensive behaviors in dentistry have been described in previous studies, their prevalence and determinants across broader populations remain insufficiently characterized (1, 6). Orthodontics may be particularly susceptible to such concerns because of its relatively long treatment duration, high aesthetic expectations, and reliance on individualized biomechanical planning (8, 10). Adverse outcomes and complications, including unmet treatment expectations, periodontal problems, and root resorption, may contribute to patient dissatisfaction and potentially increase the risk of malpractice claims (12, 13). Li *et al.* reported that patient dissatisfaction was the most frequent cause of litigation in orthodontic practice (14). Discrepancies between expected and actual treatment duration and between treatment outcomes and patient expectations have also been identified as relevant factors in orthodontic malpractice cases. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of clinical risk management in orthodontic practice (4, 14).

Defensive attitudes and behaviors have most commonly been investigated using survey-based, cross-sectional study designs, which have inherent methodological limitations (5, 6). Furthermore, differences in healthcare systems, medico-legal environments, and cultural factors may contribute to variations in defensive practices among countries and influence the extent to which concerns about litigation affect clinical decision-making (6, 9).

To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has specifically investigated the relationship between fear of malpractice, defensive medicine practices, and routine clinical decision-making among orthodontists. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the prevalence of defensive medicine practices among orthodontists, examine their relationship with fear of malpractice, and evaluate their association with clinical decision-making. The findings may contribute to a better understanding of defensive behavior in orthodontic practice and provide evidence relevant to both clinical risk management and the medico-legal aspects of orthodontic care.

Materials and Methods

Research questions

In line with the stated objectives, the following research questions were formulated: Do levels of fear of malpractice and defensive medicine practices among orthodontists vary according to demographic and professional characteristics? What is the relationship between fear of malpractice and attitudes toward defensive medicine practices among orthodontists? Are defensive attitudes among orthodontists reflected in their day-to-day clinical practice?

Sample and procedure

This study was conducted between December 16, 2025, and February 2, 2026, and targeted a total of 2,526 orthodontists registered with the Turkish Orthodontic Society (TOD). Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Van Yüzüncü Yıl University Faculty of Medicine (Decision No. 2025/11-04). Data collection was conducted with official permission from the TOD by distributing the survey to email addresses registered in the association's database. A total of 252 participants completed the questionnaire in full and were included in the study.

Data collection instruments

The survey instrument used in the study consisted of four main sections.

The first section collected demographic and professional data through seven questions covering gender, age, marital status, professional experience, parental status, institution of employment, and academic title.

The second section consisted of the Fear of Malpractice Scale (FMS), a six-item, 5-point Likert-type scale originally developed by Katz et al. (15) to assess physicians' concerns regarding malpractice liability. Each item is scored from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree), and all items are positively coded. Total scores range from 6 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater fear of malpractice, which is categorized as low (score ≤15), moderate (15–20), or high (≥20). The Turkish validity and reliability of the scale, including its factor structure and internal consistency, were established by Uğrak and Işık (16).

The third section consisted of the Defensive Medicine Practices Attitude Scale (DMPAS), a 14-item, 5-point Likert-type scale developed and validated in Turkish by Başer et al. (6), who reported a two-factor structure comprising positive and negative defensive medicine practices and high internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.84). The scale comprises two subscales: Positive Defensive Medicine Practices (first nine items; score range, 9–45) and Negative Defensive Medicine Practices (last five items; score range, 5–25). Based on the total score (14–70), defensive attitudes are classified as weak (14–27), moderate (28–41), advanced (42–55), or highly advanced (56–70). As two items were not directly applicable to dentistry or orthodontics, participants were instructed, through a parenthetical note, to interpret these items within the context of orthodontic practice. The original scale also includes a four-item knowledge assessment section that is not included in the scoring. In the present study, this unscored section was replaced with a section specifically designed for orthodontists, entitled Orthodontic Practice-Specific Clinical Preference Questions.

The fourth section consisted of the Orthodontic Practice-Specific Clinical Preference Questions. Developed in consultation with orthodontists and designed to reflect routine clinical practice, this section comprised 11 questions, each with two response options: "I do not prefer" and "I prefer."

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, NY, Armonk, USA). Quantitative variables were summarized using descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum values, whereas qualitative variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test and box plots were used to assess the normality of the data.

For comparisons between two groups of normally distributed quantitative variables, Student's t-test was used. For comparisons involving three or more groups of normally distributed variables, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Bonferroni post hoc comparisons to identify the source of significant differences. For comparisons involving three or more groups of variables that did not conform to a normal distribution, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used, followed by Dunn's post hoc test to identify the groups responsible for significant differences. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate relationships between continuous variables when the assumptions for parametric analysis were met. Pearson's chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables.

To identify predictors of Fear of Malpractice Scale scores, stepwise multiple linear regression analysis was performed, with demographic and professional characteristics and Defensive Medicine Practices Attitude Scale subscale scores entered as predictor variables. Results were evaluated using 95% confidence intervals, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

The adequacy of the sample size was evaluated using GPower version 3.1. Based on the observed correlation between the Fear of Malpractice Scale and Defensive Medicine Practices Attitude Scale total scores (r = 0.725), with a two-tailed significance level of α = 0.05, a post hoc power analysis indicated that the sample of 252 participants provided a statistical power of approximately 100%.

Results

Descriptive characteristics of participants

Of the participants included in the study, 64.3% (n = 162) were female and 35.7% (n = 90) were male. Detailed distributions according to age, marital status, professional experience, parental status, institution of employment, and academic title are presented in Table 1.

Findings regarding Fear of Malpractice Scale (FMS) scores

Total scores on the Fear of Malpractice Scale ranged from 6 to 29, with a mean score of 19.07 ± 4.89. The internal consistency coefficient (Cronbach's α) was 0.864, indicating high internal consistency. Regarding the level of fear of malpractice, 20.2% (n = 51) of participants were classified as having a low level, 28.6% (n = 72) a moderate level, and 51.2% (n = 129) a high level of fear of malpractice (Table 2).

Comparisons of FMS scores according to participant characteristics revealed statistically significant differences according to age (p = 0.014) and workplace (p = 0.001) (Table 3). Post hoc analyses showed that participants aged over 35 years had significantly lower FMS scores than those aged 23–28 and 29–34 years (p < 0.05). With respect to workplace, participants working at public oral and dental health centers (ADSM; Ağız ve Diş Sağlığı Merkezi) and universities had significantly higher FMS scores than those working in private clinics (p <0.05).

Findings regarding the Defensive Medicine Practices Attitude Scale (DMPAS)

The distribution of participants' DMPAS scores is presented in Table 4. The mean Positive Defensive Medicine Practices subscale score was 28.88 ± 6.40, the mean Negative Defensive Medicine Practices subscale score was 16.33 ± 4.63, and the mean total DMPAS score was 45.21 ± 10.01. Overall, 53.6% (n = 135) of participants exhibited an advanced level of defensive attitude, while 15.5% (n = 39) exhibited a highly advanced level. Internal consistency analyses yielded Cronbach's α coefficients of 0.856 for the Positive DMP subscale, 0.923 for the Negative DMP subscale, and 0.911 for the total scale, indicating high internal consistency.

Comparisons of DMPAS scores according to participant characteristics are presented in Table 5. For the Positive DMP subscale, participants aged over 35 years and those with children had significantly higher scores. For the Negative DMP subscale, female participants, those aged 29–34 years, and those working at public oral and dental health centers (ADSM) or universities had significantly higher scores. Regarding total DMPAS scores, female participants and those under 35 years of age exhibited significantly higher overall defensive attitudes.

Correlation and regression analyses

The relationships between Fear of Malpractice Scale scores and Defensive Medicine Practices Attitude Scale scores are presented in Table 6. Statistically significant positive correlations were observed between FMS total scores and Positive DMP subscale scores (r = 0.677, p = 0.001), Negative DMP subscale scores (r = 0.633, p = 0.001), and DMPAS total scores (r = 0.725, p = 0.001). The effect size for the relationship between the total FMS and DMPAS scores was 0.851, and the statistical power was 100%.

The effects of participant characteristics and defensive medicine attitudes on FMS scores are presented in Table 7. Stepwise multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify predictors of FMS scores. The final regression model was statistically significant (F = 95.868, p = 0.001).

Positive defensive attitude, negative defensive attitude, and employment at a public oral and dental health center (ADSM) were retained as significant predictors of FMS scores. The final model explained 53.7% of the variance in FMS scores. Both positive and negative defensive attitudes were positively associated with FMS scores (β = 0.487 and β = 0.311, respectively; p < 0.01). Employment at an ADSM was also positively associated with FMS scores (β = 0.108, p < 0.05). Age and the other workplace categories were not retained in the final stepwise regression model.

Orthodontic practice-specific clinical preferences

The overall distribution of participants' clinical preferences is presented in Table 8. Examination of treatment preferences across clinical scenarios specific to orthodontic practice revealed considerable variation according to case type. The highest preference rate was observed for cases requiring multidisciplinary collaboration (96.4%, n = 243), followed by the management of impacted tooth eruption (91.7%, n = 231) and orthognathic surgery cases (71.4%, n = 180).

Treatment preferences for patients with cleft lip and palate (CLP) varied according to patient age. While 61.9% (n = 156) of participants indicated that they would not prefer to treat infants with CLP, the majority indicated a preference for treating children (61.9%, n = 156) and adults (60.7%, n = 153) with CLP.

The clinical scenarios most commonly avoided were the treatment of patients with periodontal problems and poor oral hygiene (83.3%, n = 210) and individuals with disabilities who were considered at risk of cooperation difficulties (78.6%, n = 198). In addition, only 40.5% (n = 102) of participants indicated a preference for treating patients in whom root shortening was detected on panoramic radiographs.

Overall, 42.9% (n = 108) of participants indicated a preference for treating patients with a history of infectious disease, while 51.2% (n = 129) indicated a preference for clear aligner therapy as a means of reducing chair-side time.

Discussion

The phenomenon of defensive medicine, which has been examined from various perspectives in the literature, broadly refers to clinical decisions influenced by concerns about legal liability in addition to medical necessity. It may involve the use of non-routine investigations, even in asymptomatic patients, to reduce the risk of malpractice allegations or to protect against rare adverse outcomes (17). Defensive medicine extends beyond individual clinical preferences and may also impose a substantial economic burden through increased healthcare expenditure (18). Similarly, defensive behaviors may be particularly evident among hospital-based physicians who perform procedures that are not essential to the course of disease or avoid high-risk interventions and complex cases (19).

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine fear of malpractice and defensive dentistry attitudes among orthodontists in Türkiye while also evaluating clinical preferences specific to orthodontic practice. Our findings suggest that fear of malpractice and associated defensive attitudes are prevalent among orthodontists and may influence their clinical decision-making.

More than half of the orthodontists participating in the present study (51.2%) exhibited a high level of fear of malpractice, while 53.6% exhibited an advanced level of defensive attitude. The high prevalence of fear of malpractice observed in our sample is consistent with findings reported in other fields of medicine and dentistry (1, 2). These findings may reflect the influence of medico-legal pressures within the healthcare system on orthodontic practice.

The mean fear of malpractice score in our study was 19.07 ± 4.89. Orthodontists younger than 35 years exhibited higher levels of fear of malpractice than their older colleagues. One possible explanation is that clinicians with less professional experience may feel less confident in managing complications, communicating with patients, and navigating medico-legal issues. Consistent with our findings, Misir et al. (1), in a study conducted in 2025, reported significantly greater fear of malpractice among dentists aged 29–35 years than among their more senior colleagues. Greater professional experience and confidence in complication management may therefore contribute to lower levels of malpractice-related fear.

In the gender-based analyses, female orthodontists had significantly higher negative defensive attitude and total defensive attitude scores than male orthodontists. This finding is consistent with those reported by Varol and Fındık (20) and Saruhan et al. (21). Previous studies have suggested that female physicians may exhibit greater perceptions of risk and a stronger tendency to avoid medico-legal conflict in the patient–physician relationship. The greater tendency toward negative defensive practices observed among female orthodontists in the present study may therefore reflect differences in perceived professional and legal risk.

Regarding workplace, orthodontists working at public oral and dental health centers (ADSM) and universities exhibited higher levels of fear of malpractice and defensive attitudes than those working in private clinics. Aktaş et al. (2) noted that high volumes of complex cases and hierarchical supervision in university hospitals may increase perceived pressure on physicians. Similarly, the higher defensive attitudes observed among research assistants at universities in the present study may be related to the combined effects of clinical training, heavy patient workloads, and concerns about errors and their potential consequences. Conversely, greater flexibility in patient selection and opportunities for more individualized patient communication in private practice may contribute to lower levels of malpractice-related concern.

Orthodontists with children exhibited significantly higher positive defensive attitude scores than those without children. This finding may indicate an association between family responsibilities and greater risk-averse professional behavior. Aktaş et al. (2) reported a greater tendency toward defensive medicine among married physicians with children, while Özata et al. (22) similarly found an association between having children and protective medical behaviors among dentists. Although the underlying mechanisms cannot be established from the present cross-sectional data, greater family responsibilities may contribute to heightened sensitivity to the potential professional and financial consequences of malpractice litigation.

The finding that 53.6% of orthodontists exhibited an advanced and 15.5% a highly advanced defensive attitude indicates that defensive practices are common among the orthodontists included in this study. The mean Positive Defensive Attitude score (28.88 ± 6.40) was higher than the mean Negative Defensive Attitude score (16.33 ± 4.63). However, because the positive and negative subscales contain different numbers of items and have different score ranges, their raw mean scores should not be interpreted as a direct comparison of the relative predominance of the two forms of defensive behavior. Nevertheless, positive defensive practices, such as additional investigations and detailed documentation, may contribute to increased healthcare utilization and costs, as previously discussed in the literature (23). Banaz and Balçık (24) similarly reported above-average attitudes in both positive and negative defensive medicine subscales.

Stepwise multiple linear regression analysis showed that positive defensive attitude, negative defensive attitude, and employment at an oral and dental health center (ADSM) were significantly associated with FMS scores, with the final model explaining 53.7% of the variance. Higher positive and negative defensive attitude scores were associated with higher fear of malpractice scores. This finding is consistent with reports by Misir et al. (1) and Toraman (25), who described an association between fear of malpractice and defensive behavior. Employment at an ADSM was also independently associated with higher FMS scores, which may be related to the patient volume and case mix encountered in public dental healthcare settings. The fact that age was not retained in the final stepwise model suggests that, after accounting for the other variables included in the model, defensive attitudes and workplace were more strongly associated with FMS scores than age.

The findings from the Orthodontic Practice-Specific Clinical Preference Questions further suggest that both clinical risk and concerns related to potential adverse outcomes may influence orthodontists' treatment preferences. Among the most frequently avoided clinical scenarios were patients with periodontal problems and poor oral hygiene (83.3%) and individuals with disabilities considered at risk of cooperation difficulties (78.6%). Such preferences may be associated with the greater complexity of treatment management, cooperation-related challenges, and increased risk of complications in these patient groups. The long-term nature of orthodontic treatment and its potential biological complications may similarly influence clinicians' willingness to accept certain cases. Only 40.5% of participants indicated a preference for treating patients with root shortening detected on panoramic radiographs. This finding may reflect awareness of the risk of further root resorption during orthodontic treatment and a more cautious approach to patients considered biologically vulnerable (26). However, because the present study did not directly assess participants' reasons for accepting or avoiding individual clinical scenarios, these preferences cannot be attributed exclusively to fear of malpractice. Overall, the findings suggest that clinical decision-making in orthodontic practice may be influenced by a combination of biological, clinical, ethical, and medico-legal considerations. Evidence-based clinical protocols for higher-risk patient groups may facilitate clinical decision-making while also supporting appropriate risk management.

The present study has several limitations. Although the survey was distributed to 2,526 orthodontists registered in the TOD database, only 252 completed the questionnaire. The voluntary nature of participation introduces the possibility of self-selection bias, as orthodontists who were more concerned about malpractice or more interested in the subject may have been more likely to participate. Furthermore, because the data were self-reported, responses may have been affected by social desirability or reporting bias, with participants potentially under-reporting or over-reporting their actual defensive behaviors.

Only a limited number of clinical scenarios, including cleft lip and palate, root resorption, and cooperation-related difficulties, were evaluated. The broader and more complex range of clinical situations encountered in orthodontic practice was beyond the scope of the present study. The number of questions was intentionally limited to reduce respondent burden and minimize the potential negative effect of questionnaire length on participation and completion.

Finally, although participants' institutions of employment were recorded, potential differences in patient profiles across geographical regions, as well as regional variations in medico-legal processes and oversight mechanisms, could not be evaluated separately.

Conclusion

Given the long-term nature of orthodontic treatment, its associated biological risks, and high patient expectations, establishing a balanced and sustainable medico-legal framework that safeguards both patient safety and clinicians' rights is essential to ensure that clinical decision-making remains primarily guided by scientific evidence and patient needs rather than concerns about potential litigation. The findings of the present study indicate that fear of malpractice is strongly associated with defensive medicine attitudes among orthodontists and may influence their clinical decision-making. Defensive practices may contribute to increased healthcare utilization and may also influence treatment preferences for certain high-risk patient groups, particularly patients with periodontal problems, individuals with disabilities, and those at risk of root resorption. Such tendencies may have implications for the accessibility, quality, and equity of orthodontic care. To mitigate malpractice-related pressure on clinicians, education in health law should be strengthened for physicians, dentists, and other healthcare professionals, while malpractice legislation should aim to achieve an appropriate balance between protecting patients' rights and safeguarding healthcare professionals.
