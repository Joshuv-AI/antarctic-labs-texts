Use of Artificial Intelligence in prostate MRI: A rapid scoping review highlighting limited evidence in screening context
Authors: Deependra Singh, Juan Pablo Salazar Gutiérrez, Olivier Rouviere, Francesco Giganti, Milagros Otero-García, Roderick C.N. van den Bergh, Monique J. Roobol, Lionne D.F. Venderbos, Sarah Collen, Hendrik van Poppel, Partha Basu, Arunah Chandran, PRAISE-U consortium members
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Evidence is limited on AI assisted prostate MRI screening.•We provided insight into the current evidence on existing role of AI in MRI interpretation.•Current AI algorithms demonstrate limited applicability in screening contexts.•We highlighted need for AI models trained in large, diverse MRI datasets from screening populations.•Findings guide development and safe integration of AI into future PCa screening.

Introduction

In December 2022, the council of the European Union (EU) updated cancer screening recommendations to include prostate cancer (PCa) screening [1]. The council recommended that member states evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of organised screening for PCa using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to make biopsy decisions. Given the increased burden of prostate cancer in Europe, it is reasonable to anticipate a greater demand for MRI following the expansion of PCa screening programs including the ongoing PRostate cancer Awareness and Initiative for Screening Europe (PRAISE-U) pilot project and the EU Joint Action on cancer screening (EUCanScreen) on PCa [2], [3]. MRI being the central component of a risk-stratified protocol further highlights the need for rapid and effective image acquisition and readings scans. Greater demand for MRI comes with greater challenges to the health system with workforce shortages, limited infrastructures, longer waiting time, among several others [4], [5], [6]. This is highlighted in a recent study on MRI capacity across PRAISE-U pilot sites where key differences existed across pilot sites in image quality and protocol acquisition, radiologist expertise and training and type of MRI equipment [7].

The use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) is seen as a potential tool to reduce the burden on the health system with its potential to assist radiologists with detecting abnormalities in MRI scans, enhance diagnostic accuracy, reduce inter-reader variability, reduce radiologists time, and support biopsy decision-making [8], [9], [10]. AI supported workflows have shown valuable evidence in assisting radiologists in MRI interpretations and decision-making in diagnostic settings [11], [12], [13]. A recent systematic review of 25 studies conducted in diagnostic settings that evaluated diagnostic accuracy of deep learning (DL) models on prostate MRI showed significant potential in improving PCa diagnosis on MRI [14]. MRI-based cancer screening presents opportunities to enhance performance and streamline clinical workflows, particularly in risk-stratified approaches [15], [16].

Given the increasing potential of AI to improve MRI performance, we aimed to synthesize evidence on use of AI in MRI readings for PCa screening in the general population. We focused on published studies that utilized AI tools in prostate MRI readings in asymptomatic men through a rapid scoping review.

Methods

We conducted a rapid scoping review of literature for evaluation of AI deployment into MRI in prostate cancer screening at the population level. We used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) statement [17], supplemented by review discussion and interpretation for future implications for population-based PCa screening. Scoping review methodology was chosen to understand the field of emerging evidence [18] on AI and prostate MRI, with considerations of various aspects of utilization of AI in MRI performance (interpretations and biopsy indications) in the screening setting. We streamlined the scope and focus of our review to enable a quicker evidence synthesis according to Cochrane recommendations on rapid reviews methods guidance [19]. As we expected very few studies conducted on AI implementation for MRI performance in screening settings, we extended this rapid scoping review from ‘what is already known’ to explore discussions on ‘key areas of considerations for expected expansion’ on the use of AI in MRI in PCa screening.

Search strategy and selection criteria

We used PubMed, Embase, Web of Science Core Collection, and Google Scholar for literature retrieval published up to June 2025 and written in English language. Grey literature was identified from topic-specific websites, clinical trial registration websites (clinicaltrials.gov, isrctn.com), and abstracts of recent European Urological Association (EAU) and American Urological Association congresses. Additionally, reference lists of appropriate publications were screened for potential further relevant publications. The search strategy includes terms related to AI, MRI, PCa, and screening. Core search terms included: “Artificial Intelligence”, “Machine Learning”, “Deep Learning”, “Neural Network”, “Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN)”, “Magnetic Resonance Imaging”, “Prostate cancer”, “Prostate Imaging”, “Prostate Cancer Screening”, “organized prostate testing”. The specific search strategy is outlined in Appendix I.

Inclusion criteria

Studies that have assessed or reported the use of AI in MRI interpretation and decision-making (not image generation or developing prediction model) in screening settings and have fitted any of the follow criteria were eligible:–Have performed MRI in asymptomatic men invited or enrolled as part of a screening pathway for prostate cancer early detection–Have used AI in MRI interpretation with PI-RADS scoring as outcome–Have compared AI role in performance of biparametric (bp) MRI vs multiparametric (mp) MRI in terms of (prostate imaging reporting and data system (PI-RADS) scoring or biopsy referral or detection of clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa)–Have evaluated role of AI in biopsy referral decisions (especially PIRADS-3 cases)–Have evaluated AI assisted MRI interpretation in detection of csPCa and/or reduction in overdiagnosis–Have evaluated role of AI in MRI interpretation (PIRADS scoring) for making decision on active surveillance within screening-detected populations.

Screening and study selection

Two reviewers (DS and AC) screened the studies in phases: title, abstracts/study summaries, and full texts respectively, for potential eligibility. All relevant publications were discussed among authors for eligibility of inclusion and critical appraisal within this review. One reviewer (DS) transferred records into EndNote and manually removed any duplicates.

Results

Thimansson et al. [20] (2024) assessed the feasibility of AI-assisted MRI reading within the Swedish opportunistic testing (OPT) program, using the commercially available ProstateAI software tool. Among the 5,069 men who were invited to participate 1,920 men (37.9%) had PSA test performed. Of these, 80 men (4.2%) had PSA > 3 µg/ml and 57 men who provided informed consent underwent bpMRI. The study reported poor agreement between the DL algorithm and expert radiologists (46%, k = 0.17) as well as with local radiologists (40%, k = 0.12) with 51% (29/57) MRIs classified as PI-RADS 4–5 by DL algorithm as compared to 23% (13/57) by local radiologists and 14% (8/57) by expert radiologists. it was a threefold increase in PI-RADS 4–5 classification by DL system as compared to expert consensus. Importantly, no cases were scored as PI-RADS 3 by the DL algorithm, highlighting a tendency towards assigning more cases into high-suspicion categories. The authors concluded that current DL tools, largely trained on clinical cohorts, may not generalize well to screening populations characterized by younger men with smaller and denser prostates. Similarly, Winkel et al. (2020) investigated the performance of a similar DL algorithm in a German screening cohort of 48 asymptomatic men with mean PSA value of 2.68 µg/ml and standard deviation of 5.48 µg/ml. Radiologists identified 38/48 (80%) MRIs with PI-RADS score of ≥ 3 as compared to 33/48 (68.7%) images as classified by AI. Agreement with expert radiologists was moderate (κ = 0.42), with a higher sensitivity of 87% but a relatively low specificity of 50%. The distribution of AI-assigned PI-RADS scores showed over-detection of suspicious lesions (PI-RADS ≥ 3 in 87% of cases), with 50% classified as PI-RADS 4. The authors highlighted that reduced specificity in the screening context risks unnecessary biopsies.

Both studies reported a substantial over-detection of PI-RADS ≥ 3 cases and significant loss of specificity in screening settings. Large difference in PI-RADS 4 classification between DL algorithm and expert consensus (51% versus 14%, respectively) in Swedish study inflated high-suspicion lesions leading to unnecessary biopsies. Similarly, low specificity (50%) and higher proportion of PI-RADS ≥ 3 cases in German study flagged high false positive findings.

Discussion

Evidence on the use of AI applications in MRI scans of the prostate performed within a screening setting, are limited. Together, findings from two included studies suggest that while DL algorithms show promise for lesion detection, their application in screening settings is limited. Over-detection of PI-RADS ≥ 3 cases and low specificity reflected in greater discordance of DL algorithms classification with expert radiologists [20], [21]. This pattern contrasts with results from non-screening cohorts, where DL models have demonstrated good performance, sometimes comparable to experienced radiologists [12], [13], [22], [23]. Both studies were prone to sampling error because of the limited sample size, which further limits the generalizability of the results. The discrepancy likely reflects differences in population characteristics and the predominance of unsuspicious MRIs in screening cohorts [20]. Importantly, both screening studies included in our review underline the need for training and validating DL algorithms in large, multicentre screening datasets before their integration into organized PCa screening.

The AI tool (ProstateAI) used by both studies was trained using 2,170 bpMRI prostate examinations with more PI-RADS ≥ 3 lesions (n = 1226) than lesion-free cases (n = 944). This might have led to over-detection with reduced specificity in both studies. Another concern is the lesions’ zone. For example, algorithm trained using peripheral zone lesions cannot be valid to interpret MRI scans with transition zone lesions [21]. The lesion detection rates of transition zone lesions is difficult to differentiate between benign and malignant phase whereas detection of PI-RADS ≥ 4 lesions in peripheral zone is sufficiently high [24], [25]. AI performance needs to be evaluated and validated using lesions detected in different zones. Without such tailored development, AI risks inflating false positives leading to unnecessary biopsies and detection of clinically insignificant cancers, burdening radiology workflows, and reducing the cost-effectiveness of screening programs [20], [21]. This is more critical given the relatively low prevalence of csPCa in screened population (even with PSA-based pre-screening), which may further decrease MRI positive predictive value. In such settings, positive predictive value is highly sensitive to small reductions in test specificity. AI systems optimized primarily for lesion detection rather than risk stratification may therefore paradoxically reduce overall screening efficiency, despite appearing to improve sensitivity metrics. Without careful calibration to population prevalence and explicit optimization for zone‑specific performance, AI may unintentionally shift screening programs toward greater overdiagnosis and overtreatment.

Most AI algorithms have been trained and tested on diagnostic datasets or retrospective cohorts [26], [27]. Thus, deploying AI to improve quality of care in a screening setting is a challenge to prove. The right balance between sensitivity and specificity with adjustment for AI’s threshold for interpreting MRI scans is crucial. Training of radiologists on using AI output to provide MRI results, their trust in AI judgement, and adaptation in every day clinical decision are a few of the several key aspects to consider [28], [29]. PI-RADS is commonly used for prostate MRI reporting, but its most effective role and interpretation in screening remain under refinement, with new proposals currently being investigated [30]. The discrepancies in performance of AI assisted MRI readings as revealed in the Swedish OPT program highlights need for robust deep learning algorithms based on larger number of MRI scans prospectively collected in screening setting [20].

Another consideration is that MRI-based AI algorithms are highly susceptible to training set image processing parameters [31], [32], [33]. The generalisability of most published algorithms needs to be evaluated, considering only a few algorithms have been assessed so far on true external multi-vendor multi-center test datasets [22]. Deep-learning-accelerated MRI protocols can support radiologists through double reading, automated feedback, thus reducing inter-reader variability and enhance lesion characterization beyond the subjective PI-RADS framework [34]. AI algorithms developed using the MRI images collected in risk-stratified screening approach ‘as a triage’ might need to be further validated in settings where MRI is used a primary screening strategy. Lessons from AI integration in cervical and breast cancer screening suggest that AI can achieve comparable or superior performance to human experts while optimizing resource utilization [35], [36].

Due to the very limited number (n = 2) and exploratory nature of the included studies, we did not perform quantitative assessment of risk of bias. Both studies have substantial methodological heterogeneity. Both studies were pilot or proof‑of‑concept investigations evaluating AI‑assisted or autonomous reading of prostate MRI, rather than designed for comparative effectiveness evaluation. Given early-phase and feasibility-oriented nature of studies, formal risk of bias assessment would risk over-interpretation and false precision. Instead, we narratively assessed and transparently reported the methodological strengths and limitations such as study design, sample size, AI validation strategy, and clinical context to allow readers to judge internal validity and generalizability.

Similarly, ethical, legal, and social implications are central to AI adoption. Currently, many AI tools operate as opaque ‘black boxes’ limiting clinician trust and transparency. Cancer screening studies showed that AI was most accepted when used as a decision support tool, either concurrently with radiologists or as a triage system, rather than replacing human expertise [37], [38], [39]. One key consideration is the concern of patients informed consent and autonomy as patients may not be fully aware that AI is involved in their diagnosis or how AI influences clinical decisions, challenging the principle of informed consent [15]. Handling large, sensitive imaging datasets raises data privacy concerns under regulatory frameworks such as the General Data Protection and Regulation (GDPR) and European Council Regulation (2024/1689) ensuring use of AI tool for people with an ultimate aim of increasing human well-being [40]. Equally important is the liability and accountability in case of diagnostic error, data privacy, negligence, as it is unclear whether the AI developer, the clinician, or the hospital should be legally responsible [9], [41], [42]. Data privacy and variability across institutions can be addressed through federated learning, which offers a common platform to share models and abundant data [43].

AI systems needs to be trained using large datasets of MRI images collected from diverse settings to address existing disparities in AI-assisted interpretation of MRI images [44]. The poor to moderate agreement between AI and expert radiologists – and even lower agreement with local radiologists – underscores the need to develop better-trained algorithms, and in doing so, reduce the over-detection of indolent prostate cancers and enhance the overall performance of AI. Prospective studies should explicitly evaluate how AI‑assisted MRI interpretation influences biopsy rates, csPCa detection, false‑positive findings, and downstream clinical decisions.

Although limited evidence, our review has opened several avenues for future research. We highlighted the need to develop robust deep learning tools trained on diverse population and across different screening strategies – whether used as a triage approach within risk-stratified screening or as MRI-based primary screening. Given the anticipated introduction and expansion of PCa screening in several European countries in the coming years, it is crucial for all stakeholders to prepare. It is important to discuss insights and possible adaptation of AI approaches that are currently used for future cancer screening programs. This includes decision-makers establishing appropriate legal frameworks; efforts to build trust in AI through transparency and radiologists training; ethics committees ensuring the safe clinical use of AI; and attention to patients’ autonomy, including their ability to consent to and participate in AI-informed decision based on MRI results.

In conclusion, the use of AI in prostate MRI is not yet ready to be utilized in large-scale screening settings. The ongoing PRAISE-U and EU-CanScreen prostate cancer screening implementation trials may provide level 1 evidence on MRI performance in risk-stratified screening setting and associated outcomes [45]. AI algorithms can be developed or tested in the MRI scans to generate real-time evidence for decision-making on whether AI is non-inferior to radiologists in detecting PCa on MRI in the screening setting and to inform the optimal interaction between AI and radiologists [46]. Generating robust evidence base in the coming years will be crucial to ensure that AI integration enhances, rather than undermining, the effectiveness and acceptability of future prostate cancer screening programs.
