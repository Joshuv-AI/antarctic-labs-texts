Efficacy and safety of telitacicept in the treatment of immunoglobulin a nephropathy: a real-world, single-center, retrospective study
Authors: Lunju Luo, Yiyao Deng, Shuang Chen, Yuqi Yang, Lu Liu, Rui Zhang, Zhengsheng Li, Lu Yang, Yan Zha, Jing Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN), the most common glomerular disease, is characterized by immune complex deposition and abnormal complement system activation. Telitacicept targets B cells through multiple mechanisms, potentially offering therapeutic benefits in IgAN. This retrospective study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of telitacicept.MethodsEleven patients with IgAN treated with telitacicept (160 mg, weekly) between August 2022 and December 2024 were included. The minimum follow-up period was 24 weeks, and the primary endpoint was renal remission rate at the final visit.ResultsAmong the 11 patients, 6 (54.5%) achieved partial remission, including 3 (27.3%) with complete response. Following telitacicept treatment, 24-h proteinuria decreased from a baseline of 1.77 g/day (interquartile range [IQR]: 1.31–5.19 g/day) to 1.04 g/day (IQR: 0.27–1.41 g/day) at the last follow-up (P < 0.05), demonstrating a significant reduction in proteinuria. Urinary red blood cell counts decreased from 52.81 ± 110.95 to 9.62 ± 11.44 per high-power field. The main adverse reaction was an upper respiratory tract infection, with no serious events reported.ConclusionThis study provided preliminary evidence that telitacicept may reduce proteinuria in patients with IgAN, with no serious safety concerns observed during this study. These preliminary real-world findings support the potential role of telitacicept in IgAN management and warrant confirmation in prospective multicenter studies.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Immunoglobulin A (IgA) nephropathy (IgAN), the most prevalent primary glomerulonephritis globally, is characterized by the deposition of IgA in the glomerular mesangium [1]. While the etiology of IgAN involves genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers, a key pathogenic driver is the overproduction of galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1) by B lymphocytes and plasma cells [2]. No specific pharmacological treatments exist for IgAN, and its management primarily depends on empirical immunosuppressive therapy, particularly high-dose glucocorticoids [3]. However, the safety and efficacy of these treatments remain uncertain, and long-term use of high-dose glucocorticoids can lead to adverse reactions, including diabetes, weight gain, and Cushing’s syndrome [4, 5]. Approximately 40% of patients with IgAN eventually progress to end-stage renal disease [6], underscoring the urgent need for novel therapeutic approaches.

B lymphocyte stimulator (BLyS) and a proliferation-inducing ligand (APRIL) belong to the tumor necrosis factor superfamily and are critical cytokines in the immune system [7]. Elevated serum levels of these cytokines have been reported in patients with autoimmune diseases. A Phase II randomized controlled trial in China provided supporting clinical evidence, enrolling 44 patients with IgAN randomly assigned to receive a placebo, telitacicept 160 mg, or 240 mg via weekly subcutaneous injections. After 24 weeks of treatment, the 240 mg group demonstrated a 49% reduction in 24-h proteinuria from baseline (P < 0.05), which was significantly greater than the 25% reduction observed in the 160 mg group [8]. Further research by Zan et al. indicated that telitacicept inhibits B cell activation and antibody production by blocking BLyS and APRIL, thereby reducing circulating Gd-IgA1, IgG-IgA, and poly-IgA immune complexes by approximately 50% [9]. These findings provide a theoretical basis for using telitacicept to treat IgAN. Telitacicept is a fusion protein that targets both BLyS and a APRIL, blocking their interactions with cognate receptors. This dual inhibition suppresses B-cell activation, proliferation, and antibody production. By extension, it impedes the differentiation and maturation of plasma cells responsible for producing Gd-IgA1, thereby reducing Gd-IgA1 synthesis and its glomerular deposition, which ultimately mitigates subsequent glomerular inflammation and renal injury [10, 11]. It has been approved in China for treating systemic lupus erythematosus [12]. Recent clinical studies in China have explored its efficacy in IgAN, myasthenia gravis, rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren’s syndrome, and other conditions [12–15]. A multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase II trial revealed that telitacicept significantly reduced urinary protein levels in patients with IgAN, providing preliminary evidence of its efficacy and safety in the treatment of this condition [8].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of telitacicept in patients with IgAN using real-world data.

Methods

Study design and population

This single-center, retrospective, observational study included 11 patients with IgAN treated at Guizhou Provincial People’s Hospital between August 1, 2022, and December 1, 2024. All data were collected retrospectively from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. All participants met the following inclusion criteria: (1) diagnosed with primary IgAN by renal biopsy with 24-h proteinuria ≥ 0.75 g/day; (2) estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) > 30 mL/min/1.73 m² [calculated by the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) creatinine equation]; (3) aged 18–70 years, male or female; (4) negative urine pregnancy test, no planned pregnancy within the following year, and use of contraception; and (5) had received standard angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI) or angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) treatment for 12 weeks before initiating telitacicept treatment. The exclusion criteria for all groups were as follows: (1) Presence of secondary IgAN, such as that associated with malignancies, Henoch-Schönlein purpura nephritis, or hepatitis virus-associated nephritis; (2) Duration of telitacicept treatment less than 24 weeks; (3) Patients whose baseline or follow-up data were incomplete; (4) Unwillingness to participate in the study; (5) Pregnancy or lactation. The MEST-C scores in this study were derived from renal biopsy pathology reports at the time of diagnosis. Because this was a retrospective study, repeat renal biopsies were not performed prior to the initiation of telitacicept treatment. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Guizhou Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval No. Lunshen [Scientific Research] 2025-006), and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design. Ethical approval adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection and variables

All patients were administered standard supportive treatment for at least 12 weeks before initiating telitacicept. Supportive treatment was defined as the maximum tolerated dose of ACEI or ARB. Blood pressure was managed according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes guidelines for patients with IgAN. Additionally, patients were advised to limit their salt intake, stop smoking, and avoid nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. Furthermore, any prior immunosuppressant therapy, including glucocorticoids, was discontinued before the start of telitacicept treatment.

In this retrospective study, data on clinical laboratory tests were obtained from patient records as documented in outpatient visits. Clinical data were assessed at baseline (defined as the last laboratory examination before telitacicept initiation), at 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 weeks after treatment, and at the last follow-up. Detailed records included demographics (age, sex), clinical features, and laboratory tests (including serum creatinine [SCr], eGFR, serum albumin [ALB], white blood cells [WBC], hemoglobin [Hb], red blood cell count [RBC], 24-h proteinuria, and urinary red blood cells [U-RBCs]). After 24 weeks of treatment, clinical outcomes were evaluated. The primary evaluation indicators were urinary protein level, SCr level, and eGFR. Secondary evaluations encompassed ALB, WBC, HB, U-RBCs, and RBC to assess the therapeutic effects of telitacicept. Clinical efficacy was classified as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), non-remission (NR), or relapse [16]. CR was defined as 24-h proteinuria ≤ 0.3 g/day with SCr increase of < 15% from baseline levels. PR was defined as a >50% reduction in 24-h proteinuria compared to baseline measurements with SCr increase < 15%, whereas NR indicated failure to achieve either CR or PR. Relapse occurred in patients who had previously achieved CR or PR, defined as either 24-h proteinuria >1 g/day or a >50% increase relative to remission-phase values. Adverse reactions were monitored, including infections, abnormal liver function, blood system abnormalities, allergic reactions, and other notable effects.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of participants. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or median (interquartile range [IQR]) for non-normally distributed data. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Baseline characteristics such as age were normally distributed and are reported as mean ± SD. In contrast, longitudinal measurements of key outcome variables (e.g., urinary protein, eGFR, SCr) were non-normally distributed (P < 0.05) and are presented as median (IQR); consequently, non-parametric tests were employed for their analysis. For non-normally distributed repeated-measures data, the Friedman test was used to assess overall differences across time points. Pairwise comparisons between the time points were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and sex distributions were compared between groups using Fisher’s exact test.

Changes in eGFR were analyzed using a linear regression model with regression coefficients estimated using the least-squares method. A linear mixed-effects model was used to analyze trends in urine protein levels. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding one patient and re-analyzing the data, comparing results for 11 versus 10 patients to ensure robustness and verify that findings were not influenced by a single data point. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

The K-value was employed to quantify the instantaneous rate of change in each laboratory parameter relative to treatment duration. It is defined as follows:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K=\frac{{\Delta X}}{{\Delta t}}$$\end{document}

where X denotes the measured value of 24-h proteinuria, Scr, eGFR, or ALB, and t represents time. A positive k-value (k > 0 indicates an increasing trend in the parameter over time, a negative value (k < 0) reflects a decreasing trend, and a value approximating zero (k ≈ 0) suggests relative stability.

Missing values in covariates (0.4%) were addressed using the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) algorithm. First, we generated 20 independent imputations with different random seeds for diversity. Thereafter, a conditional model incorporating variable relationships and time-series trends was constructed. Finally, the mean of these 20 imputations, as computed via Python’s fancyimpute library, was used to replace missing values. Additionally, strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were adopted to mitigate selection bias. Regular follow-up and standardized assessment methods minimized measurement bias. Given the exploratory nature of the study and the limited sample size, all statistical analyses were considered exploratory, and no adjustments for multiple comparisons were made.

Results

Baseline characteristics of patients

This study included 11 patients (6 male and 5 female patients) aged 20–54 years, with a mean age of 37.00 ± 11.11 years. The median follow-up time was 28.0 weeks [IQR: 24.0–44.0 weeks], with a total range of 0–44 weeks. During follow-up, nine patients showed a > 50% reduction in 24-h proteinuria from baseline, while two patients experienced a < 50% reduction (Table 1). The mean 24-h proteinuria decreased from 1.77 g/day (IQR 1.31–5.19 g/day) at baseline to 1.04 g/day (IQR 0.27–1.41 g/day) after treatment. Based on changes in 24-h proteinuria, the treatment effect was significant, with an overall response rate (ORR) of 81.8%. Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics, including sex, age, eGFR, SCr, ALB, WBC, Hb, RBC, 24-h proteinuria, and U-RBCs, are presented in Table 2. To assess the potential influence of renal proteinuria on outcomes, we performed a sensitivity analysis by excluding one patient with renal proteinuria. The efficacy and safety outcomes of telitacicept were reevaluated using data from the remaining 10 patients. The results showed no significant differences following this exclusion (P > 0.05), further underscoring the robustness of telitacicept’s effect in IgAN treatment (Table 3). The analysis of baseline differences in proteinuria demonstrated that time significantly affected proteinuria levels (F(1, 53.20) = 28.28, P < 0.001). Despite notable individual variations in patients’ baseline proteinuria levels (Variance = 3.51, P < 0.05), there were no significant differences in the rate of change of proteinuria over time among individuals (Variance ≈ 0, P > 0.05). To assess the impact of prior immunosuppressant use on efficacy, we performed a subgroup analysis. Despite having a higher baseline eGFR (69.50 vs. 53.50 mL/min/1.73 m², P = 0.006) and a lower urine red blood cell count (21.70 vs. 4.50 /µL, P = 0.002) than treatment-naïve patients (n = 8), patients with a history of immunosuppressant use (n = 3) showed no significant difference in 24-h proteinuria after telitacicept treatment (1.44 vs. 1.37 g/day, P = 0.993). This finding suggests that the proteinuria-reducing efficacy of telitacicept is consistent, regardless of previous immunosuppressant exposure (Table 4).

In this study cohort, six patients (54.5%) had comorbidities, with hypertension being the most prevalent (four cases; 36.4%). Other comorbidities included hyperuricemia (three cases; 27.3%), diabetes (two cases; 18.2%), hyperlipidemia, and tuberculosis, each observed in one patient (9.1%). Notably, certain complications such as hypertension, diabetes, and hyperuricemia are associated with metabolic syndrome, which may influence the therapeutic efficacy of telitacicept and the overall prognosis of patients.

Treatment pattern

The concomitant medications used throughout the study period are detailed in Table 5. Importantly, all medications were maintained at a stable dose, with the exception of glucocorticoids and other immunosuppressants (tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil), which were initiated after the start of telitacicept treatment. This stable treatment approach provided an optimal basis for evaluating the efficacy of telitacicept.

Histological features

The Oxford classification employs the MEST-C scoring system, which includes mesangial hypercellularity (M), endocapillary hypercellularity (E), segmental glomerulosclerosis (S), tubular atrophy or interstitial fibrosis (T), and crescent lesions(C). Mesangial cell proliferation (M) is classified as M0 when absent and M1 when > 50% of glomeruli show proliferation. Intracapillary cell proliferation (E) is recorded as E1 if it resulted in lumen stenosis; otherwise, it is classified as E0. Segmental glomerulosclerosis (S) is classified as S1 when sclerosis or adhesions are present and as S0 when absent. Tubular atrophy or interstitial fibrosis (T) is graded as T0, T1, and T2 when fibrosis involves 1%–25%, 26%–50%, or > 50% of the cortical area, respectively. Crescent formation (C) is categorized as C0 (absent), C1 (< 25%), or C2 (≥ 25%), based on the percentage of glomeruli affected by crescents (Table 2).

Therapeutic outcomes

In this patient cohort, the ORR to telitacicept treatment was 81.8%. At the last follow-up, 27.3% of patients achieved CR, and 54.5% achieved PR. The proportions of NR cases among males and females were 16.7% and 20%, respectively (Fig. 1).

During the follow-up period, one patient developed upper respiratory tract infection symptoms, accompanied by an increase in 24-h proteinuria from 1.27 to 10.01 g/day. Following appropriate symptomatic treatment, the telitacicept dosage was escalated to 240 mg weekly. After 12 weeks of this adjusted treatment, the patient’s 24-h proteinuria decreased to 3.59 g/day, and the 240 mg dose was maintained. Overall, two patients received weekly telitacicept injections of 240 mg, while the remaining nine received 160 mg weekly.

Effects of telitacicept on proteinuria were assessed using non-parametric tests and linear mixed-effects models. The median 24-h proteinuria decreased from 1.77 g/day (IQR 1.31–5.19 g/day) at baseline to 1.23 g/day (IQR 0.32–1.62 g/day) at the 24-week of treatment (Z = − 1.88, P = 0.059). At the last follow-up, it further declined to 1.04 g/day (IQR 0.271.41 g/day) (Z = − 2.04, P = 0.041), showing statistically significant improvement from baseline to the last follow-up (Fig. 2a). However, further analysis with a linear mixed-effects model revealed a non-significant reduction in 24-hour urinary protein from a baseline of 3.25 g/day (95% CI: 1.97 to 4.53). The mean decrease was 1.69 g/day at 24 weeks (95% CI: -0.59 to 3.96; P = 0.519) and was sustained at 1.72 g/day by the final follow-up (95% CI: -0.61 to 4.06; P = 0.537). The wide 95% confidence intervals, which included the null value, likely reflect the limited sample size and substantial inter-individual variability. Nevertheless, the concordant direction of effect across analytical methods supports the potential efficacy of telitacicept in reducing proteinuria in IgAN.

During telitacicept treatment, serum ALB levels increased from 43.70 g/L (IQR 38.00–45.30 g/L) to 44.60 g/L (IQR 38.60–45.90 g/L) (Z = − 1.60, P = 0.110) (Fig. 2d). This finding indicates that telitacicept not only helps to lower 24-h proteinuria in patients with IgAN but may also maintain or improve their ALB levels. Following treatment, the SCr levels decreased from a baseline median of 132.00 µmol/L (IQR: 101.00 − 182.00) to 127.00 µmol/L (IQR: 97.00 − 175.00) (Z = − 1.01, P = 0.309). The mean eGFR increased by approximately 0.206 mL/min/1.73 m² every 4 weeks, although substantial interindividual variability was observed (95% CI: −0.028 to 0.440). Moreover, a positive correlation between treatment duration and eGFR was identified (β = 0.206, P = 0.083), although this trend did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2b, c).

By dynamically monitoring changes in four key parameters (24-h proteinuria, SCr, eGFR, and ALB) (Fig. 3), we systematically evaluated the clinical efficacy of telitacicept in IgAN treatment. The results demonstrated significant fluctuations in the parameter variation rates during the initial treatment phase (before 16 weeks), indicating interindividual variability in initial drug responses. Conversely, these rates stabilized in the mid-to-late treatment phase (after 16 weeks), suggesting progressive and sustained therapeutic consolidation. Notably, a sustained downward trend in 24-h proteinuria was observed starting at week 8, accompanied by a significant concurrent elevation in serum ALB levels. Furthermore, by week 20, eGFR showed a steady increase. These findings indicated that telitacicept effectively reduced proteinuria without impairing renal function; however, its long-term effects on renal function remain unclear.

Safety assessment

During telitacicept treatment, one patient experienced an upper respiratory tract infection as an adverse event; however, no serious adverse events were observed throughout the follow-up period.

Discussion

Our study observed that telitacicept treatment significantly reduced 24-h proteinuria, with a mean reduction of 54.5% (from 3.25 g/day to 1.48 g/day), without affecting kidney function.

Although the precise pathogenesis of IgAN remains unclear, the multiple-hit hypothesis provides a framework for understanding its mechanisms [17, 18]. Current therapies mainly focus on symptom management through immunosuppressive regimens. As a novel recombinant fusion protein, telitacicept uniquely inhibits both the BLyS and APRIL pathways, suppressing B cell activation and proliferation, thereby reducing pathogenic immune complex formation and deposition [19].

More than half of the patients treated with telitacicept achieved >50% reduction in proteinuria, an outcome consistent with findings from a recent larger-scale, methodologically rigorous multicenter study. In that previous trial, which employed propensity score matching (1:1:1) to compare telitacicept, supportive care, and immunosuppressive therapy, telitacicept led to a significant 54.6% reduction in proteinuria over a 3-month follow-up period. This efficacy was comparable to that of immunosuppressive therapy, but telitacicept demonstrated a more favorable safety profile [20]. A multicenter retrospective study evaluated the efficacy and safety of telitacicept versus mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)—both combined with low-dose glucocorticoids—in high-risk, progressive IgAN patients with 24-h proteinuria ≥ 0.75 g/day and an eGFR >30 mL/min/1.73 m². After 6 months of treatment, proteinuria significantly decreased from baseline in both groups and remained stable through the 12-month follow-up. At the end of follow-up, the telitacicept group demonstrated superior efficacy, with a median reduction in proteinuria of − 62.5% (IQR − 84.6 to − 46.3) compared to − 52.9% (IQR − 78.5 to − 37.6) in the MMF group (P = 0.041). Telitacicept was also associated with a more favorable safety profile. These findings suggest that telitacicept offers enhanced proteinuria reduction and potentially lowers the risk of disease progression in high-risk IgAN patients, positioning it as a promising new treatment option for this population [21]. Collectively, these data suggest telitacicept may represent a potential therapeutic option and well-tolerated therapeutic option for IgAN. Although the present study featured a heterogeneous patient population at baseline, proteinuria-lowering effects of telitacicept remained stable across various patient subsets, as confirmed by sensitivity analyses, consistent individual response trends, and key subgroup analyses (e.g., stratified by prior immunosuppressant use). Furthermore, hematuria resolved in 82% patients. Nevertheless, direct evidence demonstrating the alleviation of underlying renal inflammation or structural injury is still lacking.

Despite these promising results, some statistical comparisons did not reach significance (P > 0.05), which could be attributed to the limited sample size. During the treatment period, two patients discontinued telitacicept because of CR and financial constraints. Notably, only one patient exhibited an increase in urinary protein levels during treatment, whereas no subsequent increase was observed in other patients during follow-up. These preliminary findings suggest that telitacicept may confer therapeutic benefits to patients with IgAN and potentially contribute to delayed disease progression.

Several real-world studies have assessed the safety and efficacy of telitacicept in IgAN, consistently yielding favorable outcomes and reporting no significant adverse events [20, 22]. In our cohort, one adverse event occurred during follow-up; following an upper respiratory tract infection, the patient’s 24-h proteinuria increased from 1.27 to 10.01 g/day. After symptomatic management and dose escalation of telitacicept from 160 to 240 mg, proteinuria markedly decreased. This case suggests a generally favorable tolerability of telitacicept, while highlighting the need for vigilance regarding treatment-emergent adverse events (e.g., infections) and prompt clinical intervention when required.

In IgAN, S1 lesions—defined by segmental sclerosis or adhesion—are recognized predictors of proteinuria and disease progression [2]. A recent large-scale retrospective analysis (n = 746) found that approximately 80% IgAN patients exhibit S1 lesions, and importantly, even histologically aggressive subtypes benefited from immunosuppressive therapy [23]. Given this context, our study cohort, which had a similarly high prevalence of S1 lesions (81.8%), demonstrated a pronounced reduction in 24-h proteinuria without renal functional impairment following telitacicept treatment. This suggests that the high frequency of S1 lesions is a key contributor to the treatment response. The efficacy of telitacicept likely stems from its suppression of the BLyS/APRIL pathway, thereby interrupting the upstream immune-inflammatory processes that fuel the development of S1 lesions—particularly the more active subtypes. Further studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis.

Our study has a few limitations. First, as a single-center, real-world retrospective observational study with a small sample size (N = 11), it is subject to selection bias. These methodological constraints necessitate validation through large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies with pre-specified endpoints. Second, only two participants received the 44-week telitacicept treatment regimen. The variability in follow-up times among patients represents a study limitation that could lead to potential follow-up duration bias. Future studies with a larger cohort and a predefined, uniform follow-up endpoint are warranted to validate these findings. Furthermore, we did not systematically collect pathogenesis-related biomarkers (e.g., Gd-IgA1). The retrospective nature and absence of a standardized washout period also make it difficult to exclude residual effects from prior immunosuppressive therapies. Finally, while reflecting contemporary clinical practice patterns, the absence of a randomized controlled trial design and long-term follow-up data restricts the comprehensive evaluation of the efficacy and safety profiles of telitacicept in IgAN management. Given the retrospective design and small sample size, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger multicenter randomized trials.

In conclusion, our findings provide preliminary evidence that telitacicept reduces proteinuria in Chinese patients with IgAN, with no serious safety concerns observed during the study period. Although the sample size was limited and warrants further validation, these results offer new insights into IgAN management and provide a foundation for more extensive future investigations.
