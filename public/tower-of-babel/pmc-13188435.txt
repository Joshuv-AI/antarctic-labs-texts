The transcription factor E4F1 is crucial for spermatogonial differentiation and meiosis progression in mice
Authors: Fei-Chen Wang, Zhen He, Rong-Ge Yan, Yu-Jun Wang, Jia-Lu Wu, Qi-En Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpermatogonial differentiation is a key step in spermatogenesis, yet the transcriptional programs that control this process are not fully defined. E4f1 has been reported to be essential for embryonic development, mitochondrial function and spermatogonial stem cell (SSC) maintenance in mice. However, its function in spermatogonial differentiation and meiosis progression is unknown.ResultsThe Cre-loxP system was used to delete E4f1 in differentiated spermatogonia and early meiotic spermatocytes. E4f1 knockout significantly affected the development of spermatogonia and spermatocytes. The mitochondria in the spermatogonia of the E4f1-cKO mice were morphologically abnormal, and apoptosis was elevated. The most advanced germ cells in the seminiferous tubules of E4f1-cKO mice were spermatocytes at the early pachytene stage. Single-cell transcriptome analysis revealed that loss of E4f1 suppressed the expression of transcriptional regulators related to SSC maintenance (e.g., Uchl1, Zbtb16, Lin28a), spermatogonial differentiation and meiotic entry (e.g., Dmrt1, Sohlh1, Stra8). Furthermore, CUT&Tag analysis of a HIS-Tag knock-in mouse line revealed that 237 genes were associated with E4F1, including Cks2 and Hmgb2, two previously identified genes related to meiosis progression.ConclusionsOur results suggest that E4f1 helps ensure normal spermatogonial differentiation by regulating the expression of key differentiation-associated genes. It may also influence the meiotic procession of spermatocytes, potentially through modulating the expression of downstream candidate target genes such as Cks2 and Hmgb2. These results establish a crucial role for the E4f1-dependent transcriptional program in spermatogonial differentiation and meiosis, although further validation is required to fully elucidate the underlying mechanisms involved.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12649-3.

Background

Spermatogenesis is a complex and well-orchestrated process that generates functional sperm. Continual and robust spermatogenesis relies on the activities of spermatogonial stem cells (SSCs), which proliferate to maintain a foundational stem cell pool and differentiate to provide transit-amplifying progenitors [1]. Progenitor spermatogonia proliferate and transition into differentiating spermatogonia, which are divided into six rounds before committing to meiosis [2]. During the transition from progenitor spermatogonia to differentiating spermatogonia, genes that regulate SSC self-renewal and proliferation, such as Id4, Gfra1, Zbtb16, Foxo1 and Nanos2, are repressed, whereas genes associated with differentiation, including Kit, Stra8, Sall4, Sohlh1 and Sohlh2, are upregulated [3, 4]. Retinoid signaling from Sertoli cells is crucial for this process [5, 6]. RA is essential for the induction of Stra8 expression in germ cells and is crucial for the regulation of KIT protein synthesis [7]. Despite these important findings, our understanding of spermatogonial fate decisions is still limited.

The differentiation of spermatogenic cells is a continuous process that requires precise regulation of gene expression in precursor cells. Defective spermatogonial differentiation can lead to meiosis failure. For example, deletion of Mettl3 inhibits spermatogonial differentiation and blocks meiosis initiation [8]. Disrupting mitochondrial fusion by deleting Mfn1 and Mfn2 in the germline leads to a significant reduction in the number of differentiated spermatogonia and severe abnormalities during meiosis progression [9]. Meiosis starts from the primary spermatocyte and includes five stages: leptotene, zygotene, pachytene, diplotene and diakinesis [10]. During the leptotene and zygotene stages, DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) occur, which depend on the expression of the Prdm9, Spo11, Top6bl, Hormad1, Mei1 and Mei4 genes induced by Stra8 and Meiosin [11]. In late pachytene, these DSBs undergo homologous recombination (HR) repair via the loading of Rad51 and Dmc1 recombinase [12]. A recent study suggested that the transcription factor E4F1 interacts with BRG1 and other members of the DNA repair machinery to participate in DSB repair in somatic cells; however, its involvement in meiosis and meiotic DSBs remains to be explored [13].

E4F1 was initially identified as a cellular target of the E1A viral oncoprotein during adenovirus infection [14]. The results of functional experiments revealed that E4F1 is essential for embryonic development in mice and that it directly interacts with pRB and p53 to promote cell cycle progression [15]. Furthermore, E4F1 is a crucial regulator of mitochondrial function and pyruvate metabolism. Knockout of E4f1 causes a decrease in mitochondrial pyruvic acid dehydrogenase (PDH) activity and a disorder of pyruvic acid metabolism [16]. Interestingly, E4F1 also acts as a zinc finger protein and cooperates with PARP1 and BRG1 in human cells to promote the repair of DSBs [13]. BRG1, a member of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, facilitates DSB repair in somatic cells, and its deletion in the germline arrests spermatocytes at the pachytene stage [17, 18]. E4F1 is ubiquitously expressed in Sertoli cells and spermatogenic cells in the mouse testis [19, 20]. These findings indicate that E4f1 may play a functional role in the meiosis of male germ cells. Previously, we conditionally deleted E4f1 in fetal germ cells and reported that this gene is essential for the maintenance of SSCs and progenitor spermatogonia [19]. Unfortunately, quick depletion of the entire germline in these animals precluded the possibility of examining the potential involvement of E4f1 in meiosis.

In the present study, we conditionally deleted E4f1 in STRA8+ spermatogenic cells of the postnatal testis and examined the impact of E4f1 loss of function on spermatogenesis. To overcome the difficulties in identifying E4f1-dependent transcription programs, we generated an epitope tag knockin mouse model and identified genes associated with E4f1 in germ cells. These results suggest that E4F1 plays a crucial role in directing spermatogonial fate decisions and the development of pachytene spermatocytes. These findings revealed a novel role for E4F1 in mammalian spermatogenesis and provided insight into the molecular control of male meiosis.

Results

Deletion of the E4f1 gene in postnatal STRA8+ germ cells causes male sterility

In mice, E4f1 is expressed in Sertoli cells and spermatogonia of the testes at the neonatal stage [20]. The results of immunofluorescence staining showed that E4F1 colocalized with markers for gonocytes (LIN28), undifferentiated spermatogonia (LIN28), differentiating spermatogonia (cKIT) at post-natal day (PD) 0, PD6, PD14 and PD21. Spermatocytes marked by SYCP3 were negative for E4F1 staining (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). These data indicate that E4F1 expression is enriched in the spermatogonial population. To explore the role of E4f1 in regulating the fate decisions of spermatogenic cells, we successfully deleted its conditional alleles in the postnatal testis via Stra8-GFPCre. E4f1-cKO and littermate controls were confirmed via PCR of tail biopsy DNA (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A). The testes of E4f1-cKO mice were significantly smaller than those of control mice (Fig. 1A). The testis weight-to-body weight ratio decreased by 69.86% (1.07 ± 0.12 E4f1-cKO vs. 3.55 ± 0.15 control) and 59.40% (1.49 ± 0.24 E4f1-cKO vs. 3.67 ± 0.04 control) at PD35 and 2 months of age (2mon), respectively (n = 3) (Fig. 1B). The results of the fertility test revealed that the deletion of E4f1 in STRA8+ germ cells caused a sterile phenotype. While the controls sired an average of 7.67 ± 0.33 pups, the E4f1-cKO animals did not sire any pups (n = 5) (Fig. 1C). There was no sperm present in the epididymides of E4f1-cKO mice (n = 10) (Fig. 1D). Histological analysis revealed that the seminiferous tubules of control animals contained three layers of spermatogenic cells, including spermatogonia, spermatocytes and spermatids. In sharp contrast, the tubules of E4f1-cKO testes were devoid of spermatids and contained only spermatogonia and spermatocytes with greatly reduced density. A reduction in spermatogenic cells was observed as early as PD14 (Fig. 1E). Immunostaining for LIN28, cKIT and SYCP3 confirmed that spermatogonia and spermatocytes were present in the E4f1-cKO testis (Fig. 1F, Additional file 1: Fig. S2C). Together, these data revealed that deletion of E4f1 in postnatal STRA8+ germ cells caused male sterility due to spermatogenic arrest at the spermatocyte stage.

E4f1 deletion in germ cells impacts the differentiation of spermatogonia

Although spermatogonia were present in the testes of E4f1-cKO mice, it was unclear whether the balance between undifferentiated spermatogonia and differentiating spermatogonia was disrupted. Costaining for the pan germ cell marker TRA98 and the Sertoli cell-specific protein SOX9 in testicular cross-sections from control and E4f1-cKO mice revealed that the number of germ cells per 500 Sertoli cells was significantly lower in the tubules of E4f1-cKO mice than in those of control mice at PD14 (202.70 ± 5.36 E4f1-cKO vs. 666.70 ± 87.37 control), PD35 (282.00 ± 22.37 E4f1-cKO vs. 1343.00 ± 10.79 control) and 2 mon (191.70 ± 10.14 E4f1-cKO vs. 1204.00 ± 114.50 control) (Fig. 2A). We further quantified the number of LIN28+ undifferentiated spermatogonia and found that this population significantly increased after E4f1 deletion at PD14 (64.81 ± 4.13 E4f1-cKO vs. 34.84 ± 3.95 control), PD35 (68.57 ± 1.55 E4f1-cKO vs. 9.00 ± 0.81 control) and 2mon (66.39 ± 2.20 E4f1-cKO vs. 7.17 ± 2.24 control) (Fig. 2B); however, the number of cKIT+ cells significantly decreased by 65.92%, 63.25% and 69.25% in the testes of mice at PD14 (20.47 ± 3.89 E4f1-cKO vs. 60.07 ± 5.06 control), PD35 (13.13 ± 1.10 E4f1-cKO vs. 35.73 ± 3.54 control) and 2mon (12.73 ± 0.88 E4f1-cKO vs. 41.40 ± 1.78 control), respectively (Fig. 2C). As expected, the number of spermatocytes marked by SYCP3 was 57.89% to 62.17% lower in the E4f1-cKO than that in the control animals at PD14 (16.71 ± 1.27 E4f1-cKO vs. 40.19 ± 0.73 control), PD35 (16.43 ± 1.17 E4f1-cKO vs. 43.43 ± 1.17 control) and 2mon (17.90 ± 1.20 E4f1-cKO vs. 42.51 ± 1.52 control) (Fig. 2D). These results revealed that E4F1 plays an important role in the differentiation of spermatogonia. After deletion of E4f1 in differentiating spermatogonia, spermatogenesis was abnormal, and the number of undifferentiated spermatogonia increased significantly; however, the number of cKIT+ spermatogonia and spermatocytes decreased.

Next, we investigated the impact of E4f1 deficiency on cell proliferation and apoptosis. TUNEL was used to stain apoptotic germ cells, and the E4f1-cKO testes exhibited a large area of apoptosis at all the development time points examined. TUNEL+ germ cells increased by 45.20% to 75.37% in the testicular cross-sections of E4f1-cKO animals at PD14 (37.20 ± 0.72 E4f1-cKO vs. 13.29 ± 1.00 control), PD35 (41.42 ± 0.90 E4f1-cKO vs. 10.20 ± 0.56 control) and 2mon (28.12 ± 1.62 E4f1-cKO vs. 15.41 ± 0.44 control). We also found that TUNEL+ signaling also appeared extensively in somatic cells in the testes of E4f1-cKO mice at 2mon (Fig. 2E, Additional file 1: Fig. S2D). Compared with that in the control testes, increased apoptosis in germ cells coincided with decreased proliferation, as the number of PCNA+ germ cells was decreased by 26.06% to 51.14% in the E4f1-cKO testes at PD14 (28.21 ± 1.13 E4f1-cKO vs. 38.15 ± 3.17 control), PD35 (23.68 ± 0.94 E4f1-cKO vs. 40.72 ± 2.22 control) and 2mon (25.79 ± 0.97 E4f1-cKO vs. 52.78 ± 2.41 control) (Fig. 2F). These results showed that deletion of E4f1 in STRA8+ germ cells led to increased apoptosis and decreased cell proliferation, therefore impacting the development of spermatogonia and spermatocytes.

Mitochondrial abnormalities in early postnatal E4f1-deficient spermatogonia

Because E4f1 is a crucial regulator of mitochondrial function [21], we examined the ultrastructures of control and E4f1-deficient spermatogonia via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 3A). The results revealed that the mitochondria in the spermatogonia of the E4f1-cKO testis were morphologically different from those in the spermatogonia of the control testis. The mitochondrial diameter increased by 24.99%, and 55.95% of the mitochondria contained swollen cristae, indicating impaired function. In contrast, at this phase of development, the mitochondria from the spermatogonia of control mice were round, and the inner space containing the matrix was flattened with the outer membrane. Only a few inner spaces within the condensed mitochondria were considerably swollen. These findings revealed that the abnormal development of spermatogonial mitochondria in the testes of early postnatal mice was also one of the reasons for the infertility of E4f1-cKO males.

E4f1-deficient spermatocytes arrest at the pachytene stage and cause unsuccessful programmed DSB repair

Histological analysis revealed that the number of spermatocytes was severely reduced, but a proportion of these cells entered meiosis. Next, we explored the impact of E4f1 deletion on the progression of meiosis. Chromosome spreading was used to examine critical events during meiosis. SYCP1 and SYCP3, which are components of the synaptonemal complex, were used to detect synapsis [22]. The results indicate that the chromosomal synapses of the spermatocytes in E4f1-cKO testes are normal (Fig. 4A). The proportions of spermatocytes at different stages markedly differed between control and E4f1-cKO animals. Specifically, compared with those of the controls, the percentages of leptotene (8.05 ± 1.39 E4f1-cKO vs. 3.30 ± 0.15 control) and zygotene (61.27 ± 1.87 E4f1-cKO vs. 9.83 ± 0.72 control) spermatocytes increased, whereas the number of pachytene (30.68 ± 0.51 E4f1-cKO vs. 57.20 ± 0.73 control) spermatocytes decreased in the E4f1-cKO mice. Spermatocytes at the diplotene and diakinesis stages were not present in the E4f1-cKO mice, indicating the complete arrest of spermatocytes at the pachytene stage (Fig. 4B).

In the pachytene stage, the RAD51 protein is present in chromatin loops and promotes the pairing of homologous DNA sequences [23]. However, no Rad51 locus was detected in the pachytene spermatocytes of E4f1-cKO testes, indicating that DSB repair cannot occur in the absence of E4f1 (Fig. 4D). Interestingly, in E4f1-cKO mice, all pachytene spermatocytes accumulated γH2AX on autosomes (Fig. 4C). MLH1 is a marker of crossover and was present in zygotene and pachytene spermatocytes in controls; however, the Mlh1 locus was massively reduced on the chromosomes of E4f1-cKO testes (Fig. 4D, Additional file 1: Fig. S2E). These results revealed that the deletion of E4f1 might influence chromosome recombination and crossover, resulting in pachytene stage arrest.

Loss of E4f1 altered the gene expression profiles of spermatogonia and spermatocytes at the single-cell level

Next, we conducted single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis to examine the molecular identities of spermatogenic cells from PD21 control and E4f1-cKO testes. On average, 30,427 and 27,975 genes, 9798.89 and 9734.54 median unique molecular index (UMI) counts per cell, and 2.36% and 3.03% mitochondrial percentages were detected per library for the control and E4f1-cKO testes, respectively (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A). We obtained testicular single-cell suspensions and filtered germ cells on the basis of Ddx4 and Dazl expression [24]. A total of 27,696 and 11,857 germ cells were obtained from the control and E4f1-cKO samples, respectively. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis revealed 15 clusters for control mice and 14 clusters for E4f1-cKO mice at a resolution of 0.5 (Additional file 1: Fig. S3B, C). In control mice, the spermatogenic cell population consisted of spermatogonia (both undifferentiated and differentiated), spermatocytes at various stages of meiosis I (leptotene to diakinesis), and round spermatids. In contrast, E4f1-deficient cells were detected only within spermatogonia and spermatocytes up to the pachytene stage (Fig. 5A), which is consistent with the results of chromosome spreading analysis. Quantification of the germ cell population between the two genotypes revealed that the proportion of undifferentiated spermatogonia increased by 15.49%, and the percentage of differentiated spermatogonia decreased by 16.20% in the E4f1-cKO testis. In addition, the percentage of pachytene spermatocytes decreased by 4.39%, whereas the percentage of zygotene spermatocytes increased by 9.43% (Fig. 5E). The results of scRNA-seq were the same as those of our spermatocyte spreads, suggesting that E4f1−/− spermatocytes were arrested at the pachytene stage at the transcriptome level.

Then, we used germ cells from the control and E4f1-cKO testes for unbiased dynamic cell trajectory analysis of pseudotime; obviously, 3 distinct states of gene expression patterns were identified, whereas the E4f1-cKO testis had 4 states, and the distribution of cell types on the trajectory conforms to our basic understanding (Fig. 5B, C). The gene expression patterns of different subpopulations of cells that we integrated were different (Additional file 1: Fig. S4A). For example, the FoxO signaling pathway was specific for state 1, the ErbB and p53 signaling pathways were only in state 2, and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and pyruvate metabolism processes were abundant in state 3. Therefore, these analyses indicated that the gene expression patterns of spermatogonia and spermatocytes were affected to some extent by the loss of E4f1.

E4f1 may interact with genes related to spermatogonial differentiation and spermatocyte meiosis

After determining cell fates, we next analyzed the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in different types of spermatogonia and spermatocytes between control and E4f1-cKO mice. A total of 279 DEGs were identified in spermatogenic cells from E4f1-cKO mice compared with those from control mice. Our analysis identified a total of 279 DEGs, with 49 downregulated and 230 upregulated. Notably, many of the downregulated genes (including Uchl1, Lin28a, Zbtb16, Dmrt1 and Sohlh1) are known markers or regulators associated with spermatogonial stem cell maintenance and spermatogonial differentiation, and the upregulated genes are predominantly involved in spermatocyte development (including Dmrtb1, Dmrtc2, Sycp3 and Hormad1) (Additional file 1: Fig. S4B; Additional file 2: Supplementary Table S2). Compared with those in the control group, 377 DEGs were detected in the undifferentiated spermatogonia of the E4f1-cKO group, of which 294 were upregulated and 83 were downregulated; a total of 338 DEGs, 39 were upregulated and 299 were downregulated in the differentiated spermatogonia; leptotene spermatocytes had the most DEGs, 241 were upregulated and 313 were downregulated out of 554 DEGs; the fewest number of DEGs were detected in the zygotene spermatocytes, 5 were upregulated and 19 were downregulated out of 24; and 283 DEGs were detected in the pachytene spermatocytes, of which 231 were upregulated and 52 were downregulated (Fig. 5D, Additional file 2: Supplementary Table S3).

STRING was used to construct differential gene interaction networks, which were visualized via Cytoscape (Additional file 1: Fig. S4C). These analyses indicated that genes associated with spermatogonial differentiation were downregulated, while genes associated with spermatocyte development were upregulated, and there may be direct interactions between E4f1 and these genes. Assessment of the potential functions of these DEGs via GO enrichment and KEGG analyses revealed cell differentiation and metabolic pathway enrichment (Additional file 1: Fig. S4D, Additional file 2: Supplementary Table S4). These analyses indicated that E4f1 may interact directly with genes involved in spermatogonial differentiation and spermatocyte meiosis.

CUT&Tag identifies Cks2 and Hmgb2 as candidate targets of E4F1 in developing spermatocytes

Finally, we performed CUT&Tag analysis to detect the DNA sequence directly bound by the transcription factor E4F1. Since there is no suitable antibody for E4F1, we generated an E4f1-KI model in C57BL/6J mice via CRISPR/Cas-mediated genome engineering. For this E4f1-KI model, the 6× HIS-Tag was inserted upstream of the TAG stop codon in exon 14. Cas9, gRNA and donor oligo were coinjected into fertilized eggs for E4f1-KI mouse production, and we successfully added the sequence “CATCACCATCACCATCAC” to the sequence of the E4f1 gene (Additional file 1: Fig. S2B). E4f1-KI mice were obtained via sequence analysis, and CUT&Tag experiments were performed with a HIS-Tag antibody.

After quality control, the histogram of the length distribution of the inserted fragments was “wavy”, with a distribution of 76.74% of the peaks in promoter regions (Additional file 1: Fig. S5A, B). The base quality, base content and GC distribution were all within the normal range, indicating the reliability of these CUT&Tag data and allowing for further analysis (Additional file 1: Fig. S5C, D, E). CUT&Tag signaling was significantly enriched in the gene body region, TSS region (Additional file 1: Fig. S5F) and peak region (Fig. 6A). A total of 313 genes were bound by E4F1, and 237 genes were bound by E4F1 in the promoter region, such as Cks2, Hmgb2, Rnf126, Dazap1, Sp1, Mus81, Pcna, Zcwpw1, and E2f3 (Additional file 2: Supplementary Table S5). Moreover, we used the MEME algorithm to identify the E4f1-recognizing motif and found that the most abundant elements were TATGTAAATAT, TAGTAAACA and TTAGTAAACA (Additional file 1: Fig. S5G), which are useful for identifying potential binding sites. To identify the targets that account for the phenotypes of E4f1-cKO mice, we investigated 237 genes and 279 DEGs associated with E4f1 deficiency. We found that 3 genes, Cks2, Hmgb2 and Rnf126, were bound to the promoter region of E4f1 and were upregulated in E4f1-cKO mice (Fig. 6B). Assessment of the potential functions of genes in the promoter region via GO enrichment and KEGG analyses revealed the cell cycle and pathways involved in carbon metabolism (Additional file 1: Fig. S5H, I; Additional file 2: Supplementary Table S6). Visualization of Cks2, Hmgb2 and Rnf126 via IGV (V2.14.0) revealed some changes in the region that binds to the E4f1 promoter region (Fig. 6C). The qPCR results revealed that, compared with the IgG control, Cks2 was more enriched (Fig. 6D). Next, we selected Cks2 and Hmgb2, which were enriched at a multiplicity greater than 0.5, for validation. Western blot analysis confirmed that the expression of these genes was significantly increased at the protein level in E4f1-cKO mice (Fig. 6E). We next assessed their cellular localization by IF staining of mouse testes at 2mon. These findings revealed that CKS2 was specifically localized to spermatocytes, whereas HMGB2 was detected in both spermatogonia and spermatocytes (Fig. 6F). Taken together, our data identified Cks2 and Hmgb2 as candidate downstream targets through which E4f1 may contribute to the regulation of spermatocyte development.

Discussion

The transcription factor E4F1 is expressed in Sertoli cells and spermatogonia in the neonatal testis. E4f1 has a profound effect on the proliferation of Sertoli cells and is critical for maintaining the SSC pool [20]. E4F1 has also been shown in human breast cancer studies to be involved in signaling for DSBs and repair [13]. However, very little is known about the mechanism by which E4f1 controls spermatogonial differentiation and meiosis of spermatocytes in mouse spermatogenesis. In this study, we demonstrated that the progression of meiosis was arrested in the pachytene stage and that spermatogonial differentiation was impaired in E4f1-cKO mice. In various types of spermatogenic cells, E4F1 expression is enriched in the spermatogonial population, and we concluded that E4F1-dependent regulatory programs in these cells direct spermatogonial differentiation and meiosis progression (Fig. 7).

Conversely, the absence of E4f1 in STRA8+ germ cells impairs spermatogonial differentiation and causes meiosis to arrest at pachytene stage. Mechanistically, E4F1 directs the expression of genes related to spermatogonial differentiation and DSB repair in spermatocytes. The absence of E4f1 leads to the upregulation of meiotic genes (e.g., Dmrtb1, Hormad1, Sycp3) and causes DSB repair failure, resulting in meiotic arrest

Yan et al.. reported that germline-specific E4f1 depletion leads to the loss of undifferentiated spermatogonia and spermatogonial differentiation defects [19]. The Vasa-Cre [25] strain used in Yan’s study started to function as early as E12.5 and can be used to effectively study the phenotype caused by the early loss of E4f1. On the other hand, Stra8-Cre plays a role in the postnatal testes of the germ cell population undergoing the transition from mitosis to meiosis, including late differentiated spermatogonia and early meiotic spermatocytes [26]. In our study, we used Stra8-GFPCre to conditionally knockout E4f1 and investigated its role in the critical stage of male germ cell development. Our scRNA-seq results revealed that genes involved in spermatogonial differentiation and the transition from mitosis to meiosis, such as Uchl1, Zbtb16, Sohlh1, Lin28a, Dmrt1, Stra8 and Sox3, were significantly downregulated in E4f1-cKO testes (p < 0.05). For example, Uchl1 [27], Zbtb16 [28] and Lin28a [29] are important for the establishment and maintenance of SSCs, whereas Dmrt1 [30–32] and Stra8 [33, 34] play important roles in controlling the mitosis/meiosis switch and promoting spermatogonial differentiation. SOHLH1 is expressed in type Aal and differentiating spermatogonia, and Sohlh1-cKO mice exhibit meiotic defects at the zygotene stage during spermatogenesis [35]. SOX3 is expressed in spermatogonial populations; specifically, Sox3-null testes exhibit an increase in self-renewing spermatogonial subsets and a decrease in differentiation-primed progenitors [36]. This result further supports the findings of previous studies and suggests that disruption of this genetic network affects spermatogonial differentiation, which blocks the progression from mitosis to meiosis. However, it remains unclear whether the phenotype observed in E4f1-cKO mice is due mainly to defects in spermatogonial differentiation or abnormal development of early meiotic spermatocytes. Future studies are needed to accurately determine the timing and efficiency of E4f1 deficiency in order to determine the exact stage at which E4f1 plays a critical role in the male germline.

The expression of genes associated with meiotic DSBs and repair, such as Dmrtb1, Dmrtc2, Hormad1, Sycp3 and Tcte2, was significantly upregulated in E4f1-cKO testes (p < 0.05). Dmrtb1 (also known as Dmrb6) mutant germ cells exhibit a disruption of spermatogonial differentiation and a failure to complete meiotic prophase, which is the result of a problem with gene regulation before the initiation of the meiotic program (e.g., Stra8 and Sycp3) [37]. The DMRTC2 (also known as DMRT7) protein is expressed in germ cells specifically and is localized to the XY body of male pachytene germ cells, and spermatogenic arrest occurs at the pachytene stage in Dmrt7 mutants [38, 39]. SYCP3 and HORMAD1 are the lateral and axial components of the chromosomal synaptic complex, respectively. The absence of Sycp3 or Hormad1 disrupts the formation of synaptonemal complexes, thereby causing male sterility [40, 41]. Moreover, Tcte2 [42], Tcfl5 [43] and Psma8 [44] play important roles in meiosis. In addition, a number of genes expressed in germ cells, such as Ybx1, Fbxo36, Mrpl39, Setx, Tkfc and Hoxa4, also presented altered expression levels in E4f1-cKO testes (p < 0.05), but their functional roles in testes are poorly understood. Considering the results of scRNA-seq, we believe that the downregulation of spermatogonial differentiation genes may be responsible for the increase in the number of undifferentiated spermatogonia and decrease in the number of differentiated spermatogonia. A small number of differentiated spermatogonia, which can further differentiate into spermatocytes and enter meiosis, are present in E4f1-cKO mice. Given the observed meiotic arrest, the upregulation of these genes might represent a compensatory response or a secondary consequence of pachytene arrest rather than the primary cause.

Generally, meiotic recombination defects are significant causes of pachytene stage arrest [45]. Variants of meiotic recombination can cause subfertility or infertility [46]. In E4f1-cKO mice, spermatocytes are arrested at the pachytene stage, and programmed DSB repair fails. The number of Rad51 locus in E4f1-cKO spermatocytes was significantly decreased, suggesting defects in single-stranded DNA invasion or the formation of recombination substrates. Consistent with this observation, we also detected persistent accumulation of γH2AX locus on autosomes in E4f1-cKO spermatocytes. In mouse models, the loss of Rad51 function leads to DSB repair failure during the pachytene stage, resulting in persistent accumulation of γH2AX signals on autosomes [47]. This phenotype of E4f1-cKO is consistent with that observed in mouse models, indicating direct impairment of Rad51 function, which also leads to γH2AX retention due to failure in DSB repair. Furthermore, the number of Mlh1 locus was decreased in E4f1-cKO mice, indicating that the late recombination steps were also impaired [48]. Thus, abnormal meiotic DSB repair is also important in determining the phenotype of E4f1-cKO mice. The meiotic arrest phenotype observed in E4f1-cKO mice is similar to that of other mutants, such as Zfp541−/− mutant mice and Emi2KO mice [11, 49, 50]. However, a notable difference in E4f1-cKO mice is the widespread apoptosis observed from PD14 onward, which underscores the severe and early impact of E4f1 loss on germ cell survival. Taken together, our findings indicate that the loss of E4f1 in STAR8+ germ cells disrupts early recombinosome assembly and stabilization, which leads to defective DSB repair, inefficient crossover formation, and ultimately pachytene arrest.

Combined with the CUT&Tag data, we identified two genes that may interact with E4F1, Cks2 and Hmgb2. A key limitation of our current study is the lack of functional validation to definitively establish whether the dysregulation of Cks2 and Hmgb2 is a primary driver of the meiotic phenotype or a secondary consequence of arrest. CKS2 is overexpressed in a variety of cancers [51–54]. Cks2−/− mice arrest at metaphase I, which suggests that CKS2 is the protein needed for homologous chromosome segregation at meiotic anaphase [55]. Moreover, HMGB2 overexpression promotes cancer cell proliferation [56–58]. Hmgb2-null mice exhibit reduced fertility, and DNA replication and meiotic arrest are affected [59]. In our study, E4f1 deficiency upregulated Cks2 and Hmgb2, and the promoter regions of these two genes were directly bound by E4f1. These findings collectively suggest that E4f1 may act as a transcriptional repressor during mouse spermatogenesis, affecting meiosis by regulating the expression of Cks2 and Hmgb2. Previous studies have shown that in mouse germ cells, E4f1 deficiency increases the percentage of germ cells expressing high levels of phospho-P53 and activates the P53 signaling pathway in mouse germ cells [60]. These findings further support a broader role of E4f1 in transcriptional repression during germ cell development. In summary, our study suggests a potential regulatory relationship between E4f1 and its candidate target genes during spermatocyte development. The dysregulation of Cks2 and Hmgb2 upon E4f1 deficiency correlates with the observed meiotic defects; however, definitive causal validation (such as rescue or loss-of-function experiments) remains a necessary focus for future work to establish whether these genes are direct functional mediators downstream of E4F1. These findings enhance our understanding of spermatogenesis and reveal candidate genes that have potential roles in regulating spermatogonial differentiation and spermatocyte development.

Conclusions

In summary, we showed for the first time that E4F1 is a critical transcription factor for spermatogonial differentiation and meiosis progression in mice. Germline-specific knockdown of E4f1 caused complete male sterility, massive germ cell loss, high levels of apoptosis, swollen mitochondria in spermatogonia and impaired differentiation of spermatogonia. Moreover, E4f1 deletion caused spermatocyte arrest at the pachytene stage and unsuccessful programmed DSB repair. The primary mechanism may involve the regulation of genes such as Cks2 and Hmgb2; however, further validation is needed. This study provides insights into the molecular mechanism that regulates spermatogenesis, which may also provide new insights into the treatment of human male infertility.

Methods

Animals

All animal experiments involving animals were conducted according to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee at the Northwest Institute, Chinese Academy of Science (Approval code: NWIPB012). E4f1flox/flox mice were generously provided by Dr. Sauvageau from the University of Montreal [61]. Stra8-GFPCre+ mice were generated in Dr. Tong’s laboratory as described previously [62]. To obtain conditional knockout mice, E4f1flox/flox females were mated with Stra8-GFPCre+ males to generate Stra8-GFPCre+; E4f1flox/+ male mice. Stra8-GFPCre+; E4f1flox/+ male mice were then mated with E4f1flox/flox mice to generate Stra8-GFPCre+; E4f1flox/flox (E4f1-cKO) male mice. Stra8-GFPCre+; E4f1flox/+ mice were used as controls. For the fertility test, Stra8-GFPCre+; E4f1flox/flox male mice at 8 weeks of age were paired with 3 fertile adult females at 4 months of age as previously described [24]. All the mice were on a 129S2/SvPasCrl; C57BL6N mixed background.

C57BL/6J mice with E4f1 knock-in were generated via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome engineering. Briefly, the hexahistidine tag sequence (6× HIS-Tag) was inserted upstream of the TAG stop codon in exon 14 of the E4f1 gene on chromosome 17. The gRNA-targeting vector and donor oligo (with the targeting sequence flanked by 120 bp homologous sequences combined on both sides) were designed. Cas9, gRNA and donor oligos were coinjected into fertilized eggs for KI mouse production. The primers used for genotyping and all the reagents used for all the experiments are listed in Additional file 2: Supplementary Table S1.

The mice used in the paper were anesthetized with the anesthetic agent avertin (tribromoethanol, TBE), followed by neck breakage and sacrifice. The anesthetic dose was injected intraperitoneally at 0.125–0.24 mg/g. Avertin is associated with a short anesthesia time and fast onset of anesthesia. After alcohol disinfection, the abdomen was cut with sterile surgical scissors. After fat and other tissues are removed, they can be used for tissue fixation, RNA extraction, protein extraction and other experiments.

Histology and Immunofluorescence staining

Histology and immunofluorescence (IF) staining of testicular tissues were conducted as described previously [24]. In brief, paraffin-embedded testicular tissues were sectioned (5 μm thick) and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological evaluation under a microscope (Nikon ECLIPSE E200). For immunofluorescence staining, the sections were subjected to antigen retrieval and then incubated in 10% blocking serum to reduce nonspecific binding of the antibody. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and then with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. The images were observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica, Stellaris5 SR).

Proliferation and apoptosis analysis

Proliferation was assessed by costaining PCNA with TRA98, whereas apoptosis was examined by using the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Roche, Switzerland). The TRA98 antibody was diluted in label solution, which was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After antigen retrieval with 10 mM sodium citrate (pH 6.0), the sections were incubated with a label solution containing TRA98 antibody overnight at 4 °C, washed in PBS three times, and incubated with secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. All operations were performed in the dark.

Cleavage Under Targets and Tag mentation (CUT&Tag) experiment

Testicular single-cell suspensions were prepared as described previously [20]. We used concanavalin A magnetic beads to bind the cells. After washing, the 6× HIS-Tag and IgG antibodies were mixed evenly in 50 µL of antibody buffer, and the mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C. After incubation with secondary antibodies, the pAG-Tn5 transposon was incubated, allowing the transposon to enter the cell and bind to the antibody. Subsequently, Mg2+ was added to activate the cleavage activity of the Tn5 enzyme, moving the DNA region bound to the target protein. Next, the DNA was extracted and amplified via PCR to construct a library. The DNA was processed for deep sequencing (IIIumina PE150). The original image data file obtained by the Illumina sequencing platform was transformed into sequenced reads through base calling analysis using bcl2fastq (V2.20, RRID: SCR_015058) software. The results were stored in the FASTQ file format. Next, filter out reads containing adapter sequences, paired reads in which the content of N in single-end sequencing reads exceeded 10% of the length ratio of this read, and paired reads in which the number of low-quality (< 5) bases in single-end sequencing reads exceeded 50% of the length ratio of this read were filtered out. After splicing and filtering low-quality reads from the raw sequencing data, the clean data were compared with the mouse genome sequence (GRCm38) via Bowtie2 software (V2.1.0). MACS2 software (V2.1.1) was used to analyze the peak regions within the genome, perform gene associations and perform GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on the obtained associated genes. The sequence of the DNA fragments bound by E4f1 was analyzed with MEME (V4.12.0). Promoter-specific primers were analyzed via qPCR (TransGen Biotech, AQ601-02).

Single-cell RNA sequencing and analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing was conducted as described previously [63, 64]. The testes from control and E4f1-cKO male mice at 21 days of age were digested with collagenase, trypsin and DNase I to obtain single cells. Adherent cells were removed with 40 µm cell strainers. Red blood cells were removed via red blood cell lysis buffer (Solarbio, China). The number of cells and viability were tested via an automated cell counter (CountessTM 3 FL, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific). After passing the quality test, gel beads and oil were added to different channels of chromium chip G to form water in the oil structure system (GEM). The cells were subsequently lysed, and the gel beads automatically dissolved and released a large number of primer sequences, which were reverse transcribed with polyA mRNA to generate the first strand of cDNA with UMI information of 10⋅ barcodes. After GEM was broken, the first-strand cDNA was purified via magnetic beads and amplified via PCR to obtain stable cDNA. The amplified cDNA was segmented into enzyme sections, and fragments of the appropriate length were screened. The 3’-terminal expression library containing P5 and P7 connectors and the DTI was constructed via terminal repair, the addition of A, and the connection of the reads2 sequencing primers. Short-read long next-generation sequencing (NGS) was subsequently carried out on an Illumina sequencer. After sequencing, basecall files were output and then transformed into raw sequencing data (raw data) without quality control via bcl2fastq (V2.20) software. Then, primary and secondary quality controls were performed on the FASTQ files. First, paired reads with an N content greater than 3 in the reads, bases with a base quality value lower than 5, paired reads with a low base number exceeding 20% of the read length ratio, and adapter sequences with matching lower than 8 bp were removed. The first 1,000,000 (1 M) reads were extracted from the FASTQ file after primary quality control, and genome alignment was performed via bwa (V0.7.15-r1140) software. Moreover, the first 10,000 (10 K) reads were extracted from the FASTQ file after primary quality control, and rRNA alignment was performed via Bowtie2 (V2.3.2) software. After the raw data were obtained, Cell Ranger (V1.1.0) was used to extract the UMI and barcode of the cells, and STAR software was used to compare them to the reference genome, correct the barcode, filter and correct the UMI. Finally, the real single-cell data were extracted, and the gene expression matrix of each cell was obtained. Next, we input the data into R and use the Seurat (V4.3.0) package to perform cell typing on the standardized and filtered data through dimensionality reduction (PCA) and clustering methods. According to the clustering results, the UMAP dimensionality reduction algorithm was used to display the cell distribution. The cell types were annotated with known marker genes. Ddx4 and Dazl were used for germ cells, Sox9 and Amh for Sertoli cells, Cyp11a1 and Hsd3b1 for Leydig cells, Tie1 and Tek for endothelial, Acta2 and Myh11 for myoid cells, Adgre1 and Dab2 for macrophages. To further investigate germ cells, we extracted and annotated germ cells. The marker genes used for annotation of germ cell cell types were as follows: Uchl1 and Etv5 for undifferentiated spermatogonia; Kit and Sohlh1 for differentiated spermatogonia; Prdm9 for leptotene spermatocytes; Prss50 for zygotene spermatocytes; Aurka and Nme8 for pachytene spermatocytes; Pou5f2 for diplotene and diakinesis spermatocytes; and Smcp for round sperm. The cell-gene expression matrix files were imported into R, and we used the Seurat (V4.3.1) package for data cleaning, dimensionality reduction and clustering, cell type and differentially expressed gene (DEG) identification, and functional analysis of the DEGs. The search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING, V12.0) and Cytoscape (V3.9.1) software were used to map the DEG networks. The Monocle (V2.30.0) package was used to conduct pseudotime trajectory analysis.

Transmission electron microscopy of testes

Testicular tissues were prefixed with 3% glutaraldehyde, postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated in a series of acetone solutions, infiltrated with Epox 812, and embedded. The semithin sections were stained with methylene blue, and ultrathin sections were cut with a diamond knife and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The sections were examined with a JEM-1400-FLASH transmission electron microscope.

Chromosome spreading in spermatocytes

Chromosome spreading was conducted according to a previously established drying-down technique [65]. Briefly, seminiferous tubules were dispersed and treated in hypotonic solution. The cell suspension was evenly spread on adhesive slides soaked in 1% PFA and placed in a hot and wet box overnight. After drying at room temperature, the antibody was diluted with ADB, dropped onto slides, sealed with rubber cement, and incubated at 37 °C. The slices were stained with H33342, mounted in 50% glycerin, and examined under a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany).

Western blot

Total testicular protein was extracted from control and E4f1-cKO mice via the KeyGEN Biological Whole Protein Extraction Kit (No. KGP 2100). The protein concentration was determined with a BCA protein assay kit (Lot: 17D01A46). An SDS‒PAGE gel preparation kit (Lot: 17G26C38) was used to prepare a 12% separating gel and a 5% stacking gel. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes, and nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% skim milk powder. The antibodies were incubated at 4 °C, followed by exposure to view the results.

Statistical analysis

Data from at least three biologically independent replicates per group (n ≥ 3) are presented as the means ± SDs. GraphPad Prism v7 software was used for all the statistical analyses. Specifically, comparisons of the means between two groups (e.g., control and E4f1-cKO at PD14, PD35, or 2 months) were performed via an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test. This test was chosen after confirming the homogeneity of variances between the compared groups by using the F test. The data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). Statistical significance is expressed as follows: * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001, **** P < 0.0001.
