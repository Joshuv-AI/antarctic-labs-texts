Interannual variation in freshwater and marine phycotoxins quantified from oysters cultured across diverse coastal sites
Authors: Tal Ben-Horin, Kalle Simpson, Madeline Anderson, Jonathan Lucas, Barrett Rose, Emory Gardner, Miranda Hart, Marco Valera, Astrid Schnetzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The expanding footprint of global aquaculture has pushed production systems into regions with unknown or limited baseline information about associated hazards and risks. Oyster (Crassostrea virginica) aquaculture, for example, has expanded throughout euhaline coastal sounds across the southeastern United States yet there exists limited information on phycotoxin levels in oysters cultured in these environments. Here we investigated domoic acid and microcystin accumulation in cultured oysters at three coastal sites in North Carolina, with all sites varying in surrounding land use. Oyster deployments throughout 2021 and 2023 were complemented by harmful algal bloom monitoring methods including the use of solid-phase adsorption toxin tracking devices and microscopy analyses to determine microalgal cell concentrations from water samples. Domoic acid was detected in 20% and 38%, and microcystin in 48% and 11%, of the tested oysters during 2021 and 2023 (N = 178 total oysters tested), with both toxins co-occurring in 7% of the animals. While domoic acid concentrations remained far below seafood safety action levels, maximum microcystin levels at some sites approached tolerable daily intake guidelines in 2021 but were nearly absent across all sites in 2023. This latter observation implicated environmental drivers of cyanotoxin production, transport, and accumulation in oysters operating at regional scales greater than individual oyster farms. Fully understanding the environmental drivers of phycotoxin production within a landscape context, including how and when these toxins accumulate in shellfish, therefore represents a critical research gap. Given the regional synchrony in phycotoxin concentrations observed in oysters, there holds considerable promise for developing adaptive sanitation and control programs in response to conditions when perceived toxin risks are high.

Introduction

Global aquaculture has expanded and diversified production systems into new frontiers (Merino et al., 2010; Gephart et al., 2020; Naylor et al., 2021), often with unknown or limited baseline information about associated hazards and risks. Oyster (Crassostrea virginica) aquaculture, for example, has expanded into euhaline coastal sounds across the southeastern United States, which stands in contrast to the meso- and polyhaline estuary regions that have historically supported extensive commercial oyster fisheries (Banta et al., 2003; Powell et al., 2012; Schulte, 2017). While oyster aquaculture industry growth and its public perception have been supported in part by wide recognition of the ecosystem services oysters provide by removing nutrients and organic material from the water column (Alleway and Connell, 2015; van der Schatte Olivier et al., 2020; Walton and Swann, 2021), the efficiency of filter feeding bivalves allows them to concentrate various environmental toxins and contaminants to levels that are dangerous to human health (Shumway, 1990; James et al., 2010). For this and other reasons aquaculture industry growth into emerging regions has challenged effective shellfish sanitation and control programs (James et al., 2010; Fernandes-Salvador et al., 2021).

Recent field surveys have accumulated evidence that toxins produced by both freshwater and marine phytoplankton co-occur in coastal oyster growing areas and readily accumulate in oyster tissues (Gibble and Kudela, 2014; Preece et al., 2017; Peacock et al., 2018; Onofrio et al., 2021; Anderson et al., 2023). In fact, lower estuary regions have been identified as a focal area for phycotoxin diversity (Onofrio et al., 2021), and phytoplankton diversity in general (Smith et al., 2005; Olli et al., 2015), with both coastal marine and estuarine taxa co-occurring and capable of producing toxins that accumulate in shellfish (Thessen and Stoecker, 2008; Shuler et al., 2012; Clark et al., 2019). In addition, phycotoxins associated with freshwater taxa, such as microcystins, can be environmentally persistent and have been found to be ubiquitous even in coastal ecosystems (Jones and Negri, 1997; Schmidt et al., 2014; Zastepa et al., 2014; Peacock et al., 2018; Pease et al., 2023; Wallace et al., 2025a; 2025b). Evidence also suggests that oysters can often maintain significantly higher tissue concentrations of phycotoxins compared to other bivalve species (Neves et al., 2021; Pease et al., 2023; Wallace et al., 2025b). This observed distinction for oysters highlights how diverse bivalve taxa, each with unique biogeographic, life history, behavioral, and immunological traits, interact with sympatric toxin-producing algae and their produced toxins to shape toxin exposures and health risks to humans (Tan et al., 2023). Generalizing patterns of phycotoxins associated with coastal oyster aquaculture expansion will therefore help establish baselines for assessing public health risks and set targets for shellfish sanitation and control measures, particularly as state and federal resource management agencies consider best approaches to assess and regulate algae toxins in seafood (Wu et al., 2022).

Here we deployed oysters at diverse coastal farm sites in North Carolina to characterize phycotoxins associated with coastal oyster aquaculture and how they vary across space and time. We used two targets, domoic acid and microcystins, to investigate toxins produced by autochthonous marine algae as well as toxins produced by algae in fresh and brackish systems that are then transported to lower estuary margins (Paerl et al., 2018b). Co-occurrence of these phycotoxins has been widely reported across diverse systems (Gibble and Kudela, 2014; Peacock et al., 2018; Tatters et al., 2021), including coastal North Carolina (Anderson et al., 2023), and both have also been observed to occur in oysters (Pease et al., 2023). These phycotoxins also present significant human health risks. Domoic acid is a kainic acid-type neurotoxin produced by toxigenic species that belong to the marine diatom genus Pseudo-nitzschia (Bates et al., 1998), and blooms of this harmful algae pose a risk for animal and human health in US waters as well as globally (Lelong et al., 2012; Hallegraeff et al., 2021). Exposure commonly occurs through consumption of contaminated shellfish that have accumulated the toxin by filter feeding on toxigenic cells during blooms (Smith et al., 2018). Clinical signs of acute toxicity, from mild gastrointestinal issues to severe neurological damage and death, have led to illness associated with domoic acid exposure to be known as amnesic shellfish poisoning, or ASP (Jeffery et al., 2004). While toxigenic Pseudo-nitzchia have also been documented in large estuaries within the mid-Atlantic Bight region, including Chesapeake Bay and the Pamlico-Albemarle Sound, the associated risks from toxin production in these systems are yet to be established (Thessen and Stoecker, 2008; Anderson et al., 2023). Microcystins are a group of over 270 known cyclic heptapeptides (congeners) produced by numerous and in some instances distantly related genera of cyanobacteria (Rantala et al., 2004; Bouaïcha et al., 2019). Microcystin producing genera include Microcystis, Planktothrix, Dolichospermum, Oscillatoria, and Nostoc, and these toxins are among the most ubiquitous cyanobacterial toxins in freshwater and estuarine ecosystems (Loftin et al., 2016; Preece et al., 2017; Chorus et al., 2021). Human exposure can occur during recreational activities (e.g., swimming and kayaking) through skin contact, the ingestion of contaminated water, or the inhalation of aerosolized cyanotoxins (Hilborn and Beasley, 2015). Similar to domoic acid, fish and shellfish can become a source of intoxication (Ibelings and Chorus, 2007; Svirčev et al., 2019). Acute health effects upon microcystin exposure include dermal and respiratory symptoms, abdominal pain, nausea, and headaches, with more severe exposure potentially leading to hepatoxicity and acute liver failure (hepatotoxic shellfish poisoning or HSP) (Vareli et al., 2013; Lad et al., 2022). Evidence also suggests that chronic exposure may also be associated with liver and colorectal cancers (Hernandez et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023). Our goal for this study was to investigate the prevalence and spatiotemporal trends of domoic acid and microcystin in coastal oyster aquaculture and to assess how various harmful algal bloom monitoring methods, including the co-deployment of oysters with solid phase adsorption toxin tracking (SPATT) devices and the use of algal community characterization by microscopy, may be applied to risk assessments for phycotoxins in seafood.

Methods

Experimental sites and deployment

Triploid oysters (~ 50 mm) from the HNRY high salinity line (Allen et al., 2021) were purchased from a commercial oyster nursery and deployed to three coastal shellfish leases in North Carolina (Fig. 1) in April 2021 and again in April 2023. The three sites included two research leases. The southernmost site, Masonboro Sound, was immediately adjacent to the University of North Carolina Wilmington Shellfish Research Hatchery and located in an urbanized setting and connected via nearby canal to the Cape Fear River, the largest and most industrial river in North Carolina. The central station was located at the study site of the Bogue Sound plankton time-series adjacent to the North Carolina State University Center for Marine Sciences and Technology. The site is characterized by low nutrient levels (Anderson et al., 2023) and high turnover of continental shelf water through nearby Beaufort Inlet (Logan et al., 2000). The third site was in Core Sound at a commercial shellfish lease, with the specific place name and site location omitted here to maintain the confidentiality of our industry partners. Core Sound, lying immediately east of Bogue Sound, connects Beaufort Inlet to the broader Pamlico Sound, and the surrounding watershed is extensively developed for corn and soybean production. All sites are working shellfish farms, with various aquaculture gear utilized at the sites including the floating aquaculture bags used in this study. The floating bags at all sites were arranged along parallel lines affixed to the substrate along perpendicular ground lines, with ground lines anchored by auger screws or hydraulic shallow water anchors. We placed 200 oysters in each bag in April of each sampling year and removed oysters from the bags during our monthly sampling and when oysters were found gaping or moribund. All sites were visited monthly for sensor maintenance and sampling from the spring through fall 2021 for the April 2021 deployment and again from the spring through fall 2023 for the April 2023 deployment.

Physiochemical parameters

We deployed in situ HOBO saltwater conductivity/temperature sensors (Onset Computer Corporation, Bourne, MA USA) in vented PVC housings directly below the surface on one of the perpendicular lines holding the floating oyster bags at each site. The sensors were programed to record and store temperature and salinity data hourly, and at each site visit we took point measurements of conductivity and temperature using a YSI ProSolo handheld meter with optical conductivity and temperature probes (Xylem Inc., Washington, DC USA). The point measurements were used to routinely calibrate the in situ HOBO sensors. At each site visit in 2021 we also collected surface water samples for dissolved nutrient analysis. Samples were pre-filtered through a 0.45 μm Acrodisk filter (Pall Corporation, Port Washington, NY USA) to remove particulates, and we stored the dissolved fraction at −80°C until analysis for NO2 + NO3, PO4, and NH4 at the University of South Carolina Marine Biogeochemistry Laboratory.

Microplankton community structure

We collected surface seawater from each site at each sampling event, preserving 50 mL in Acid Lugol’s (5% final concentration) to characterize the microplankton community (20–200 μm) using standard settling techniques of Utermöhl (1958). Organisms were assigned to major taxonomic groups (diatoms, dinoflagellates, and ciliates) via light microscopy (20 × Olympus BX53). Taxa were identified to genus-level whenever possible. In 2023, archived samples made additional analyses feasible (63 × oil immersion) to screen for cyanobacterial colonies and filaments.

Toxin analyses

Solid phase adsorption tracking (SPATTs) units and oysters (N = 5) were collected at each monthly sampling event to measure for toxins using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs). The SPATT bags were deployed just below the surface along the line adjacent to the vented PVC housings holding the conductivity/temperature sensors. Upon collection, the SPATT and oyster samples were kept out of sunlight and transported on ice to the lab and stored at −20°C until analysis. SPATTs filled with HP20 resin were constructed and activated following established protocols (Negrey et al., 2019) and deployed in duplicate directly to the main line holding the floating oyster bags. Prior to freezing, oysters were scrubbed and shucked, and total wet weights recorded. Extraction of accumulated dissolved microcystins and domoic acid from SPATTs followed established protocols (Lane et al., 2010; Kudela, 2011) with the following modifications: the resin beads were vortexed for one minute immediately after 10 mL of MeOH was added, and the three extracts for each toxin were combined and run together. After thawing, single oysters were homogenized in triplicates (Benchmark D1000 Tissue Homogenizer). Extractions followed the protocols established by the ELISA manufacturer. Microcystin analyses on SPATTs and homogenized oysters were conducted using an Abraxis ELISA kit (Gold Standard Diagnostics, Westminster, PA, USA; Product #520011; sensitive to MC-LR, -YR, -LF, -RR, LW, and nodularin; LDL = 0.10 μg L−1). Domoic acid analyses were performed using an Abraxis ELISA kit (Gold Standard Diagnostics, Westminster, PA, USA; Product # PNON0021; LDL = 0.17 μg L−1). Final MeOH concentrations were diluted to < 5% for microcystin assays and < 1% for domoic acid as recommended. When samples returned absorbance coefficients varying above the recommended range, samples were re-analyzed. Absorbances were read at 450 nm using a BioTek ELx800 Absorbance Microplate Reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). All SPATT and oyster phycotoxin results were normalized to nanograms toxin per gram resin or oyster wet weight (ng g−1).

Chlorophyll a

Surface water samples (200 mL) were filtered onto Whatman GF/F filters (0.7 m) for Chl-a analyses. Filters were thawed, suspended in 7 mL of 100% acetone, sonicated for 5 s at 50% intensity (Fisher Scientific, Hampton, NH, USA, Model 120 Sonic Dismembrator) and extracted in the dark at −20 °C for 24 h (Strickland and Parsons, 1972). The chl-a concentration (μg L−1) for each sample was then determined fluorometrically (Turner Designs Trilogy, San Jose, CA, USA) using previously published protocols (Welschmeyer, 1994). All filters were stored at −80°C until extraction and detection via fluorometry.

Data management and statistical analyses

Environmental data (temperature and salinity) were offloaded from the in situ sensors monthly and calibrated to the collected point samples (Thirstrup and Deleebeeck, 2021). The temperature and salinity data were visually assessed for non-conformity and sensor function, with all data points varying by > 2 standard deviations from the mean values collected over the three previous days removed. A Savitzky-Golay finite impulse response (FIR) smoothing filter with a three-day frame length was applied to the quality-controlled temperature and salinity data to smooth high-frequency variation typically observed with environmental sensor data. Mean temperature and salinity values were estimated from the filtered data. We processed the temperature and salinity time series’ using the sgolayfilt function in MATLAB version R2024b.

Differences in dissolved nutrient concentrations (NOx, PO4, and NH4) across the sites in 2021 were tested by multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA), with the quantified nutrient concentrations log-transformed to meet normality assumptions. We tested whether the different coastal sites differed in quantified Chl-a concentrations using a general linear mixed model. In this case we treated the sampling sites as independent fixed effects and the sampling month each year as random intercept.

We used the microplankton community structure data to draw inferences about how implicit site-specific characteristics may shape plankton communities and how these varied over our sampling years. Shannon diversity indices were calculated for all microplankton samples, and we tested for differences in Shannon diversity across the sites using a general linear mixed model, treating the sampling sites as independent fixed effects and the sampling month and year as random intercept parameter. We then used permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) to assess differences in community composition across the sites over the two sampling years and visualized microplankton community composition across sites and years with non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS). We implemented NMDS and PERMANOVA using the vegan package (Oksanen et al., 2025) in R (R Core Team 2024).

Microcystin and domoic acid concentrations in SPATTs and oysters across the sites and sampling years were synthesized using a two-part hurdle mixed effects (Hsu and Liu, 2008). Phycotoxin concentrations quantified from environmental samples are often at or below assay detection limits, and we replaced all values for samples below assay detection limits with zeros, leading to zero-inflated toxin concentration data. The hurdle model used here specified a logistic regression for a dichotomous indicator specifying whether phycotoxin concentrations were zero or not, and then a linear mixed effects model for the non-zero responses. We specified the sampling sites and years as independent fixed effects in the linear mixed model, and to control for intra-annual variation in regional domoic acid and microcystin concentrations, we used an ordinal variable specifying the individual sampling events as a random intercept parameter. Likelihood ratio tests were used to test for differences in domoic acid and microcystin concentrations in SPATTs across the sites and sampling years. Hurdle models were implemented using the GLMMadaptive package (Rizopoulos, 2018) in R (R Core Team 2024).

Results

Physicochemical parameters and chlorophyll biomass

Measured surface water temperatures, salinities, and Chl-a were all marked by seasonal variability (Fig. 2). In 2021, the three sampling sites varied in dissolved nutrient concentrations during 2021 (MANOVA: F2,25 = 4.21, P = 0.002), with Bogue Sound consistently measuring lower concentrations of NO2 + NO3, PO4 and NH4 compared to the other two sites (Table 1). Phytoplankton biomass (i.e. Chl-a concentrations) varied across the sites (Linear mixed effects model: F2,36 = 6.14, P = 0.005) in line with dissolved nutrient concentrations, with Bogue Sound characterized by lower Chl-a concentrations relative to the other sites (Fig. 2).

Microplankton community structure and diversity

A total of 40 microeukaryote genera or major taxonomic groups were identified belonging to the diatoms, dinoflagellates, and ciliates (Table 2). Total cell densities across all sampling events ranged from 3 to 788 cells mL−1 with an average of 113 cells mL−1 (Fig. 3). Diatoms dominated the assemblages in terms of relative abundance with an average contribution of 75% (range = 20% – 100%) and 71% (range = 10% – 96%) across the three sites during 2021 and 2023, respectively (Fig. 3). Relative abundances for dinoflagellates averaged 8% and 18% during 2021 and 2023, respectively (ranges = 0% – 40% and 0% – 74%). Ciliates contributed on average 17% and 12% across the three sites during both years (ranges = 0% – 58% and 0% – 52%, respectively). The most common chain-forming diatom genera included Skeletonema, Pseudo-nitzschia, Leptocylindrus and Chaetoceros with total cell densities for Skeletonema and Pseudo-nitzschia exceeding 500 cells per mL−1 during blooms in Core Sound during June/July of 2023 and in Masonboro Sound in August of 2021, respectively (panels B and E in Fig. 3). The toxigenic diatom Pseudo-nitzschia spp. varied in its contributions across locations and was generally more prevalent during 2023 (Fig. 3). Pseudo-nitzschia contributed on average 3% to the microeukaryote assemblage in 2021 (range = 0% – 29%) and 12% in 2023 (range = 0% – 63%). The dinoflagellate assemblage was dominated by members of the genera Prorocentrum and Scripsiella while ciliates included a mix of aloricates and tintinnids. Quantified Shannon diversity indices varied across the two sampling years (ANOVA; F1,49 = 26.15, P < 0.001) but did not vary across the sites (ANOVA; F2,48 = 2.21, P < 0.12). Overall microplankton community structure varied across the sites (Permutational Multivariate ANOVA: F2,48 = 2.67, P < 0.001; Fig. 4) and more strongly between years (Permutational Multivariate ANOVA: F1,49 = 9.65, P < 0.001; Fig. 4).

Cyanobacterial presence

In 2023, additional screening of the settled samples via light microscopy at higher resolution (63x) allowed to document the presence of three potential toxin-producing cyanobacterial taxa. Most commonly observed were filaments of Dolichospermum, followed by Cylindrospermopsis raciborskii and Planktolyngbbia limnetica (Antunes et al., 2015; Li et al., 2016; Bukowska et al., 2017; Fig. 5). We were not able to conduct any additional screening of settled samples for plankton samples collected in 2021.

Phycotoxin dynamics

Microcystins (Fig. 6) and domoic acid (Fig. 7) were detected at all sites and in both sampling years. The toxins co-occurred in 2% of the sampled SPATTs (N = 1) and 7% of the sampled oysters (N = 11). Accumulated dissolved microcystin was detected in 44% (N = 18) of the deployed SPATTs, however domoic acid was detected in only 7% (N = 3) of the SPATTs, with single detections of domoic acid at Core Sound and Bogue Sound in 2021 and a single detection at Bogue Sound in 2023 (Fig. 7). We therefore did not test for differences in accumulated dissolved domoic acid concentrations from SPATTs due to too few data points. Dissolved accumulated microcystin concentrations quantified from SPATTs extracts differed between the sampling years (Hurdle model: Likelihood Ratio χ2 = 4.11, df = 1, P = 0.04), with only rare observations in 2023 (Fig. 6). We did not observe significant differences in dissolved microcystin concentrations in the SPATTs deployed across the sampling sites (Hurdle model: Likelihood Ratio χ2 = 1.06, df = 2, P = 0.59).

Microcystin concentrations in oysters ranged from below the assay detection limit to 6.50 ng g−1 with 57% (N = 50/84) and 11% (N = 10/96) of the oysters testing positive in 2021 and 2023, respectively. Like SPATTs, microcystin concentrations in oysters varied between the 2021 and 2023 sampling years, where oyster samples from 2021 were 6.7 times more likely than samples collected in 2023 to be above ELISA detection limits (Hurdle model: zero-part coefficient = 1.91, SE = 0.46, z = 4.17, P < 0.001). We did not observe differences in microcystin concentrations from oyster samples across the three sites (Hurdle model: Likelihood Ratio χ2 = 1.36, df = 2, P = 0.51).

Domoic acid, although only infrequently observed in SPATTs, was widely observed in oyster samples (Fig. 7) with 20% (N = 18/88) and 43% (N = 38/88) of animals testing positive in 2021 and 2023, respectively. Domoic acid concentrations did not differ across the three sites (Hurdle model: Likelihood Ratio χ2 = 4.69, df = 2, P = 0.09), although there was weak evidence that oysters from Bogue Sound were marginally more likely than samples from Core and Masonboro Sounds to be above ELISA detection limits (Hurdle model: zero-part coefficient = 0.12, SE = 0.36, z = 0.34, P = 0.74). Unlike microcystin concentrations, domoic acid concentrations in oyster samples did not differ between 2021 and 2023 (Hurdle model: Likelihood Ratio χ2 = 3.13, df = 1, P = 0.08).

Discussion

Aquaculture and phycotoxins of concern in North Carolina’s coastal waters

This is the first time freshwater and marine phycotoxins are reported to co-occur in oysters cultured at multiple coastal farm sites in North Carolina. Albeit only less than 10% of observations presented with both quantifiable domoic acid and microcystin, both phycotoxins were frequently detected separately with > 33% of the total animals being positive for either toxin. These findings are relevant for the assessment of current and future seafood safety and exposure risk assessments in multiple ways. For one, these data are necessary to guide monitoring approaches for regional toxins of concern to address potential harm due to acute as well as chronic exposure to low-level phycotoxin concentrations via seafood consumption (Ferriss et al., 2017; Farabegoli et al., 2018; Petroff et al., 2021). And moreover, our environmental toxin data can guide the development of regionally relevant guidelines, particularly given the expansion of shellfish aquaculture in this region (Walton and Swann, 2021).

The prevalence of microcystins we observed in oysters suggests that phycotoxins produced by brackish and freshwater taxa are a valid concern in North Carolina euhaline areas. We found that the variability in toxin levels was more apparent over time rather than over the spatial resolution of our coastal sampling sites. A long-term study (2015–2020) at the Bogue Sound site also showed strong interannual variability in phycotoxin dynamics based on SPATT observations (Anderson et al., 2023). In this present study, microcystins were common across oyster and SPATT samples from all oyster farm sites throughout much of 2021, however, they were noticeably absent across much of 2023. Based on synthesized climate data from 2021 to 2023 (Amatya et al., 2024; Tran et al., 2024), although average patterns of rainfall, temperature, and coastal salinity varied little between our sampling years, 2021 was a year of drastic change (NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information 2025) from unusually dry conditions in the spring (March through May precipitation = 184.15 mm) followed by more frequent heavy rainfall along the coastal plain throughout summer (June - August precipitation = 611.38 mm). We speculate that these overall conditions may have aided the transport of freshwater algae and/or toxins to the coastal areas. Overall, this study aligns with an increasing number of reports on freshwater phycotoxins in coastal ecosystems and accumulation in marine food webs, including in oysters and other bivalves (Gibble and Kudela, 2014; Preece et al., 2017; Peacock et al., 2018; Onofrio et al., 2021; Anderson et al., 2023). Our findings also underscore the necessity to characterize microcystin production and fate in relation to processes operating at broader spatial scales. The dominant role that regional processes play in the development of toxigenic cyanobacteria blooms has been documented in numerous studies, especially the role of pulsed nutrient inputs, occurring both when flood-stage river waters are diverted into estuarine systems (Bukowska et al., 2017; Bargu et al., 2023; Wallace et al., 2025a) and when episodic rainfall and flooding occurs in association with extreme weather events such as tropical cyclones (Paerl et al., 2018a; Chen et al., 2019).

Although our observational study was not originally designed to implicate or identify cyanobacteria taxa that may be the source of cyanotoxins accumulating in oysters cultured at coastal farm sites, we were able to identify three cyanobacterial genera in samples collected in 2023: Dolichospermum, Cylindrospermopsis, and Planktolyngbbia. Of these three filamentous toxigenic taxa, which had all been previously reported within North Carolina waters (Calandrino and Paerl, 2011; Touchette et al., 2007; Pierce et al., 2024), only Dolichospermum species are currently known as producers of microcystin (Li et al., 2016). However, expanding testing to include other common cyanotoxins may be warranted given that across all three genera have been found to produce additional toxins, including anatoxin (all three taxa), saxitoxin (Dolichospermum and Cylindrospermopsis), and cylindrospermopsin (Dolichospermum and Cylindrospermopsis) (Somdee et al., 2013; Antunes et al., 2015; Li et al., 2016). Notably, we did not detect Microcystis spp., which often is a prominent member of cyanobacterial assemblages and have dominated toxic blooms reported in North Carolina (Pierce et al., 2024). However, toxic cyanobacteria such as Microcystis are known to be intolerant of higher salinities, and their presence in lower estuary waters such as our oyster farm sites has been found to be more associated with freshwater influxes than local productivity (Wang et al., 2022). Because cyanobacteria colonies are typically disrupted during transport or hard to identify when incorporated into the abundant detrital aggregates, future studies could complement microscopy approaches with genetic methods to assess potential downstream contributions from Microcystis spp. and other potential cyanobacterial toxin producers (Feist and Lance, 2021).

The marine toxin domoic acid was also observed in oysters, and to a lesser extent in SPATTs, across the sites in both sampling years, particularly in the spring and then late summer to fall, which was broadly in line with our direct observations of Pseudo-nitzschia spp. The fact that the diatom can be a common member of the overall assemblage along North Carolina coasts was also seen for the Bogue Sound time-series from 2015 to 2020, which documented cell densities ranging from an average and maximum of 12 and 67 cells mL−1, respectively, during monthly sampling (Anderson et al., 2023). These cell abundances are within the low end of the observed range of our 2021 and 2023 observations for both Bogue Sound (mean = 6 cells mL−1; max = 49 cells mL−1) and Masonboro Sound (mean = 5 cells mL−1; max = 28 cells mL−1). Sampling in Core Sound, however, provided insight into abundances that may be reached when surveys happen to coincide with an ongoing bloom event, as we detected a maximum of 530 cells mL−1 (67% of total assemblage) during June 2023 (Fig. 5B). Despite the high-density bloom, only very low levels of domoic acid contamination were seen in the oysters tested. Blooms conditions have shown to reduce oyster feeding and increase pseudofeces production when Pseudo-nitzschia dominate microalgal assemblages (Mafra et al., 2009), suggesting oysters preferentially avoid potentially toxigenic algae or at least modify feeding to preferentially select more nutritious food items from dynamic and mixed phytoplankton assembles (Newell and Jordan, 1983; Espinosa et al., 2009; Rosa et al., 2013). We did not detect any domoic acid in SPATT samples during this time, which may have also been partially due to the presence of non-toxic Pseudo-nitzschia and/or toxigenic members of this diatom genus experiencing conditions that did not induce toxin production (Kudela et al., 2002; Anderson et al., 2010; Sandoval-Belmar et al., 2023). Notably, depending on the prevalent species, highly toxic blooms may occur even if Pseudo-nitzschia remains a minor component of a mixed phytoplankton assemblage (i.e., <10% of the overall community; Schnetzer et al., 2007, 2013). Based on our findings, future resolution of Pseudo-nitzschia species composition is warranted, as the distinction between non-toxigenic and toxigenic species is not afforded by microscopy (Turk Dermastia et al., 2022; He et al., 2024), and will help assess risks associated with Pseudo-nitzschia blooms in North Carolina coastal waters.

Guidelines for shellfish sanitation and control

Beyond effective environmental toxin monitoring and prediction, addressing health and illness risks from phycotoxins in seafood and establishing effective control programs requires understanding exposures, particularly across diverse exposure groups. Our observed microcystin concentrations in oysters (maximum ≈ 6.5 ng microcystin g−1; Fig. 6) across coastal farm sites approached international guidelines for establishing regulatory controls for this toxin. The World Health Organization tolerable daily intake guideline for microcystins associated with fish consumption is 40 ng microcystin kg body mass−1 day−1 (Chorus and Welker, 2021). Using the exposure assumption typical for environmental toxins in seafood (e.g. 200 g for a typical meal; Wekell et al., 2004), a baseline regulatory control limit of 16.14 ng microcystin g−1 can be estimated assuming a mean North American adult body mass of 80.7 kg (Walpole et al., 2012). Although this regulatory control remains above our maximum observed concentrations, this limit does not include an additional safety factor to account for patient-to-patient variability in health outcomes associated with microcystin intoxication, which are typically applied to regulatory controls established for other toxins (Wekell et al., 2004). Additional safety factors may also consider patient-to-patient variability in exposure to different and potentially more toxic microcystin congeners as well as variability in per capita oyster consumption. Considering the former will lead to lower and more restrictive regulatory control limits, particularly with uncertainties in the environmental microcystin congeners present and their comparative toxicity. However, uncertainties in understanding broad patterns of oyster consumption will complicate regulatory controls (Froelich and Noble, 2016) and hold the potential to reduce restrictions if oyster consumption and toxin exposure is lower than expectations. Oyster industry shifts in recent decades away from the historical extensive harvesting of naturally occurring oyster reefs toward intensive planting of hatchery-produced oyster stocks in managed bags and cages has led to shifting consumer preferences toward raw, half-shell oysters. This contrasts with historical harvest and distribution of oysters as commodity items that are shucked and served in bulk (Botta et al., 2020; Iitembu et al., 2023). Also, because raw, half-shell oysters are more associated with pleasure and luxury than seafood commodity items (Guillou et al., 2019), per capita and per diem oyster consumption may be much less than the 200 g typically assumed for seafood products (Shamshak et al., 2019). Although oyster consumption unquestionably varies across diverse user groups (Botta et al., 2020), as do perceptions of toxin risks associated with consumption (Roberts et al., 2016), understanding broad oyster consumption patterns is a crucial step toward understanding phycotoxin exposure risks and establishing regulatory control criteria.

The domoic acid concentrations we quantified in North Carolina oysters were far below the established regulatory control of 20,000 ng domoic acid g−1 (maximum in Bogue Sound in 2023 ≈ 150 ng domoic acid g−1; Fig. 7B), which was estimated from domoic acid concentrations quantified from uneaten mussels recovered from an ASP outbreak in eastern Canada in 1987 (Perl et al., 1990). That outbreak, where three patients died and an additional 107 met the case definition of gastrointestinal and/or neurologic symptoms within 24–48 of ingesting mussels harvested from the Cardigan Bay region of Prince Edward Island (Perl et al., 1990), presents a successful case study for both effective monitoring and exposure control. Toxin monitoring programs initiated by the Canadian Food Inspection Agency, where domoic acid is now quantified in representative samples of harvested mussels before commercial distribution, coupled with increased awareness and implementation of control programs has led to no documented ASP episodes since the initial 1987 incident. Shellfish monitoring for domoic acid has since been widely implemented from Southern California to Alaska, as recurrent blooms on the West Coast are linked to mass mortality events in marine mammals (Scholin et al., 2000; Smith et al., 2018).

Future considerations

Our results demonstrate the importance of developing regular phycotoxin monitoring across coastal environments used for oyster aquaculture. Using bivalves as sentinels for pollution and human health risks in coastal waters has proven a highly successful strategy in numerous systems (Boening, 1999; El-Sikaily et al., 2004; Zuykov et al., 2013; Steinhardt et al., 2016; Yap et al., 2021) and based on our study, promises to be a powerful tool to address exposure concerns from phycotoxins, both freshwater and marine in origin, in large and complex estuarine systems like those in North Carolina. We implemented a subset of various harmful algal bloom monitoring methods which underscored the importance of diverse sampling and sample types when addressing phycotoxins across estuaries (Howard et al., 2023). These approaches, particularly when conducted in collaboration with aquaculturists and aquaculture industry user groups, are crucial for informing risk assessment and management in coastal aquaculture. The SPATT approach and the analyses of oysters delivered accumulated toxin signals and insights into the spatial and temporal extent of algal bloom impacts at three coastal aquaculture sites. As the first study focusing on shellfish contamination within North Carolina, our findings are meant to guide follow up work to optimize toxin monitoring strategies. For instance, the prevalence of microcystin in oysters shown in this study based on the ELISA method, which delivers a combined signal for multiple microcystin derivates, warrants further follow up using analytical approaches such as liquid chromatography mass spectrometry to distinguish microcystin composition and identify what congeners dominate to account for the fact that different analogs have been linked to varying toxicity (Chernoff et al., 2021, 2020). The relevance of this had been shown more recently for the upper Albemarle Sound region where blooms reached unprecedented toxic concentrations of several orders of magnitude above recreational guidelines, and the microcystin mixture consisted of derivates of varying toxicity (Pierce et al., 2024). More specifically, in this latter study, applying the current guidelines for human exposure based primarily on the more toxic microcystin-LR congener, could have led to exposure risks being overestimated. The difference in congeners can also affect which detection approaches may be most effective and how long varying congeners might reside in the water, which impacts the time window for shellfish uptake and potentially depuration times (Chaffin et al., 2023; Pierce and Schnetzer, 2023). We also recommend broadening the range of cyanotoxins tested in coastal waters based on main genera we encountered. Finally, the prevalence of domoic acid and the documentation of a high-density Pseudo-nitzschia bloom within Core Sound raises questions to whether ASP outbreaks could become an issue in the future (Anderson et al., 2010). This finding justifies further investigations using genetic approaches to verify whether and what toxigenic species contribute to Pseudo-nitzschia assemblages in North Carolina estuaries and how species composition may be tied to shifts in environmental drivers that favor domoic acid production.

Conclusion

Our goal here was to test the generality of domoic acid and microcystin occurrence in coastal aquaculture in order to begin to understand phycotoxin exposure risks associated with aquaculture expansion into coastal sounds across the southeastern USA. We found domoic acid and microcystin to co-occur, but only in less than 10% of our observations and typically at concentrations below seafood safety action levels (Fawell et al., 1999; Heinze, 1999; Chorus and Welker, 2021). Domoic acid in particular was found at concentrations orders of magnitude below established regulatory control limits, however, we documented a high-density bloom of Pseudo-nitzschia which warrants future efforts to address potential emergent issues tied to domoic acid production. Maximum microcystin levels at times approached World Health Organization guidelines for establishing regulatory control limits but varied significantly year-to-year, with microcystins nearly absent across all sites in 2023. This latter observation suggests that environmental drivers of microcystin accumulation in oysters may operate at regional scales greater than individual oyster farms. Fully understanding the environmental drivers of toxin production within a landscape context, including how and when these toxins accumulate in shellfish, therefore represents a critical research gap. Given the regional synchrony observed here there holds considerable promise for developing adaptive sanitation and control programs when perceived toxin risks are high.
