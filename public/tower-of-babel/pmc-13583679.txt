Response to Sotorasib in a patient with recurrent KRAS G12C-mutated low-grade serous ovarian cancer: a case report and literature review
Authors: Erin Hickey Zacholski, Wen Jing Liu, Ryan Clayton, Jaclyn Wall, Chelsea Salyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives: Low-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (LGSOC) is a rare subtype of ovarian cancer characterized by slow disease progression and relative resistance to conventional chemotherapy. Disease recurrence is common, and guideline-directed treatment options may become exhausted, underscoring the need for novel therapeutic approaches. This case report describes the clinical course and treatment response of a patient with recurrent, KRAS G12C-mutated LGSOC after 5 lines of previous therapy who was treated with the KRAS G12C small-molecule inhibitor sotorasib.Methods: In this case study, a patient with recurrent Stage IC3 LGSOC, whose tumor tested positive for a KRAS G12C mutation, received sotorasib 960 mg as an off-label therapy after disease progression on available guideline-directed therapies.Results: Treatment with sotorasib resulted in an ongoing, partial response on CT scans at 2 and 7 months after therapy initiation. CA-125 normalized rapidly and remains within normal limits to date. The patient has experienced no adverse events and has been able to maintain adherence to therapy.Conclusions: This case study documents a clinically successful treatment using sotorasib in LGSOC. This represents a novel targeted therapy approach for patients with KRAS G12-mutated recurrent LGSOC, suggesting benefit in this population that has not been documented to date.Keywords: Advanced ovarian cancer, Low-grade serous ovarian cancer, KRAS G12C mutation

Introduction

Low-grade serous ovarian cancer (LGSOC) is a rare subtype of epithelial ovarian cancer with distinct clinical and pathophysiologic features, representing 2 to 5% of ovarian carcinomas (Matsuo et al., 2018). Though LGSOC follows a more indolent growth process than other epithelial ovarian cancers, it is notably chemoresistant, and the median overall survival rate is poor at 97 months (Zwimpfer et al., 2023).

Evidence suggests that the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway is involved in the pathophysiology of LGSOC. Over half of LGSOC cases have mutations in the MAPK pathway, including the KRAS (19–55%), NRAS (8%–25%), and BRAF (5%–12%) genes. In an analysis of 119 patients with LGSOC, KRAS hotspot mutations were among the most common mutations detected (33%, G12V, n = 19; G12D, n = 14; G12C, n = 3; G12R, n = 2; and G12A, n = 1) (Manning-Geist et al., 2022). KRAS hotspot mutations impair GAP-mediated GTP hydrolysis, locking KRAS in its active GTP-bound state and driving persistent downstream signaling that promotes uncontrolled proliferation and cancer cell survival (Manning-Geist et al., 2022; Parikh et al., 2022). Elucidating this pathologic driver has generated evidence for use of MEK inhibitors, including trametinib, and most recently, a BRAF-MEK clamp and FAK inhibitor combination for treatment of LGSOC, avutometinib and dafactinib. In KRAS-mutated recurrent LGSOC, these therapies yield objective response rates (ORR) of 44%–50% (Banerjee et al., 2025; Gershenson et al., 2022). Despite recent progress in the targeted treatment of recurrent LGSOC, patients are expected to have disease progression, and therapy options after targeted and hormonal therapies are limited.

Sotorasib is a selective and irreversible KRASG12C inhibitor currently used for the treatment of KRAS G12C-mutated non-small cell lung cancer (Skoulidis et al., 2021). In this report, we present the case of a patient with recurrent KRAS G12C-mutated LGSOC and favorable response to sotorasib after failure of five previous therapies. Written patient consent was obtained to share this case.

Case presentation

Diagnosis and treatment history

The patient is a 44-year-old female with recurrent Stage IC3 LGSOC. Her past medical history includes obesity (BMI = 51 kg/m2), sleep apnea, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), and type 2 diabetes. The patient had a long history of abdominal discomfort with bilateral adnexal masses on imaging first noted in 2010; per patient preference, surgery was delayed until six years later when masses grew significantly and CA-125 was 1718 units/mL. At that time, the patient underwent a total abdominal hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (TAH-BSO) with pathology interpreted as high grade serous ovarian cancer in the background of mixed serous and mucinous borderline tumor. Adjuvant chemotherapy with six cycles of carboplatin and paclitaxel was recommended. The patient elected to omit paclitaxel and received three cycles of single-agent carboplatin AUC 6 IV every 3 weeks. Scans to assess treatment response are not available, but CA-125 normalized after chemotherapy. At first disease recurrence five years later, she received six cycles of carboplatin AUC 6 IV every 3 weeks, with disease progression on CT scan after cycle six. A biopsy was taken from a vaginal mass that confirmed pathology of LGSOC and the tumor was sent for next generation sequencing (NGS) with estrogen receptor positivity (IHC; 2+, 80%), PD-L1 positivity (CPS = 1), and a pathogenic KRAS G12C mutation (alteration: c34G > T, variant frequency 44%). For treatment of this second recurrence, the patient received trametinib 2 mg by mouth daily for two years with partial response. Upon progression, she received letrozole 2.5 mg daily for three months and CT scans showed progressive disease. At this point, the patient was screened for the RAMP 301 Phase 3 clinical trial evaluating avutometinib and dafactinib and was deemed ineligible. Finally, she was treated with fulvestrant intramuscularly for three months with progression on CT scans. A simplified treatment history is shown in Fig. 1.

The team pursued combination CDK4/6 inhibitor and aromatase inhibitor therapy as the next line of therapy, but this was denied by the patient's public insurance despite multiple peer-to-peer submissions providing supporting clinical data. At this point, guideline-recommended and investigational therapy options for treatment of the patient's recurrent, progressive LGSOC were exhausted.

Sotorasib therapy and response

Upon multidisciplinary team review, considering presence of a pathogenic somatic KRAS G12C mutation without accessible alternative treatment options, the patient was started on sotorasib at the prescribing information-recommended starting flat dose of 960 mg (three 320 mg tablets) by mouth daily. Manufacturer patient assistance was obtained for the drug after insurance authorization denial.

The patient was counseled extensively by her oncologist and clinical pharmacist on the intended effect, adverse reactions, and monitoring with sotorasib. The team discussed the risks and benefits of this therapy, noting that use is off-label for LGSOC and controlled data supporting use in this setting have not been established. The following adverse effects were discussed: diarrhea, nausea, hepatotoxicity, musculoskeletal pain, edema, rash, and interstitial lung disease (ILD). A drug-drug interaction analysis was completed and interactions with concomitant omeprazole and herbal supplements were identified. Due to severe GERD symptoms affecting the patient at night, acid-suppressing medications could not be avoided per the sotorasib manufacturer recommendations (Amgen Inc, 2026). Omeprazole was discontinued and famotidine was started nightly, separated from sotorasib administration by 12 h. The patient was instructed to discontinue turmeric due to potential inhibition of CYP3A4-mediated metabolism of sotorasib hepatotoxicity risk.

At oncology clinic visits one week post-medication initiation and then monthly for months 1–4, the patient did not report any adverse effects associated with sotorasib. The patient reported excellent adherence to sotorasib 960 mg daily, confirmed by pharmacy dispense records. She noted complete resolution of gastrointestinal symptoms related to carcinomatosis, particularly within the ventral hernia. Her liver function tests were monitored per the sotorasib package insert and were within normal limits. CA-125 decreased from 79 units/mL at progression to 24 units/mL after two months on sotorasib and 9 units/mL after four months on therapy.

CT scans to monitor disease response were completed at two and seven months post-sotorasib initiation. Her scans at 2 months post-treatment showed significant reduction in size of multiple tumors in the vaginal cuff, pelvis and abdomen while other lesions remained stable in size. At seven months, CT scans demonstrated ongoing response with further decrease in size of target lesions, including complete resolution of the omental mass (Table 1). Overall, her treatment with sotorasib resulted in a partial response per RECIST 1.1, with a 57% reduction in tumor burden after seven months of therapy (Table 1, Fig. 2) (CancerCalc. Discussion

Small molecule inhibitors of proteins in the MAPK pathway, have been shown to improve survival in LGSOC (Banerjee et al., 2025; Gershenson et al., 2022). Despite this progress, patients will often develop progressive disease on these therapies. There are a limited number of treatment options at that point and NGS can support a personalized treatment approach upon confirmation of targetable pathogenic mutations. This patient with recurrent KRAS G12C-mutated LGSOC experienced rapid, significant, sustained tumor regression on CT scans and normalization of CA125 after treatment with sotorasib 960 mg daily. To the authors knowledge, this is the first documented case of successful targeted monotherapy with sotorasib for a patient with LGSOC. This provides an opportunity to discuss current evidence for KRAS inhibitors, pharmacodynamics of sotorasib, and health disparities to treatments in this population.

The Phase 1/2 CodeBreaK-100 basket trial was open to patients with advanced solid tumors other than non-small cell lung cancer harboring a p.KRAS12C mutation; however, no patients with ovarian cancer were enrolled (Skoulidis et al., 2021; Hong et al., 2020). In a subgroup of the Phase 1b CodeBreaK-101 study evaluating sotorasib and RMC-4630, a SHP2 inhibitor, one patient with ovarian cancer achieved a confirmed partial response with an 81% reduction in tumor burden (Falchook et al., 2022). There are currently multiple ongoing clinical trials evaluating KRAS G12C inhibitors among patients with advanced solid tumors, including ovarian cancer, that can provide further information regarding efficacy in non-NSCLC populations (National Cancer Institute. Clinical Trials Using Sotorasib. U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2026).

It is important to note that KRAS G12C inhibitors like sotorasib are not active against all KRAS-mutated cancers, and this mutation is rare within LGSOC. Sotorasib covalently binds to the mutated cysteine residue in the switch-II pocket when KRAS G12C is in the GDP inactive state, preventing protein activation and resultant downstream signaling and cell proliferation (Skoulidis et al., 2021). Therefore, KRAS G12C inhibitors should not be used for other KRAS hotspot mutations resulting in amino acid substitutions other than cysteine. In a query of the Caris Life Sciences© database, there were only 25 patients with KRAS G12C mutation among 1717 patients with LGSOC (1.5%). While sotorasib was highly effective in the patient presented, it may only benefit a small subset of patients with low-grade histology.

Though this patient was able to tolerate therapy at full-dose, she was monitored routinely for common and/or serious adverse effects to anticipate necessary holding for any grade ILD or other Grade 3 or 4 toxicities. Per the sotorasib prescribing information, up to two dose reductions are permitted following resolution of Grade 3 or 4 adverse effects, to 480 mg once daily and then 240 mg once daily. However, sotorasib should be permanently discontinued in patients with confirmed ILD.

Notably, this patient continued acid-suppressing therapy with famotidine during treatment with sotorasib. The maximum concentration (Cmax) and area-under-the-curve (AUC) of sotorasib are significantly reduced with use of concomitant proton-pump inhibitors (PPIs) and histamine-2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs). Therefore, the manufacturer recommends avoiding use with PPIs, H2RAs, and to separate local antacid medications by 4 h before or 10 h after sotorasib administration if an antacid cannot be avoided (Amgen Inc, 2026). Interestingly, the effect of acid-reducing agents on concentration of sotorasib can be mitigated by intake of acidic beverages, which was studied prospectively using Coco-Cola (Cardona et al., 2025). This patient routinely drinks lemonade, which has a similar pH as Coca-Cola and could account for positive response despite use of acid-reducing therapy (Reddy et al., 2016).

Resistance within the MAPK pathway is well-studied and can result from the development of secondary KRAS mutations, upstream MAPK reactivation, and bypass via non-MAPK pathways (Chour et al., 2024). These principles serve as the basis for the combination therapy in the CodeBreaK-101 trial, and the mechanistic rationale for avutometinib and dafactinib (Gershenson et al., 2022). Therefore, resistance to single-agent sotorasib in LGSOC is a likely outcome and represents a potential disadvantage of this therapy.

Finally, this case highlights challenges related to disparities in LGSOC treatment. Access to sotorasib was only achieved through a manufacturer patient assistance program after coverage was not approved by public insurance. Sotorasib was pursued after therapies for LGSOC with evidence of efficacy in Phase II trials were similarly not covered. The limited sample sizes inherent to clinical trials of rare malignancies such as LGSOC, particularly those defined by specific molecular alterations, pose additional barriers to access to evidence-based therapies.

Conclusions

We present a case of KRAS G12C-mutated recurrent LGSOC with extensive abdominal metastases after five prior lines of therapy. Sotorasib 960 mg daily resulted in an ongoing partial response at 7 months with excellent tolerability. This represents a novel targeted therapy approach for patients with KRAS G12-mutated recurrent LGSOC, suggesting benefit in this population that has not been documented to date. Targeting KRAS G12C in ovarian cancer warrants further research in controlled trials to determine impact on long-term outcomes.
