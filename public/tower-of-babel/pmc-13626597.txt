The effect of ertugliflozin on serum insulin and glucose in healthy horses receiving dexamethasone
Authors: Florence Dupuis-Dowd, Philip H Kass, Emily H Berryhill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i) can be used to manage insulin dysregulation (ID). Their effect on corticosteroid-induced ID is unknown.Hypothesis/ObjectivesEvaluate effects of ertugliflozin on serum insulin and glucose concentrations in horses receiving dexamethasone (DEX). We hypothesized that DEX would induce ID and ertugliflozin coadministration would mitigate that effect.AnimalsSeven healthy horses.MethodsRandomized crossover design; horses received DEX (0.06 mg/kg IV q48h; DEX)or ertugliflozin (0.05 mg/kg PO q24h) plus DEX (SGLT-2i group) for seven days. Introduction

Corticosteroids are potent anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory drugs that may cause an increase in serum insulin concentration and induce insulin dysregulation (ID).1–3 This iatrogenic induction of hyperinsulinemia could explain the development of laminitis in certain horses treated with corticosteroids.4–7 Indeed, hyperinsulinemia induced in research settings leads to structural and metabolic changes of the lamellae, which worsen with time and can lead to laminitis within as few as 3 days.8–10 The risk of laminitis may be exacerbated in equids requiring corticosteroid treatment for concurrent conditions leading to ID, such as underlying equine metabolic syndrome or systemic inflammatory response syndrome.11

Sodium-glucose cotransporter type-2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i) lower blood glucose by increasing its renal excretion and have been used in humans to treat type 2 diabetes.12 They recently have gained popularity among equine veterinarians for the management of horses with ID and hyperinsulinemia. In clinical trials, SGLT-2i decreased circulating insulin concentrations after ingestion of sugar-rich meals in ponies suffering from insulin dysregulation, as well as in horses as part of management for equine metabolic syndrome.13–15 Treatment with SGLT-2i has been suggested to decrease the occurrence of laminitis and improve clinical outcomes in hyperinsulinemia-associated laminitis.13,16,17 Use of velagliflozin, canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, and ertugliflozin has been reported in horses, but in humans, ertugliflozin may be more effective than dapagliflozin and empagliflozin.18,19

In horses that require corticosteroids to treat a primary disease but may be at risk of laminitis (eg, purpura hemorrhagica, immune-mediated myopathy), adjunctive treatments are needed to decrease the risk of hyperinsulinemia-associated laminitis. Concurrent use of SGLT-2i may mitigate corticosteroid-induced ID. Our primary objectives were therefore to: (1) determine the effect of ertugliflozin on daily insulin and glucose concentrations measured over the course of the day and across various prandial states when administered with dexamethasone (DEX), and (2) evaluate the effect of co-administration of ertugliflozin with DEX on dynamic tests used to diagnose ID. A secondary outcome was to confirm that the selected IV dose of DEX administered every other day would induce ID in a cohort of healthy horses, with changes in insulin sensitivity reflected by dynamic test results. We hypothesized that coadministration of ertugliflozin would lower serum insulin and glucose concentrations when compared with administration of DEX alone. Our secondary hypothesis was that DEX alone would increase serum insulin and glucose concentrations and induce ID based on dynamic testing, and that ertugliflozin would mitigate that ID.

Materials and methods

Animals

Seven healthy horses a mean (± SD) age of 12.9 ± 5.6 years and free of endocrinopathies, were enrolled, including 3 mares and 4 geldings (Appendix).

Study design

The study followed a randomized cross-over design, taking place over the course of 11 weeks (June to August). The first study period (Block 1) took place over 15 days: 3 days of acclimation, 2 days of dynamic testing pre-treatment, 7 days of treatment (Days 7 through 13), and 2 days of dynamic testing post-treatment (Figure 1). After Block 1, horses completed a 7-week washout before Block 2, which followed the same 15-day protocol. During Block 1, 3 horses received DEX sodium phosphate (DEX group, Dexamethasone-SP, VetOne, MWI Health, Boise, ID) 0.06 mg/kg IV q48h, and 4 horses received ertugliflozin (SGLT-2i group, Steglatro, 15 mg/tablet, Merck, Rahway, NJ) 0.05 mg/kg PO q24h in addition to the DEX. After washout, horses were assigned to the other treatment group. During both blocks, horses received water ad libitum and orchard grass hay twice daily (Appendix) that was given at 0600 hours near the time of drug administration and 1700 hours. Physical examinations were performed daily. Body weights and serum biochemistry panels were obtained at the beginning and end of each block.

The darkly shaded section details blood draws (syringe icons) and medication administration during each day of treatment. Experimental procedures

The primary outcome was to determine the effect of ertugliflozin on mean daily serum insulin and glucose concentrations. Daily blood samples were obtained immediately before drug administration at 0600 hours (fasted state), then 4 hours (1000 hours, post-prandial state) and 12 hours later (1800 hours, fed state) to measure serum insulin and glucose concentrations. To encompass different prandial states, circadian rhythm influence, and variation in drug concentration throughout the day, the mean of the 3-time-point collection was used for analysis.

Oral sugar (OST) and insulin tolerance (ITT) tests were performed before and after each treatment period (Figure 1; Appendix).20,21 The ITT followed published guidelines.21 The residual percentage of glucose between baseline and post-insulin samples was calculated, with a > 50% increase in residual glucose percentage indicative of insulin resistance.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata/BE 17.0, StataCorp (College Station, Texas), and GraphPad Prism version 10.5.0 (La Jolla, California). Data were analyzed using multiple linear regression mixed effects models that assessed the effect of each treatment on serum insulin and glucose concentrations, while controlling for blocks, sampling time, and their interactions. Results were considered significant with P-values < .05.

Results

Mean daily serum insulin and glucose concentrations

Mean daily insulin concentrations during treatment were higher in the DEX group compared with the SGLT-2i group (P < .001), without an interaction between day and treatment (P = .15). A block effect was observed (P < .001; Table 1; Figure 2). A significant interaction between day and treatment was identified for glucose concentrations (P < .001). A difference in the mean daily glucose concentration between groups was present on the days of DEX administration (ie, days 3 and 5, P < .001; Table 2; Figure 3). A similar difference between treatments was noted on Day 4 but did not reach significance (P = .05).

Clinical data pre- and post-treatment

All horses lost weight during the study. A mean weight loss of 21.5 kg (95% CI, 12.3-30.7; P = .001) was identified in the DEX group and 40.1 kg (95% CI, 25.9-54.3); P < .001) in the SGLT-2i group. Horses in the SGLT-2i group lost more weight (P = .02), with a day and treatment interaction (P < .001). There was no significant block effect (P = .52).

Horses in the SGLT-2i group had increased triglyceride (TGL) concentrations (P < .001), sorbitol dehydrogenase activity (SDH37, P < .001), and gamma glutamyl transferase activity (GGT, P = .001; Figure 7). A significant day and treatment interaction was not present for GGT but was present for TGL and SDH37 (P = .003 and P = .04, respectively). There was no significant block effect (TGL, P = .25; SDH37, P = .70; GGT, P = .77).

Adverse effects

No adverse clinical signs directly attributed to DEX or ertugliflozin administration were noted during the study, and all horses enrolled completed the study. One horse in the DEX group became subjectively more polyuric toward the end of the treatment period, but without changes in serum biochemistry variables or physical examination findings. Another horse injured itself in its stall and became transiently acutely lame on day 13 while in the DEX group, but no additional medication was administered.

The higher dose of 0.45 mL of Karo syrup/kg was well tolerated for the OST. The ITT induced hypoglycemia in 1 horse in the second block of the study during the pre-treatment ITT. The horse developed partial seizure-like activity 30 minutes after insulin administration. The horse was given Karo syrup PO and dextrose IV, after which the episode resolved and the horse returned to normal.

Discussion

Two important results of our study were: (1) ertugliflozin co-administered with DEX significantly decreased mean daily serum insulin concentrations throughout the treatment period by 30%-45% compared with DEX alone, and (2) ID was induced in healthy horses using the selected dosing regimen of DEX. These findings support 1 of the study’s hypotheses, because the study objectives were reliant on first inducing ID. Importantly, these results also lend support to the hypothesis that co-administration of SGLT-2i may decrease the risk of hyperinsulinemia in horses receiving corticosteroids.

Increased serum insulin and glucose concentrations previously have been reported in healthy horses treated with DEX, with published dosages ranging from 0.08 mg/kg PO q24h to 0.08 mg/kg IV q48h.2,3 The dosage of 0.06 mg/kg IV used in our study was extrapolated from the published 0.08 mg/kg PO dosage, taking into consideration that the reported PO bioavailability of DEX ranges from 33% to 88%, with a mean of 60%.3,22 The q48h frequency selected for our study was based on a previous study,2 with the goal of decreasing potential hyperinsulinemia-associated laminitis risk as much as possible in the study horses. Although the dosage of DEX used in our study was lower than what has been reported previously, induction of ID was confirmed by the increase in OST insulinT60 after treatment with DEX. The increase was such that 5 of the 7 healthy horses that initially were categorized as having a normal insulin response would have been considered insulin dysregulated after DEX administration. The ID also was reflected by the higher values in glucoseAUC documented during the post-treatment OST. DEX affects the ability to metabolize exogenous glucose by decreasing glucose uptake into tissues and insulin sensitivity, leading to higher insulin secretion.2,23,24 In a clinical context, the impact of DEX on insulin concentrations, even in normal horses, supports a recommendation for serial serum insulin monitoring while the horse is undergoing corticosteroid treatment to appropriately manage risks associated with hyperinsulinemia.

The co-administration of the SGLT-2i ertugliflozin effectively lowered daily mean serum insulin concentrations throughout the treatment period (Days 7 through 13). The most well-described mechanism of action of SGLT-2i drugs is inhibition of glucose reabsorption in the proximal renal tubules, decreasing blood glucose concentrations and the stimulus for pancreatic insulin secretion, but this mechanism has not been directly studied in horses.25 As supported by our study and others, there appears to be a disproportionate decrease in circulating insulin concentrations relative to glucose, and additional mechanisms of action of SGLT-2is are suspected. Sodium-glucose cotransporters 1 and 2 were confirmed to be present in equine renal tissue, but SGLT-2 also were found in both liver and pancreatic tissue, supporting the potential for extrarenal effects.25 In addition, a previous study26 described a decrease in pancreatic beta-cell sensitivity in equids with ID that received the SGLT-2i canagliflozin, suggesting that decreased beta-cell responsiveness to glucose is another important mechanism in lowering serum insulin concentrations. In our study, ertugliflozin administration also decreased mean serum glucose concentrations compared with the DEX group, but the effect was of lower magnitude and less consistent compared with the effect on insulin. Interestingly, the difference in mean serum glucose concentrations between the DEX and SGLT-2i groups was significant on days when DEX was administered, highlighting the exaggerated serum glucose response to DEX and the ability of the SGLT-2i to dampen that effect. The milder effect of SGLT-2i drugs on blood glucose concentration is suggested to be caused by a compensatory increase in renal SGLT-1 receptor activity that decreases renal excretion of glucose, as well as an increase in endogenous glucose production (EGP).27,28 From a clinical standpoint, the relatively modest effect on glucose but robust effect on insulin concentrations is beneficial, contributing to increased safety with decreased risk of hypoglycemia while meeting the therapeutic goal of decreasing hyperinsulinemia.26However, our study was performed in animals without underlying endocrinopathy, and the effect of SGLT-2i on animals with underlying ID that require corticosteroids remains unknown.

Ertugliflozin had a beneficial effect on daily mean serum insulin and glucose concentrations during treatment, but in our study SGLT-2i administration did not alter the underlying ID induced by DEX as shown by dynamic testing. No difference in OST insulinT60 and glucoseAUC was found after treatment, whether horses received ertugliflozin in addition to DEX or not. The lack of difference between groups may reflect a failure of ertugliflozin to mitigate corticosteroid-induced ID, but the delay between the last treatment dose (Day 13) and initiation of dynamic testing (Day 14 for OST) also may have precluded detection of an effect. In contrast to DEX, which can affect the hypothalamus and downstream axis for 2-3 days after a single dose, the effect of ertugliflozin on renal glucose excretion decreases within 24 hours in humans, but has not been studied in horses.28,29 Additional studies are warranted to specifically evaluate the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of SGLT-2i drugs in horses at steady state after discontinuation.

Surprisingly, the results of the ITT remained similar before and after administration of DEX. Peripheral insulin resistance, indicated by an increase in the residual glucose percentage (ie, smaller difference in absolute glucose concentration) after insulin administration, was expected but was not observed in the DEX group. Although the effects of DEX can last up to 72 hours after administration, it is possible that the effect on EGP and insulin secretion had faded between the last dose of DEX administered on Day 13 and performance of the ITT on Day 15, 48 hours later.29 It is possible that the lower dosage of DEX used in our study also might explain a more rapid return to baseline insulin sensitivity than what has been reported previously. Whether results would be similar in horses with underlying endocrinopathies is unknown and requires additional study.

Unexpectedly, the post-treatment ITT showed an increase in residual blood glucose percentage (ie, decreased difference in absolute glucose concentration between pre and post samples) in the SGLT-2i group, suggesting development of peripheral insulin resistance. A possible explanation for decreased glucose suppression can be extrapolated from drug pharmacodynamics established in humans. The use of SGLT-2is in humans with diabetes has been shown to increase plasma glucagon concentrations and, secondarily, EGP by induction of hepatic gluconeogenesis.30 A recent study demonstrated that SGLT-2 receptors are present in the equine endocrine pancreas and specifically found in the central islets where α-cells typically are located, and the authors hypothesized that this finding may indicate an association between SGLT-2 and glucagon secretion in horses.25 Additionally, increased EGP in humans receiving SGLT-2i also may occur secondary to neuroendocrine signaling arising from renal nerves and stimulating hepatic gluconeogenesis in a non-glucagon-dependent mechanism.31

Another contributing factor for our study’s ITT results could have been the testing protocol itself. The dose of regular insulin administered as part of the ITT was relatively small and with transient activity, limiting the administered insulin’s ability to suppress glucose production, particularly if EGP is already increased from chronic SGLT-2i use. Additionally, the timing of the post-ITT (72 hours after SGLT-2i discontinuation) could have an unknown impact on glucose concentrations, with potentially decreased renal excretion of glucose after SGLT-2i discontinuation, but some continuation of increased hepatic gluconeogenesis or residual EGP. In summary, transient effect of insulin administration, decreased glucosuria, and increased EGP from the mechanisms described above all could have contributed to a more narrowed difference in absolute glucose concentration between pre- and post-test samples in horses receiving SGLT-2i. Research is needed to more completely describe the pharmacodynamics of chronic SGLT-2i use in horses to support these hypotheses, including assessing the repeatability of our results in horses treated only with SGLT-2i but without DEX.

Horses in both groups lost weight during the study. Because short-term corticosteroid treatment should not have an influence on weight or could stimulate weight gain, some of the weight loss may have been caused by a change in management of the horses.1,32 The horses enrolled had lived in paddocks with free access to hay. In contrast, during the study, they were fed hay twice daily and were kept in a different environment, which could have caused stress and increased their daily energy expenditure. However, weight loss was more pronounced in horses that received the SGLT-2i, consistent with previous studies.17,33 In humans, SGLT-2i modulate adipose tissue metabolism, upregulate synthesis of adiponectin, and shift energy source to free fatty acid oxidation.26 These changes enhance weight loss mainly by decreasing fat mass.34 Similar processes leading to weight loss are suspected in horses. The horses did regain their weight during the washout period, and no significant difference was found in the weight of the animals at the beginning of both study periods.

The increase in TGL concentration after SGLT-2i administration is a known occurrence.15,17,33,35 Enhancement of free fatty acid oxidation as a substrate for energy is triggered by SGLT-2i in humans.26 Because an increase in blood ketone concentrations has been described in humans receiving SGLT-2i35, similar pathways can be suspected in horses, which could lead to an increase in TGL. Similar to other reports, the modest increase in TGL was subclinical, and all horses maintained a good appetite.35 Additionally, increases in hepatocellular and hepatobiliary enzyme activities were documented after SGLT-2i administration previously35 and were also detected in our study. This mild increase in SDH37 and GGT activity is both incompletely understood and of unknown clinical relevance.

The observed block effect, in which insulin concentrations were higher in block 1 than in block 2, could be attributed to an unanticipated change in hay batch during the study. A consistent hay supply could not be maintained because of wider herd management, and 3 batches of orchard grass were fed (2 during the first block and 1 during the second block). Hay analysis (Appendix) showed similar non-structural carbohydrate (NSC) percentages for the hays in block 1 (22.4% and 26.7% as sampled), but the hay in block 2 had an as sampled percentage of 10.5%. This difference in dietary NSC between the 2 blocks is a reasonable explanation for the block effect and shows that dietary NSCs may influence serum insulin concentrations in non-endocrinopathic horses receiving corticosteroids.

Our study had some limitations. Small sample size may have decreased the robustness of the conclusions. In addition, the use of ertugliflozin was tested in horses that were healthy, free of endocrinopathies, and considered low risk for development of hyperinsulinemia and associated complications. Future studies to evaluate the potential of SGLT-2i along with corticosteroids in horses with naturally occurring metabolic disease, or in horses that are systemically ill and are at higher risk of hyperinsulinemia, are warranted. Additionally, other markers of lipoprotein metabolism, such as evaluation of high-density lipoprotein and non-esterified fatty acids, glucagon, and cortisol, could provide more information on the effects of a combination of SGLT-2i and DEX on the endocrine system. Study design would have been strengthened by including more sampling time points when performing the OST, allowing more detailed characterization of the insulin response. Moreover, ertugliflozin and DEX administration should have been continued until completion of the post-treatment OST and ITT to clearly define the effects of the drugs on dynamic testing. Discontinuation of the SGLT-2i specifically limits interpretation of post-treatment dynamic testing results because of its relatively rapid decrease in activity after withdrawal.

Conclusions

Co-administration of ertugliflozin lowers daily mean serum insulin concentrations in horses with corticosteroid-induced ID. This finding supports a potential role for administration of SGLT-2i along with corticosteroids in horses at risk of hyperinsulinemia and adds to the existing data that insulin concentrations should be monitored in horses receiving corticosteroids. No difference was detected between horses treated with DEX alone or with ertugliflozin during dynamic testing, but this finding may reflect a limitation of the study design. Additional studies evaluating the effect of SGLT-2i on insulin resistance exacerbated by corticosteroids in horses with naturally occurring metabolic disease are warranted.

Appendix

Materials and methods

Animals

This study was approved by the University of California, Davis IACUC (protocol #23690). Seven animals selected from the research herd of the UC Davis Center for Equine Health were included in the study. Breeds included 3 warmbloods, 2 thoroughbreds, 1 standardbred, and 1 mixed. Horses were a mean 12.9 ± 5.6 years of age with a mean body weight of 595 ± 102 kg. Body condition score (BCS) was 6 (IQR = 1) out of 9-point scale, and cresty neck score (CNS) was 2.0 (IQR = 2) out of 5-point scale. All horses were deemed healthy based on physical examination and normal CBC and biochemistry results performed before each treatment block. None of the mares was pregnant, lactating, or ovariectomized, and none of the horses had a history of laminitis. Baseline adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) concentrations (available in supplemental data) were all within normal reference range for the season.

Study design

The trial followed a randomized cross-over study design, taking place over the course of 11 weeks. The study took place between June 1st and August 14th to limit seasonal influence on the results. Selected horses were randomly allocated into 2 groups by flipping a virtual coin. The first study period (Block 1) took place over 15 days: 3 days of acclimation to decrease catecholamine effects on blood insulin and glucose concentrations, 2 days of dynamic testing pre-treatment, 7 days of treatment (Days 7 through 13), and 2 days of dynamic testing post-treatment. After completion of Block 1, horses returned to paddocks for a 7-week washout, followed by the second study period (Block 2) of 15 days using the same format described for Block 1. During both study periods, orchard grass hay twice daily. Samples of hay were saved and submitted for nutritional analysis (EquiAnalytical Laboratory Services, Ithaca, NY). The management at the research facility was unable to use a single batch of hay for the blocks. Block 1 had 2 types of grass hay, 1 with an as sampled NSC percentage of 22.4%, and the other of 26.7%. Block 2 had a single type of hay fed, with an as sampled NSC percentage of 10.5%.

During treatment period (Days 7 to 13) horses received DEX alone or in combination with ertugliflozin. Blood was obtained immediately before drug administration and then 4 hours and 12 hours later to measure serum insulin and glucose concentrations (Figure 1). The 0600 hours blood draw represented a fasted state, the 1000 hours represent an approximately 3 hours post-prandial state, and the 1800 hours represented a fed state, giving a complete representation of the different states of a horse throughout the day to recreate clinical use. Physical examinations were performed each morning, including assessment of lameness, digital pulse, appetite, and urine and fecal production. Horses were weighed on Days 1 and 13 of each block. Complete serum biochemistries were repeated on Day 14. On Day 15, horses were returned to their paddocks for the washout period.

Experimental procedures

Oral sugar and ITT tests were performed before and after each treatment period during 2 consecutive days (pretesting on Days 4 and 5, and post-treatment testing on Days 14 and 15). The initial OST was performed to ensure that the horses did not have underlying ID as part of enrollment criteria. The OST was performed by administering 0.45 mL/kg of corn syrup (Karo Syrup, ACH Food Companies), PO, because of the increased sensitivity of the test reported with this higher dose.20 Horses were fasted for 3-6 hours before syrup administration.21 Blood was obtained at baseline and then at times 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 minutes. Blood draws were performed by direct sticks in the jugular or cephalic vein. Glucose concentration was measured at every time point to establish the area under the curve. Insulin concentration was determined at times 0 and 60 minutes after sugar administration.

The ITT followed published guidelines and consisted of the administration of 0.1 IU/kg regular insulin (Novolin R, Novo Nordisk Inc., Plainsboro Township, NJ, 100 U/mL) administered IV.21 Blood was obtained before insulin administration and 30 minutes later. Horses were not fasted. Immediately after the 30-minute blood draw, horses were given 0.5 flakes of grass hay and 1 lb of a complete senior feed to mitigate risks of iatrogenic hypoglycemia, with further intervention in the case of measured hypoglycemia. The residual percentage of glucose between baseline and post-insulin samples was calculated, with a > 50% increase in residual glucose percentage indicative of insulin resistance.

During the OST, glucose concentrations obtained at times 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 to calculate the area under the curve (glucoseAUC) and were measured with a stall-side glucometer (AlphaTrak3, Zoetis) that was calibrated before each testing day. The stall-side glucometer was used as it is a validated, cost-effective measurement that allowed for comparison of trend pre- and post-treatment. Blood samples were allowed to clot for 45-60 minutes at room temperature before centrifugation to separate serum (1530 × g for 8 minutes at 20 °C). Samples were stored at -80 °C until they were analyzed in batches at Cornell University Animal Health Diagnostic Center (AHDC; Ithaca, New York). Serum glucose concentrations were measured using a Roche Cobas 6000, and insulin concentration was assessed by radioimmunoassay, internally validated by the laboratory. Baseline ACTH concentrations were analyzed at the Cornell AHDC using an internally validated Immulite 2000xpi assay. Complete blood counts and serum biochemistry panels were analyzed at the UC Davis Veterinary Clinical Diagnostic Laboratory using an Advia 2120 and Roche Cobas 6000 C501, respectively.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was to determine the effect of ertugliflozin on mean daily serum insulin and glucose concentrations. Secondary outcomes related to ID included: (1) confirming induction of ID with IV DEX and (2) determining the effect of ertugliflozin on development of ID by dynamic testing, including measurement of insulinT60 and glucoseAUC during the OST and calculation of the mean residual percentage of glucoseT30 during the ITT. An additional secondary outcome was to determine the presence and significance of other possible effects of drug administration, including changes in body weight, observed physical examination variables, or biochemistry results that occurred with treatment.

Data analysis

The sample size (n = 7) was determined as the smallest number of horses that would allow statistical comparison of serum insulin concentrations in horses treated with DEX compared to a control group using a paired Student’s t-test with an alpha of 0.05 and power of 80%. The sample size calculation was performed using an online calculator (https://clincalc.com/stats/samplesize.aspx), and data from a previous study in which DEX administration resulted in a mean serum insulin concentration of 30 ± 8 mU/L compared with 15 ± 2 mU/L in controls after 7 days.2

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata/BE 17.0, StataCorp (College Station, Texas), and GraphPad Prism version 10.5.0 (La Jolla, California). Weight and age were assessed for normality using a Shapiro–Wilk test. Data were analyzed using multiple linear regression mixed effects models that assessed the effect of each treatment on blood insulin and glucose concentrations, while controlling for blocks and sampling time, and their interactions. Block, day of sampling, time of sampling, and treatment were treated as fixed variables, and the individual horse as a random variable. Model fit was evaluated by creating standardized residuals and evaluating their distribution using standardized normal probability plots. When appropriate, post-hoc analyses were performed using a Bonferroni multiple comparison adjustment. Results were considered significant with P-values < .05. All statistics for glucose and insulin concentrations were performed using results measured at the Cornell Animal Health Diagnostic Center, except for the glucoseAUC data for which the point-of-care results were utilized.
