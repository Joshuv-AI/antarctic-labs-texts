Mechanistic investigation of artesunate against head and neck squamous cell carcinoma through network pharmacology and experimental validation
Authors: Lei Wei, Dan He, LiFeng Jia, HaiLan Mo, HaiZhu Ma, XiaoLu Wu, Wei Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtesunate (ART), a semisynthetic derivative of artemisinin, has demonstrated antitumor activity in multiple malignancies; however, its cytotoxic activity and underlying mechanisms in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) remain incompletely understood.MethodsNetwork pharmacology and molecular docking were employed to predict potential molecular targets of ART in HNSCC. The effects of ART on cell viability, migration, invasion, and apoptosis were evaluated in SCC-25 and CAL27 cells in vitro. Expression changes in the Bcl-2/Bax/Caspase-3 axis were assessed using Western blotting and RT-qPCR. A xenograft model was further established to examine the in vivo antitumor activity of ART.ResultsNetwork pharmacology analysis identified 77 potential ART-associated targets related to HNSCC. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses suggested that these targets were involved in several cancer-related pathways. PPI analysis highlighted six core targets, among which CASP3 exhibited the most favorable predicted interaction with ART in docking analysis. Experimental results showed that ART treatment was associated with reduced cell viability, migration, and invasion, along with increased apoptotic activity in HNSCC cells. These effects were accompanied by an increased Bax/Bcl-2 ratio and elevated cleaved Caspase-3 levels. In the xenograft model, ART treatment suppressed tumor growth and increased tumor cell apoptosis.ConclusionART demonstrates preclinical antitumor activity in HNSCC and is associated with activation of Bcl-2/Bax/Caspase-3 axis. These findings provide preliminary mechanistic insight and support further investigation of ART in preclinical models of HNSCC.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is the seventh most frequent cancer worldwide, with approximately 890,000 new cases reported annually [1, 2]. Currently, the incidence of HNSCC is on the rise, and projections indicate a 30% increase by 2030, leading to an estimated 1.08 million new cases each year [3, 4]. Treatment for HNSCC typically involves cytotoxic platinum-based radiotherapy or initial surgical intervention combined with risk-adapted adjuvant therapy [5]. Although there have been significant advancements in treatments, the 5-year survival rate for HNSCC patients has not seen improvement over the last few decades [6]. Therefore, further elucidation of the molecular mechanisms underlying HNSCC progression remains essential for guiding future therapeutic research.

Artesunate (ART), a semi-synthetic derivative of artemisinin, widely used as a first-line antimalarial drug [7, 8], has garnered considerable attention for its broad antitumor activity [9, 10]. Numerous studies have shown that ART modulates multiple cancer-related signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways, NF-κB signaling, and apoptotic cascades involving Bcl-2 family proteins and caspases [11–14]. These findings suggest that ART exerts pleiotropic regulatory effects in diverse malignancies. However, its specific molecular mechanisms and downstream targets in HNSCC remain insufficiently characterized. Although research by Okamoto et al. [15] demonstrated that ART enhances cisplatin sensitivity in HNSCC via cell-cycle regulation, their study did not explore apoptosis-related pathways or identify the key molecular targets through which ART exerts its intrinsic antitumor activity.

Among apoptosis regulators, Caspase-3 represents the terminal executioner enzyme that integrates upstream mitochondrial signals mediated by the Bcl-2/Bax axis and irreversibly commits cells to apoptotic death. In the present study, network pharmacology analysis was performed to predict potential molecular targets of ART, and Caspase-3 emerged as a candidate target of interest based on topological features and docking affinity. However, despite its central role in apoptosis, the regulatory role of ART on Caspase-3 and its associated apoptotic pathways has not been fully clarified in HNSCC.

To address these gaps, we employed an integrated research strategy combining network pharmacology, molecular docking, and in vitro and in vivo validation to identify candidate molecular targets of ART in HNSCC and elucidate the underlying mechanisms. This study aimed to provide mechanistic insight into ART-mediated antitumor effects in HNSCC and to establish a foundation for further preclinical investigation.

Materials and methods

Screening of ART targets

The target genes of ART were predicted using the Swiss Target Prediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/), SuperPred database (http://prediction.charite.de/), and CHEMBL database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/). Data for ART-target interactions were retrieved directly from these web-based tools, which utilize molecular structure similarity and known protein-ligand interactions to predict potential targets. The target prediction was based on the chemical structure of ART, and interactions were considered with a threshold of predictive binding affinity > 0.5.

Obtaining HNSCC potential targets

Potential targets associated with HNSCC were identified using the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) and the CTD database (https://ctdbase.org/). Relevant HNSCC-associated genes were retrieved by querying disease-specific terms related to HNSCC within these platforms. Genes related to HNSCC were filtered based on evidence strength, with the top-ranked genes based on disease association included for further analysis.

ART-target network construction and core target screening

Venn diagrams of ART and HNSCC disease genes were generated using the Microbiotics platform (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/) to identify the intersecting targets. The core key targets were further screened using Cytoscape software (versions 3.9.1 and 3.7.0) to construct a comprehensive network of ART-HNSCC interactions. The STRING database (http://www.string-db.org/) was employed to analyze protein-protein interactions (PPIs) and to identify the most significant core targets based on the interaction confidence scores (cutoff > 0.4).

Gene ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analysis for GO terms and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were performed on the intersecting ART-HNSCC targets. DAVID Bioinformatics Resources (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) and Enrichr (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) were used to perform GO and KEGG enrichment analysis, focusing on biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC). For KEGG pathway analysis, we considered pathways with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and ranked them by GeneRatio and gene count to identify the most significantly enriched pathways.

Molecular docking

The 3D structure of ART was downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and processed using Open Babel (version 3.1.1) for energy minimization, hydrogen addition, and conversion to PDBQT format. Crystal structures of the target proteins were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (http://www.rcsb.org/), and the corresponding PDB IDs and resolutions used were as follows: CASP3 (PDB ID: 4QUJ; resolution: 1.50 Å), STAT3 (PDB ID: 6NJS; resolution: 2.70 Å), ESR1 (PDB ID: 5DXE; resolution: 1.50 Å), HSP90AA1 (PDB ID: 8FFV; resolution: 3.01 Å), and NF-κB (PDB ID: 7LFC; resolution: 2.10 Å). Because no experimentally resolved full-length structure of HIF1A exists in the PDB, its 3D structure was obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (UniProt ID: Q16665).

Protein preprocessing was performed using AutoDockTools, including removal of water molecules and native ligands, deletion of non-relevant macromolecular components, addition of polar hydrogens, and assignment of Gasteiger charges. All proteins and the ligand were then converted to PDBQT format. Molecular docking experiments were carried out using AutoDock Vina (version 1.1.2) with exhaustiveness set to 10. Grid box dimensions were centered on the native ligand-binding pocket of each protein. The top-scoring binding modes were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL (version 2.6.0).

Cell culture

The HNSCC cell line SCC-25 (CRL-1628, ATCC) was cultured in Ham’s F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin, and 400 ng/mL hydrocortisone. Similarly, the HNSCC cell line CAL27 (CRL-2095, ATCC) was cultured in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin. All cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Periodic testing for mycoplasma contamination was conducted using the Mycoplasma Detection Kit (FM321, TransGen, China). Every 2–3 days, cells were passaged, and only those in the log phase of growth were selected for experiments.

Cell viability assay

During the logarithmic growth stage, cells were planted in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h under 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Subsequently, different concentrations of ART (20, 40, and 80 µg/mL; A3731, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in DMSO and diluted in culture medium, with the final DMSO concentration kept constant (< 0.1%) in all wells. Control cells received culture medium containing the same concentration of DMSO without ART. Once the treatment was completed, 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; C0038, Beyotime, China) solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using an enzyme marker (Bio-Tek, USA).

Wound healing assay

A 10 µL pipette tip was utilized to produce straight, consistent linear scratches on the cell monolayer. Subsequently, the monolayer was gently washed with PBS to eliminate cellular debris. Cells were then incubated in serum-reduced medium containing either vehicle (control; medium with 0.1% DMSO) or ART at 20, 40, or 80 µg/mL with the final DMSO concentration matched across all groups. Wound closure was photographed at 0 h and 24 h after treatment using an inverted microscope (Nikon, Japan), and the scratch area was quantified as previously described [13].

Cell invasion assay

The Transwell test was performed in a 24-well chamber (Corning, USA), equipped with a Matrigel-coated polycarbonate membrane filter of 8 μm pore size (BD Biosciences, USA). In the upper chamber, 2 × 105 cells were cultivated in 200 µL of a serum-free solution with ART (20, 40, or 80 µg/mL), with equal DMSO concentration in all groups. The lower chamber was filled with 500 µL of a medium containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24 h incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2, non-invading cells on the upper surface were removed, and invading cells on the lower surface were fixed in methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted in five randomly selected fields under a microscope.

TUNEL assay

Apoptosis in cells was evaluated using a TUNEL BrightGreen Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, China). Briefly, cells were seeded onto glass coverslips in 24-well plates and treated vehicle (0.1% DMSO; control) or ART at the indicated concentrations. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 5 min and incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope. TUNEL-positive cells were quantified from five randomly selected fields per group.

Western blotting

Total cellular proteins were extracted from SCC-25 and CAL27 cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein (20 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE on 10–12% polyacrylamide gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1.5 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Bcl-2 (CST, #4223, dilution 1:1000), Bax (CST, #2772, dilution 1:1000), total Caspase-3 (CST, #9662, dilution 1:1000), cleaved Caspase-3 (CST, #9664, dilution 1:1000), and β-actin (CST, #4970, dilution 1:1000). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (CST, #7074, dilution 1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and imaged using a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to β-actin.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from SCC-25 and CAL27 cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RNA concentration and purity were evaluated by NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Takara, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara, Japan) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). The cycling conditions were: 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH, and relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences used were as follows: Bcl-2: Forward 5’-GGTGGGGTCATGTGTGTGG-3’ and Reverse 5’-CGGTTCAGGTACTCAGTCATCC-3’; Bax: Forward 5’-CCCGAGAGGTCTTTTTCCGAG-3’ and Reverse 5’-CCAGCCCATGATGGTTCTGAT-3’; GAPDH: Forward 5’-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3’ and Reverse 5’-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3’.

Tumor xenograft model

The xenograft model was established as previously described [13], with minor modifications. Nude Balb/c-nu mice (5 weeks old, 18–20 g; Lai ‘an Technology Co., LTD, Guangzhou, China) were randomly assigned into four groups (n = 3 per group): Control, ART-L (100 mg/kg), ART-M (200 mg/kg), and ART-H (400 mg/kg). SCC-25 or CAL27 cells (2 × 106) were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of each mouse. Tumors became palpable approximately 7 days post-inoculation, which is consistent with previously reported HNSCC xenograft models [16].

Once tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm³, mice were treated with ART. ART was freshly prepared prior to administration by initially dissolving it in a mixture of ethanol and dimethyl sulfoxide (EtOH/DMSO, 1:1, v/v) to generate a concentrated stock solution, which was subsequently diluted with 5% sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) solution to the desired working concentrations. Mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of ART at the indicated doses (100, 200, or 400 mg/kg) with a dosing volume of 0.2 mL per mouse, while control mice received equal volumes of vehicle solution containing EtOH, DMSO, and Na₂CO₃. The same solvent concentration was maintained across all experimental groups to minimize potential solvent-related effects.

Tumor size was measured weekly using vernier calipers, and the volume was calculated as 1/2 × long diameter × short diameter². After 4 weeks of treatment, all mice were euthanized using carbon dioxide, and tumors were excised, weighed, and processed for downstream flow cytometry analysis to quantify apoptosis. During the treatment period, mice were monitored regularly for body weight and general health status. No obvious behavioral abnormalities or significant body weight loss were observed, and no animals required premature euthanasia due to toxicity or other complications.

Flow cytometry

Tumor tissues were processed to obtain single-cell suspensions through digestion and filtration. Apoptosis of tumor cells was assessed using the Annexin V/PI kit (BD Biosciences 556547, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Flow cytometric analysis was performed on a BD FACSAria III system, and a standardized gating strategy was applied: cell debris was excluded using FSC/SSC parameters, doublets were removed via FSC-A/FSC-H discrimination, and apoptotic cell subsets were identified as Annexin V⁺/PI⁻ (early apoptosis, Q3) and Annexin V⁺/PI⁺ (late apoptosis, Q2). The total apoptosis rate (%) was calculated as Q2 + Q3, and data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc. CA, USA). All experiments were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates, and each treatment condition was tested in triplicate unless otherwise specified. Data distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and variance homogeneity was evaluated using Levene’s test. All results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was employed, while two-group comparisons were performed using the Student’s t-test. P < 0.05 was indicated as statistically significant.

Results

Screening and enrichment analysis of ART therapeutic HNSCC targets

Target prediction identified 148 ART-related targets, while 1618 HNSCC-associated genes were obtained from disease databases. Intersecting the two datasets yielded 77 common targets that may be associated with the biological effects of ART against HNSCC (Fig. 1A). GO enrichment analysis (Fig. 1B) revealed that these intersecting targets were primarily enriched in apoptosis- and stress-related Biological Processes (BP), including positive regulation of nitric oxide biosynthetic process, response to hypoxia, and epidermal growth factor receptor signaling pathway. In the Cellular Component (CC) category, enriched terms were predominantly associated with membrane-bound and cytosolic structures, such as membrane raft, cytoplasm, and cytosol. For Molecular Functions (MF), the targets were enriched in protein-binding-related categories, including transcription coactivator binding, enzyme binding, and protein homodimerization activity, suggesting regulatory roles in signaling and transcriptional control. Additionally, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 1C) revealed enrichment in pathways such as PI3K–Akt signaling, HIF-1 signaling, IL-17 signaling, PD-L1/PD-1 checkpoint pathway in cancer. These findings suggested potential involvement of ART-associated targets in apoptosis-related and oncogenic signaling pathways in HNSCC.

Network-based identification of candidate targets of ART in HNSCC

To further identify candidate core targets of ART in HNSCC, a PPI network was constructed by analyzing 77 targets with the STRING database. The network was subsequently analyzed in Cytoscape, where each node was quantitatively evaluated using three topological metrics: betweenness centrality (BC), reflecting the extent to which a node serves as a bridge within the network; closeness centrality (CC), representing the average shortest path from a node to all other nodes; and degree centrality (DC), indicating the number of direct interactions. Targets with DC, BC, and CC values above their respective median thresholds were defined as high-importance nodes. This analysis identified six candidate hub targets of ART for HNSCC: STAT3, ESR1, CASP3, HSP90AA1, NFKB1, and HIF1A (Fig. 2A).

Subsequently, molecular docking analysis was performed to explore the potential interactions between ART with the six identified targets. The results indicated that the binding energies (kcal/mol) of ART with CASP3, ESR1, HIF1A, HSP90AA1, NFKB1, and STAT3 were − 8.0, -7.5, -6.9, -7.0, -6.5, and − 7.5, respectively (Fig. 2B–G). Among these targets, CASP3 exhibited the lowest predicted binding energy. Docking analysis further revealed that ART may form multiple hydrogen bond interactions with key amino acid residues of CASP3, including ARG64, GLN161, SER205, and ARG207. These residues are located within or adjacent to the catalytic pocket of CASP3, suggesting a potential structural compatibility between ART and the enzyme. Based on its topological prominence and predicted docking affinity, CASP3 was selected for subsequent experimental validation.

ART reduces viability and promotes apoptosis in HNSCC cells

Further experiments examined the functional effects of ART on the HNSCC cell lines SCC-25 and CAL27. CCK-8 assay demonstrated that ART at 20, 40, and 80 µg/mL significantly reduced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3A). Consistently, wound-healing and Transwell invasion assays demonstrated that treatment with 20, 40, and 80 µg/mL ART progressively inhibited cellular migration (Fig. 3B) and invasion (Fig. 3C). TUNEL staining further revealed increased apoptotic signals following ART treatment, as indicated by the progressive increase in TUNEL-positive nuclei in both cells (Fig. 3D). These findings indicated that ART treatment was associated with decreased cell viability, migration, and invasion, along with increased apoptotic activity in HNSCC cells.

ART is associated with modulation of the Bcl-2/Bax/Caspase-3 axis in vitro

To determine whether ART activates the classical mitochondrial apoptotic pathway, we assessed alterations in the Bcl-2/Bax/Caspase 3 axis in SCC-25 and CAL27 cells. Western blot analysis showed that ART treatment led to an increase in Bax and a decrease in Bcl-2, accompanied by a marked elevation in cleaved Caspase-3, whereas total Caspase-3 levels remained relatively unchanged (Fig. 4A). Consistent with these protein-level changes, qPCR analysis demonstrated upregulation of Bax mRNA and downregulation of Bcl-2 mRNA in both cell lines (Fig. 4B). These findings indicated that ART treatment was associated with a shift toward pro-apoptotic signaling and Caspase-3 activation in HNSCC cells.

ART suppresses tumor growth and increases apoptotic cell populations in vivo

To evaluate the antitumor effects of ART in vivo, xenograft models were established using SCC-25 and CAL27 cells. ART treatment suppressed tumor growth in a dose-dependent manner, as evidenced by smaller tumors and reduced tumor volume and weight compared with the control group (Fig. 5A). Flow cytometry of dissociated tumor tissues further indicated increased apoptotic cell populations following ART treatment (Fig. 5B). In SCC-25 xenografts, early apoptosis increased from 1.42% (control) to 3.71%, 5.00%, and 7.19% in the ART-L, ART-M, and ART-H groups, respectively, while late apoptosis increased from 2.14% to 8.25%, 16.5%, and 26.6%. A similar pattern was observed in CAL27 tumors, where early apoptosis rose from 1.75% to 2.65%, 3.41%, and 5.38%, and late apoptosis increased from 3.08% to 8.84%, 12.6%, and 17.9%, respectively. Although the cohort size was limited, these findings were consistent with the in vitro pro-apoptotic effects observed in HNSCC cells.

Discussion

In this study, we screened 77 ART-associated targets related to HNSCC using network pharmacology, and PPI analysis further highlighted six candidate hub targets. Enrichment analyses suggested that these targets were functionally associated with cancer-related pathways, including IL-17, HIF-1, and PI3K-Akt signaling cascades. Molecular docking indicated a potential interaction between ART and Caspase-3. Subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that ART was associated with reduced cell viability, migration, and invasion, along with increased apoptotic activity. These findings collectively suggested that modulation of the Bcl-2/Bax/Caspase-3 axis may contribute to the observed antitumor effects of ART in HNSCC.

HNSCC remains a major public health burden, with over 60% of patients in China presenting with stage III–IV disease and exhibiting a five-year survival rate below 50% [17]. Although multimodal therapies have improved local control, these treatments often compromise quality of life and remain limited by postoperative recurrence and distant metastasis [18–20]. Identifying molecular pathways involved in tumor progression is therefore important for guiding future therapeutic research. ART has recently gained growing attention in cancer research due to its ability to modulate diverse tumor-related biological processes, which include proliferation, migration, and invasion [21], apoptosis and autophagy [22], angiogenesis [23], and ferroptosis [24]. Clinical and preclinical evidence has demonstrated its efficacy across multiple malignancies such as bladder cancer [25], squamous cell carcinoma of the skin [26], and ovarian cancer [27], suggesting that ART may exert broad pan-cancer activity. Indeed, many of ART’s reported effects, including modulation of Bcl-2 family proteins, activation of Caspase-3, and inhibition of PI3K–Akt and MAPK signaling, represent core apoptotic and survival pathways shared across diverse cancer types. However, whether these common mechanisms fully account for ART’s effects in HNSCC remains unclear, highlighting the need to delineate ART’s mechanistic roles specifically within the context of HNSCC biology.

Our enrichment analysis suggested potential involvement of apoptosis-related and oncogenic signaling pathways such as IL-17/MAPK, HIF-1, and PI3K-AKT axis. The IL-17/MAPK cascade plays a well-established oncogenic role in HNSCC progression [28]. In the hypoxic microenvironment, activation of HIF-1α contributes to metabolic reprogramming and resistance to apoptosis [29]. Inhibition of PI3K-AKT signaling has been reported to enhance apoptosis and suppress migration in oral squamous carcinoma [30]. These enriched pathways converge on the mitochondrial apoptotic machinery by regulating Bcl-2 family proteins, creating a mechanistic basis that connects ART’s predicted targets to downstream apoptotic execution. In this context, Caspase-3 emerged from the PPI network and docking analysis as a central node. Caspase-3 is a key executioner protease responsible for cleaving substrate proteins during apoptosis and plays a central role in cell survival, proliferation, and tumorigenesis [31, 32]. Bcl-2 inhibits apoptosis by blocking Caspase-3 activation, whereas Bax promotes mitochondrial permeabilization and Caspase-3 activation [33]. In the present study, ART treatment was associated with increased Bax expression, reduced Bcl-2 expression, and elevated cleaved Caspase-3 levels. These findings are consistent with activation of apoptotic signaling. However, activation of Caspase-3 does not establish functional indispensability, and genetic or pharmacological inhibition studies would be required to confirm mechanistic dependency.

Resistance to apoptosis is a well-recognized hallmark of HNSCC progression and therapeutic resistance, highlighting the potential biological relevance of targeting apoptotic pathways in this disease. In this context, the complementary use of Annexin V/PI flow cytometry and TUNEL assays in the present study provides supportive evidence of apoptotic responses following ART treatment. Flow cytometry differentiates early apoptotic cells from late apoptotic or necrotic cells, providing insight into the initiation and progression of apoptosis. TUNEL detects DNA fragmentation, a characteristic feature of the execution phase of apoptosis that is often associated with downstream caspase activation. Together, these assays capture different stages of apoptotic events and support the interpretation that ART treatment is associated with activation of apoptotic processes in HNSCC cells. Consistent with these observations, ART exposure was accompanied by alterations in the Bcl-2/Bax/Caspase-3 axis, suggesting involvement of mitochondrial apoptotic signaling. However, these findings reflect association rather than definitive mechanistic dependency. From a translational perspective, although prior studies have reported favorable safety profiles of ART in non-oncologic contexts [34], the clinical safety, optimal dosing, and therapeutic index of ART in HNSCC remain to be systematically evaluated.

In addition, ART possesses pharmacokinetic characteristics that have been well characterized in antimalarial applications, including rapid absorption and conversion to its active metabolite dihydroartemisinin. However, the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of ART in oncologic settings, particularly in solid tumors such as HNSCC, require further investigation. Factors such as bioavailability, tumor tissue penetration, optimal dosing schedules, and long-term safety must be carefully evaluated before clinical translation can be considered. Thus, while the present findings provide mechanistic insight, additional studies are necessary to determine the feasibility of ART in cancer therapy.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the experimental validation relied on two tongue squamous cell carcinoma cell lines, which may not fully capture the biological heterogeneity of HNSCC. Additional models derived from diverse anatomical sites, including HPV-positive HNSCC cell lines, will be required to further assess the generalizability of these findings. Second, the concentrations used in vitro and the dosing regimen applied in vivo may exceed clinically achievable exposure levels, and comprehensive pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies will be necessary to determine the translational feasibility of ART in HNSCC treatment. Third, although Caspase-3 was identified as a central node and its activation correlated with ART treatment, functional inhibition or genetic manipulation experiments were not performed to establish causal dependency, and therefore the current findings support association rather than definitive mechanistic proof. In addition, while network pharmacology analysis suggested the involvement of multiple oncogenic and immune-related pathways, experimental validation was primarily confined to the mitochondrial apoptotic axis, and other predicted pathways remain to be systematically investigated. Furthermore, the cytotoxic effects of ART on non-cancerous epithelial cells were not evaluated in the present study, and comparisons with standard chemotherapeutic agents were not performed. Finally, the relatively small animal cohort size may limit statistical power and reduce the generalizability of the in vivo findings. Collectively, these limitations indicate that the present work provides preclinical mechanistic insight rather than translational validation, and further investigation is required before clinical extrapolation can be considered.

Conclusion

This study integrated network pharmacology analysis and experimental validation to investigate the potential anticancer effects of ART in HNSCC. The results suggest that ART treatment is associated with modulation of the Bcl-2/Bax/Caspase-3 apoptotic axis and suppression of tumor growth in preclinical models. These findings provide preliminary mechanistic insight into the pro-apoptotic activity of ART and offer a basis for further investigation of its therapeutic potential in HNSCC.
