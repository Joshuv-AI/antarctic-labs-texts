Assessment of cultural competence in pharmacy students before and after social and behavioural pharmacy education – a cross-sectional study
Authors: Basmah H Alfageh, Jawza F Alsabhan, Rana Aljadeed, Marwan A Alrasheed, Tahani K Alshammari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCultural competence is a crucial outcome of pharmacy programs because it may affect the medical care that patients receive. This study evaluates the level of cultural competence in pharmacy students before and after completing a social and behavioural pharmacy course.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, third (P3)- and fourth-year (P4) pharmacy students were assessed using an adapted version of a Self-Assessment of Perceived Level of Cultural Competence (SAPLCC)-validated instrument. Each domain and factor’s score differences between P3 and P4 students were calculated and the total score for each student was calculated and compared between the two groups. The normalised scores of the students were categorised into low (< 2), moderate (2–3), or high (> 3) and the percentages of students in each category were calculated for both groups.ResultsA total of 272 pharmacy students have participated in the study. The knowledge domain showed the greatest increase in mean scores after the course, with a rise of 0.8 points (from 33.84 ± 9.42 to 34.64 ± 8.75). Understanding the context of care (Factor 2), showed the greatest increase in mean points after taking the course with a raise of 0.63 points (from 15.58 ± 4.44 to 16.21 ± 4.08). However, no significant differences were found between the scores of the two groups across any of the domains and factors covered by the SAPLCC tool. Both groups displayed intermediate normalised mean scores in the Knowledge, Skills, Encounters, and Abilities categories, and high normalised mean scores in the Attitudes and Awareness domains.ConclusionsThe maximum points were not reached in any domain by P3 or P4 students. Therefore, to ensure that pharmacy students are prepared to deliver optimal healthcare services to patients from diverse cultural backgrounds, it is essential to incorporate sessions on health disparities and cultural competencies into traditional courses and practical training in the pharmacy curriculum.Keywords: Cultural competence, Pharmacy education, Social and behavioural pharmacy, Pharmacy students, Cultural awareness, Pharmacy curriculum

Background

Cultural competence in Pharmacy practice is critical to providing proficient patient care and promoting medication adherence, thereby improving treatment outcomes. In fact, health disparities are well-acknowledged, and in pharmacy practice settings, pharmacists deliver services to diverse groups of individuals with different racial, socioeconomic, and religious background [1, 2]. Pharmacy education includes the capacity and strategic educational process that generates a diverse workforce, ranging from research and academia to clinical settings. At the global level, the International Pharmaceutical Federation (FIP) has issued a recommendation and action plan [1] that has been reviewed [2–4]. The plan developed a global competency framework to enhance pharmacy practitioner education [1].

The pharmacy competency framework includes various components, such as patient-centred care, pharmaceutical science, management, and technical support [5]. Pharmacy competency is also a core requirement for achieving quality standards and accreditation, such as those outlined by the Accreditation Council for Pharmacy Education (ACPE) [4]. A culturally competent patient care system respects diversity, demonstrates the ability to engage with cross-cultural communities, and adapts to deliver professional care in diverse cultures [3]. Spady introduced the concept of competence-based education (CBE) in the 1970 s [4]. The term ‘competency’ encompasses a combination of attitudes, skills, and knowledge essential for professional performance. The application of CBE in medical and pharmacy schools has been reviewed and discussed. CBE is rationalised into four main components: (1) defining the educational outcome in all aspects, (2) determining the baseline of expected students’ abilities, (3) applying flexible timeframe training, and (4) tailoring assessments of various competencies [5, 6].

ACPE accreditation standards emphasize the importance of addressing cultural diversity and patient care disparities within the curriculum of schools of pharmacy, a practice that has been implemented in the United States (US) [7] and other countries, including Saudi Arabia (SA) [8]. However, the optimal cultural competence teaching strategy is far from being determined, and future studies are required to fill this knowledge gap [9]. Furthermore, the systematic implementation of a cultural competency course within the pharmacy curriculum faces significant challenges, primarily because sociocultural factors and norms have profound impacts on health [10, 11].

Pharm-D program at The College of Pharmacy at King Saud University is ACPE accredited program. The curriculum is structured as a six-year program (4 + 1 + 1 years) comprising 193 credit hours. The program begins with a foundational year (First Common Year, FCY), which is provided to all the students enrolled in different Heath Sciences Colleges and consists of 32 credit hours. The courses offered in this year provide students with essential general science knowledge along with English language courses for entry into the professional curriculum. The subsequent four years of professional study at The College of Pharmacy (P2 to P5), encompass courses in biomedical sciences, pharmaceutical, clinical sciences, social and administrative sciences, and introductory pharmacy practice experiences given in English language. The curriculum also provides opportunities to take elective courses from various disciplines of pharmacy practice. The sixth year is the final year and is dedicated to advanced pharmacy practice experiences (APPEs).

Social and Behavioural Pharmacy course is a two credits mandatory course provided in the fourth academic year (P4). This course introduces students to sociological theories and concepts that have an impact on health and health care, as well as the ways in which individual psychology forms and influences health and healthcare. Topics such as the social determinants of health and related ethical issues; the social construction of disease; and the exploration of when and why people seek health care services are discussed in this course. Theories and models are used to stimulate discussion about how social forces impact pharmaceutical care. The course’s lectures are prepared using a number of current and significant sources in the field of social behavioural pharmacy [12–17]. The course’s main objectives are to develop a deeper understanding of how social and behavioural sciences are applied to problems in pharmacy, health, and illness. By the end of the course, students are expected to acquire the skills required to apply social and behavioural principles in the design, delivery, and evaluation of pharmaceutical care. The Pharmacy Education Unite (PEU) periodically revise and update the course’s contents to ensure its appropriateness and the alignment of Course Learning Outcomes (CLOs) to the Program Learning Outcomes (PLOs). Qualified faculties are assigned by the PEU to teach the course based on their expertise to ensure that the course’s objectives were met and the outcomes are effectively delivered. The assessment tools included written exams, quizzes, classroom case-based discussions, and individual and group assignments. A previous report documented that most cultural competency courses were developed using a combination of didactic and active teaching components [18].

Previous studies have evaluated the impact of courses focusing on similar objectives [19, 20]. Many have been customised for specialised healthcare system cultural competency education, such as in dentistry [21], nursing [22], and medical schools [23]. However, compared to other medical specialties, studies examining cultural competency using an integrated set of social determinants of culture and complementary health modules targeting pharmacy students are sparse [24–26]. Kucherepa and O’Connell examined the applicability of the Cultural Competence and Social Determinants of Health Survey to assess pharmacy students’ cultural competence and the ability to address suboptimal social determinants of health [24].

This study assesses the cultural competency of two groups of pharmacy students: P4 students who had completed the course and P3 students who had not yet taken the Social and behavioural pharmacy course. This work contributes to the advancement of pharmacy education and highlights areas where students may need to further develop their cultural competence.

Methods

Study design: participants, setting, and ethical considerations

This cross-sectional study was conducted among undergraduate pharmacy students at the College of Pharmacy of King Saud University in Saudi Arabia from January to June 2024 (corresponding to the second semester of the academic year). The Social and behavioural pharmacy course was offered during the first semester of the fourth academic year. The lists of P3 students who were expected to take the course in the next semester and P4 students who had completed the course in the previous semester were obtained from academic affairs records. Although the different student groups may differ in some baseline characteristics (e.g., prior academic performance or cultural exposure), their comparison was considered appropriate for evaluating the impact of the course on cultural competence, as all students were enrolled in the same Pharm-D program, exposed to comparable instructional content, and assessed using standardized methods. Similar approaches have been used in previous educational studies comparing consecutive student cohorts to assess curricular interventions and learning outcomes [27].

An adapted version of the Self-Assessment of Perceived Level of Cultural Competence (SAPLCC) tool was used with permission from Professor Margarita Echeverri [28]. The objectives of the study were explained at the beginning, followed by a section for obtaining written informed consent. Ethical approval was obtained from the Standing Committee for Scientific Research Ethics of King Saud University (reference no. KSU-HE-23–1277, dated 24 January 2024).

Data collection, data source, and study variables

P3 and P4 students identified from their academic affairs records were invited to complete the self-administered SAPLCC tool by sending a Google Form link via email or by completing it directly in class. Data on participants’ general characteristics, including age, gender, and GPA were collected using Google Forms. Names and university ID were obtained to certify the completeness of the responses and ensure that the participating students belonged to either level P3 or P4.

A number of instruments have been used to evaluate cultural competency, but they have limitations for our stud, such as not including the social determinants of health (SDOH) questions, not being utilized for course or curriculum longitudinal assessment, and lacking validation in student pharmacists [29]. In this study, an adapted version of the revised SAPLCC tool was used to assess students’ cultural competence. Items of the Clinical Cultural Competency Questionnaire (CCCQ) which had been developed to analyse physician cultural competencies and California Brief Multicultural Competency Scale (CBMCS) which had been developed to analyse mental health providers’ cultural competence were combined to develop the first version of SAPLCC tool [30]. A revised version of SAPLCC tool was developed after adding SDOH items and deleting some items during the validation process and evaluated in first to fourth-year pharmacy students [28]. This self-administered survey has been validated and used as a reliable tool to measure pharmacy students’ perceived levels of cultural competence [24, 28]. The SAPLCC comprises six major domains: Knowledge, Skills, Attitudes, Encounters, Abilities, and Awareness. Some of the 75 items within these domains of the revised tool were altered or eliminated to conform to Saudi cultural norms. The six domains of the SAPLCC tool are further divided into two or three factors, each with several items addressing specific aspects of cultural competence. The survey in this study included 14 factors comprising 68 items. Each item was rated on a four-point Likert scale, where a score of one indicated the lowest level of perceived cultural competence, and a score of four indicated the highest.

Statistical analysis

For each student, the total score was calculated from the maximum of 272 points that could be achieved (4 * 68). Descriptive statistics were used to calculate the mean, median, and standard deviations for survey responses related to the demographic variables, as well as for each factor, domain, and overall survey score for both P3 and P4 students. Differences in scores after taking the course were calculated for each domain and factor by subtracting the mean score before taking the course (P3) from the mean score after taking the course (P4). Normalised mean scores, both before and after taking the course, were calculated by dividing the mean score by the number of items in each domain or factor. These normalised scores were categorised as low (< 2), moderate (2–3), or high (> 3). The percentages of students in each category were calculated. The scores of P3 and P4 students for all domains, factors, and overall scores were compared using a two-sample t-test or Mann–Whitney U statistical test, as appropriate. A p-value of 0.05 or less was set as statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Statistical Analysis System (SAS) version 9.4.

Results

Demographics

All the students who invited to join this study were participated. Among the 272 pharmacy students who enrolled in this study, 117 were registered at the P3 level and 155 at the P4 level. Male participants comprised 44.4% and 51.6% of the P3 and P4 students, respectively. The mean age for P3 students was 20.55 ± 1.09 and, for P4 students, it was 21.57 ± 0.86. Discussion

This study assessed the cultural competence of pharmacy students before and after taking a social behavioural pharmacy course. P3 students demonstrated high scores in the Attitudes and Awareness domains before taking the course. This finding aligns with previous research conducted in the United States, which examined the development of cultural competence and learning related to SDOH among pharmacy students [24]. The elevated scores of students in the Attitudes and Awareness domains before being exposed to the social and behavioural pharmacy course could be explained by the fact that both studies were conducted in academic institutions located in central cities, where most students live in diverse communities and learn about culture and SDOH from their environment. One possible explanation for the higher scores observed among P3 students is that they may have overestimated their level of cultural competence. At this stage, students have not yet completed the social behavioural pharmacy course given in the P4 year, which provides a more comprehensive understanding of cultural awareness, and self-reflection. Consequently, P4 students who completed this course may have developed a more realistic and critical self-perception, leading to relatively lower but more accurate self-assessment scores. The students in the study by Kucherepa et al., and our study had intermediate scores in the Encounters and Abilities domains before the course, whereas the students in the Kucherepa et al., study had the lowest scores in the knowledge and Skills domains [24]. The students assessed prior to the course in our study were in their third year, whereas those in the another study [24] were in their first year. This could be the reason for the difference in scores in the Knowledge and Skills domains.

Beyond institutional setting, students’ cultural competence can be influenced by several factors. Parenting style, for instance, shapes early attitudes toward diversity and empathy, which later influence students’ openness to cultural differences [31]. Language background also plays a critical role, as proficiency in multiple languages often enhances communication skills and cultural sensitivity [31]. Additionally, academic performance and engagement in reflective or experiential learning activities contribute to the development of self-awareness and critical thinking—key components of cultural competence [31, 32]. Furthermore, cross-cultural encounters, such as interactions with patients or peers from diverse backgrounds, provide valuable opportunities to apply and reinforce culturally competent behaviours [33].

Our study found that the most notable increase in mean scores occurred in the Knowledge domain after course completion. However, no statistically significant differences were observed in students’ scores before and after the course across all domains, contributing factors, or overall totals. The observed increase in mean scores provides preliminary evidence that the implemented course elements such as interactive learning activities and reflective exercises may have had a meaningful impact on students’ cultural awareness and sensitivity.

A subsequent course that discusses ethics in pharmacy practice, which is typically offered during the second semester of the fourth academic year in accordance with the Doctor of Pharmacy curriculum, covers some of the items that were not covered in this course, such as the Skills domain (Factor 4), Attitudes domain (Factor 5), and Awareness domain (Factor 14). Instead of conventional or theoretical courses, training and experience can be used to improve the remaining issues, such as Factors 8 (increasing comfort during cross-cultural encounters) and 10 (assessing population health requirements). Different pharmacy programs incorporate cultural competency into their curricula in various ways [1]. This variation could be the result of differing conceptualisations of cultural competency among educators in different programs.

Further, based on the maximum points achieved compared to the maximum points per domain, our findings suggest that additional training could enhance students’ knowledge and cultural competencies. While the course effectively enhanced students’ knowledge in cultural competence, improvements in skills-based domains were less evident. Although active learning strategies such as case-based discussion is incorporated, further development of these skills may occur during the advanced experiential training year, where students apply cultural competence in real clinical settings.

A growing body of literature demonstrates that strengthening cultural competence requires intentional, multifaceted educational strategies. Experiential learning has been repeatedly identified as one of the most effective approaches for developing cultural competence. Studies in health-professions education consistently show that community engagement, service-learning activities, and internships in diverse clinical or community settings enhance students’ empathy, communication skills, and ability to navigate culturally complex situations [34–36]. Health professions curricula need to go beyond awareness and knowledge, and should be expanded to cover all cultural competence aspects. While knowledge-based gains are commonly achieved through lectures and readings, evidence indicates that cultural skills such as conducting culturally sensitive assessments or adapting communication styles develop only when students are provided with structured opportunities for guided practice, feedback, and reflection [1, 37]. Adopting innovative interactive teaching methods can help address these gaps. For instance, pharmacy students in a UK MPharm program responded positively to a simulation-oriented assessment that followed an introductory intercultural-competency lecture, suggesting that experiential and case-based learning resonate with learners [38]. However, challenges remain: a mixed-methods case study highlighted significant barriers (technical, curricular, and resource-wise) to integrating computer-based simulations in pharmacy programs, underscoring the need for thoughtful implementation [39]. At the same time, broader reviews of pedagogical strategies affirm that combining case-based learning, simulation, role play, and other interactive exercises aligns with the developmental nature of cultural competence and supports sustained student growth [40].

A previous study evaluated cultural competence in second-year pharmacy students using the CCCQ [41]. The majority of the students who participated in this study expressed confidence in identifying and recognising the elements of cultural competence. At the same time, participants indicated that they were only slightly or somewhat comfortable when asked questions regarding their knowledge, skills, and comfort. They had ‘quite a bit’ of competence regarding attitudes towards other cultures. In our study, the students were in their third and fourth years of study; they had to finish their fifth year of pharmacy school and a compulsory one-year training and practice program to graduate with a Pharm-D degree. Students’ scores demonstrated a high degree of cultural competency at this level of study despite the fact that the mean scores were all below the maximum. By the end of their academic experience, they should have acquired the necessary cultural competencies. The results of our study underscore the importance of providing training in both the didactic and experiential components of the pharmacy curriculum to enhance pharmacy students’ cultural competency.

Furthermore, our findings highlight the crucial role of cultural competence in promoting quality and equity in healthcare delivery. Strengthening cultural competence among pharmacy students can enhance patient outcomes through improved communication, trust, and adherence, while also helping to reduce healthcare disparities. In Saudi Arabia’s multicultural context, developing these competencies is particularly important as pharmacists increasingly interact with patients from varied linguistic and cultural backgrounds. On a broader scale, embedding cultural competence within health professions education supports global efforts to advance equitable and patient-centred care across diverse healthcare settings.

This study directly evaluated a real-world pharmacy course to enhance its relevance to academic and professional practice. Additionally, the focus on cultural competence addresses a key area of pharmacy education that is often under-evaluated. The use of a structured assessment tool and the high participation rate further strengthened the validity and reliability of the findings. However, this study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design limited the ability to infer causality between course participation and changes in cultural competence. Although the assessments were conducted before and after taking the social behavioural pharmacy course, the study did not follow the same students longitudinally, which may introduce a potential selection bias and restricted the conclusions regarding individual-level changes. Future studies using longitudinal designs may provide stronger evidence of the impact of education. The cultural competence was measured using a self-administered tool, which may have been subject to social desirability and recall bias. The study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other pharmacy schools and cultural contexts. This study did not collect information on students’ ethnic backgrounds, which may have provided additional insight into variations in cultural competence. However, the majority of students enrolled in the College of Pharmacy are of Saudi origin, with a very limited proportion of international students from other Middle Eastern countries who share a similar Arab ethnic background. Therefore, while nationality data may vary slightly, ethnic diversity within the student population is relatively limited. This demographic homogeneity may have influenced the range of cultural experiences and perspectives represented in the study. Nonetheless, differences in students’ exposure to other cultures, such as through travel or interactions during introductory pharmacy practice experiences, may still contribute to variability in cultural competence. Additionally, unmeasured factors, such as prior cultural exposure or extracurricular experiences, may have influenced students’ responses. Finally, SAPLCC is a validated instrument; however, modifying survey instruments by removing or altering items may lower their psychometric properties.

Conclusions

Although, an increase in the mean score after taking the social behavioural pharmacy course was noticed in the Knowledge and Attitudes domains, there was a decline in the mean score of the remaining domains. However, all of these changes are not statistically significant. Being culturally competent is an important pharmacy program outcome. To guarantee that pharmacy graduates are equipped to provide the best possible healthcare services to patients from a variety of cultural backgrounds, it is crucial to include sessions on health inequalities and cultural competences in both regular courses and practical training. Future longitudinal research is required to evaluate how pharmacy students’ cultural competency is developing.
