Deep medullary vein drainage and unfavorable outcomes in stroke patients after endovascular therapy: a retrospective study
Authors: Jiating Wei, Tao Lu, Siyu Li, Jianle Li, Yuhua Fan, Jinsheng Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIschemic stroke due to middle cerebral artery (MCA) or internal carotid artery (ICA) occlusion often involves deep white matter, which drains through deep medullary veins (DMVs). Whether disruption of the DMVs drainage contributes to unfavorable outcomes following endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) remains unknown. This study aimed to investigate the association between DMVs continuity and post-EVT prognosis.MethodsIn this retrospective observational study from two stroke centers, we included stroke patients due to MCA or ICA occlusion who underwent EVT with successful recanalization between January 2023 and December 2024. The modified Rankin Scale (mRS) was recorded at 90 days. DMVs continuity was assessed on susceptibility weighted imaging (SWI) after EVT. Clinical data were analyzed using multivariable logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.ResultsA total of 61 patients was included (mean age: 65.4 ± 12.2 years; 63.9% male). Thirty patients (49.2%) achieved unfavorable outcomes (mRS 2–6). Compared to the favorable group, ipsilateral DMVs discontinuity was more frequent in the unfavorable group (70.0% vs. 41.9%, P = 0.040). DMV discontinuity remained independently associated with unfavorable outcomes post-EVT after adjusting for confounders (aOR = 3.23, 95% CI: 1.12–9.30, P = 0.030). ROC curve analysis indicated that the combined model of DMVs continuity status, baseline NIHSS score, and arterial collateral status showed good predictive value for prognosis post-EVT (AUC = 0.81, sensitivity = 73.3%, specificity = 80.6%).ConclusionsImpaired DMVs drainage is associated with unfavorable outcomes after EVT in ischemic stroke patients with MCA or ICA occlusion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04993-4.

Introduction

Endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) has been proven to significantly reduce mortality and improve outcome of stroke patients, and recommended as the standard treatment for acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion according to the latest guidelines for the early management of patients with acute ischemic stroke [1, 2]. However, there is growing concern that despite successful recanalization within the time window following EVT, approximately half of patients undergo unfavorable outcome at 3 months, a phenomenon called “futile recanalization” [3, 4]. The underlying mechanisms of futile recanalization have not been fully elucidated and may involve no-reflow, early arterial re-occlusion and hemorrhagic transformation [5].

Animal studies have shown that arterial collateral status is a crucial factor in regulating reperfusion and reducing the risk of futile recanalization after stroke [6, 7]. Clinical imaging analysis supports these findings, demonstrating that poor arterial collaterals predict unfavorable outcomes after EVT [3, 8, 9]. Veins are indispensable parts of the neurovascular network. Recent studies have suggested that reperfusion after EVT relies not only on arterial inflow but also on efficient venous outflow, with evidence showing that compromised cortical venous drainage is independently associated with futile recanalization [10, 11]. An integrated assessment of both arterial and venous collateral status shows potential for predicting futile recanalization following EVT [12, 13]. However, these findings focus only on cortical veins. Whereas cortical veins collect blood from the cerebral cortex and subcortical white matter, the DMVs are responsible for venous drainage from deep white matter [14]. Infarcts due to middle cerebral artery (MCA) or internal carotid artery (ICA) occlusion often involve deep white matter such as the corona radiata. Up to now, the role of the deep venous system, such as deep medullary veins (DMV), in prognosis for post-EVT has been overlooked.

Thus, in this study, we hypothesized that pathological changes in the DMVs might impair venous drainage of the deep white matter and disrupt cerebral circulation, thereby contributing to unfavorable outcome following EVT. We retrospectively analyzed DMV discontinuity on susceptibility weighted imaging (SWI) to investigate the its relationship with unfavorable outcome in stroke patients due to MCA or ICA occlusion following EVT.

Methods

Study and participants

This retrospective observational study was designed and conducted in compliance with the STROBE guidelines for observational research. We included stroke patients due to MCA or ICA occlusion who underwent EVT at two independent stroke centers between January 2023 and December 2024: The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (n = 49) and The Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (n = 12). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional review boards and performed in accordance with the ethical standards according to Declaration of Helsinki. Given its retrospective design and use of anonymized data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

Functional independence was measured by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) [15]. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) pre-stroke mRS score of 0; (3) acute ischemic stroke caused by stenosis or occlusion of the MCA or ICA; (4) received EVT within 24 h of symptom onset and achieved successful recanalization (defined as a Modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction [mTICI] grade of 2b or 3); (5) underwent SWI scan after recanalization; (6) completed mRS assessment at 90-day follow-up. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) presence of intracranial space-occupying lesions; (2) severe systemic complications, including heart failure, renal failure, hepatic failure, respiratory failure, or severe gastrointestinal bleeding; (3) severe artifacts on SWI that precluded image analysis.

Demographics and clinical variables

The following variables were collected: sex, hypertension (defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, a documented history of hypertension, or the use of antihypertensive medication), diabetes mellitus (defined as a fasting plasma glucose level ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, glycated hemoglobin ≥ 6.5%, a documented history of diabetes, or the use of antidiabetic medication), hyperlipidemia (defined as low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) ≥ 3.62 mmol/L, total cholesterol ≥ 5.69 mmol/L, triglycerides ≥ 1.69 mmol/L, a documented history of hyperlipidemia, or the use of lipid-lowering medication), stroke history, current smoking, vessel occlusion site, stroke etiology, stroke severity at admission treatment of intravenous thrombolysis, time from onset to recanalization, and degree of reperfusion assessed by mTICI. Stroke severity was assessed by the National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) [16]. The arterial collateral status was evaluated using the modified Tan scale based on CT angiography (CTA) before EVT, which defined collaterals as “good” if they are visible in ≥ 50% of the MCA territory, and as “poor” when involving in < 50% of the territory [17, 18]. The final infarct volume was measured using MRIcroGL software, and the presence of hemorrhagic transformation was assessed on SWI sequences.

Assessment of DMV visual grade

SWI scan was performed after EVT. The continuity of DMVs was assessed on SWI images as previously described, from the level of the ventricles immediately above the basal ganglia to the level of the ventricles immediately disappeared for each patient, as these slices encompassed the majority of the DMVs. A four-point scale was applied to assess the DMVs continuity: Grade 0 as each vein was continuous and had homogeneous signal; Grade 1 as each vein was continuous, but one or more than one vein had inhomogeneous signal; Grade 2 as one or more than one vein was not continuous, presented with spot-like hypointensity; Grade 3 as no observed vein was found continuous (Fig. 1) [19–21]. An increased score indicated greater disruption of DMVs. In this study, DMVs visual grade of 0–1 was classified as good DMVs continuity, whereas grade of 2–3 was considered as DMVs discontinuity. DMV visual grade was independently evaluated by two experienced neurologists who were blinded to patient outcomes. The inter-rater reliability for DMV grading showed substantial agreement, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.768 (95% CI: 0.607–0.929, P < 0.001).

Outcomes

The mRS score at 90 days post-EVT was assessed by a neurologist during a routine outpatient follow-up or by a trained nurse through telephone interview if the patients were unable to attend outpatient visits. In this study, a favorable outcome was defined as a mRS score of 0–1, whereas an unfavorable outcome was defined as a mRS score of 2–6 [22].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 software. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data, or as median with interquartile range for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Comparative analyses between the two groups were conducted using an independent t-test for data with normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney U test for skewed data, or the chi-square test for categorical variables. A two-step logistic regression analysis was performed to investigate the relationship between DMVs discontinuity and unfavorable 90-day outcomes, including variables with P < 0.05 in univariable analysis. Covariates included vascular risk factors, stroke history, vessel occlusion site, stroke etiology, baseline NIHSS score, intravenous thrombolysis, and poor collateral circulation. Receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive value of the models with DMVs discontinuity alone and DMVs discontinuity in combination with other factors for prognosis in stroke patients who underwent EVT. Comparison of ROC curves was performed using the DeLong test. The significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 61 patients with ischemic stroke due to MCA or ICA occlusion were included. All patients underwent EVT within 24 h of symptom onset and achieved successful recanalization. Demographics, clinical and imaging characters were summarized in the Table 1. The mean age of patients was 65.4 ± 12.2 years, and 39 (63.9%) were male. The proportions of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, and smoking were 72.1%, 34.4%, 39.3%, and 31.1% respectively. Large artery atherosclerosis was the most common stroke etiology, accounting for 54.1% of cases. The mean baseline NIHSS score was 11.6 ± 4.1. Sixteen patients (26.2%) receive intravenous thrombolysis prior to EVT. The median time from symptom onset to recanalization was 410 min (IQR: 241–847). Good arterial collaterals were observed in 20 patients (32.8%). Ipsilateral DMVs discontinuity was found in 34 patients (55.7%), and contralateral DMVs discontinuity was observed in 27 patients (44.3%). The median of time from symptom onset to SWI was 5 days (IQR: 4–7).

Of the 61 patients, 31 achieved favorable outcomes with a mRS score of 0–1, while the remaining 30 patients had unfavorable outcomes with a mRS score of 2–6. Compared with the group with favorable outcomes, the group with unfavorable outcomes exhibited significantly elevated baseline NIHSS scores (12.7 ± 4.3 vs. 10.5 ± 3.5, P = 0.030), a reduced proportion of good collateral circulation (16.7% vs. 48.4%, P = 0.008), and a higher rate of discontinuity of the ipsilateral DMVs (70.0% vs. 41.9%, P = 0.040, Table 1). Furthermore, compared with the group with favorable outcomes, the group with unfavorable outcomes had larger infarct volume (52.4 [37.0, 121.3] vs. 24.0 [6.2, 43.5], P < 0.001 ) and higher rates of hemorrhagic transformation (50.0% vs. 12.9%, P = 0.002) post-EVT (Table 1). No significant differences were observed between the two groups with respect to age, sex, vascular risk factors, prior stroke history, stroke etiology, vessel occlusion site, or the intravenous thrombolysis treatment (Table 1).

Association between DMVs discontinuity and unfavorable outcomes following EVT

A two-step logistic regression analysis was conducted to investigate the relationship between DMVs discontinuity and unfavorable 90-day outcomes. In the univariable logistic regression analysis, higher baseline NIHSS score (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.01–1.32, P = 0.034), poor arterial collaterals (OR = 4.69, 95% CI: 1.43–15.42, P = 0.011), and discontinuity of the ipsilateral DMVs (OR = 3.23, 95% CI: 1.12–9.30, P = 0.030) were identified as significant risk factors for unfavorable outcomes. In contrast, vascular risk factors (age, hypertension, diabetes and hyperlipidemia), prior stroke history, vessel occlusion site, stroke etiology, and intravenous thrombolysis treatment were not significantly associated with outcomes. Subsequently, variables with statistical significance in the univariable analysis, including baseline NIHSS score, arterial collateral status, and DMVs continuity, were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. The results also showed that a higher baseline NIHSS score (aOR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.00–1.34, P = 0.048), poor arterial collaterals (aOR = 4.71, 95% CI: 1.27–17.56, P = 0.021), and discontinuity of the ipsilateral DMVs (aOR = 3.78, 95% CI: 1.14–12.56, P = 0.030) remained independent predictors of unfavorable outcomes post-EVT (Table 2).

Furthermore, we found that compared with the patients with good DMVs continuity, patients with DMVs discontinuity had significantly larger infarct volume (31.4 [9.4, 46.3] vs. 47.2 [21.2, 113.3], P = 0.035), but rates of hemorrhagic transformation were not different between these two groups (35.3% vs. 25.9%, P = 0.58, Fig. 3). We proposed that DMVs discontinuity and disrupted vein drainage might promote infarct growth, ultimately leading to poor outcomes. To further elucidate this relationship, we performed a Bootstrap mediation analysis. Notably, we found that the effect of DMV discontinuity on unfavorable outcomes was mediated by increased infarct volume (indirect effect: 1.146, 95% BootCI: 0.242–2.975, Table 3).

ROC curve analysis

ROC curve analysis demonstrated that ipsilateral DMVs discontinuity, higher baseline NIHSS score, and poor arterial collaterals were each predictive of unfavorable 90-day outcomes after EVT in patients with acute ischemic stroke due to MCA or ICA occlusion, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.64 (95% CI: 0.50–0.78), 0.65 (95% CI: 0.51–0.79), and 0.66 (95% CI: 0.52–0.80), respectively. When DMVs continuity, baseline NIHSS score, and collateral status were combined in a multivariable ROC model, the AUC increased to 0.81 (95% CI: 0.69–0.92), outperforming any of the individual predictors (DeLong test, P = 0.008 vs. DMVs discontinuity; P = 0.017 vs. higher baseline NIHSS; P = 0.007 vs. poor arterial collaterals). At the optimal cutoff, the multivariable ROC model achieved a sensitivity of 73.3% and a specificity of 80.6%. Removal of DMVs continuity from the model resulted in a reduced AUC of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.61–0.86), although the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.107, Fig. 4).

Sensitive analysis

We performed sensitivity analysis using the conventional threshold of mRS 0–2 vs. 3–6. Notably, DMV discontinuity exhibited an elevated risk for poor prognosis (OR: 2.76 [0.89, 8.57]), albeit with a p-value of 0.078 (Supplemental Table 1). Although this did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the small sample size, the high odds ratio suggests a potential clinically relevant impact. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes are required to validate these findings and confirm the independent prognostic value of DMV status.

Discussion

This retrospective observational study explored the relationship between DMVs disruption and clinical outcomes in patients with ischemic stroke due to MCA or ICA occlusion who underwent EVT. Despite successful recanalization, approximately half of the patients experienced unfavorable outcomes at 90 days. Multivariable regression analysis identified DMV discontinuity as an independent predictor of unfavorable prognosis, even after adjusting for confounding factors. ROC curve analysis further supported its predictive. Taking together, these findings suggest that DMV continuity status is independently associated with post-EVT outcomes in ischemic stroke patients due to MCA or ICA occlusion.

Growing evidence indicates that even after prompt and effective recanalization through EVT, a substantial proportion of patients still experience unfavorable functional recovery at 90 days, a phenomenon known as “futile recanalization” [3, 4]. Previous stroke research has mainly focused on the arterial system, particularly the role of collateral circulation in maintaining perfusion to ischemic brain regions. Both animal studies and clinical research have demonstrated that poor arterial collaterals is one of the key determinants of futile recanalization following EVT [6–8]. In this study, we also observed a positive relationship between poor arterial collaterals and unfavorable outcomes post-EVT.

In recent years, increasing attention has been paid to the cerebral venous system. Rather than serving merely as a passive drainage network, cerebral veins play an active role in cerebral hemodynamics, homeostasis, microcirculation, and tissue oxygen metabolism [14]. It has been shown that impaired cortical venous outflow is independently associated with futile recanalization [10, 11]. Integrating both arterial and venous collateral assessments improves the predictive value of outcomes after EVT [12, 13]. Nevertheless, previous studies have largely concentrated on cortical veins, with limited attention paid to the potential contribution of the deep venous system, such as DMVs. Different from cortical veins which collect blood from the cerebral cortex and subcortical white matter, the DMVs are responsible for venous drainage from deep white matter, such as the corona radiata and internal capsule, which are often involved in stroke caused by MCA or ICA occlusion. DMVs disruption may lead to impaired venous drainage and microcirculatory failure in deep white matter, eventually contributing to futile recanalization. This study is the first to demonstrate that DMVs discontinuity, as an indicator of venous structural integrity disruption, is independently associated with unfavorable outcomes after EVT. It provides a novel perspective for assessing prognosis after EVT treatment. Recent studies have demonstrated that delayed cerebral circulation time assessed using 2D perfusion iFlow and the presence of no-reflow on CT perfusion after successful recanalization are associated with poor functional outcomes [23, 24]. Our study suggests that DMV discontinuity may represent a novel, clinically accessible radiological marker of this post-recanalization microcirculatory failure.

Previous studies have shown that despite full reperfusion, infarct growth is relatively frequent, and slower infarct growth rates post-EVT are associated with better outcome. As endovascular therapy aims to salvage the ischemic penumbra and arrest its conversion to a permanent infarct, the successful reperfusion’s effect on functional outcome was mediated through reduction in infarct growth and cerebral edema [25–28]. Consistent with previous studies, our data showed that compared with patients with favorable outcomes, patients with unfavorable outcomes had significantly larger post-EVT infarct volumes and higher rates of hemorrhagic transformation. Also, compared with the patients with good DMV continuity, patients with DMV discontinuity had significantly larger infarct volume, while rates of hemorrhagic transformation were not significantly different between two groups. Mediation analysis demonstrated that the effect of DMV discontinuity on unfavorable outcomes was mediated by increased infarct volume. These findings suggest that impaired DMV drainage is associated with expanded infarct volume and unfavorable outcome post-EVT. We hypothesize that by exacerbating venous congestion and microcirculatory failure, DMV disruption promotes the conversion of salvageable tissue into a necrotic core, which ultimately dictates the poor functional outcome. However, because DMV status and final infarct volume were assessed during the same subacute phase after stroke (median 5 days), temporal causality cannot be clearly established. It is possible that DMV discontinuity reflects tissue necrosis secondary to the initial stroke and DMV discontinuity might also serve as a surrogate for infarct severity or cerebral edema. In addition, our analysis showed that the rate of hemorrhagic transformation was numerically higher in patients with DMV discontinuity compared to those with good DMV continuity, although this difference did not reach formal statistical significance. This result suggested that impaired venous drainage might promote hemorrhagic transformation. Future prospective studies are warranted to further elucidate the intricate relationship between DMV status and the extent of tissue injury or edema.

Previous studies have reported that in patients with acute ischemic stroke due to MCA stenosis or occlusion, the ipsilateral DMVs may appear increased in number and diameter than the contralateral side, a phenomenon referred to as prominent DMVs, asymmetric DMVs or “brush sign”. This sign is considered an indicator of insufficient collateral flow, with proposed mechanisms including venous stasis-induced small vein dilation, and elevated oxygen extraction in hypoperfused regions, which increases the proportion of deoxygenated hemoglobin. The “brush sign” is associated with poor prognosis in acute ischemic stroke [21, 29–32]. However, in our study, patients did not exhibit the ipsilateral DMVs brush sign. This might be attributed to two main factors. First, the SWI scans in this study were performed at a median of 5 days after symptom onset, which was significantly later than the ≤ 24-hour imaging window used in previous studies [21, 29]. Prior research has shown that the brush sign may gradually diminish over time [33]. Second, all SWI images in this study were acquired after successful recanalization through EVT, whereas earlier studies typically obtained imaging before recanalization [31, 32]. The accumulation of deoxygenated hemoglobin within occluded veins, which enhances SWI signal, is more likely to occur under conditions of artery occlusion. In contrast, since all patients in this study underwent SWI after successful recanalization, the ipsilateral DMV brush sign was not observed. Therefore, the observed discontinuity of DMVs in this study may reflect structural venous damage or fibrosis rather than purely hemodynamic alterations.

It has been reported that advanced age, hypertension, high admission glucose levels, prolonged time from symptom onset to admission and elevated baseline NIHSS score have been identified as risk factors for futile recanalization [3, 34–36]. However, this study did not find significant associations between vascular risk factors, stroke history, intravenous thrombolysis, or time from symptom onset to recanalization and unfavorable outcomes, which is inconsistent with some previous studies. This discrepancy may be attributed to the relatively small sample size of the current study, as well as variations in the definition of unfavorable outcomes across studies (e.g., mRS ≥ 2 or ≥ 3).

There were several limitations in this study. First, it was a retrospective observational analysis involving only two centers, with a relatively small sample size. Second, in clinical practice, patients with severe neurological deterioration, physiological instability, or life-threatening complications, such as symptomatic hemorrhagic transformation, were often unable to tolerate the prolonged duration of an MRI scan (including SWI imaging) post-EVT. Out of strict adherence to patient safety and ethical standards, these critically ill patients were naturally excluded from the study. Therefore, this may have introduced selection bias, and affected the generalizability of our findings. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes are required to validate these findings and confirm the independent prognostic value of DMV status.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this cross-sectional study hints that impaired DMVs drainage is associated with unfavorable outcomes after EVT in ischemic stroke patients with MCA or ICA occlusion. Future studies with prospective design, larger sample sizes, and multicenter participation are warranted to further validate the predictive value of DMVs for prognosis in patients with ischemic stroke following EVT.
