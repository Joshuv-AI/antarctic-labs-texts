Genome-wide insights into genetic diversity and selection signatures during the formation of Yanyun sheep
Authors: Pengkun Yang, Zhixu Pang, Changsong Xu, Quanhong Yao, Zhongfa Wang, Yangyang Pan, Kaijie Yang, Liying Qiao, Wenzhong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundYanyun (YY) sheep are a composite breed developed by targeted crossbreeding of Dorper, Hu, and East Friesian sheep to improve growth, reproduction, and environmental adaptability, yet the genomic basis underlying their formation and selective improvement remains unclear.ResultsWhole-genome analyses showed that YY sheep retain moderate genetic diversity with low genomic inbreeding (FROH), indicating limited autozygosity. Population structure analyses supported that YY sheep form a genetically distinct yet admixed group consistent with their breeding history. Genome-wide selection scans identified multiple candidate regions associated with growth, metabolism, reproduction, immune function, and environmental adaptability, including loci showing consistent differentiation and haplotype-based selection signals across multiple population comparisons.ConclusionsThese results provide a genome-wide view of the genetic diversity, population structure, and selection landscape of YY sheep, and identify candidate genomic regions for future investigation of economically important and adaptive traits in composite sheep breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12901-w.

Introduction

Excellent livestock and poultry breeds are core elements supporting the sustainable development of modern animal husbandry [1]. With continuous growth in the global demand for high-quality livestock products, the mutton industry faces new opportunities and challenges [2]. Traditional mutton breeds often do not fully meet the requirements of rapid growth, high reproductive efficiency, and strong environmental adaptability in intensive breeding models [3]. Therefore, it is of great theoretical and industrial value to cultivate new, high-quality mutton sheep breeds that are adapted to specific ecological and breeding conditions.

In mutton sheep breeding, excellent traits of different breeds provide key genetic resources for cultivating new breeds. For example, Dorper sheep are famous for their excellent growth rate and meat quality, but their reproduction rate is relatively low [4]; Hu sheep, a prolific sheep breed native to China, are known for their strong environmental adaptability and high reproductive performance [5], but their meat production performance needs to be improved, whereas East Friesian sheep are a highly productive dairy breed [6]. Based on the complementary traits of the parental breeds, Yanyun (YY) sheep were obtained by hybridizing Dorper and Hu sheep to obtain the F1 generation, which was triple-hybridized with East Friesian sheep and bred for many years. YY sheep successfully integrated the advantages of their parents, showing characteristics such as early maturity, rapid growth, excellent meat quality, and low dystocia rate, which significantly improved the efficiency of mutton production and the economic benefits of breeding.

Although YY sheep have shown many excellent traits in production practices, their genetic basis has not yet been systematically analyzed. With the development of high-throughput sequencing technology, whole-genome resequencing has become an important method for studying the genetic structure, population evolution, and selection signals of livestock [7]. This technology can accurately capture single-nucleotide polymorphisms and other variation information on a whole-genome scale, which helps elucidate the genetic diversity, genetic relatedness, and formation process of the population [8]. Furthermore, through the selective scanning method combined with population comparative analysis, it is possible to identify gene regions that have been selected during natural selection or artificial breeding [9], revealing the underlying biological functions, helping to discover candidate genes, and carrying out molecular marker-assisted breeding.

This study systematically evaluated the genetic diversity, population structure, and genomic relatedness of YY sheep through comprehensive whole-genome resequencing analysis, integrating genomic data from YY sheep, their parent breeds, and additional Chinese and international sheep breeds. Furthermore, to elucidate the genetic divergence of YY sheep from one of its primary local parental breeds, we performed selective sweep analyses comparing YY sheep with Hu sheep and representative Chinese native breeds. This comparison allows for the identification of genomic regions responsible for the retained environmental adaptability and the novel economic traits introduced during the composite breeding process. This study not only provides an important molecular basis for the breeding and promotion of YY sheep but also provides theoretical support and a practical reference for the genetic improvement and resource utilization of meat sheep in China.

Materials and methods

DNA sample preparation and SNP genotyping

Blood samples were collected from 43 YY sheep, 32 Hu sheep, and 4 East Friesian sheep at the Genyuan Animal Husbandry Company in Shanxi Province. Genomic DNA was extracted using a CWE9600 Magbead Blood DNA Kit (Beijing Kangwei Century Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a Qubit fluorometer and NanoDrop spectrophotometer (OD260/280 = 1.7–2.1), and integrity was verified by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis. Sequencing libraries with an average insert size of ~ 300 bp were constructed and sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform using paired-end 2 × 150 bp reads.

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanxi Agricultural University (Approval No. SXAU-EAW-2022G.WX.0116211).

Data collection and preprocessing

In total, 79 newly sequenced individuals were included (43 YY, 32 Hu, and 4 East Friesian). Additionally, whole-genome resequencing data from 99 individuals representing 13 sheep populations were obtained from public databases [10–13] (Table S1). Public datasets were originally generated on Illumina HiSeq X Ten platforms. A total of eight East Friesian (EF) sheep were included in this study, comprising four newly sequenced individuals and four publicly available whole-genome resequencing datasets retrieved from the NCBI SRA database. These public datasets were selected based on sequencing depth and data quality to ensure comparability with the newly generated data.

To minimize potential platform-related batch effects, all raw sequencing reads (both newly generated and public) were reprocessed from FASTQ files using a unified bioinformatic pipeline, including identical quality control, alignment to the same reference genome (Oar_rambouillet_v1.0), and consistent variant calling and filtering procedures.

Genotype data quality control

Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control using fastp (v0.23.4) [14] to obtain clean high-quality data for downstream analysis. The software was run with parameters -n 1 -q 5 -u 50 -l 100. Specifically, reads containing > 1% ambiguous bases (N), reads with adapter contamination, reads in which more than 50% of bases had a Phred quality score ≤ 5, and reads shorter than 100 bp after filtering were removed. Clean reads were then aligned to the reference genome (Ovis aries, https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/all/GCF/002/742/125/GCF_002742125.1_Oar_rambouillet_v1.0/GCF_002742125.1_Oar_rambouillet_v1.0_genomic.gff.gz) using BWA (v0.7.17-r1188) [15].

SNP quality control was performed using PLINK (v1.9) [16], retaining only autosomal SNPs. Variants were filtered using a call rate > 90% and a minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.05. The 90% call rate threshold was adopted to balance stringency and marker retention across datasets generated from different sequencing platforms, thereby minimizing excessive marker loss due to platform-specific missingness. Given the high average sequencing depth and subsequent quality control procedures, this threshold was considered sufficient to ensure robust downstream analyses. These filtering criteria were selected according to established standards in livestock whole-genome resequencing studies [11] to ensure reproducibility and analytical consistency. For specific population genomic analyses, additional linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning was applied to further reduce the potential impact of missing genotypes and marker redundancy.

Population structure and genetic diversity analysis

To investigate the genetic relationships between YY sheep and 14 other sheep breeds, SNPs in strong linkage disequilibrium (LD) were pruned using PLINK v1.9 [16] with the parameter --indep-pairwise 50 10 0.2. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to evaluate genetic differentiation among breeds. The ancestral population structure was inferred using ADMIXTURE v1.30 [17], with the number of ancestral populations (K) ranging from 2 to 14. To determine the optimal K value, cross-validation (CV) was conducted using the --cv option, and the CV error for each K was recorded. The optimal K was selected based on both the minimum CV error and biological interpretability.

Within-breed genetic diversity indices, including observed heterozygosity (Ho) and expected heterozygosity (He), were calculated for all breeds using PLINK v1.9 [16] to enable comparative assessment among YY sheep and its parental populations. Pairwise genetic distances were computed based on the genetic distance matrix. To evaluate relatedness structure within the YY population, pairwise identity-by-state (IBS) distances were calculated using PLINK v1.9 [16] with the --mds-plot 4 option. Close relatives were identified using KING software with the parameters --kinship --degree 2, and a kinship threshold of 0.125 was applied to define second-degree relatives. Individuals identified as close relatives were removed prior to downstream analyses to avoid bias in population genetic inference. The genomic relationship matrix (GRM) was subsequently constructed using GCTA v1.94.1 [18] to quantify pairwise genomic relatedness among the remaining unrelated individuals.

ROH detection and genomic inbreeding analysis

Runs of homozygosity (ROH) were detected using PLINK v1.9 [16] with the following parameters: --homozyg-window-snp 50, --homozyg-snp 100, --homozyg-kb 1000, --homozyg-gap 1000, --homozyg-density 50, and --homozyg-window-het 1. A minimum ROH length of 1 Mb was applied to mitigate the impact of background linkage disequilibrium on ROH detection. The genomic inbreeding coefficient based on ROH (FROH) was calculated as the ratio of the total length of all detected ROH segments to the total length of autosomal chromosomes (1–26) in the Oar_rambouillet_v1.0 reference assembly (2,655,680 kb). To characterize the temporal patterns of inbreeding, ROH segments were further categorized into four length classes (1–4 Mb, 4–8 Mb, 8–16 Mb, and > 16 Mb), which are commonly interpreted as reflecting older, intermediate, and more recent inbreeding signals. Additionally, the total number of ROH segments and the average ROH length per individual were quantified to describe ROH structure. These ROH calling parameters and length-class definitions were chosen following commonly used guidelines in livestock population genomic studies [19, 20]. In addition, class-specific FROH values were derived to summarize the contribution of different ROH length classes to total genomic inbreeding. To evaluate the consistency between different genomic estimates of inbreeding, the correlation between FROH and FGRM was assessed in YY sheep using Pearson’s correlation coefficient, where FGRM was derived from the diagonal elements of the genomic relationship matrix after subtracting 1.

Selection signature detection and origin analysis

To identify genomic signatures of artificial selection in Yanyun (YY) sheep, multiple complementary approaches were jointly applied to detect selective sweeps across the genome. Intrapopulation selection scans were conducted within the YY population using composite likelihood ratio (CLR), Tajima’s D, and nucleotide diversity (θπ) statistics. Tajima’s D and θπ were estimated using VCFtools (v0.1.13) [21] under a sliding-window framework with a window size of 50 kb and a step size of 10 kb. Genomic regions showing reduced genetic diversity and deviations from neutrality were defined as candidate selective sweeps by selecting the top 1% of the −log10(θπ) distribution and the lowest 1% of Tajima’s D values, respectively. CLR statistics were calculated using SweeD v4.0.0 [22] with a 50 kb window to capture shifts in the site frequency spectrum toward high-frequency derived alleles, and windows within the top 1% of CLR values were retained as putative targets of selection.

To distinguish breeding-related selection signals in YY sheep from ancestral or background variation, pairwise population comparisons were performed between YY sheep and four reference populations: Hu (HU) sheep and three representative Chinese indigenous breeds (DLS, SXW, and WDS). Genetic differentiation and selective sweeps between populations were evaluated using FST, θπ ratio, and cross-population extended haplotype homozygosity (XP-EHH). For XP-EHH analysis, haplotypes were phased using Beagle v5.2 [23], a population-based phasing algorithm suitable for moderate-coverage whole-genome resequencing data, and XP-EHH scores were calculated with selscan v2.1.1 [24]. A sliding-window approach with a window size of 50 kb and a step size of 10 kb was applied to obtain genome-wide statistics. The mean XP-EHH value within each window was calculated, and windows within the top 1% of the empirical distribution were defined as candidate selective regions.

To enhance robustness and reduce false positives, only genomic windows concurrently identified by at least two independent methods under the top 1% threshold were retained as high-confidence signals. Concordance of signals across multiple pairwise comparisons (YY vs. HU, YY vs. DLS, YY vs. SXW, and YY vs. WDS) was used to further assess the stability and reproducibility of candidate regions. Genes located within these regions were annotated using Bedtools v2.25.0 [25] for downstream analyses.

Identification and functional annotation of candidate genes

The detected genomic regions were mapped to the sheep gene annotation file in the NCBI database for gene annotation (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_002742125.1/). To analyze the functions of the target genes, we used DAVID [26] online software to perform Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis, in which we retained functional entries with P < 0.05.

Results

Genomic data landscapes and quality assessment

To minimize potential batch effects between newly sequenced and public datasets, we implemented a unified bioinformatic pipeline for all 178 sheep individuals. After stringent quality control and alignment to the Oar_rambouillet_v1.0 reference genome, variants were called using a unified pipeline. The overall average sequencing depth across all samples was 15.73×, with newly generated samples showing a mean depth of 11.49×. The average genome coverage reached 97.60% at ≥ 1× and 75.22% at ≥ 10× depth, indicating sufficient sequencing quality for reliable variant detection. (Table S1).

Following the removal of close relatives identified by KING (kinship > 0.125) and applying strict filtering criteria (call rate > 90%, MAF ≥ 0.05), a final set of 15,802,592 high-confidence autosomal SNPs was retained. These markers exhibited a uniform distribution across the 26 autosomes, providing sufficient resolution for subsequent population structure and selection signature analyses.

Genetic diversity

Whole-genome resequencing initially identified 25,350,601 raw single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) across the YY population prior to quality filtering. After stringent quality control and filtering procedures, 15,802,592 high-confidence autosomal SNPs were retained for downstream analyses, and these SNPs were evenly distributed across all autosomes (Fig. 1A). After quality control, the average observed heterozygosity (Ho) and expected heterozygosity (He) of YY sheep were 0.306 and 0.303, respectively, indicating a moderate level of genetic variation. Compared with its parental and commercial breeds, YY sheep exhibited intermediate heterozygosity levels, being higher than Hu sheep (Ho = 0.293; He = 0.301) but slightly lower than East Friesian (Ho = 0.316; He = 0.331) and Dorper sheep (Ho = 0.324; He = 0.324), suggesting that composite breeding has preserved genetic diversity without substantial erosion of heterozygosity.

Minor allele frequency (MAF) analysis revealed a broad distribution (0.010–0.500), with low-frequency variants (0.0–0.1) accounting for the largest proportion (34.8%) (Fig. 1B). Pairwise identity-by-state (IBS) distances ranged from 0.197 to 0.335 (mean 0.296 ± 0.0211; Fig. 1C). After removing close relatives identified by KING analysis, genomic relationship matrix (GRM) analysis showed that the mean pairwise genomic relatedness within the YY population was − 0.024 (range: −0.107 to 0.362). Although a small proportion of pairwise comparisons (7.97%) exceeded the kinship threshold of 0.125, the overall relatedness was low at the population level, indicating that close relatedness was limited at the population level (Fig. 1D).

ROH and genomic inbreeding

The genomic inbreeding coefficient (FROH) differed markedly among breeds (Fig. 2A). YY sheep exhibited a substantially lower mean FROH value (0.00497 ± 0.0161) than Hu (0.00945), East Friesian (0.06709), and Dorper sheep (0.08570). Although the standard deviation was relatively large relative to the mean, the distribution of FROH in YY sheep was strongly right-skewed. The median FROH was 0.000706 (IQR = 0.00164), and 93.0% of individuals displayed FROH values below 0.02, indicating generally low levels of genomic inbreeding in YY sheep. The observed variance was mainly driven by a small subset of individuals with elevated FROH values (maximum = 0.0977).

To further characterize the structure of autozygosity in YY sheep, we summarized the contribution of different ROH length classes to genomic inbreeding using class-specific FROH values (Table S2). Intermediate-length ROH segments (4–16 Mb) accounted for 84.7% of total FROH, whereas short ROH segments (1–4 Mb) and long ROH segments (> 16 Mb) contributed 3.1% and 12.2%, respectively. This pattern suggests that most autozygosity in YY sheep reflects older or intermediate relatedness rather than widespread recent inbreeding. Nevertheless, a subset of individuals exhibited elevated ROH burden and higher FROH values, indicating inter-individual heterogeneity that should be interpreted with caution.

Consistent with this interpretation, the distribution of ROH segments at the individual level (Fig. 2B) showed that although some individuals carried relatively high numbers of ROH segments, these were predominantly short in length. No widespread accumulation of long ROH segments was observed, further supporting the interpretation that recent inbreeding appears limited at the population level. In addition, FROH was positively correlated with FGRM in YY sheep (Pearson’s r = 0.637, p = 4.40 × 10⁻⁶; Figure S1), indicating general consistency between different genomic estimates of inbreeding.

Population structure analysis

To investigate genetic relationships among YY sheep and other breeds, genome-wide SNP data from 15 sheep populations representing diverse geographic origins were analyzed (Fig. 3A). After linkage disequilibrium pruning, 920,490 autosomal SNPs were retained for population structure analyses. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear genetic differentiation among populations. The first three principal components explained 4.17%, 2.41%, and 2.41% of the total genetic variance, respectively (Fig. 3B). Chinese native sheep populations, including YY sheep, were largely separated from non-Chinese populations along the major principal components. YY sheep occupied an intermediate position between Chinese indigenous breeds and European commercial breeds in the PCA space.

Ancestry inference using ADMIXTURE showed that YY sheep exhibited a composite genetic background. Cross-validation analysis demonstrated that the CV error decreased from K = 1 to K = 4, reaching its minimum at K = 4 (CV = 0.54886), with only a marginal difference compared to K = 3 (CV = 0.55095) (Supplementary Figure S2). Although K = 4 provided the lowest CV error, the improvement relative to K = 3 was minimal, suggesting limited improvement in model fit. Inspection of the ancestry patterns indicated that the additional component at K = 4 did not substantially alter the inferred ancestry composition of YY sheep relative to its major parental or reference populations but instead partitioned existing ancestry components into finer substructure in other breeds. Therefore, considering both statistical support and biological interpretability, K = 3 was selected for downstream interpretation. At K = 3, individuals from the YY population displayed mixed ancestry components corresponding to their parental breeds (Fig. 4A).

Within the Chinese sheep cluster, YY sheep showed a shifted position toward Dorper and East Friesian sheep (Fig. 4B). Selective signal analysis

To identify genomic regions under selection within the YY population, Tajima’s D, nucleotide diversity (θπ), and composite likelihood ratio (CLR) analyses were conducted across the genome. Tajima’s D analysis identified 280 candidate regions within the lowest 1% of the genome-wide distribution, encompassing 347 annotated genes (Table S3). The strongest signals were detected on chromosomes 1, 3, and 17 (Fig. 5A). These regions harbored genes such as IGF2BP2 [27], ORMDL2 [28], KNTC1 [29], and SARNP, which have been previously reported to be involved in reproductive regulation, lipid metabolism, and cell cycle control, respectively. θπ analysis identified 234 genomic windows within the top 1% of −log10(θπ) values, corresponding to 102 annotated genes (Fig. 5B; Table S4). The most prominent peaks were located on chromosomes 1, 11, and 20 and included genes such as DPYD [30], TANC2 [31], and GFRAL [32], which have been reported to participate in immune-related processes, lipid metabolism, and energy homeostasis. CLR analysis identified 236 candidate regions in the top 1% of CLR values, covering 312 annotated genes (Fig. 5C; Table S5). Among the regions showing the strongest CLR signals were loci containing UHMK1 [33], ZNF746 [34], and WDR36 [35], genes previously associated with cell proliferation, apoptosis, and early developmental processes.

A total of 78 genes were detected by at least two of the three methods (Tajima’s D, θπ, and CLR) (Fig. 5E). Functional enrichment analysis of these overlapping genes revealed significant enrichment in Gene Ontology (GO) terms related to multicellular organism growth, protein binding, protein homodimerization activity, and chromatin organization (Fig. 5D; Table S6).

Comparative selection scans between YY sheep and Hu sheep (HU) were performed using fixation index (FST), θπ ratio, and XP-EHH analyses. A total of 51 candidate genes were consistently identified by all three methods and were distributed across multiple autosomes (Fig. 6E).

The FST analysis identified 1,488 candidate regions encompassing 631 annotated genes (Table S7). The most pronounced differentiation signals were detected on chromosomes 1, 7, and 16 (Fig. 5A). These regions harbored genes such as LRP8 [36] and CARD6 [37], which have been reported to participate in immune regulation, as well as genes associated with reproductive and developmental processes, including SPTLC2 [38] and RPL37 [39]. In addition, candidate genes related to metabolic and environmental responsiveness (PRKAA1) [40] and angiogenesis (ISM2) [41] were also detected among the most differentiated regions. The θπ ratio analysis identified 1,403 candidate regions covering 612 genes (Table S8). The strongest signals were located on chromosomes 3, 18, and 25 (Fig. 6B). These regions included genes such as ORMDL2 [42] and SARNP [43], which have been reported to be involved in lipid metabolism and cellular regulatory processes, as well as genes associated with wool and pigmentation traits (RCOR1) [32] and skeletal development (KCNMA1) [44]. XP-EHH analysis detected 1,546 candidate regions encompassing 572 genes (Table S9), with the strongest extended haplotype homozygosity signals observed on chromosomes 7, 14, and 20 (Fig. 6C). Highly differentiated regions harbored genes related to skeletal development (IGSF23) [45], immune modulation (PVR) [46], sperm migration and maturation (NQO2) [47], and reproductive processes (DPP8) [48].

Functional enrichment analysis of these candidate genes revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to growth and skeletal development, energy metabolism, reproductive function, immune regulation, and environmental responsiveness (Fig. 6D) (Table S10).

The genome-wide distributions for these independent contrasts are shown in Supplementary Figure S3. Among the candidate regions identified in these comparisons, a region on chromosome 1 containing LRP8 was repeatedly detected within the top 1% candidate intervals across multiple pairwise contrasts (Fig. 7E), indicating consistent differentiation signals across independent population comparisons.

Regional population genetic analyses further identified a candidate sweep signal within a ~ 0.081 Mb interval (29.38–29.46 Mb) on chromosome 1 encompassing LRP8. This region showed reduced θπ and negative Tajima’s D values in YY sheep relative to comparison populations, together with elevated FST and XP-EHH signals (Figs. 7A–D), suggesting that it may have contributed to the genomic differentiation of YY sheep during breed formation. Variant annotation within this interval identified a nonsynonymous substitution in exon 5 of LRP8 (c.G520A; p.G174R). Allele frequency analysis at Chr1:29,414,639 showed population differences, with the alternative allele occurring at low frequency in YY sheep (AF = 0.047) and remaining polymorphic in HU, WDS, DLS, and SXW populations (Figs. 7F–G). These results support population-specific differentiation at this representative candidate region, although functional validation is still required.

Discussion

Whole-genome resequencing of Yanyun (YY) sheep provides an integrated view of how targeted introgression and artificial selection have shaped the genomic architecture of a modern composite breed. By combining population structure analyses with complementary selection scans, this study demonstrates that recent selection in YY sheep has acted on multiple genomic regions associated with growth, metabolism, reproduction, immune function, and environmental adaptability, reflecting the multifaceted objectives of contemporary sheep breeding.

Despite sustained artificial selection, YY sheep retain moderate heterozygosity and a low FROH, indicating that their breeding strategy did not result in excessive erosion of genetic diversity. This pattern contrasts with that reported for some intensively selected commercial breeds and suggests that controlled introgression and population expansion effectively limited the accumulation of autozygosity, thereby preserving long-term improvement potential.

Population structure analyses further revealed that YY sheep occupy an intermediate genetic position between Chinese indigenous breeds and European commercial breeds, consistent with their documented breeding history. The composite ancestry pattern observed reflects targeted introgression from Dorper and East Friesian sheep rather than uncontrolled admixture, facilitating the incorporation of production-enhancing alleles while retaining adaptive genetic components inherited from Hu sheep.

Genome-wide selection scans indicate that genetic improvement in YY sheep has been driven by coordinated selection acting on interconnected biological systems rather than isolated loci. A prominent group of candidate genes is associated with growth and skeletal development. Genes such as HMGA2 [49], IGSF23 [45], and SAR1B [50] have been repeatedly implicated in body size regulation and tissue development across livestock species. In particular, HMGA2 is a well-established regulator of somatic growth and has been associated with body size variation in multiple sheep populations [51], suggesting that selection on this locus contributed to enhanced growth performance in YY sheep.

Selection signals also highlighted genes involved in energy metabolism and metabolic regulation, including PRKAA1 and PPRC1. PRKAA1 encodes the catalytic subunit of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) [40], a key energy sensor that has been repeatedly implicated in metabolic regulation and adaptive responses to hypoxic or nutrient-limited conditions in sheep and other ruminants [52, 53]. PPRC1, a transcriptional coactivator linked to mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism, further points to selection pressures acting on cellular energy production and metabolic homeostasis [54]. Efficient metabolic regulation is fundamental for sustaining rapid growth and high reproductive output in modern production systems [55]. The identification of genes involved in cellular energy sensing and mitochondrial function suggests that artificial selection in YY sheep may have acted on pathways related to feed utilization efficiency and energy allocation. Such coordinated regulations of metabolic and growth-related pathways are consistent with the breeding objectives of composite meat sheep.

Reproductive performance, a defining characteristic of YY sheep, also emerged as a major target of selection. Candidate genes such as DMC1 [56], MAP1LC3A [57], LEPR [58], and BRIP1 [59] are involved in meiosis, follicular development, and reproductive hormone signaling. DMC1 is essential for homologous recombination during meiosis, in sheep and other ruminants. Proper meiotic recombination is fundamental for maintaining reproductive efficiency and genetic stability across generations [60]. while MAP1LC3A plays a key role in autophagy-mediated cellular quality control during gametogenesis. Selection on these loci likely contributed to reproductive stability and efficiency in YY sheep.

Immune regulation and environmental adaptability represent additional dimensions of selection. Several candidate genes, including LRP8 [36], CARD6 [37], ITCH [61], and PVR [46], are involved in immune signaling, inflammatory regulation, and stress responses. CARD6 participates in NF-κB–mediated immune pathways, while ITCH functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase maintaining immune homeostasis [62]. Selection on these genes suggests that breeding efforts preserved genetic components underlying disease resistance and physiological resilience.

Among the candidate regions, the region containing LRP8 was repeatedly detected across multiple selection statistics and independent population comparisons, suggesting population-specific differentiation during the formation of YY sheep. Combined evidence from reduced nucleotide diversity, negative Tajima’s D values, elevated interpopulation differentiation, and haplotype-based signals supports the view that this region may have been involved in the genomic divergence of YY sheep. In addition, a nonsynonymous variant in exon 5 of LRP8 showed allele-frequency differences between YY sheep and comparison populations, further indicating differentiation at this locus. Previous studies have implicated LRP8 in lipid metabolism, energy-related processes, and other physiological functions in mammals [36, 63]. However, the biological effect of this variant in sheep remains unclear, and functional validation will be required before drawing conclusions about its contribution to economically important or adaptive traits. Therefore, LRP8 is best regarded as one representative candidate locus within the broader selection landscape identified in YY sheep, rather than as an isolated determinant of breed improvement.

Nevertheless, the functional consequences of the identified nonsynonymous substitution (p.G174R) remain to be experimentally validated. Future studies employing genotype–phenotype association analyses, gene expression profiling in relevant tissues, and functional assays such as CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing or in vitro overexpression experiments will be essential to determine the biological impact of this variant on metabolic regulation and reproductive performance.

Finally, although genome-wide selection scans are effective for identifying candidate regions under selection, they cannot by themselves establish causal relationships between genetic variants and phenotypic traits. The empirical top 1% threshold used in this study may not completely eliminate false positives, and the relatively limited sample size for certain breeds, particularly East Friesian sheep, may reduce statistical power in allele frequency estimation and haplotype-based analyses. To mitigate this potential bias, candidate regions were required to be supported by at least two independent statistics and were further validated across multiple pairwise comparisons. Although EF was not used as the sole reference population and the main conclusions were supported by multiple independent comparisons, future studies incorporating expanded sampling of EF and other commercial breeds will be important to further validate the stability of the detected selection signals. Integration of larger population datasets, functional genomics approaches, and experimental validation will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the genetic architecture underlying growth performance, reproduction, and environmental adaptability in YY sheep.

Conclusion

In conclusion, whole-genome resequencing revealed that YY sheep maintain moderate genomic diversity with low FROH and a composite genetic background shaped by introgression from Dorper, Hu, and East Friesian sheep. Multi-statistic selection scans further characterized the selection landscape of YY sheep and identified candidate genomic regions associated with growth, metabolism, reproduction, immune function, and environmental adaptability. These findings provide a valuable genome-wide resource for understanding the formation of YY sheep and for supporting future genetic improvement of composite meat sheep in China.
