Atherogenic index of plasma and cognitive impairment risk among hypertensive adults: evidence from a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Weimin Bai, Bing Feng, Yaowen Li, Raoman Feng, Lijie Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive impairment is a major complication in individuals with hypertension. Emerging evidence suggests that metabolic abnormalities may contribute to its pathogenesis. This study examined the association between the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) and risk of cognitive impairment in middle-aged and older adults with hypertension.MethodsData were from the 2011 baseline and 2018 follow-up waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess the association between baseline AIP and subsequent cognitive impairment. Dose-response patterns were explored using restricted cubic spline (RCS) models, and subgroup analyses evaluated effect modification. Predictive performance of AIP versus traditional lipids was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.ResultsAmong 3,739 hypertensive participants (mean age: 60.5 ± 9.1 years; 50.0% female), 43.0% developed cognitive impairment. Compared with quartile 1 (Q1), higher AIP quartiles were significantly associated with increased risk of cognitive impairment: Q2 (odds ratio [OR], 1.44; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.15–1.80), Q3 (OR, 1.76; 95% CI, 1.40–2.20), and Q4 (OR, 1.85; 95% CI, 1.45–2.37). RCS indicated a linear dose-response (P for nonlinearity = 0.061). Subgroup analyses showed consistent associations across age, gender, body mass index, residence, and diabetes, with no significant interactions detected. AIP outperformed traditional lipid markers in predicting cognitive impairment.ConclusionElevated AIP was independently associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment among middle-aged and older adults with hypertension, suggesting its potential as a practical biomarker for early identification of cognitive risk in this high-risk population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03290-3.Keywords: Atherogenic index of plasma, Hypertension, Cognitive impairment, CHARLS, Risk factors

Introduction

Cognitive impairment represents a major contributor to functional decline among middle-aged and older adults and has become a growing global public health concern [1, 2]. It is primarily characterized by deficits in multiple cognitive domains, including memory, comprehension, attention, and calculation, and is widely recognized as a prodromal stage of dementia [1, 2]. Given the absence of effective treatments, early identification of individuals at elevated risk and the implementation of timely interventions are critical for delaying disease progression and improving long-term outcomes [1, 3, 4].

Hypertension is one of the most prevalent chronic conditions in aging populations. Despite its high prevalence, treatment and control rates remain suboptimal. A robust body of literature has demonstrated a strong association between hypertension and cognitive decline [5, 6]. Notably, hypertension beginning in midlife has been shown to exert more sustained adverse effects on brain structure and function compared to late-onset hypertension [7, 8]. However, existing research has largely focused on the presence and duration of hypertension, while giving limited attention to concomitant metabolic disturbances such as dyslipidemia, which may modulate the risk of cognitive impairment. In this context, identifying lipid-related biomarkers associated with cognitive dysfunction may facilitate early screening and enable preventive strategies tailored to individuals with hypertension.

Recent evidence suggests a significant relationship between dyslipidemia and cognitive decline. Traditional lipid metrics, such as total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), have shown modest associations with cognitive outcomes [9–11]. However, these conventional measures provide an incomplete assessment of lipid metabolism and are limited in their predictive capacity. The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), calculated as the base-10 logarithm of the TG-to-HDL-C ratio, has been proposed as a more sensitive indicator of lipid metabolic imbalance [12, 13]. AIP has been independently associated with multiple metabolic disorders, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome [14, 15]. Although one prior study has reported an association between AIP and cognitive impairment in the general population, it remains unclear whether this relationship extends to individuals with hypertension, a population characterized by a high burden of metabolic dysregulation [16]. As a low-cost and easily obtainable biomarker derived from routine biochemical testing, AIP holds promise for the early identification of individuals at increased risk for cognitive decline. Nevertheless, no large-scale epidemiologic studies have specifically examined the association between AIP and cognitive impairment in hypertensive populations, a knowledge gap of particular relevance in China, where both hypertension prevalence and population aging are rapidly increasing.

In light of these considerations, the present study aimed to systematically examine the association between AIP and cognitive impairment among middle-aged and older adults with hypertension in China. The objective was to assess the potential value of AIP as a lipid-based indicator for early risk stratification in this high-risk population.

Materials and methods

Study population

Participants in this study were drawn from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a longitudinal cohort initiated in 2011 to assess the health and socioeconomic status of community-dwelling adults aged 45 years and older in China (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/) [17]. CHARLS employed a multi-stage, stratified sampling strategy based on probability proportional to population size. Individuals were selected from 150 counties or districts and 450 village or urban communities across the country. Follow-up surveys are conducted at 2- to 3-year intervals to systematically track changes in participants’ health status and sociodemographic characteristics. The study protocol has been described in detail elsewhere. Ethical approval was obtained from the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to data collection.

For the present analysis, data were obtained from the 2011 baseline survey and the 2018 follow-up wave. Of the 17,708 individuals initially enrolled in the 2011 cohort, 7,654 participants diagnosed with hypertension were identified as the target population. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 45 years at baseline (n = 175); (2) missing data on TG, HDL-C, or other key covariates (n = 2,355); (3) a history of memory disorders or mental health conditions (n = 202); and (4) missing cognitive function data at baseline, cognitive impairment at baseline, or loss to follow-up in 2018 (n = 1,183). After applying these criteria, a total of 3,739 hypertensive participants were included in the final analysis. A flow diagram outlining the inclusion and exclusion process is provided in Fig. 1.

Measurement of cognitive function and AIP

Cognitive function was assessed at both the 2011 baseline and the 2018 follow-up waves of CHARLS, employing instruments aligned with those utilized in the Health and Retirement Study conducted in the United States [17]. Two cognitive domains were evaluated: episodic memory and mental intactness. Episodic memory was measured using immediate and delayed word recall tasks. Participants were instructed to recall a list of ten words immediately after hearing them read aloud by the interviewer, and again following an approximately four-minute delay. The mean of the immediate and delayed recall scores (range: 0–10) was used to quantify episodic memory. Mental intactness was evaluated through a series of cognitive tasks, including visual-spatial reproduction (redrawing a predefined figure), temporal orientation (identifying the current date, season, and day of the week), and serial subtraction (subtracting 7 from 100 over five consecutive steps). One point was assigned for each correct response, with a maximum score of 11 [18, 19]. The total cognitive function score was derived by summing the episodic memory and mental intactness scores, resulting in a composite score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating superior cognitive function. Cognitive impairment was defined as a score at least 1.0 standard deviation below the mean, in accordance with previously established criteria [20–22].

Plasma lipid concentrations were measured using standard enzymatic colorimetric assays [23]. The AIP was calculated as the base-10 logarithm of the ratio of TG to HDL-C, both expressed in mg/dL: Log10 (TG/HDL-C). Participants were categorized into quartiles based on AIP values: Q1 (< 0.174), Q2 (0.174–0.388), Q3 (0.388–0.620), and Q4 (≥ 0.620).

Data collection and definitions

Baseline characteristics included a comprehensive set of sociodemographic, behavioral, clinical, anthropometric, and laboratory variables. Sociodemographic data included age, gender, marital status, residence (urban or rural), and educational level. Health-related behaviors were categorized by smoking status (never or former smoker; current smoker) and drinking status (never or former drinker; current drinker). Clinical conditions assessed at baseline included physician-diagnosed hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, coronary heart disease (CHD), stroke, other chronic diseases, and lipid-lowering treatment. Anthropometric data were represented by body mass index (BMI), while laboratory parameters comprised TC, TG, HDL-C, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and C-reactive protein (CRP). Hypertension was defined as a self-reported diagnosis, current treatment for hypertension, or measured systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg. Diabetes was defined as FBG ≥ 126 mg/dL, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, self-reported physician diagnosis, or current use of hypoglycemic agents [24]. Dyslipidemia was defined by either a self-reported diagnosis, use of lipid-lowering therapy, or laboratory evidence of TC ≥ 240 mg/dL, TG ≥ 150 mg/dL, HDL-C < 40 mg/dL, or LDL-C ≥ 160 mg/dL [25].

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations for normally distributed data and as medians with interquartile ranges for skewed distributions. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test for categorical variables and either one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, as appropriate. To assess potential selection bias, baseline characteristics were compared between included participants and those excluded due to missing or impaired cognitive function or loss to follow-up.

The association between AIP and cognitive impairment was examined using logistic regression models, with results expressed as odds ratios (OR) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Covariates were selected based on established evidence and clinical relevance [26–28]. Three models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age and gender; and Model 3 was further adjusted for BMI, residence, education level, marital status, overall health status, smoking and drinking behaviors, diabetes, presence of chronic diseases, CRP, TC, LDL-C, FBG, HbA1c, and baseline cognitive score in 2011. To explore the dose-response relationship between AIP and cognitive impairment, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was performed, using the median AIP level as the reference. The discriminative performance of AIP and related lipid indices in identifying cognitive impairment was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with model performance quantified by the area under the curve (AUC).

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess potential effect modification by demographic and clinical factors, including age (45–60 years vs. ≥ 60 years), gender (male vs. female), BMI (< 24 kg/m² vs. ≥ 24 kg/m²), residence (urban vs. rural), and diabetes (yes vs. no). Sensitivity analyses were carried out to examine the robustness of the findings in two ways. First, the sample was additionally stratified by BP severity after excluding participants receiving antihypertensive therapy, using prespecified cutoffs of < 140/90, 140–159/90–99, and ≥ 160/≥100 mmHg (based on the higher of SBP/DBP, calculated from the mean of three seated readings). Multivariable associations between AIP and cognitive impairment were re-estimated within each stratum, and an interaction between AIP and BP strata was evaluated. Second, lipid-lowering treatment at baseline was incorporated as an additional covariate in Model 3 to further account for potential confounding. All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P-value of < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 3,739 participants with hypertension were included in the analysis, with a mean age of 60.5 ± 9.1 years at baseline. Of these, 50.0% (n = 1,870) were female. Participants were stratified into quartiles based on AIP levels. Compared with individuals in the lowest quartile, those in higher quartiles tended to be younger, more frequently married, and more likely to reside in urban areas. The prevalence of diabetes, dyslipidemia, stroke, CHD, and other chronic conditions progressively increased with higher AIP levels. Similarly, levels of TC, TG, FBG, HbA1c, and CRP were elevated in participants with higher AIP, while HDL-C levels demonstrated a decreasing trend. Association between AIP and cognitive impairment

Cognitive function assessed at the 2018 follow-up revealed a mean score of 7.40 ± 5.61. When AIP was analyzed as a continuous variable, higher levels were consistently associated with increased odds of cognitive impairment across all models: Model 1 (OR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.04–1.51, P = 0.016), Model 2 (OR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.29–1.92, P < 0.001), and Model 3 (OR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.51–2.60, P < 0.001). When AIP was categorized into quartiles, with Q1 as the reference, a statistically significant association was observed for Q2 only in the fully adjusted model (Model 3: OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.15–1.80, P = 0.001), but not in Model 1 (P = 0.087) or Model 2 (P = 0.063). In contrast, both Q3 and Q4 were significantly associated with increased odds of cognitive impairment across all models. In Model 3, the ORs for Q3 and Q4 were 1.76 (95% CI: 1.40–2.20, P < 0.001) and 1.85 (95% CI: 1.45–2.37, P < 0.001, respectively. The P for trend across quartiles was < 0.001 in all models. RCS analysis further demonstrated a linear association between AIP and cognitive impairment in the fully adjusted model (P for overall < 0.001; P for nonlinearity = 0.061; Fig. 2).

Predictive performance of AIP and traditional lipid parameters for cognitive impairment

ROC curve analysis was used to evaluate the predictive performance of AIP and traditional lipid parameters (TG, LDL-C, TC, and HDL-C) for cognitive impairment (Fig. 3). AIP exhibited the highest AUC (0.666), exceeding that of TG (0.630), LDL-C (0.563), TC (0.528), and HDL-C (0.508).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Stratified analyses by age, gender, BMI, residence, and diabetes consistently demonstrated a significant association between AIP and cognitive impairment. No significant interaction effects were detected for any subgroup (P-interaction > 0.05). Notably, individuals in the highest AIP quartile (Q4) exhibited a significantly elevated risk of cognitive impairment compared with those in the lowest quartile (Q1) across all subgroups (Table 3). In the sensitivity analysis stratified by BP category, the results were consistent with the primary analysis. Higher AIP was positively associated with cognitive impairment within each category and showed a graded increase across AIP quartiles. No heterogeneity across BP strata was detected (P-interaction = 0.482, Supplementary Table 2). Furthermore, the association between AIP and cognitive impairment persisted after additional adjustment for baseline lipid-lowering treatment, with effect sizes and directions remaining stable (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

In this large, population-based cohort of middle-aged and older adults with hypertension, higher baseline levels of AIP were independently associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment. This association demonstrated a clear linear dose-response relationship and remained robust after adjusting for a broad spectrum of potential confounding variables. Subgroup analyses stratified by age, gender, BMI, residence, and diabetes revealed no significant interactions, underscoring the consistency of the observed association across diverse demographic and clinical subgroups. Furthermore, AIP exhibited superior predictive performance for cognitive impairment compared with traditional lipid parameters. These findings suggest that AIP may serve as a practical and accessible biomarker for identifying individuals with hypertension who are at elevated risk of cognitive impairment.

Cognitive impairment, a recognized precursor to dementia, profoundly compromises the quality of life and autonomy of affected individuals, particularly among the aging population, and its societal burden is expected to escalate with continued demographic shifts [1, 2]. Hypertension, which increases in prevalence with advancing age [5], has been established as an independent risk factor for cognitive impairment. Notably, hypertension arising in midlife exerts more enduring and deleterious effects on brain structure and function than late-onset hypertension [7, 8]. Given the high prevalence of metabolic abnormalities in individuals with hypertension, increasing attention has been directed toward the role of lipid dysregulation in the pathogenesis of cognitive impairment. Several epidemiological studies have examined associations between traditional lipid markers and cognitive impairment. TC, TG, and HDL-C have all been shown to be associated with cognitive decline to varying degrees [9, 10]. In particular, elevated TG levels have been linked to increased risk of cognitive impairment in both individuals with neurological disorders [29, 30] and general middle-aged and older populations [31, 32]. Additionally, higher levels of non-HDL-C have been shown to elevate the likelihood of cognitive impairment in middle-aged adults [9–11], supporting the potential contribution of lipid abnormalities to cognitive decline.

Compared with individual lipid markers, the AIP, which integrates the TG-to-HDL-C ratio, has gained widespread attention in recent years as a more comprehensive and sensitive metabolic risk marker. AIP has demonstrated strong predictive value and stability in relation to cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome [12–14], and its relevance to cognitive outcomes has begun to emerge. A prior study in the general population reported that elevated AIP levels were associated with a heightened risk of cognitive impairment [16], highlighting its potential utility in cognitive risk assessment. However, this study did not specifically focus on individuals with hypertension, who are typically burdened with higher rates of metabolic abnormalities. Moreover, previous studies have noted the frequent co-occurrence of hypertension and dyslipidemia in individuals with cognitive impairment [33, 34], suggesting a synergistic impact of these metabolic abnormalities. Although metabolic syndrome has also been implicated in cognitive impairment [35–37], some investigations in low-income populations have not identified significant associations, raising concerns that reliance on a composite diagnosis may obscure the contributions of specific components, such as lipid dysfunction.

This study, based on data from a large-scale national cohort of middle-aged and older adults with hypertension, systematically examined the relationship between AIP and cognitive impairment, thereby contributing novel evidence to an area with limited prior data. In contrast to previous investigations conducted in general populations or without consideration of metabolic status, this analysis focused exclusively on individuals with hypertension who are more likely to exhibit metabolic abnormalities. This targeted approach enhances the clinical relevance of the findings by highlighting the utility of AIP as a risk stratification tool within a high-risk population. Higher AIP levels were independently associated with an elevated risk of cognitive impairment, and this association remained robust across multiple adjustment models and stratified subgroup analyses, supporting the reliability and robustness of the findings. In a sensitivity analysis limited to participants not receiving antihypertensive therapy and stratified by BP severity, the results were consistent with the primary analysis and showed no evidence of heterogeneity across strata. Moreover, the observed linear dose-response relationship suggests a potential cumulative burden of lipid-related metabolic dysfunction, reinforcing the importance of early detection of at-risk individuals. Notably, AIP outperformed conventional lipid indices in identifying individuals at risk of cognitive impairment, underscoring its potential utility as a clinically meaningful marker of lipid-driven metabolic risk.

Several biological mechanisms may underlie the association between elevated AIP and cognitive impairment in individuals with hypertension. First, elevated TG levels have been consistently associated with impaired cognitive performance, and AIP increases typically coincide with TG elevation. This dyslipidemic profile may exacerbate systemic inflammation, promote endothelial dysfunction, and impair cerebral perfusion, leading to damage in brain regions responsible for cognitive processing [38]. Second, disturbances in brain cholesterol homeostasis may enhance amyloid-beta aggregation through alterations in amyloid precursor protein metabolism, thereby contributing to neurodegenerative changes. Hypercholesterolemia can also accelerate atherosclerotic processes and disrupt microvascular cerebral circulation, further exacerbating cognitive impairment [39, 40]. Third, AIP is closely associated with insulin resistance (IR), a metabolic abnormality strongly implicated in cognitive impairment. IR may impair cognition through multiple mechanisms, including sustained neuronal exposure to elevated insulin levels, region-specific glucose hypometabolism, and altered synaptic plasticity mediated by impaired insulin signaling pathways [41, 42]. Additionally, elevated TG levels may cross the blood–brain barrier, contributing to central insulin receptor resistance and impairing leptin transport into the brain, thereby attenuating its neuroprotective effects [43, 44]. Finally, vascular brain injury, particularly small vessel disease, white matter lesions, cerebral atrophy, and impaired glymphatic clearance, represents a fundamental neuropathological mechanism in cognitive impairment associated with hypertension. AIP elevation, which often parallels these vascular abnormalities, may reflect underlying metabolic-vascular dysregulation contributing to progressive cognitive deterioration in this high-risk group [45–47].

AIP, derived from routine lipid profiles, offers notable advantages in clinical practice due to its simplicity, low cost, and scalability. Unlike individual lipid parameters, AIP encapsulates broader aspects of lipid metabolic imbalance and, in this study, demonstrated superior predictive capacity for identifying cognitive impairment risk among individuals with hypertension, a population characterized by a high prevalence of metabolic disturbances. Given the lack of effective therapies to reverse cognitive impairment, early identification of high-risk individuals is crucial. As an easily accessible biomarker, AIP may contribute to routine hypertension management by supporting early risk stratification of patients at elevated likelihood of cognitive decline. Particularly in resource-constrained primary care settings, AIP may represent a feasible and scalable option for initial risk stratification. Although further prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings, the present results suggest that AIP may serve as a promising adjunctive tool for cognitive risk assessment in hypertensive populations and may complement existing clinical risk assessment frameworks.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, all participants were drawn from a Chinese population, which may limit the generalizability of the findings across different racial or geographic groups, highlighting the need for external validation in more diverse populations. Second, the analysis relied solely on baseline AIP measurements obtained in 2011 and did not account for longitudinal changes in AIP that may influence cognitive outcomes over time. Third, as with all observational studies, the potential for residual confounding remains, despite comprehensive adjustment for a wide range of known covariates. Unmeasured or unknown confounders may still influence the observed associations, and complex sampling adjustments were not applied given the focus on association estimation rather than population inference. Fourth, the sensitivity analysis, which excluded participants receiving antihypertensive therapy and stratified BP severity within the hypertensive sample, reduces treatment-related confounding but may introduce selection and limit generalizability to treated patients. It also does not establish whether the association between AIP and cognitive impairment differs by hypertension status in the overall population. In addition, due to missing mortality timing data, we were unable to account for potential competing risks from death before follow-up. Future research should aim to address these limitations by incorporating dynamic AIP assessments, enrolling multiethnic populations, conducting a more comprehensive evaluation of the influence of hypertension in broader populations, and examining specific clinically diagnosed subtypes of cognitive impairment to further clarify and extend these findings.

Conclusions

In this cohort of middle-aged and older adults with hypertension, higher baseline AIP levels were independently and linearly associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment. AIP demonstrated superior predictive capacity compared with traditional lipid measures, underscoring its potential utility as a practical and accessible biomarker for early risk stratification among individuals with hypertension. Further studies are needed to replicate these findings in more diverse populations and to evaluate the clinical applicability of incorporating AIP into cognitive risk assessment frameworks.
