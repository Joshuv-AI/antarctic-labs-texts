Silymarin inhibits osteoclastogenesis and alleviates inflammatory injury in RAW264.7 cells via activating the AMPK/Sirt1-autophagy axis
Authors: Juan He, Jianyong Kang, Jing Zeng, Jianqin Tan, Mianmian Duan, Yanyan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeriodontitis is a prevalent oral inflammatory condition and a major cause of tooth loss. This study sought to assess the impact of silymarin on LPS-triggered inflammatory responses in RAW 264.7 murine macrophages. The Osteoclast precursor cell line RAW264.7 was treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to induce inflammation and cellular injury, and then with silymarin. Cell viability, apoptosis, autophagy, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, proinflammatory cytokine levels, osteoclast formation, and protein levels in apoptotic, autophagic, and AMPK/Sirt1-autophagy axis were assessed using flow cytometry, Western blot analysis and CCK8 assay. LPS induced apoptosis, increased ROS production, triggered an inflammatory response, and reduced the viability of RAW 264.7 cells. Western blot analysis showed an elevation in Bax and cleaved caspase-3 expressions, while Bcl-2 levels decreased following LPS stimulation. After silymarin treatment, LPS-induced oxidative stress was reduced, and cell viability was improved. Meanwhile, the expressions of Bax and caspase-3 were downregulated. The LPS-induced inflammatory response was also reduced, resulting in lower levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, as well as downregulation of theNF-κB/Rel pathway. Conversely, silymarin administration enhanced autophagic activity in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages, as evidenced by elevated autophagosome formation and modulated expression of Beclin-1 and p62. Furthermore, silymarin suppressed RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation in these cells, accompanied by reduced expression of osteoclast-specific markers. Notably, silymarin-treated cells exhibited increased AMPKα phosphorylation and upregulated Sirt1 levels, suggesting the activation of these regulatory pathways. This study demonstrates that silymarin alleviates LPS-induced cellular damage, inflammation, and suppresses RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation in RAW 264.7 macrophages. These protective effects are closely associated with enhanced autophagic activity and the activation of the AMPK/Sirt1-autophagy regulatory axis. Introduction

Periodontitis, a prevalent inflammatory dental condition, represents the primary cause of tooth loss in individuals, and it affects over 3.5 billion people globally1. It is now widely recognized that periodontitis is a multifactorial disease, driven by a complex interplay between dysbiotic microbial communities, host immune-inflammatory responses, and environmental risk factors such as smoking and diabetes‌.‌ This dysregulated host response to the subgingival microbiota leads to progressive destruction of periodontal tissues and alveolar bone. Therefore, the prevention and treatment of periodontal diseases are essential for maintaining oral and general health. Currently, the main clinical treatment for periodontitis is the mechanical removal of plaque and calculus to control local irritant factors. However, for cases with persistent inflammatory lesions or deep periodontal pockets, antibiotics are often used as adjuvant therapy to control infection. Long-term and frequent use of such adjuvant antibiotics can lead to increased drug resistance, disrupt oral and intestinal flora, and even cause adverse reactions such as tooth stains, vomiting, and diarrhea—highlighting the need for new therapeutic agents with fewer side effects2. Therefore, new medications with fewer adverse reactions and superior therapeutic outcomes are needed to treat periodontitis.

At the cellular level, periodontitis is an inflammatory process resulting from the accumulation of immune cells. As a result of the aggregation and activation of innate immune cells in the oral environment, innate immunity is converted to acquired immunity in periodontitis. During this transition, the predominant cell populations comprise dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, natural killer cells (NK cells), and additional innate immune cell types. Following immune cell activation, dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, and other antigen-presenting cells (APCs) initiate adaptive immune responses through coordinated interactions with T lymphocytes and B lymphocytes3. This stage results in advanced lesions characterized by irreversible destruction of the periodontal attachment tissue and bone loss. This process involves osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and various innate and adaptive immune cells4. Notably, activated innate and adaptive immune cells (e.g., macrophages, T cells) secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α, IL-1β), which not only amplify the local inflammatory response but also directly or indirectly promote the activation and differentiation of osteoclast precursors—making osteoclast-mediated bone resorption a core pathological feature of periodontitis progression. Therefore, inhibiting osteoclastogenesis has become a critical target for mitigating periodontal bone loss.

Osteoclasts, multinucleated terminal-differentiation cells originating from hematopoietic precursors in bone marrow, serve as the principal mediators of periodontal bone resorption5. The RAW264.7 cell line refers to a macrophage-like lineage originating from an Abelson murine leukemia virus-inducedtumor6. Due to its metabolic and phagocytic functions, it is increasingly being utilized as a cellular model of osteoclasts. Receptor activator of nuclear factor-kappa B ligand (RANKL) may enter osteoclast precursor cells, such as RAW264.7 cells, to activate transcription factors that promote osteoclastogenesis7. Specifically, RANKL binds to its specific receptor RANK (expressed on the surface of osteoclast precursors) to activate downstream signaling pathways (e.g., NF-κB, MAPK), which in turn upregulate the expression of osteoclast-specific transcription factors (such as NFATc2) and drive the differentiation of precursor cells into mature, bone-resorbing osteoclasts. This RANK/RANKL axis is the core regulatory pathway of osteoclastogenesis, which is why we used RANKL to induce osteoclast differentiation in RAW264.7 cells in this study. Since this cell line can be induced to differentiate into osteoclasts upon receptor activation, it serves as an ideal cellular model for investigating osteoclastogenesis and related regulatory mechanisms8. and to generate inflammatory stress using lipopolysaccharide (LPS)9. It was used to assess the protective effect of silymarin. Silymarin, a polyphenolic flavonoid primarily derived from Silybum marianum L., exhibits dose-dependent inhibition of oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis in acute kidney injury (AKI)10. The main active component of silymarin is silybin (accounting for ~ 60–70% of its total polyphenolic content), which is responsible for most of its biological activities. In this study, we used silymarin (purity ≥ 95%) purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (cat. no. S0292)—a well-characterized reagent widely used in preclinical studies—to ensure the consistency and reproducibility of our experimental results. Although silymarin is commonly included in health food products, its standardized form (as used here) has been extensively studied in cellular and animal models of inflammatory and metabolic diseases11,12. Studies have also demonstrated that silymarin reduces autophagy-mediated apoptosis in gastric cancer by downregulating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway13. It prevents the formation of mitochondrial ROS and induces autophagy through the IL6ST/JAK2/Stat3 signaling pathway, leading to reduced DOX-induced cardiomyocyte injury14.

It is noteworthy that previous studies have demonstrated that silibinin, which is the major active component in silymarin, can inhibit RANKL-induced osteoclasts differentiation15, and has demonstrated well-known anti-inflammatory activity. However, very little is known about the mechanisms involved in the anti-inflammatory activity of silymarin, particularly related to autophagy and signaling through the AMPK/Sirt1 pathway. This study prioritized the AMPK/Sirt1 pathway over other osteoclastogenic pathways (e.g., MAPKs) because of its dual regulatory role in energy homeostasis and inflammation, and its well-documented crosstalk with autophagy—a process increasingly implicated in periodontitis pathogenesis. Notably, AMPK activation has been shown to negatively regulate osteoclast differentiation by suppressing RANKL-induced NF-κB and mTOR signaling, while Sirt1 deacetylates NFATc1 and FoxOs, thereby inhibiting osteoclastogenesis at the transcriptional level. Furthermore, AMPK and Sirt1 reciprocally activate each other and jointly induce autophagy, which can mitigate oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in macrophages. Given that silymarin enhanced autophagy in our model, the AMPK/Sirt1 axis represents a plausible and integrative mechanism linking its anti-inflammatory and anti-osteoclastogenic effects. Notably, while silibinin—the major active component of silymarin—has been shown to inhibit RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis, the role of the AMPK/Sirt1-autophagy axis in mediating these effects under combined inflammatory and osteoclastogenic stimuli remains unexplored. Furthermore, most existing studies employ pretreatment regimens, which may reflect prophylactic rather than therapeutic application. Our study uniquely demonstrates that silymarin exerts protective effects even when administered after LPS and RANKL stimulation, mimicking clinical intervention in established disease. This post-treatment design, combined with the identification of the AMPK/Sirt1-autophagy axis as a central regulatory hub, represents a significant advance in understanding silymarin’s therapeutic potential in periodontitis. Additionally, using LPS in combination with RANKL in a dual-stimulation model more closely mimics the inflammatory and bone resorption environment of periodontitis than that can be modelled with RANKL alone. Thus, this study aims to contribute to current knowledge by investigating the association between silymarin-mediated regulation of inflammation, apoptosis, autophagy, and osteoclast differentiation and AMPK/Sirt1 activation in LPS and RANKL-stimulated RAW264.7 cells.

For this study, RAW264.7 cells were utilized as a cellular model to assess the effect of silymarin on the regulation of apoptosis, autophagy, inflammation, and osteoclastogenesis, to explore its potential preclinical application value for periodontitis.

Materials and methods

Cell line and culture

Mouse peritoneal macrophages (RAW264.7) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultivated in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% FBS, penicillin and streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. After digestion with 0.25% trypsin for 3 min, the cells were passaged once every 3–4 days, and the 4th–8th generation cells were used for the experiment.

Cell viability assay

Viability was assessed via CCK-8 assay. Cells (5 × 10³/well) were plated in 96-well plates for 24 h, centrifuged (850×g, 25 °C, 10 min), PBS-washed, then treated with LPS (0–16 µg/ml) ± silymarin (0–100 µM) for 24 h. After adding 10 µL CCK-8 solution and incubating at 37 °C (2 h), absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Flow cytometry

Apoptosis was detected using a flow cytometer and an apoptosis detection kit, following the manufacturer’s protocols. Following treatment, cells were centrifuged (850 × g, 25 °C, 10 min), successively washed with ice-chilled PBS and reconstituted in 1× binding buffer. After staining with YO-PRO-1/PI solution (15 min, 25 °C, dark incubation), flow cytometric analysis was performed. The apoptotic rate was determined using built-in software. For ROS analysis, treated cells were stained with the cell-permeable redox probe DCFH-DA (10 µmol/L) in serum-free medium (at a 1:1000 dilution). Following a 20-minute incubation at 37 °C, cells were collected and washed twice using serum-free medium, and analyzed using a flow cytometer based on the supplier’s methods. To assess autophagy, cells were labeled using autophagy-specific fluorescent markers from a commercial assay kit, following the manufacturer’s recommended procedures. All flow cytometry experiments were performed with 6 independent biological replicates, and each biological replicate included 3 technical replicates.

ELISA

RAW264.7 cells were inoculated into the wells of a 6-well plate at 1.0 × 106 cells/well for 24 h. LPS was administered to the cells (2 µg/ml) for 8 h and then the cells were treated with silymarin (0, 50, and 100 µM) for 2 h. Following treatment, the culture medium was harvested and centrifuged (1200 × g, 4 °C, 20 min) to isolate the supernatant. Cytokine levels (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) were quantified using a murine-specific inflammatory cytokine multiplex ELISA kit (cat. no. ARG80929, Arigo Biolaboratories, Hsinchu, Taiwan, China), which has been validated for the quantitative detection of mouse inflammatory cytokines in cell culture supernatants with high specificity and no cross-reactivity with human cytokines. All ELISA experiments were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, with 6 independent biological replicates, and each biological replicate included 3 technical replicates.

Western blot

Following treatment, cells were centrifuged (850 × g, 25 °C, 10 min) and the resulting pellet was lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors, as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein concentrations were quantified via BCA assay. Samples (50 µg) underwent heat denaturation and SDS-PAGE separation, followed by PVDF membrane transfer. After 2 h blocking in 5% skim milk (25 °C), membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (4 °C/overnight) and subsequently with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (2 h/RT). The membranes were incubated with ECL Plus solution to develop immunoreactive bands according to the supplier’s protocols. GAPDH served as the loading control. Western blot analysis was performed with 6 independent biological replicates, and band densities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Osteoclast differentiation and TRAP activity measurement

RANKL is the key inducer of osteoclast differentiation in this study. The concentration of RANKL (50 ng/mL) was selected based on two considerations: (1) Preliminary experiments showed that this concentration can effectively induce RAW264.7 cells to differentiate into mature osteoclasts (characterized by multinucleation and positive TRAP staining) within 5 days, without causing excessive cytotoxicity; (2) This concentration is consistent with that used in most published studies on RAW264.7-derived osteoclast differentiation, facilitating the comparison of our results with previous research. The role of RANKL in the model is to mimic the physiological/pathological signal that promotes osteoclastogenesis in periodontitis—during periodontal inflammation, stromal cells (e.g., periodontal ligament cells) and immune cells secrete RANKL, which binds to RANK on osteoclast precursors to trigger downstream signaling (e.g., NF-κB, NFATc2) and drive osteoclast differentiation, leading to alveolar bone resorption. Thus, RANKL stimulation in our model directly simulates the core pathological process of periodontitis-related bone loss.

Following a 5-day co-treatment with RANKL (50 ng/mL) and silymarin (50–100 µM), cells were centrifuged (850 × g, 10 min, 25 °C) for collection. For osteoclast observation, the cells were washed three times with PBS, stained using Fast Garnet GBC Base solution according to the supplier’s protocols, and viewed under an inverted microscope to count the osteoclasts. Standardized quantification criteria for TRAP-positive multinucleated osteoclasts were strictly followed: (1) TRAP-positive cells with ≥ 3 intact nuclei were defined as mature osteoclasts for counting; (2) only intact cells with continuous cytoplasmic TRAP staining, clear cellular boundaries, and no pyknotic nuclei were included in the analysis; (3) cells with fragmented cytoplasm, discontinuous TRAP staining, or overlapping cell clusters were excluded from counting; (4) five non-overlapping high-power fields (HPF, 200× magnification) were randomly selected from each replicate well for quantification; (5) all counting procedures were performed by two independent researchers in a blinded manner to the experimental group assignments, with inconsistent counts re-evaluated by a third senior researcher to ensure objectivity. Data from five randomly selected fields were used for osteoclast counts, with the final count results expressed as the mean number of TRAP-positive multinucleated osteoclasts per HPF. The TRAP assay was performed as previously described16. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, TRAP enzymatic activity was quantified spectrophotometrically at 405 nm. Cells underwent three PBS washes prior to lysis in 0.1% Triton X-100 solution. Each experimental condition was assessed with 6 independent biological replicates, and each biological replicate included 3 technical replicates for TRAP activity quantification; osteoclast counting was performed in 5 randomly selected fields per biological replicate.

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For all experiments, “N” refers to the number of independent biological replicates (i.e., experiments performed on separate cell cultures/passages on different days), while technical replicates refer to repeated measurements of the same biological sample. All core experiments were performed with 6 independent biological replicates, and each biological replicate included 3 technical replicates. Statistical analysis was performed using the mean values of technical replicates for each biological replicate. Comparisons between multiple groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc test after homogeneity of variance verification, using SPSS 19.0 software. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Post-hoc power analysis was performed for all primary experimental endpoints using G*Power 3.1 software (F-test, one-way ANOVA, fixed effects, omnibus, between groups), with an α error probability of 0.05. The analysis confirmed that the statistical power (1-β) for all core results in this study exceeded 0.8, which meets the widely accepted statistical rigor standard for preclinical in vitro studies.

Results

LPS induces apoptosis and oxidative stress (OS)

Periodontitis is closely associated with OS, characterized by increased production of activated ROS. As a result, in periodontitis, cell differentiation is often abnormal, and cell apoptosis or necrosis occurs. To assess the effect of silymarin on periodontitis-related cellular injury, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was used to induce oxidative stress (OS) and apoptosis in RAW 264.7 cells. After treatment with LPS, the viability of the RAW 264.7 cells decreased from 99.5% to 25.3% when the concentration of LPS increased from 0 µg/ml to 16 µg/ml (Fig. 1A). Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that treatment with 4 µg/ml LPS markedly elevated the percentage of apoptotic cells from 0.7% to 8.5% (p < 0.01; Fig. 1B). Similarly, there was a 10-fold increase in ROS production between LPS concentrations (p < 0.01, Fig. 1C). Immunoblotting demonstrated marked elevation of pro-apoptotic regulators, including Bax and cleaved caspase-3, with concomitant reduction in anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 expression in RAW 264.7 macrophages following LPS treatment (Fig. 1D). These data indicate that LPS treatment reduces viability, induces apoptosis, and triggers ROS synthesis in RAW 264.7 cells, leading to elevated expression of pro-apoptotic factors and reduced levels of anti-apoptotic proteins.

Silymarin attenuates LPS-induced OS

To investigate silymarin’s cytoprotective properties against LPS-mediated cytotoxicity, RAW 264.7 macrophages were exposed to LPS (2 µg/ml) in combination with varying silymarin concentrations (0, 50, and 100 µM) for 24 h. The CCK-8 viability assay demonstrated dose-dependent enhancement of cellular survival following silymarin administration (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, Fig. 2A). Flow cytometry results showed that silymarin treatment also reduced apoptosis and ROS production (p < 0.01, Fig. 2B,C). Immunoblotting demonstrated differential regulation of apoptosis markers in silymarin-treated RAW 264.7 cells relative to LPS-only controls: downregulation of Bax and cleaved caspase-3 (p < 0.05) alongside Bcl-2 upregulation (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2D). These results indicate that silymarin could protect RAW 264.7 cells from LPS-induced damage.

Silymarin attenuates inflammation

RAW 264.7 macrophages were first exposed to LPS (2 µg/ml) for 8 h, followed by a 2-hour incubation with varying concentrations of silymarin (0, 50, and 100 µM). Pro-inflammatory cytokine concentrations (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) were quantified intracellularly. The findings demonstrated that LPS stimulation elevated TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β expression, while silymarin treatment dose-dependently attenuated these pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (p < 0.01, Fig. 3A–C). Given the pivotal involvement of NF-κB signaling in inflammatory responses, we examined the expression profiles of NF-κB/Rel family proteins. Immunoblotting revealed elevated phosphorylation levels of IκBα and p65 following LPS treatment, which were subsequently reduced by silymarin administration (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, Fig. 3D). These results indicate that silymarin attenuated the LPS-induced inflammatory response.

Silymarin induces autophagy

To investigate whether silymarin induces autophagy in LPS-exposed RAW 264.7 cells, cells were exposed to varying concentrations of silymarin (0, 50, and 100 µM) for 24 h. Flow cytometry demonstrated a significant elevation in autophagy-positive cells following silymarin administration. In contrast, LPS treatment alone did not significantly increase autophagic cells compared with the untreated control (p < 0.01 and p > 0.05, Fig. 4A). Western blot results confirmed that levels of autophagy-related proteins Beclin 1 and p62 were downor up-regulated after LPS treatment as compared with untreated cells and were reversed in a dose-dependent manner following silymarin treatment (p < 0.01, Fig. 4B). These data suggest that silymarin induces autophagy in LPS-treated RAW 264.7 cells.

Silymarin inhibits osteoclast formation

To investigate the role of silymarin in osteoclast formation, RAW 264.7 macrophages were stimulated with 50 ng/ml RANKL to induce osteoclastogenesis. The experimental data showed that after exposure to RANKL, osteoclasts were formed in RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 5A,B). At the same time, TRAPase activity was significantly increased (Fig. 5C) compared to cells not treated with RANKL. In contrast, silymarin treatment (50 and 100 µM) significantly reduced the number of osteoclasts and TRAPase activity (p < 0.01; Fig. 5B,C). Western blotting demonstrated RANKL-mediated upregulation of NFAT2, CTSK, and MMP-9 expression relative to controls (p < 0.01, Fig. 5D). However, silymarin treatment significantly downregulated the expression of these proteins (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively; Fig. 5D). These data indicate that silymarin inhibits RANKL-induced osteoclast formation, resulting in the downregulation of proteins involved in osteoclastogenesis.

Silymarin treatment is associated with activation of the AMPK/Sirt1 pathway

5’ AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and Sirtuin1 (Sirt1) are positively correlated. The AMPK/Sirt1 pathway is essential for regulating energy metabolism and bone metabolism, and AMPK negatively regulates osteoclast differentiation. To gain insight into silymarin-mediated blockage of osteoclast differentiation, we investigated the expression of AMPKα and Sirt1 in this pathway. The experimental data of this study indicated that silymarin increased the phosphorylation of AMPKα and upregulated Sirt1 levels in RAW 264.7 cells treated with RANKL (Fig. 5E).

Discussion

Using the osteoclast precursor RAW 264.7 cells, activation of autophagy and the AMPK/Sirt1 pathway was observed after silymarin treatment, along with reduced LPS-induced oxidative stress, apoptosis, inflammation, and RANKL-induced osteoclast formation. These in vitro results indicate that silymarin may have preclinical exploratory value for alleviating periodontitis-related progressive bone resorption and osteolytic lesions, which requires further validation in more biologically complex in vivo models.

Absorption of alveolar bone is one of the major pathological changes in periodontitis, and osteoclasts are the key functional cells involved in bone resorption17. Blockage of osteoclastogenesis would therefore attenuate osteoclast-regulated bone loss in periodontitis18. To investigate the role of silymarin in treating periodontitis-related diseases, we used osteoclast precursor RAW 264.7 cells as a cellular model. The RAW 264.7 cells can be induced by cellular stimulators such as RANKL or macrophage colony-stimulating factor to form osteoclasts and are widely used as a cellular model to study the biological function of various stimuli in periodontitis19. To generate such stimulation, LPS from E. coli, rather than P. gingivitis, was applied to RAW 264.7 cells because TLR4 predominantly mediates LPS recognition. In contrast, the TLR4 response to LPS-PG requires other accessory molecules, such as CD14 and MD2, which may be absent in the cell system tested. Therefore, the use of E. coli LPS would be more readily able to generate an inflammatory reaction for subsequent study.

Nonetheless, it must be noted that this study only utilized a single cell type (RAW264.7 cells), and therefore its translational application may be limited. There are many complex cellular interactions in periodontitis, involving not only osteoclasts but also fibroblasts, osteoblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells, all of which are missing in this simplified in vitro system. All of these interactions may importantly affect the inflammatory microenvironment and particularly the dynamics of bone remodeling. Therefore, the study warrants further validation using in vivo animal models or multicellular co-culture systems to better mirror the complex pathophysiology of periodontitis and further confirm the therapeutic relevance of silymarin. It is worth further elaborating on the selection of the murine RAW264.7 cell line in this study, as it relates to the practicality and reliability of the experimental model. RAW264.7 cells are widely recognized in relevant research for their stable ability to differentiate into osteoclasts under RANKL stimulation and their consistent response to LPS-induced inflammation, which ensures the reproducibility of the data obtained. In contrast, the use of human cells for such initial mechanistic exploration faces inherent challenges that could affect experimental consistency, including the need for ethical clearance for acquisition, unavoidable individual differences from donors, and higher demands for culture conditions. Additionally, the key pathways involved in osteoclastogenesis and inflammation (e.g., RANKL-RANK and TLR4-NF-κB signaling) are evolutionarily conserved between mice and humans, providing a basis for the translational relevance of the findings here. This choice does not negate the limitation of a single cell line, and as previously noted, future in vivo and multicellular studies will further address the complexity of human periodontitis.

An important limitation of the present study is the lack of in vivo experimental verification. Although our in vitro experiments using RAW 264.7 cells have initially revealed that silymarin alleviates LPS-induced cellular damage and inhibits RANKL-mediated osteoclast differentiation in association with the regulation of the AMPK/Sirt1 pathway and autophagy, the single cell line model cannot fully replicate the complex pathophysiological environment of periodontitis in vivo. A second critical limitation is that autophagic activity was only assessed via autophagy-related marker protein expression and flow cytometric detection of autophagosomes, with no functional validation using pharmacological autophagy inhibitors (e.g., 3-methyladenine, chloroquine) or genetic silencing of key autophagy genes. Without these loss-of-function experiments, we cannot definitively confirm the causal relationship between silymarin-induced autophagy and its protective effects, including the observed attenuation of apoptosis, anti-inflammatory activity, and inhibition of osteoclastogenesis. Specifically, the current data only demonstrate a positive correlation between enhanced autophagy and reduced apoptosis in our model, and whether silymarin alleviates apoptosis in an autophagy-dependent manner remains to be verified by targeted autophagy inhibition experiments. The causal relationship between silymarin-induced autophagy and its protective effects requires further systematic validation in future studies. A fourth critical limitation is that no functional validation (e.g., pharmacological inhibition with AMPK/Sirt1-specific inhibitors, or genetic knockdown of AMPK/Sirt1) was performed to verify whether the activation of the AMPK/Sirt1 pathway is necessary for the protective effects of silymarin. Therefore, our study only demonstrates a positive correlation between AMPK/Sirt1-autophagy axis activation and silymarin-mediated anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and anti-osteoclastogenic effects, and cannot confirm a causal dependency between these phenomena. The causal role of this pathway will be systematically validated in our future studies through specific pharmacological inhibitors and gene editing techniques, to further clarify the core regulatory mechanism of silymarin in periodontitis. In real-world periodontal lesions, the disease progression involves interactions among multiple cell types (such as periodontal ligament cells, osteoblasts, and various immune cells), as well as the influence of oral microbial flora and systemic metabolic factors—all of which are not reflected in the current in vitro system. Furthermore, this study did not assess the potential long-term toxicity or dose-dependent side effects of silymarin, and all findings lack direct validation from human clinical samples, which limits the direct translational reference value of the current preclinical data. Therefore, the therapeutic potential of silymarin for periodontitis demonstrated in this in vitro study still needs to be further confirmed by in vivo research. Future work will focus on conducting in vivo studies to verify the findings of the present in vitro experiments. These in vivo studies will help evaluate whether silymarin can exert similar protective effects (such as reducing gingival inflammation and alleviating alveolar bone resorption) in the actual pathological context of periodontitis, and further confirm whether the AMPK/Sirt1 pathway plays a key regulatory role in this process in vivo. The results of these in vivo studies will help to more comprehensively clarify the therapeutic value of silymarin for periodontitis and provide more solid evidence for its potential clinical application.

The present study adopted the protocol of treating RAW 264.7 cells with silymarin after LPS or RANKL stimulation, which was designed to mimic the clinical reality of periodontitis management—clinically, periodontitis is often diagnosed and treated after patients present with symptoms such as gingival inflammation and alveolar bone resorption (i.e., after the pathological processes of inflammation and osteoclast activation have been triggered). This design aimed to evaluate the therapeutic effect of silymarin on established periodontitis-related cellular damage, which is more in line with practical clinical needs. It is worth noting that the scenario of pre-treating cells with silymarin (before LPS/RANKL stimulation) is scientifically meaningful: pre-treatment might allow silymarin to pre-activate the AMPK/Sirt1 pathway and upregulate autophagy in RAW 264.7 cells in advance, thereby enhancing the cells’ resistance to subsequent inflammatory and osteoclastogenic stimuli, and potentially achieving a more robust inhibitory effect on inflammation, cell death, and osteoclast differentiation. Unfortunately, this pretreatment regimen was not incorporated into the present study design, which is a limitation of this study. Future research will supplement this experimental group to systematically compare the therapeutic effects of silymarin in pre-treatment and post-treatment modes, and further clarify its optimal application timing in the context of periodontitis.

Our findings that silymarin alleviates LPS-induced oxidative stress, apoptosis, and inflammatory cytokine release in RAW264.7 cells are consistent with the well-documented antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, and anti-inflammatory properties of flavonoid compounds, including silymarin and its major active component silibinin, in multiple in vitro inflammatory models. The observed suppression of NF-κB signaling by silymarin further aligns with its established role in inhibiting pro-inflammatory cascades in macrophages, which is a key mechanism underlying its protective effects in inflammatory conditions.

Autophagy helps eliminate defective cellular organelles and protein aggregates, facilitating organelle turnover and thereby enhancing cell viability20. Accumulating experimental evidence suggests that enhancing autophagy could also reduce apoptosis resulting from various stress conditions such as nutritional deprivation, metabolic imbalance, and inflammation21,22. These data suggest that silymarin induces autophagy in LPS-treated RAW 264.7 cells, with enhanced autophagic activity showing a positive correlation with reduced oxidative stress, inflammatory response, and apoptosis in silymarin-treated cells23. However, the causal relationship between silymarin-induced autophagy and its anti-apoptotic effects has not been verified by functional validation experiments in this study, and the underlying mechanism requires further systematic investigation. The association between silymarin-induced autophagy and its protective activity against LPS-induced damage warrants further exploration as a potential therapeutic avenue for periodontitis-related bone diseases.

Alveolar bone resorption represents a defining feature of periodontitis advancement, making its inhibition a critical therapeutic objective in dentistry. The bone-resorbing osteoclasts are fundamentally involved in the pathological bone degradation characteristic of periodontal disease24. Monocyte-derived macrophage progenitors can differentiate into osteoclasts through cytokine-mediated regulation, notably by macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL), and osteoprotegerin (OPG). Moreover, key mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1, and PGE2 potentiate osteoclast activity in periodontitis25. Consistent with previous reports on silibinin’s anti-osteoclastogenic activity, our study found that silymarin inhibits RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation in RAW264.7 cells, with downregulation of key osteoclast-specific markers including NFATc2, CTSK, and MMP-9. Notably, our study extends these findings by demonstrating these effects under the combined LPS and RANKL stimulation, which more closely mimics the inflammatory bone resorption microenvironment of periodontitis. The downregulation of these genes by silymarin suggests that it interacts with the regulatory frameworks that control these genes26, however, mechanistic elucidation requires further investigation.

The AMPK/Sirt1 pathway governs key energy-metabolic processes and mitochondrial biogenesis27 and apoptosis28. The activation of AMPK by either chemical activators or calorie restriction markedly protects against lipotoxicity by increasing autophagy through Sirt1 upregulation, a process that is facilitated by AMPK activation29–31. Moreover, AMPK enhances osteogenesis, as genetic ablation of its α or β subunits reduces murine bone mass.Osteoblast differentiation has been previously shown to be correlated with cellular metabolism and mitochondrial activity, which are associated with AMPK32. In contrast, studies have demonstrated that AMPK negatively regulates osteoclast differentiation and function33. To further investigate the mechanisms underlying the silymarin-mediated protective activity, we assayed the effect of silymarin on the AMPK/Sirt1 pathway. The experimental data of this study indicated that silymarin induced the phosphorylation of AMPKα and upregulated Sirt1 levels in RAW264.7 cells. This activation was positively correlated with the inhibitory effect of silymarin on osteoclast differentiation, consistent with previous reports that AMPK serves as a negative regulator of osteoclastogenesis34.

The anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and anti-osteoclastogenic effects of silymarin show a positive correlation with the activation of the AMPK/Sirt1-autophagy signaling axis in this in vitro model. We observed that silymarin treatment concurrently enhances autophagic activity, increases AMPKα phosphorylation, and upregulates Sirt1 expression, alongside reduced oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokine release, and osteoclast differentiation. However, in the absence of targeted loss-of-function validation (e.g., pharmacological inhibition or genetic silencing of AMPK/Sirt1 or key autophagy genes), we cannot confirm a causal dependency between the activation of this axis and the protective effects of silymarin. Thus, the AMPK/Sirt1-autophagy axis may represent a potential correlative regulatory pathway associated with silymarin’s bioactivity in this model, and its role as a core mechanistic mediator requires systematic functional validation in future studies.

In addition, activation of the AMPK-Sirt1 pathway can induce autophagy, reduce LPS-induced renal tubular injury, and inhibit apoptosis27, most likely by increasing autophagy and reducing ROS production35. Sirtuin1 (Sirt1) can be activated by natural or synthetic compounds, such as resveratrol, to attenuate the loss of bone mass, while Sirt1 deletion in osteoclast progenitor cells increases osteoclast formation and bone resorption36. Multiple Sirt1 activators suppress RANKL-stimulated osteoclast differentiation, fusion, and resorptive function; activate the AMPK pathway; and inhibit RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis.

Overall, the activation of the AMPK-Sirt1 pathway by silymarin is closely correlated with its protective action against LPS-induced damage and inhibition of osteoclastogenesis in this in vitro system. However, additional in vivo studies using animal models or clinical samples remain necessary to validate the translational impact of these in vitro findings anddirectly support the therapeutic implications of silymarin’s use in periodontitis.

Conclusion

This in vitro study demonstrates that silymarin alleviates LPS-induced cellular injury, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses, while concurrently inhibiting RANKL-stimulated osteoclast differentiation in the murine RAW264.7 macrophage/osteoclast precursor cell line. These protective effects are associated with enhanced autophagic activity and activation of the AMPK/Sirt1 signaling axis in this cell model. Importantly, all findings presented here are strictly limited to this in vitro monoculture system, and thus represent only preliminary exploratory evidence of silymarin’s bioactivity. Further systematic validation is critically required, including: (1) in vivo verification of silymarin’s protective effects against alveolar bone loss and gingival inflammation in animal models of periodontitis; (2) functional mechanistic validation (via pharmacological inhibition or genetic silencing) to confirm the causal dependency of silymarin’s effects on the AMPK/Sirt1-autophagy axis; (3) evaluation of silymarin’s activity in multicellular co-culture systems that more closely mimic the complex pathophysiological microenvironment of human periodontitis.
