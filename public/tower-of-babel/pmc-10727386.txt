Right Heart Remodeling After Pulmonary Valve Replacement in Patients With Pulmonary Atresia or Critical Stenosis With Intact Ventricular Septum
Authors: Margaret Irwin, Lindsey Reynolds, Geoffrey Binney, Stuart Lipsitz, Sunil J Ghelani, David M Harrild, Christopher W Baird, Tal Geva, David W Brown
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with pulmonary atresia or critical pulmonary stenosis with intact ventricular septum (PA/IVS) and biventricular circulation may require pulmonary valve replacement (PVR). Right ventricular (RV) remodeling after PVR is well described in tetralogy of Fallot (TOF); we sought to investigate RV changes in PA/IVS using cardiac magnetic resonance imaging.Methods and ResultsA retrospective cohort of patients with PA/IVS who underwent PVR at Boston Children's Hospital from 1995 to 2021 with cardiac magnetic resonance imaging before and after PVR was matched 1:3 with patients with TOF by age at PVR. Median regression modeling was performed with post‐PVR indexed RV end‐diastolic volume as the primary outcome. A total of 20 patients with PA/IVS (cases) were matched with 60 patients with TOF (controls), with median age at PVR of 14 years. Pre‐PVR indexed RV end‐diastolic volume was similar between groups; cases had higher RV ejection fraction (51.4% versus 48.6%; P=0.03). Pre‐PVR RV free wall and left ventricular (LV) longitudinal strain were similar, although LV midcavity circumferential strain was decreased in cases (−15.6 versus −17.1; P=0.001). At a median of 2 years after PVR, indexed RV end‐diastolic volume was similarly reduced; cases continued to have higher RV ejection fraction (52.3% versus 46.9%; P=0.007) with less reduction in RV mass (Δ4.5 versus 9.6 g/m2; P=0.004). Post‐PVR, RV and LV longitudinal strain remained unchanged, and LV circumferential strain was similar, although lower in cases.ConclusionsCompared with patients with TOF, patients with PA/IVS demonstrate similar RV remodeling after PVR, with lower reduction in RV mass and comparatively higher RV ejection fraction. Although no differences were detected in peak systolic RV or LV strain values, further investigation of diastolic parameters is needed.Keywords: congenital heart disease, pulmonary atresia, strain imaging, valve replacementSubject Categories: Clinical Studies, Congenital Heart Disease, Remodeling, Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

What Are the Clinical Implications?

These data offer support to the common clinical practice of basing the timing of pulmonary valve replacement in patients with pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum on studies from patients with repaired tetralogy of Fallot; however, further investigation is needed to evaluate other metrics, such as differences in diastolic function, that may influence long‐term outcomes.

Patients with pulmonary atresia or critical pulmonary stenosis (CPS) with intact ventricular septum (PA/IVS) typically have right ventricular (RV) hypertrophy, hypoplasia, and both systolic and diastolic dysfunction at birth. The preferred treatment is the establishment of antegrade pulmonary blood flow through interventional catheterization or surgical techniques, which creates the potential for progressive RV dilation in the absence of a competent pulmonary valve and severe pulmonary regurgitation (PR). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Chronic volume load on the RV results in dilation and eventual dysfunction of the ventricle, and pulmonary valve replacement (PVR) is required in most of these patients. 8 , 9

Changes in RV size and function following PVR have been well studied in patients with tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 ; however, similar data are not available for patients with PA/IVS undergoing PVR. The goal of PVR in patients with both PA/IVS and TOF is to preserve myocardial function and reverse the RV dilation resulting from the volume load caused by chronic PR. Understanding the remodeling response to PVR in this population is important as the timing of PVR is often empiric, imaging based, and commonly performed in asymptomatic patients. In the absence of data specific to PA/IVS, decisions about timing of PVR often rely on studies on patients with TOF. Hence, this study seeks to characterize volumetric and functional changes in the RV after PVR in patients with PA/IVS compared with those with TOF.

Methods

Patients

We conducted a single‐center retrospective cohort review of patients with PA/IVS or CPS who underwent PVR for severe PR at Boston Children's Hospital from 1995 to 2021. Native pulmonary valve anatomy and ventricular septum status was determined by initial preoperative echocardiography. CPS was defined as ductal‐dependent circulation, initial intervention on the pulmonary valve at age <2 weeks, or both. Before matching, patients managed with single‐ventricle circulation or a subsequent 1.5‐ventricle repair were excluded; those without cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging <5 years before and >5 years following PVR were also excluded. Twenty patients with PA/IVS were then matched in a 1:3 ratio with 60 control patients with TOF by age (±1 year) at PVR, with similar requirements for pre‐PVR and post‐PVR CMR imaging at our institution. The study was approved by the institutional review board at Boston Children's Hospital, and individual patient consent was waived. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon request.

Demographic, Surgical, and Electrophysiological Data

Baseline and clinical characteristics were extracted from the medical record, including demographic information, presence of a genetic disorder, weight, and body surface area at the time of PVR. The following anatomic variables were recorded: native pulmonary valve morphology, status of the ventricular septum, and additional anatomic anomalies. Anatomic criteria for patients with PA/IVS and CPS were confirmed in both the preoperative echocardiogram report and operative note. The following preoperative surgical and electrophysiological data were recorded for the PA/IVS cohort and TOF controls: age at initial pulmonary repair, age at and type of prior cardiac interventions, time between initial pulmonary repair and PVR, pre‐PVR cardiopulmonary exercise testing data if performed, and presence of arrhythmia on Holter monitor. In addition, operative notes were reviewed to collect data about PVR implantation, including surgical techniques, age at PVR, valve size, valve morphology, presence of tricuspid valve repair, additional interventions, and status of the atrial septum. Postoperative data included documented cardiac reintervention, age, clinical status, post‐PVR cardiopulmonary exercise testing data if performed, and New York Heart Status evaluation at most recent follow‐up.

Echocardiography

Echocardiographic data were obtained from clinical reports, or, if missing, on secondary review of primary images (D.W.B.). Data collected included qualitative assessments of RV and left ventricular (LV) function and degree of dilation, pulmonary and tricuspid valve regurgitation (TR), and quantitative assessments of RV systolic pressure and pulmonary stenosis. Qualitative ventricular dysfunction or dilation was characterized as none or trivial, mild, moderate, or severe.

Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance

CMR imaging data were collected from clinical reports, or, if missing, on secondary review of images (S.J.G.); data included LV and RV ejection fractions, end‐diastolic and end‐systolic volumes, mass, and pulmonary and TR fractions. Volumetric data were indexed to body surface area. Details of the CMR protocol have been previously published. 14 Briefly, all CMRs were performed on a 1.5‐T scanner (Achieva; Phillips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands) using surface coils selected on the basis of patient size. Imaging included a 12 to 14 slice stack (slice thickness, 8–10 mm) of breath‐hold, ECG‐gated, balanced steady‐state free precession cine acquisitions in the short‐axis plane to completely cover both ventricles. Twenty images per cardiac cycle were acquired and reconstructed to 30 images per cardiac cycle. Feature tracking analysis was performed using commercial software (cvi42; Circle Cardiovascular Imaging version 5.13, Calgary, AB, Canada). The following strain variables were collected: RV longitudinal strain (free wall strain on a 4‐chamber view); LV longitudinal strain (4‐chamber view); and LV circumferential strain (midcavity). To perform the analysis, ventricular endocardial and epicardial contours were manually drawn at 1 phase of the cardiac cycle and automatically propagated for the full cardiac cycle by the software (Figure 1A–1C). Strain measurements were performed by 2 trained operators (L.R. and M.I.) who each interpreted a random half sample of the entire study population. Tracking was inspected, and the contours were manually adjusted in case of poor tracking.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize variables. Frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and mean±SD and median (interquartile range) for continuous and ordinal variables, were provided as appropriate. Categorical patient characteristics were compared between the PA/IVS and TOF groups using a Rao‐Scott χ2 test, 15 and continuous patient characteristics were compared using a Mann‐Whitney‐Wilcoxon test, 16 accounting for clustering attributable to matching.

Because the main outcome data were not distributed normally, generalized estimating equations for the median 17 , 18 (taking into account clustering attributable to matching) were fit with a fixed effect for diagnosis (PA/IVS versus TOF), with reduction (change) in indexed RV end‐diastolic volume (RVEDV) post‐PVR as the primary outcome. Secondary analyses provided covariate‐adjusted estimates of the diagnosis effect by fitting the model above adjusting for 2 covariates deemed as potential confounding variables: PVR size and degree of TR. These analyses were repeated for the secondary outcomes.

The entire population of patients with PA/IVS who met criteria for the study were included, and a power calculation was performed to generate an estimate of an appropriately sized control TOF cohort. To have at least 80% power (with a 5% type 1 error) to detect a ratio of medians of at least 1.4 in the 2 groups, a 1:3 subjects with PA/IVS to TOF match design was selected for this study, with 20 subjects with PA/IVS and 60 matched subjects with TOF. In this power calculation, the test statistic used is a robust generalized estimating equations Wilcoxon rank‐sum test (not assuming normality and accounting for clustering attributable to matching), and we assume an intracluster (matching) correlation coefficient of 0.01 and a coefficient of variation of 0.5. A few patients in each group (1 with PA/IVS, 3 with TOF controls, 5% respectively) had CMR sequences that were of insufficient quality for strain analysis. As strain was a secondary outcome, these patients were retained in the analysis, and generalized estimating equations were used to impute the missing values for these patients. 19 A missing value rate of ≤5% has been shown in the statistical literature to be inconsequential on biasing the results. 20 We considered a 2‐sided P≤0.05 as statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 20 patients with PA/IVS or CPS met study inclusion criteria. Baseline patient characteristics are listed in Table 1. PA/IVS was the primary diagnosis in 15 subjects, whereas 5 had CPS; median age at PVR was 14.8 years (interquartile range, 5–32 years), and 12 patients were men (60%). Initial neonatal intervention was surgical in 15 (75%), consisting of Blalock‐Taussig‐Thomas shunt in 5, Blalock‐Taussig‐Thomas shunt with RV outflow tract patch in 3, and both in 8. Initial neonatal intervention was transcatheter in 9 patients (45%), with pulmonary valve perforation and balloon dilation. Of the patients managed with initial catheter intervention, 4 underwent additional surgery in the first month of life, with RV outflow tract patch in 4 as well as Blalock‐Taussig‐Thomas shunt in 2 of these. For the entire cohort, 6 (30%) had an additional surgical intervention following initial repair, including TV plasty in 3 (15%).

PVR Data

Median age at PVR was similar between groups, 14.7 years for the PA/IVS group and 14.9 years for the TOF group (P=0.51). Tricuspid valve repair was performed with PVR surgery in 14 subjects with PA/IVS (70%) and 7 patients with TOF (12%) (P=0.001). All patients received a bioprosthetic pulmonary valve, with the most common being Carpentier Edwards Magna in 33 (41%) and Sorin Mitroflow in 26 (33%). The median valve size was similar between PA/IVS (25 mm) and TOF (24 mm) cohorts (P=0.12). In terms of placement technique, 1 patient (5%) in the PA/IVS cohort had transcatheter PVR, and 11 patients (18%) in the TOF cohort had transcatheter PVR (P<0.001).

Post‐PVR Imaging Data

Post‐PVR echocardiographic imaging was performed at a median of 18.6 months post‐PVR with a statistically significant difference between groups in timing (33 months for PA/IVS versus 12 months for TOF; P=0.004) (Table 2). Findings included similar reduction in RV size, with moderate or greater dilation present in 20% of both PA/IVS and TOF cohorts following PVR (P=0.98). No subjects with PA/IVS had moderate or greater post‐PVR RV dysfunction by echocardiography, compared with 4 patients with TOF (7%; P=0.93); similarly, no subjects with PA/IVS had moderate or greater LV dysfunction post‐PVR, compared with 2 patients with TOF (3%; P=0.95). However, TR was more common in the PA/IVS cohort, with moderate or greater TR in 35% versus only 5% of patients with TOF (P=0.014).

CMR data were obtained at a median of 2 years after PVR, with no significant difference in timing between cohorts (2.85 years for PA/IVS versus 1.65 years for TOF cohort; P=0.13). Both cohorts had significant and similar reductions in indexed RVEDV after PVR, from 166 to 118 mL/m2 in the PA/IVS group and from 168 to 116 mL/m2 in the TOF group (Table 3 and Figure 2A and 2B). Patients with PA/IVS had higher post‐PVR RV EF (52.3% versus 46.9%; P=0.007) with less reduction in indexed RV mass (Δ4.5 versus 9.6 g/m2; P=0.004) compared with the TOF group. LV volumes and LV EF were similar.

CMR strain analyses post‐PVR (Table 4 and Figure 2C and 2D) demonstrated that RV free wall longitudinal strain was similar between PA/IVS and TOF patient groups after PVR (−18.1 versus −18.1; P=0.2). Moreover, LV longitudinal strain also remained similar both within and between PA/IVS versus TOF groups (−14.7 versus −15.7; P=0.18). However, the LV midcavity circumferential strain remained reduced in patients with PA/IVS versus TOF post‐PVR (−15.9 versus −18.7; P=0.001), with no significant pre‐post PVR differences within groups (Δ −0.3 versus −1.6; P=0.47).

Post‐PVR Follow‐Up Clinical Data

Median age at last clinical follow‐up was 22 years for both cohorts, with no statistically signficant difference in follow‐up time between cohorts (P=0.55). There was no mortality after PVR in either PA/IVS or TOF cohort. At follow‐up, 95% of both patients with PA/IVS or CPS and TOF were in New York Heart Association status class 1 (P=0.99). No patients in either group were diagnosed with atrial or ventricular tachyarrhythmias, including by surveillance 24‐hour Holter monitor testing performed in 60% of patients with PA/IVS and 45% of patients with TOF. Cardiopulmonary exercise tests were performed in a subset of both cohorts; paired pre‐PVR/post‐PVR exercise tests were available in 10 subjects with PA/IVS and 18 age‐matched TOF control patients with similarly paired testing. There were no statistically significant between‐group differences in pre‐PVR and post‐PVR exercise parameters (peak and percentage predicted peak VO2, heart rate, and O2 pulse).

Discussion

In this study, we sought to characterize volumetric and functional changes in the RV after PVR in patients with PA/IVS and CPS using CMR imaging with comparison to a control group of age‐matched patients with TOF. In this cohort of 20 patients, we found that volumetric changes following PVR were remarkably similar to TOF controls, with similar marked reductions in indexed RVEDV. RV indexed mass changed less in patients with PA/IVS after PVR than in TOF controls, although TOF controls had generally greater pre‐PVR RV mass to begin with. Similarly, RV EF was slightly better in patients with PA/IVS than TOF controls post‐PVR, although that was also true of pre‐PVR as well. LV volumes and EF did not significantly change in either group after PVR.

Several large cohort studies in the TOF population have demonstrated the impact of PVR on the RV, with generally consistent findings that the RV will often remodel with 30% to 40% reduction in RVEDV and generally no improvement in RV or LV EF. 10 Prior studies have shown that RV response to volume overload is different for patients with PA/IVS. 8 One single‐center study compared the effect of PVR in patients with PA/IVS with patients with TOF 21 ; however, this study was limited by lack of post‐PVR CMR imaging follow‐up (only 5/13 patients) and focused largely on TR and RV size. Our study confirms that the same volumetric changes occur after PVR in the PA/IVS population to those with TOF, albeit with slightly better pre‐PVR and post‐PVR RV EF. More important, these similar volumetric changes in the PA/IVS group occurred in the setting of significant TR in most patients, and this residual TR may be masking what would otherwise be more significant reductions in RV volume after PVR. Further analysis with a larger cohort of subjects (such as a multicenter study) may yield additional insight into this issue. Furthermore, all patients in our study received a bioprosthetic PVR, and the choice of PVR type likely has a significant relationship to long‐term remodeling of the RV in PA/IVS. One recent study in the TOF population demonstrated more rapid structural valve deterioration in those receiving bioprosthetic versus homograft conduits. 22 Further research is necessary to determine if patients with PA/IVS would have better long‐term outcomes with alternative valve replacement strategies.

The present study also demonstrated that patients with PA/IVS had no significant change in any of the strain components (including LV and RV longitudinal, or LV circumferential) following PVR. The lack of change in RV strain parameters in PA/IVS is consistent with prior published data in patients with TOF, 10 demonstrating again similarities in RV functional characteristics in these 2 populations in response to PVR. However, patients with TOF in published reports have been demonstrated to have some improvement in LV strain parameters following PVR, 10 which we did not find in the present study in either group. This is perhaps related to the small sample sizes in our study; the finding of reduced circumferential strain in patients with PA/IVS is an interesting observation that deserves further investigation.

There may be significant differences in diastolic RV function in these 2 patient populations that merit further study. CMR diastolic parameters, such as diastolic strain rate and transatrioventricular valve filling rate, have been much less evaluated to date and require significant postprocessing, but may yield additional insights. 23 , 24 The response of the right atrium and the inferior vena cava to PVR and tricuspid valve repair in patients with TOF and PA/IVS by CMR has not been well investigated and is worthy of further investigation. 25 , 26 Other CMR techniques may hold promise as well, such as wave‐intensity analysis to evaluate ventriculoarterial coupling; these have been applied in patients with TOF, with demonstrated associations between PR and adverse ventriculoarterial interactions 27 ; these and other emerging imaging techniques will no doubt improve our understanding of this complex physiology. Given the lack of improvement in RV function or exercise test performance after PVR, changes in diastolic parameters may prove to be more important considerations to incorporate into clinical decision‐making in the PA/IVS population.

Limitations

Because of the retrospective design, our study has several limitations, perhaps most important the enrollment criteria for both subject and controls of CMR data within specified time frames pre‐PVR and post‐PVR. This bias may be mitigated by the fact that CMR has become a frequently used diagnostic tool for the clinical management of patients with TOF, and by extension, for patients with PA/IVS with severe PR and dilated RVs. The small number of patients with CPS included in the study cohort is technically a different cardiac diagnosis than membranous PA/IVS; however, these diagnoses are so closely related in cardiac physiology, neonatal management, and subsequent need for potential PVR for severe PR and dilated RV that they were included in the present study. In addition, a small number of CMR images were not of sufficient quality for strain analysis. The CMR RV strain measured in this study was the 4‐chamber free‐wall longitudinal strain, which represents only a portion of the RV and thus may not be able to detect other changes that may have occurred post‐PVR. Finally, we did not perform a formal interobserver variability study for the 2 CMR strain interpreters; however, this technique has been previously demonstrated to have excellent intraobserver and interobserver reproducibility. 28

Conclusions

Patients with PA/IVS with dilated RVs demonstrate similar RV remodeling post‐PVR to patients with TOF, with similar reduction in RVEDV, albeit lower reduction in RV mass and slightly better post‐PVR RV EF. CMR strain imaging found no significant within‐ or between‐group differences pre‐post PVR in RV or LV systolic parameters. These data offer support to the common clinical practice of basing the timing of PVR in patients with PA/IVS on studies from patients with repaired TOF. However, further investigation is needed to evaluate other metrics, such as differences in diastolic function, that may influence long‐term outcomes.
