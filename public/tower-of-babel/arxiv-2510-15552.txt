Think Parallax: Solving Multi-Hop Problems via Multi-View Knowledge-Graph-Based Retrieval-Augmented Generation
Jinliang Liu

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) excel at language understanding but often hallucinate and struggle with multi-hop reasoning. Knowledge-graph-based retrieval-augmented generation (KG-RAG) offers grounding, yet most methods rely on flat embeddings and noisy path exploration. We propose ParallaxRAG, a framework that symmetrically decouples queries and graph triples into multi-view spaces, enabling a robust retrieval architecture that explicitly enforces head diversity while constraining weakly related paths. Central to our approach is the observation that different attention heads specialize in semantic relations at distinct reasoning stages, contributing to different hops of the reasoning chain. This specialization allows ParallaxRAG to construct cleaner subgraphs and guide LLMs through grounded, step-wise reasoning. Experiments on WebQSP and CWQ, under our unified, reproducible setup (BGE-M3 + Llama3.1-8B), demonstrate competitive retrieval and QA performance, alongside reduced hallucination and good generalization. Our results highlight multi-view head specialization as a principled direction for knowledge-grounded multi-hop reasoning. Our implementation will be released as soon as the paper is accepted.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have shown impressive proficiency in processing natural language tasks
Brown et al. 2020
;
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Bubeck et al. 2023
;
Touvron et al. 2023
. Nonetheless, challenges such as hallucinations
Huang et al. 2025
;
Tonmoy et al. 2024
, outdated knowledge
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Zhang and Choi 2023
, and limited expertise in specialized domains
Ling et al. 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2023
undermine their reliability. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) offers a robust solution by integrating real-time external data into LLM inference
Lewis et al. 2020
;
Gao et al. 2025
. Beyond text-centric RAG, recent research suggests that incorporating knowledge graphs (KGs) can provide a superior foundation
Jin et al. 2024
;
Wu and Tsioutsiouliklis 2024
and improved reasoning capabilities
Luo et al. 2023
;
Mavromatis and Karypis 2024
. However, extracting answers from KGs remains challenging
Yasunaga et al. 2021
, especially for questions requiring multi-hop reasoning—where the model must traverse multiple entities and relations to infer the correct answer
Fu et al. 2020
;
Saxena et al. 2020
. This process is non-trivial: it often involves long reasoning chains
Yasunaga et al. 2021
;
Luo et al. 2023
, error propagation across hops
Mavromatis and Karypis 2024
, and combinatorial growth of irrelevant paths
He et al. 2024
, all of which hinder the scalability and reliability of KG-RAG systems.

However, multi-hop KG-RAG faces fundamental limitations in both knowledge representation and reasoning. On one hand, embedding the entire knowledge base into a single, monolithic space creates a flat representation ill-suited for the step-wise traversal required for multi-hop inference
Ji et al. 2021
;
Fu et al. 2020
. On the other hand, current reasoning strategies present a stark trade-off. Iterative LLM calls, while powerful, incur prohibitive computational overhead and latency
Sun et al. 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2024
. Concurrently, exhaustive path exploration often accumulates noise along excessively long chains, which degrades answer accuracy
Liu et al. 2023
;
Yao et al. 2023
;
He et al. 2024
. Thus, we argue that an ideal multi–hop retriever requires a fundamental shift from monolithic embeddings
Fu et al. 2020
;
Ji et al. 2021
. It should operate on two principles: first, the ability to decouple the query and KG into step-focused views
Min et al. 2019
;
Guo et al. 2022
, and second, a mechanism to dynamically compose these views into a coherent reasoning path
Chen et al. 2022
;
Sun et al. 2023
. This decompose-and-compose philosophy is essential for any lightweight framework aiming to tackle complex reasoning tasks.

To actualize these principles, we propose ParallaxRAG, a framework centered on a core mechanism of symmetric decoupling. We leverage a Transformer’s multi-head activations (Figure
1
) to decompose the query into a multi-view representation
Vaswani et al. 2017
;
Besta et al. 2024
, while concurrently projecting the graph’s triples into an aligned, multi-faceted latent space
Mavromatis and Karypis 2024
;
Li et al. 2024
. To operationalize this principle, we design a synergistic framework comprising two key strategies. First, a novel Pairwise Similarity Regularization (PSR) mechanism (detailed derivation in Appendix
A.1
), integrated into the Directional Distance Encoding (DDE) phase, explicitly encourages functional specialization and prevents representational collapse by penalizing the similarity between head representations. Second, guided by these diverse representations, a lightweight MLP retriever performs parallel triple-scoring to effectively prune the search space and mitigate subgraph noise. This holistic approach moves beyond single-vector paradigms, enabling a more nuanced and fine-grained matching of a complex multi-hop question to the corresponding structural evidence in the graph
Khattab and Zaharia 2020
.

The contributions of this paper are outlined as follows:

•

Pioneering a Multi-Head Decoupling Architecture for KG-RAG.
To our knowledge, we are the first to introduce this design to KG-RAG, where different heads are enabled to capture complementary relational cues at varying reasoning depths through end-to-end training tailored for retrieval tasks. we also developed a synergistic exploration–exploitation method, including Pairwise Similarity Regulation (PSR), to enhance robust retrieval and head specialization.

•

Providing an In-Depth Analysis of Head Specialization in Multi-Hop Reasoning.
Our work is the first to provide strong empirical evidence for a relay effect, where distinct sets of heads are activated for different reasoning stages. This analysis is enabled by a novel set of metrics we introduce to quantify head-level contribution and effectiveness (detailed methodology in Appendix
C
).

•

Demonstrating Robust Performance and Generalizability.
Our model not only shows impressive performance for in-domain performance in terms of Hit Rate and Macro-F1 on WebQSP and CWQ, but proves far more robust than prior work. In a challenging zero-shot transfer, it outperforms the SOTA baseline by a substantial 7.68 Macro-F1 points, demonstrating its ability to learn transferable reasoning mechanisms that provide higher-quality context for the downstream generator.

The architecture of ParallaxRAG, illustrating its three main stages: Preprocess, Retrieval, and Generate. 3
The ParallaxRAG Framework

To address the challenges of multi-hop question answering in knowledge graphs, we design ParallaxRAG around a core principle: a single embedding collapses complex reasoning into one view, whereas multi-hop reasoning demands multiple perspectives
Fu et al. 2020
. Our framework therefore follows a split-and-compose philosophy
Min et al. 2019
: first, we symmetrically decouple both the query and the graph’s components into head-specific views; second, we introduce a synergistic exploration–exploitation dynamic to balance representational diversity and retrieval precision. This section details how these components interact to construct clean, reasoning-ready subgraphs.

3.1
Symmetric Decoupling of Query and Graph Views

To break the informational bottleneck of single-vector representations, our first step is to create a multi-view embedding space. In standard KG-RAG, a complex query is encoded into a single vector
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which inevitably conflates semantics required for different reasoning stages. Instead, we leverage the internal structure of a pre-trained text encoder. From its final layer, we extract the distinct output of each attention head for the query, yielding
H
diverse and specialized query views:

Q^{\text{views}}=\{\mathbf{q}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}}\}_{k=1}^{H}.

To enable effective matching, the textual components of each triple
\tau=(h,r,t)
on the graph side—that is, the entities and relations—are symmetrically decomposed into the same
H
latent spaces. This process yields head-specific representations for each entity (
\mathbf{e}_{k}
) and relation (
\mathbf{r}_{k}
).

This symmetric decoupling creates parallel retrieval spaces where each head can match a particular semantic aspect (e.g., entity localization vs. relation chaining). Unlike MRAG
Besta et al. 2024
, which treats multi-heads as parallel embedding channels without structural alignment, our symmetric decomposition of both queries and graph components ensures that each head operates in a grounded, matched semantic space, rather than acting as an isolated retriever.

3.2
Exploration–Exploitation for Robust Multi-View Retrieval

Decoupling alone is insufficient, as naively retrieving from
H
views risks two issues: (1)
representational collapse
, where heads converge to redundant features; and (2)
subgraph noise
, where weakly-related signals accumulate. To overcome these, we introduce two complementary mechanisms that form a robust retrieval dynamic.

Exploration: Pairwise Similarity Regulation (PSR).

Our exploration mechanism is integrated into the Directional Distance Encoding (DDE) framework
Li et al. 2024
, which propagates structural signals from topic entities. A standard head-wise DDE risks representational collapse. We mitigate this by introducing a novel Pairwise Similarity Regulation (PSR) mechanism into each DDE propagation layer.

Let
\tilde{H}_{k}^{(\ell+1)}
be the node representation matrix for head
k
after a standard message passing step at layer
\ell
. Before this update is finalized, we first derive a summary vector
\mathbf{s}_{k}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|}
for each head by summing the feature dimensions of its node representations and applying L2 normalization. This vector captures the head’s activation distribution across all nodes. We then quantify the representational overlap for head
k
via a redundancy score:

r_{k}^{(\ell)}=\sum_{j\neq k}\langle\mathbf{s}_{k}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{s}_{j}^{(\ell)}\rangle.

This score is used to compute a dynamic, diversity-promoting coefficient
\alpha_{k}^{(\ell)}
, which directly modulates the preliminary update:

\alpha_{k}^{(\ell)}=\exp(-\beta\cdot r_{k}^{(\ell)}),\qquad H_{k}^{(\ell+1)}=\alpha_{k}^{(\ell)}\cdot\tilde{H}_{k}^{(\ell+1)},

where
\beta
is a hyperparameter controlling the penalty strength (sensitivity analysis in Appendix
B.4
). This process dynamically scales down the updates for heads that are representationally similar to others, incentivizing them to capture unique relational patterns and ensuring a diverse set of "experts" for the downstream task.

Exploitation: Weakly-Supervised Gating.

We introduce a query-adaptive gating mechanism to exploit the most relevant expert heads while suppressing noise. This gate is a lightweight linear layer that maps a global query embedding
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to a set of head-importance logits. This global embedding
\mathbf{q}
is the final-layer ‘[CLS]‘ token representation from the text encoder, capturing a holistic understanding of the query. This is distinct from the specialized head-level views
\{\mathbf{q}_{k}\}
used for fine-grained matching. The logits are transformed via softmax into a query-specific attention vector
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{H}
:

\bm{\alpha}=\text{softmax}(W_{g}\mathbf{q}).

For each of the
H
views, a shared prediction MLP scores all candidate triples, producing a score matrix
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|\times H}
. We then use the attention vector
\bm{\alpha}
to compute a weighted sum, yielding the final aggregated scores
\mathbf{s}
and the predicted retrieval distribution
P_{\text{pred}}
:

\mathbf{s}=Z\bm{\alpha},\qquad P_{\text{pred}}=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{s}).

To train the system end-to-end, we use a weakly-supervised listwise objective
Cao et al. 2007
(detailed formulation in Appendix
A.2
). Following
Zhang et al. 2022
, we identify "gold-standard" triples from shortest paths to form a target distribution
P_{\text{true}}
. The model is trained by minimizing the KL divergence between
P_{\text{pred}}
and
P_{\text{true}}
, which jointly optimizes the gate to select relevant heads and the heads to score correct reasoning paths.

3.3
LLM-Based Grounded Reasoning

Finally, the top-k triples from the retrieved subgraph
G_{q}
are linearized into a textual context and concatenated with the question. This prompt is fed to an LLM, which is guided to ground its reasoning on this retrieved evidence rather than relying solely on its parametric memory
Lewis et al. 2020
. In essence, ParallaxRAG transforms the static multi-head attention architecture into a dynamic, task-aware mechanism for division of labor across reasoning steps, achieving both efficient and precise multi-hop KGQA.

Retrieval Performance (
RQ1.

As presented in Table
1
, our proposed ParallaxRAG consistently achieves impressive performance across almost all evaluation settings and metrics. It demonstrates exceptional robustness and the highest retrieval effectiveness, particularly on complex questions that require multi-hop reasoning chains.

To provide a fine-grained analysis, we break down the performance based on the number of reasoning hops. ParallaxRAG outperforms all baselines across the vast majority of settings on both WebQSP and CWQ answer entity recall as shown in Table
1
. On WebQSP, our model consistently outperforms all baselines, achieving top scores of 0.986 in 1-hop entity recall and 0.899 in 2-hop entity recall. The performance gains are more pronounced on complex queries; on CWQ 2-hop and
\geq
3-hops questions, ParallaxRAG shows competitive entity recall metric to 0.928, surpassing the strong SubgraphRAG baseline.

For simple queries, ParallaxRAG demonstrates a strong capability on fundamental retrieval tasks, achieving Shortest Path recall scores of 0.966 on WebQSP and 0.916 on CWQ. On the most challenging queries (CWQ
\geq
3-hops), a key trade-off reveals our method’s unique advantage: while strong baselines are competitive on Shortest Path Triple Recall (0.626), ParallaxRAG excels on the more impactful Answer Entity Recall metric (0.753 vs. SubgraphRAG’s 0.741). We attribute this advanced capability to our multi-view framework with its synergistic exploration-exploitation mechanisms. The Pairwise Similarity Regulation (PSR) explicitly incentivizes heads to explore diverse reasoning paths beyond the shortest one, while the query-adaptive gate learns to select the combination of heads most likely to construct a subgraph that contains the answer. This confirms our approach is better equipped to capture the complex structural information essential for multi-hop reasoning.

Regarding efficiency, baseline latencies were measured on a professional-grade NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPU, while ParallaxRAG was evaluated on a consumer-grade RTX 4090. Our method achieves wall-clock times of 42 seconds on WebQSP and 90 seconds on CWQ (excluding KG I/O and precomputation), substantially outperforming multi-turn LLM-based baselines like RoG (948s on WebQSP, 2327s on CWQ) and general RAG methods such as G-Retriever (672s on WebQSP, 1530s on CWQ). This demonstrates ParallaxRAG’s superior computational efficiency for multi-hop KG-RAG tasks, enabling high-quality retrieval with reduced hardware demands.

4.3
Attention Head Specialization
(RQ2.)

Our model’s attention heads exhibit a dynamic handoff pattern contingent on query complexity (Figure
3
) , employing a direct strategy with early-stage heads on short-hop queries (WebQSP), while dynamically switching to late-stage heads for long-chain reasoning (CWQ). This functional specialization is evident as a linear probe can predict the reasoning step from head activations with 46.5% accuracy (vs. 25% random baseline). To causally validate this division of labor, we designed a Difference-in-Differences-in-Differences (DDD) intervention to isolate the effect of ablating specialist vs. random heads, defined as:

\text{DDD}=\left[(\Delta_{\text{Long}}^{\text{Expert}}-\Delta_{\text{Short}}^{\text{Expert}})\right]-\left[(\Delta_{\text{Long}}^{\text{Random}}-\Delta_{\text{Short}}^{\text{Random}})\right]

(1)

where
\Delta
denotes performance on queries of a specific length (Long,
\geq
2-hops/Short, 1-hop) after ablating a specific type of head (Specialist/Random).

The significant DDD estimate of -0.0184 (95% CI: [-0.0248, -0.0045],
p
=.0055) provides strong causal evidence that these specialist heads are functionally non-substitutable for complex reasoning. Furthermore, this mechanism’s robustness is confirmed by its strong zero-shot transfer performance from WebQSP to CWQ, where our model improves over a strong baseline by 7.68 Macro-F1 points (Table
3
). Collectively, these results validate that dynamic head specialization is the core adaptive strategy enabling our model’s multi-hop reasoning capabilities.

4.5
Ablation Study

To validate the core architecture of ParallaxRAG and the effectiveness of its synergistic components, we conducted a comprehensive ablation study on the WebQSP and CWQ datasets. Table
4
presents the performance comparison across different ablation configurations. We focused on evaluating the contributions of the following key components:

Act I: Validating the Core Multi-Head Learned Architecture

(a)
Split Vector Baseline: This splits the query vector into pseudo-heads without employing a multi-head architecture. This configuration yields a Macro-F1 drop of 2.31 points on WebQSP and 6.06 points on CWQ, illustrating that the multi-head architecture is not merely naive parallel retrieval. Instead, it leverages specialized head representations to capture distinct relational cues across reasoning stages, enhancing the model’s ability to construct coherent multi-hop reasoning paths.

(b)
Single Vector Baseline: This employs a standard single-vector representation. This variant exhibits a substantial performance decline (Macro-F1 drop of 1.13 points on WebQSP and 1.47 points on CWQ), underscoring the necessity of multi-head representations for handling multi-hop reasoning, as single vectors are prone to representational collapse and noise accumulation.

Act II: Validating Synergistic Components

(c) w/o PSR
Removing Pairwise Similarity Regularization (PSR) leads to redundant head representations. The performance drop showcase (Macro-F1 decline of 0.73 points on WebQSP and 1.21 points on CWQ) PSR’s role in enforcing head diversity and preventing representational collapse.

(d) w/o Query-aware Gating
Replacing the adaptive gating mechanism with simple averaging. This setup results (Hit rate decline of 12.16 points on WebQSP and 20.15 points on CWQ), demonstrating that query-aware gating facilitates dynamic weighting of head contributions, thereby enhancing retrieval precision.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we addressed the critical challenge of multi-hop reasoning in KG-RAG, a task often hindered by the representational bottleneck of single-vector embeddings. We introduced ParallaxRAG, a novel framework centered on a symmetric, multi-view decoupling of queries and knowledge graphs. Our core contribution is the demonstration that the internal multi-head attention mechanism of a Transformer can be repurposed into a powerful, specialized retrieval architecture.
Our in-depth analysis revealed, for the first time to our knowledge, different attention heads are specialized for processing semantic relations pertinent to distinct stages of the reasoning process. This finding moves beyond treating multi-head attention as a monolithic block, highlighting its potential for fine-grained, interpretable reasoning. This principled design translates directly into robust performance, where ParallaxRAG achieves impressive results on the WebQSP and CWQ benchmarks and exhibits remarkable generalization in challenging zero-shot settings. We believe that this principle of leveraging head specialization for multi-view reasoning offers a promising and scalable direction for enhancing the reliability and depth of future knowledge-grounded language models.

Appendix A
Implementation Details of the ParallaxRAG Framework

A.1
Detailed Derivation of Pairwise Similarity Regulation (PSR)

The Pairwise Similarity Regulation (PSR) mechanism is integrated into each layer of the Directional Distance Encoding (DDE) to foster representational diversity.

1. DDE Propagation.

Given the initial one-hot topic entity features
\mathbf{X}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2}
, the model performs
L_{f}
forward and
L_{r}
reverse propagation layers. Each layer
\ell
computes a preliminary update
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{k}^{(\ell+1)}
for each head
k
via message passing (
MP
):

\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{k}^{(\ell+1)}=\text{MP}(\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H}_{k}^{(\ell)})\quad(\text{forward pass, using edge index }\mathbf{E})

The reverse pass is analogous, using the transposed edge index
\mathbf{E}^{T}
.

2. PSR Computation and Application.

After each propagation step, the preliminary updates
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}
are modulated by PSR. The process begins by computing a node intensity vector
\mathbf{s}_{k}^{(\ell)}
for each head to capture its activation distribution:

\mathbf{s}_{k}^{(\ell)}=\text{L2Norm}\left(\sum_{d}\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{k}^{(\ell)}[:,d]\right)

These vectors are then used to derive a redundancy score
r_{k}^{(\ell)}
from pairwise similarities, which in turn defines the regulation coefficient
\alpha_{k}^{(\ell)}
:

r_{k}^{(\ell)}=\sum_{j\neq k}\langle\mathbf{s}_{i}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{s}_{j}^{(\ell)}\rangle\quad\implies\quad\alpha_{k}^{(\ell)}=\exp(-\beta\cdot r_{k}^{(\ell)})

Finally, this coefficient performs the final update by scaling the preliminary update to produce the final layer output:

\mathbf{H}_{k}^{(\ell+1)}=\alpha_{k}^{(\ell)}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{k}^{(\ell+1)}

3. Final Representation.

The final structural representations
\mathcal{H}_{k}
for each head are formed by collecting the outputs
\{\mathbf{H}_{k}^{(\ell)}\}
from all layers, which are then used for triple scoring.

A.2
Weighted Listwise Training Objective

To handle the severe class imbalance in retrieval, we convert the binary weak supervision signal
\mathbf{y}\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{E}|}
into a weighted target distribution for our listwise objective.

1. Positive Reweighting.

The binary vector
\mathbf{y}
is first normalized. Then, a weight factor
w_{\tau}
is applied to construct the final weighted distribution. In our implementation, we use
w_{\tau}=10
for positive triples (
y_{\tau}=1
) and
w_{\tau}=1
otherwise. This is conceptually similar to the
\alpha
-balancing in Focal Loss
Lin et al. 2017
.

y_{\tau}^{\text{norm}}=\frac{y_{\tau}}{\sum_{\tau^{\prime}}y_{\tau^{\prime}}},\quad y_{\tau}^{\text{weighted}}=\frac{y_{\tau}\cdot w_{\tau}}{\sum_{\tau^{\prime}}(y_{\tau^{\prime}}\cdot w_{\tau^{\prime}})}

2. Final Loss Formulation.

The model is trained by minimizing the weighted listwise cross-entropy (equivalent to KL divergence) between the predicted distribution
P_{\text{pred}}
and the target
y^{\text{weighted}}
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{listwise}}=-\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{E}}y_{\tau}^{\text{weighted}}\,\log\big(P_{\text{pred}}(\tau)+\epsilon\big)

In this section, we analyze the sensitivity of our model to the key hyperparameter

\beta

Appendix C
Attention Head Analysis Details

This section provides the detailed methodology for the attention head analysis presented in Section
4.3
. We formally define the concepts of reasoning
steps
and the three metrics used to evaluate head-level performance.

C.1
Defining Reasoning Steps

To formally quantify head performance during multi-hop reasoning, we define
steps
as the Breadth-First Search (BFS) expansion depth along reasoning paths. This is distinct from the conventional definition of a
hop
(i.e., the final shortest-path length) and allows us to capture head behaviors at each layer of candidate exploration. For example, if the topic entity is “Barack Obama,” Step-1 includes triples directly connected to him, such as
(\text{Obama},\text{born\_in},\text{Honolulu})
. Step-2 then expands from the entities retrieved in Step-1, considering candidates like
(\text{Honolulu},\text{located\_in},\text{Hawaii})
.

C.2
Head Performance Metrics

We define three complementary metrics to evaluate the role and effectiveness of each attention head during the reasoning process.

Contribution

Measures a head’s share of credit for retrieving correct triples at a given step. It is defined as the proportion of correctly retrieved triples for which this head provided the highest score.

\text{Contribution}(h,t)=\frac{|C(h,t)\cap A_{t}\cap G_{t}|}{|A_{t}\cap G_{t}|}

Use Rate

Measures the model’s overall reliance on a head. It is the proportion of samples where a triple scored highest by head
h
was ultimately selected by the model’s gating mechanism at step
t
.

\text{Use Rate}(h,t)=\frac{|\mathcal{S}(h,t)|}{|\mathcal{S}_{t}|}

Hit Rate

Measures the precision of a head’s top-scoring suggestions. It is the percentage of a head’s suggestions selected by the gate that are actually correct.

\text{Hit Rate}(h,t)=\frac{|C(h,t)\cap A_{t}\cap G_{t}|}{|C(h,t)\cap A_{t}|}

where, for any given head
h
and reasoning step
t
,
G_{t}
is the set of ground-truth triples, and
A_{t}
is the set of triples actually retrieved by the gated model. The term
C(h,t)
represents the set of candidate triples for which head
h
provided the highest score among all heads. Finally,
\mathcal{S}_{t}
is the set of all samples that require reasoning at step
t
, while
\mathcal{S}(h,t)
is the subset of those samples where a triple from
C(h,t)
was selected by the final gating mechanism.

C.4
Head Specialization Verification Details

C.4.1
Linear Probing

To quantitatively verify that different attention heads specialize in distinct reasoning stages, we conducted a linear probing experiment. For each reasoning step, we aggregated the output scores from all attention heads to form a feature vector. A logistic regression classifier was then trained on these features to decode the current step number (from 1 to 4). Evaluated under 5-fold cross-validation grouped by sample, the classifier achieved a decoding accuracy that significantly outperformed a random baseline. This result confirms that the activation patterns of the attention heads contain sufficient information to distinguish between different stages of the reasoning process, supporting the hypothesis of functional specialization.

C.4.2
Difference in Difference in Difference

To causally validate the functional importance of specialist heads, we designed a Triple-Difference (DDD) intervention. This analysis isolates the additional performance degradation from ablating specialist heads versus random heads on long-hop (
>1
-hop) questions, relative to short-hop (1-hop) questions. We first compute the Difference-in-Differences (DID) for both the expert-ablation (
\text{DID}_{\text{exp}}
) and random-ablation (
\text{DID}_{\text{rand}}
) scenarios. The final causal estimate is then given by the Triple-Difference:
DDD=\text{DID}_{\text{exp}}-\text{DID}_{\text{rand}}
. Our experiment yielded a statistically significant DDD estimate, confirmed via bootstrap confidence intervals and permutation tests. This provides strong causal evidence that specialist heads are functionally non-substitutable and disproportionately crucial for complex, multi-hop reasoning.

Appendix D
Case Study

We present several representative cases from the CWQ dataset to demonstrate ParallaxRAG’s efficacy in multi-hop question answering, emphasizing its refined subgraph construction, reduced hallucinations, and robust handling of constraints through multi-view decoupling and head specialization.

Case 1 (Figure
7
):

Single-Vector RAG retrieves a noisy subgraph, incorrectly following the containment path
(Nijmegen, …, Netherlands)
while ignoring the adjacency constraint to France, thus outputting
Netherlands
. This failure highlights how its undifferentiated embeddings struggle to enforce multiple constraints. In contrast, ParallaxRAG’s multi-view retrieval isolates the correct evidence path:
(Nijmegen, nearby_airports, Weeze Airport)
,
(Weeze Airport, containedby, Germany)
, and
(Germany, adjoins, France)
. By decoupling and prioritizing geographic constraints, it correctly answers
Germany
.

Case 2 (Figure
8
):

Single-Vector RAG’s flat representation fails to manage two distinct constraints (symbol and bisection). It retrieves a diffuse subgraph, conflates relations, and incorrectly links the Ring-necked Pheasant to Missouri, thus outputting
Missouri
. ParallaxRAG, however, uses constraint-specific heads to retrieve precise and separate facts:
(Ring-necked Pheasant, official_symbol_of, South Dakota)
and
(South Dakota, partially_contains, Missouri River)
. This decomposition allows it to satisfy both constraints and correctly identify
South Dakota
.

Case 3 (Figure
9
):

For this simple transitive query, Single-Vector RAG retrieves sufficient facts, enabling the downstream LLM to correctly infer all three championship years. However, ParallaxRAG’s multi-head architecture retrieves additional constraint-related triples that create conflicting signals for the LLM. The diverse, multi-faceted evidence from different attention heads introduces competing interpretations that overwhelm the LLM’s reasoning process. Instead of performing the straightforward transitive inference, the LLM becomes paralyzed by the abundance of potentially relevant but ultimately distracting contextual information, incorrectly concluding that an answer cannot be determined and outputting
None
