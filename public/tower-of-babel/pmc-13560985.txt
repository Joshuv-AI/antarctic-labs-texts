Magnitude and correlates of modern contraceptive non-use among women of reproductive age in Eswatini: a secondary analysis of data from a population-based cross-sectional survey
Authors: Lindelwa Portia Dlamini, Nondumiso Satiso Dlamini, Mduduzi Colani Shongwe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesWe investigated the magnitude and correlates of modern contraceptive non-use among women of reproductive age (15–49 years) in Eswatini (formerly Swaziland).DesignSecondary analysis of a cross-sectional, nationally representative, population-based, household surveySettingEswatiniParticipantsWomen of reproductive age in the Swaziland HIV Incidence Measurement Survey 3 2021 datasetPrimary outcome measureThe primary outcome was non-use of modern contraceptive methods.ResultsOf 3080 reproductive age women whose data were analysed in this study, 53% were aged 20–34 years, and the majority were from rural areas (66.8%). The weighted prevalence of non-use of modern contraceptives was 33%. The risk of non-use of modern contraceptives was lower among women: aged 20–34 (adjusted risk ratio (ARR)=0.85, 95% CI 0.75 to 0.97); from households of middle wealth quintile (ARR=0.82, 95% CI 0.68 to 0.97); who had given birth at least once (ARR=0.84, 95% CI 0.73 to 0.96); who were living with HIV (ARR=0.79, 95% CI 0.70 to 0.89); and among those who reported being sexually active in the past 12 months (ARR=0.54, 95% CI 0.48 to 0.61). On the other hand, adolescent girls aged 15–19 years (ARR=1.31, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.67) and women who lived in the Manzini (ARR=1.21, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.46) and Shiselweni regions (ARR=1.21, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.45) had a higher risk of not using modern contraceptives.ConclusionGiven that a third of women in Eswatini did not use modern contraceptive methods, there is a need to promote modern contraceptive methods in Eswatini, particularly long-acting methods. The Eswatini Sexual and Reproductive Health Program should strengthen initiatives aimed at promoting modern contraceptive use among adolescent girls and those living in the Manzini and Shiselweni regions, who showed a higher risk of not using modern contraceptives.Keywords: Reproductive medicine, Health Services Accessibility, Midwifery, Maternal medicine, Observational Study

Introduction

Since invention, modern contraceptives have helped women prevent unintended pregnancies, which are often associated with unsafe abortions and maternal mortality.1 An estimated 40% of women report unintended pregnancy globally,2 34–54% in Europe,3 35% in Africa4 and 70% in Eswatini.5 According to the WHO,6 the maternal mortality ratio (MMR) is nearly 20 times higher in developing countries than in developed countries, with the Sub-Saharan African region reporting the highest MMR (at 546 per 100 000 live births),7 yet it has been reported that investing in modern contraceptives to prevent unintended pregnancy could reduce up to 30% of these deaths.8 Modern contraceptive use is defined as the use of a product or medical procedure such as sterilisation, implants, oral pills, injections, emergency contraceptive pills, male and female condoms, cervical caps, spermicidal agents, vaginal rings and intrauterine devices (IUDs), to interfere with reproduction from acts of sexual intercourse.5 Contrary to traditional methods, modern contraceptive methods are known for their high effectiveness in birth spacing, prevention of unintended pregnancy and prevention of sexually transmitted infections.9

Approximately 645 million women have their contraceptive needs met through modern contraceptives,10 with the global prevalence of modern contraception use estimated at 57.4%,11 73% in Northern Europe and much lower at 23.6% in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA).11 African countries have reported varying prevalence of modern contraceptive use, ranging from 71.9% in Ethiopia,12 53.9% in Tanzania13 and 66.1% in Eswatini.14 The growing use of modern contraceptives across the globe has given couples the option to choose the number of children they plan to have as well as the desired spacing between their children.12 Utilisation of contraceptives is fundamental in promoting the utilisation of sexual, reproductive, maternal and child health services, and it is key in preventing maternal deaths, reducing preterm births, low-birth-weight infants and neonatal and infant mortality.8 However, despite numerous efforts by governments and non-governmental organisations to improve the use of modern contraceptives in SSA, there is still poor use of modern contraceptives in this region,11 underscoring the need to explore the risk and protective factors associated with their non-use. Commonly cited reasons for non-use of modern contraceptives include fear of side effects, desire for more children, partner’s disapproval and religious prohibitions.15 Socioeconomic, geographic, cultural, demographic and psychological factors have also been reported as some of the determinants of modern contraception use.11 The utilisation of modern contraceptives is prevalent among specific groups of women. These include older women,16 those with a higher education level, employed individuals,12 women from affluent households,17 those living with HIV,18 unmarried women,19 20 sexually active women,17 childless women5 and urban residents.15

Investigating modern contraceptive use in Eswatini is important considering the sexual and reproductive health challenges faced by the country. Eswatini has one of the world’s highest prevalences of HIV at 23.7% among adults aged 15–49 years, with women disproportionately affected as 31.6% of them live with HIV compared with 15.6% of men in the same age group (15–49 years).21 This pronounced gender disparity underscores the critical importance of examining modern contraceptive use among women, who remain central to achieving the country’s vision of an HIV-free nation, as consistent condom use remains key in the prevention of mother to child transmission of HIV.22–24 The prevalence of unintended pregnancies in Eswatini is also relatively high at 70%,5 further underscoring the need to investigate the determinants of modern contraceptive non-use in the country. More to that, the country’s MMR is far higher (at 389 per 100 000 live births)25 than the Sustainable Development Goal 3 target 3.1 of 70 per 100 000 live births by 2030.26 Finally, discussing sexual and reproductive matters is considered taboo, making it even harder for Swati women to negotiate contraception choices with their partners.15 To curb some of these problems, Eswatini has implemented several strategies, one of which includes a nationwide promotion and mass distribution of condoms; however, their uptake has either remained low or they are being inconsistently used, especially among young people.27

Even though several studies have been conducted on contraceptive methods in Eswatini,5 28 29 these studies have focused mainly on condom use,29 while others further narrowed their focus to studying the use of contraceptives in special populations.5 28 29 The one study that used a nationally representative sample used data from a 2007 survey,30 which may no longer reflect the current realities. It is due to these drawbacks in the current literature that we analysed data from the 2021 Swaziland (Eswatini) HIV Incidence Measurement Survey, a nationally representative sample of women of reproductive age (15–49 years) to determine the magnitude and correlates of non-use of modern contraceptives in Eswatini to inform sexual and reproductive health policy-decision making in the country.

Methods

Data source, study setting and sampling design

This study used secondary data from a nationally representative, cross-sectional population-based household survey, the Swaziland HIV Incidence Measurement Survey (SHIMS3).21 Data for SHIMS3 were collected between May and November 2021 under the multi-country Population-based HIV Impact Assessment Project,31 with the primary purpose of measuring the status of Eswatini’s national HIV response. The SHIMS3 dataset was the latest available dataset from the surveys at the time of writing this manuscript. SHIMS3 used a two-stage stratified cluster sample design, based on the 2017 Swaziland Population and Housing Census, to sample households in the four administrative regions of the country. In the first stage, the probability proportional to size method was used to randomly sample 200 of 2064 enumeration areas (EAs) (clusters). The 200 EAs were further stratified into eight strata defined by rural and urban status. At the second stage of sampling, an equal probability method was used to randomly sample household clusters within each EA. In total, 7000 households were sampled, 5413 of which were interviewed, giving a household weighted response rate of 82.8% (adjusted for differential sampling probabilities and non-response). The detailed SHIMS3 methodology is described elsewhere.21

Current study sample

This study used census sampling, targeting all women of reproductive age (defined as those aged 15–49 years old) within the SHIMS3 dataset. All who met the eligibility criteria were included in the present analysis, with no predefined sample size limits. After applying the eligibility criteria, the sample size was more than 1000, which is the recommended size for large population surveys. Research has shown that the precision of estimates of surveys only increases very slightly beyond a sample size of 1000.32 Therefore, there was no added statistical benefit in calculating the sample size in this study.

A total of 6545 women were interviewed in SHIMS3. From these, we analysed data for women aged 15–49 years, who completed the interviews, who had ever had sex and who had complete data on use of contraception. We excluded women who had indefinite HIV laboratory results (valid biomarker HIV result) and those who had missing data on any of the covariates. A total of 3080 met the criteria and were included in the present analysis (figure 1).

Study sample flowchart from SHIMS3 dataset.

Data collection and study variables

In SHIMS3, mobile tablet devices were used to collect the data using a structured questionnaire and field laboratory data. The questionnaire was administered in two languages, English and SiSwati, which are the official languages in Eswatini. Data on household characteristics, individual’s demographic characteristics, sexual and reproductive health, HIV testing and treatment history, domestic violence and non-communicable diseases were collected. After each interview, a blood specimen from each interviewee was collected for HIV testing and the biomarker results of this test were used in the present study.21

Outcome variable

We considered non-use of modern contraceptive methods to include any report of not using any oral contraceptive pills, IUDs, injectables, diaphragm, male/female condoms, female/male sterilisation and implants. In SHIMS3, this variable was measured by asking respondents to state the method of contraception they were using at the time of the survey. Respondents who reported using any of the above-mentioned methods were categorised as using modern contraceptive methods (coded 0) while those who used any other methods, that is, abstinence (rhythm/calendar method) or withdrawal, were categorised as not using modern contraceptives (coded 1).

Explanatory variables

Several independent variables were used to predict modern contraceptive non-use. Among these were sociodemographic characteristics (maternal age: how old are you?; highest level of education: what is your highest level of school you attended?; marital status: what is your marital status now?; employment status: have you done any work in the last 12 months for which you received cash or goods as payment?; region of residence; and household wealth index). The wealth index of a household was constructed from baseline housing characteristics and asset data, which was then analysed and divided into wealth quintiles (from poorest to richest). Other correlates included sexual and reproductive health characteristics (number of live births: how many times have you had a pregnancy that resulted in a live birth?; sexually active status in the past 12 months: how many different people have you had sex with in the last 12 months?; and the HIV serostatus).

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were conducted in Stata V.17 (StataCorp LP, College Station, Texas, USA), using the svyset command and jackknife replication sample weights to produce accurate standard errors and unbiased estimates and to account for the complex SHIMS3 sampling design. We computed frequencies and their corresponding weighted proportions to describe the sample characteristics. Since our outcome was not rare, we fitted a modified Poisson regression model (ie, a Poisson regression with a robust error variance) and obtained relative risk (or risk ratios), which are equivalent to prevalence ratios.33 Modified Poisson regression produces parameters with narrower intervals and thus does not overestimate the regression coefficients as it is the case with logistic regression. To select the correlates of non-use of modern contraceptives to be included in the final model, we performed an exploratory bivariate analysis, retaining covariates that were statistically significant at an alpha level of <0.25.34 In the multivariable analysis, estimates for covariates were considered to be statistically significant if the p value from a two-tailed test was <0.05.

Modern contraceptive methods used

The weighted magnitude of non-use of modern contraceptives was 33% (n=999; 95% CI 30.9% to 35.2%). Among the 2081 women who used modern contraceptive methods, 49.5% (n=1007) used the female or male condoms followed by 24.2% (n=504) who reported to be on the pill and 12.8% (n=294) who were on the injection (figure 2).

Correlates of non-use of modern contraceptives

Bivariate and multivariate analyses of the determinants of non-use of modern contraception are presented in table 2. After controlling for other covariates in the final model, the risk of non-use of modern contraceptives was 31% higher among women aged 15–19 years (adjusted risk ratio (ARR) = 1.31, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.67) compared with those aged 35–49 years and 21% higher among those from the Manzini (ARR=1.21, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.46) and the Shiselweni regions (ARR=1.21, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.45) compared with those from the Hhohho region. However, a lower risk of non-use of modern contraceptives was observed in women aged 20–34 years (ARR=0.85, 95% CI 0.75 to 0.97) compared with those aged 35–49 years; those who were from households with a middle wealth quintile (ARR=0.82, 95% CI 0.68 to 0.97) compared with those who were from households with a rich/richest wealth quintile; those who had given birth at least once (ARR=0.84, 95% CI 0.73 to 0.96) compared with those who had not; those who had sex in the past 12 months (ARR=0.54, 95% CI 0.48 to 0.61) compared with those who had not; and those who were living with HIV (ARR=0.79, 95% CI 0.70 to 0.89) compared with those who were not.

Discussion

This study sought to describe the magnitude and correlates of non-use of modern contraceptive methods among women of childbearing age in Eswatini. About one in three women in our sample reported not to be using modern contraceptives. This finding is similar to those from studies done in Togo19 and Ethiopia.12 The magnitude of non-use of modern contraceptives in our sample was however slightly lower than those reported in the country’s previous reports.5 14 The slight difference in the magnitudes may have been brought about by the modern contraceptive promotion campaigns which were at peak before the time of the survey,35 as well as the decentralisation of non-to-minimal-invasive family planning services to lower-level health facilities.

This study also found that condoms were most common among modern contraceptive users, followed by hormonal pills and injectables. Unlike in our study, Tekelab et al12 and Yussuf et al17 reported higher use of injectables in their respective countries. The differences in the recorded HIV prevalence in these countries may account for this disparity in findings. In an HIV high prevalence country like Eswatini, women are likely to use condoms for dual protection against unwanted pregnancy as well as sexually transmitted diseases, including HIV.13 19 In our study, to assess this, a crosstabulation showed a higher proportion of condom use among the HIV sero-positive group (results not presented). Condoms, especially the male condom, may also be commonly used in Eswatini for their ease of accessibility and availability compared with the other modern contraceptive methods.

The present study also identified several correlates associated with non-use of modern contraceptives. Worth noting is that most studies on the subject reported ORs rather than risk ratios; therefore, direct comparison of findings may not be possible. Thus, our comparison is based on the direction of the associations.

In this study, the risk of not using modern contraceptives was lower among young adults (20–34 years), similar to Aviisah’s study,16 who found that across the years, the younger aged Ghanaian women had higher odds of using modern contraceptives. Hultstrand et al5 also reported similar findings from a peri-urban sample in Eswatini. Young adults may use contraceptives to prevent unwanted pregnancy and social stigmatisation.36 This age group also represents the prime years of career women, who may use contraceptives to balance their family and career goals.

The present findings also showed that the risk of non-use of modern contraceptives was lower among those from the middle wealth quintile compared with the rich/richest wealth quintile, comparable to those of Moreira and colleagues.37 Women in the middle-income class may have higher contraceptive uptake and adherence than high-income women, likely due to the great economic consequences of unintended pregnancy. Wealthier women may also be dissatisfied with available options, such as with the side effects of hormonal contraceptives, leading to their poor use of modern contraceptives.37

We also found that women with at least one child had a lower risk of not using modern contraceptives, similar to findings from previous studies.15 16 Women with at least one child may use modern contraceptives for child spacing purposes. On the other hand, women who have given birth may be satisfied with the number of children they have and therefore use modern contraceptives to prevent further pregnancies.

The current study also found a lower risk of non-use of modern contraceptive methods among women who had been sexually active in the 12 months preceding the survey, similar to findings from other studies.13 17 Sexually active women may be exposed to sexually transmitted infections and also at risk of pregnancy. They may therefore use modern contraceptives for purposes of timing and preventing pregnancy as well as preventing STIs, which sexually inactive women may not.

The finding that women living with HIV had lower risk of non-use of modern contraceptives is consistent with other studies,18 partly because these women receive ongoing counselling, care and treatment from healthcare providers in the various HIV units, such as under prevention of mother to child transmission, among others.

Women residing in the central and southern regions of Eswatini had a higher risk of not using modern contraceptives. The Shiselweni region is rural, and the Manzini region is mainly peri-urban and thus accessibility to modern contraceptive services may be scanty in such settings. A previous survey among sex workers in Eswatini reported that women residing in the Hhohho region were frequently using condoms compared with those from the Manzini and southern regions,28 which speaks to the uneven distribution of modern contraceptive methods across the regions. There are more misconceptions regarding side effects of contraceptives in rural and peri-urban areas than in urban areas. Historical sociocultural beliefs in these areas continue to constrain the utilisation of modern contraceptive methods.

In this study, the risk of non-use of modern contraceptives was also higher among adolescent girls compared with adults. Only 6–27% of adolescent girls in developing countries are reported to use modern contraceptives.38 The social stigma associated with early sexual activity among adolescent girls often acts as a barrier, discouraging them from seeking modern contraceptive care.36 They often are financially dependent, and therefore cannot self-sustain the use of modern contraceptives. Local misconceptions such as the belief that early use of modern contraceptives may lead to infertility later in life may also explain this finding.36

Recommendations

Our findings highlight the need for healthcare providers of modern contraceptives to pay special attention to women who present with the risk factors for non-use that were identified in this study. There is still a need to further promote these methods, particularly the long-acting methods such as implants and IUDs, as women have shown low use of these methods. Family planning awareness campaigns and distribution of equipment should also spread to the southern and central regions of the country. There is a need to promote modern contraceptive methods among sexually active adolescent girls. Future studies can follow the trends of modern contraceptive use to date.

Strengths and limitations

The study used data from the nationally representative SHIMS3 survey and applied survey weighting, which ensures the generalisability of the study findings to non-institutionalised women of reproductive age in Eswatini. The inclusion of 3080 participants provided sufficient statistical power to perform multivariable analysis for multiple demographic, socioeconomic and reproductive covariates. Also, applying a modified Poisson regression model rather than standard logistic regression prevents overestimation of the effect sizes for a common outcome.

The study design inherits a few limitations that cannot be ignored. The observational, cross-sectional nature of the SHIMS3 dataset prevents establishing temporality between the studied relationships. Critical potential predictors of modern contraceptive non-use such as fear of side effects and distance to health facility were not captured on the used dataset; hence, they could not be part of the present analysis. Since the SHIMS3 survey excluded institutionalised populations, the present findings cannot be generalised to institutionalised women in Eswatini. Some variables on SHIMS3 were asked retrospectively, which subjects the study to recall bias, though recall was mostly limited to the past year.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest a moderate use of modern contraceptive methods in Eswatini, with short-acting methods being the methods of preference among many. The study also highlights that those of the middle wealth quintile, young adults, those with at least one child, those living with HIV and those who are sexually active were associated with a lower risk of not using modern contraceptives, while adolescent girls and those in the Manzini and Shiselweni regions had a higher risk of non-use of modern contraceptive.
