Diophantine approximation and the subspace theorem
Shivani Goel, Rashi Lunia, Anwesh Ray

Abstract
. Diophantine approximation explores how well irrational numbers can be approximated by rationals, with foundational results by Dirichlet, Hurwitz, and Liouville culminating in Roth’s theorem. Schmidt’s subspace theorem extends Roth’s result to higher dimensions, with profound implications for Diophantine equations and transcendence theory. This article provides a self-contained and accessible exposition of Roth’s theorem and Schlickewei’s refinement of Schmidt’s subspace theorem, with an emphasis on proofs. The arguments presented are classical and approachable for readers with a background in algebraic number theory, serving as a streamlined, yet condensed reference for these fundamental results.

11J87

1.
Introduction

Diophantine approximation originates from the study of how well irrational numbers can be
approximated
by rational numbers. The first result in this direction was due to Dirichlet, who showed that if
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is an irrational number, then there are infinitely many rational numbers
\frac{p}{q}
(with
p
,
q
coprime) such that
|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}|<\frac{1}{q^{2}}
. Note that this result is clearly false when
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
. Dirichlet’s result motivates further questions about how well an irrational number may be approximated by rationals. Indeed, one would like to know if better approximations than those given by Dirichlet’s theorem do exist. Hurwitz improved upon Dirichlet’s result, by showing that the bound
\frac{1}{q^{2}}
can be improved to
\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}q^{2}}
. The constant
\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}
is optimal in the sense that replacing
\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}
with a smaller constant makes the result false for certain quadratic irrationals
\alpha
. Liouville
[
14
]
was the first to provide a significant result in this direction by proving that algebraic numbers of degree greater than 1 cannot be approximated "too well" by rationals. Specifically, he showed that if
\alpha
is an algebraic number of degree
d\geq 2
, then there exists a constant
C>0
such that
|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}|>\frac{C}{q^{d}}
for all rational numbers
\frac{p}{q}
. However, Liouville’s bound is not sharp for algebraic numbers of degree greater than 2, and this led to the eventual development of sharper results due to Thue
[
23
]
, Siegel
[
22
]
, Dyson
[
4
]
and Gelfond
[
10
]
, finally culminating in Roth’s theorem.

Roth’s theorem states that for any
\varepsilon>0
, there are only finitely many rational approximations
\frac{p}{q}
such that
|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}|<\frac{1}{q^{2+\varepsilon}}
. Unlike Liouville’s theorem, this does not depend on the degree of
\alpha
. Lang
[
12
]
and Leveque
[
13
]
generalized Roth’s theorem to include approximations involving valuations at places in any number field
K
. In earlier work Ridout
[
17
]
had obtained such results over
\mathbb{Q}
. Schmidt
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
further extended the ideas and introduced the
subspace theorem
, which generalizes Roth’s result to higher-dimensional settings. The subspace theorem states that if a set of linear forms satisfies certain non-degeneracy conditions, then the solutions to an inequality involving these forms lie in a finite union of proper subspaces. Schmidt’s subspace theorem has had profound implications in Diophantine equations, transcendence theory, and the distribution of rational and integral points on varieties. For further details, we refer to
[
2
,
1
,
3
]
. An extension of Schmidt’s theorem allowing for completions over a finite set of places, entirely analogous to Ridout’s and Lang’s generalizations of Roth’s theorem, was later obtained by Schlickewei
[
18
]
. Faltings
[
9
]
subsequently introduced powerful geometric techniques that complemented and extended Schmidt’s work, leading to a significant generalization now known as the product theorem. For some of the details pertaining to the proof, the reader may also refer to also the article by van der Put
[
24
]
.

Building on these developments, Faltings and Wüstholz
[
8
]
established a deep and far-reaching extension of the subspace theorem which applies to inequalities governed by forms of arbitrary degree. The central finiteness result in this generalized context asserts that the set of solutions to such inequalities is not merely sparse but is, in fact, confined to a proper algebraic subvariety of the ambient space. This represents a significant strengthening of earlier results, as it shifts the conclusion from a mere quantitative bound on the number of solutions to a strong structural constraint on their distribution.

Quantitative versions of the subspace theorem were obtained by Evertse
[
7
]
, and Evertse and Schlickewei
[
6
]
. In these works one is able to control the number of hyperplanes containing the solutions to the subspace equation. Further refinements of these results have been obtained by Evertse and Ferreti
[
5
]
.

This article provides a self-contained yet concise reference for the proofs of Roth’s theorem and Schmidt’s subspace theorem, following the treatments of Bombieri and Gubler
[
1
, Chapter 6 and 7]
, Hindry and Silverman
[
11
, Part D]
and Schmidt
[
20
]
. Section
2
develops the necessary preliminaries, beginning with absolute values on number fields, Ostrowski’s theorem, places of a number field, and the product formula. We then introduce height functions, establishing key properties of the Weil height and proving fundamental results such as Northcott’s and Kronecker’s theorems. The discussion extends to polynomial heights, where we prove Gauss’s lemma and derive essential height inequalities. We further examine Mahler’s measure, various formulations of Siegel’s lemma, and the properties of the index associated with polynomial functions in multiple variables. Finally, we introduce the generalized Wronskian and present a criterion for establishing the linear independence of polynomials in several variables.

In Section
3
, we present a proof of Roth’s theorem. To motivate the result, we first introduce and prove Liouville’s theorem, which states that for any real algebraic number
\alpha
of degree
n>1
, there exists a constant
c(\alpha)>0
such that for every rational number
p/q\in\mathbb{Q}
, we have
\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|>\frac{c(\alpha)}{q^{n}}
. We then formulate two versions of Roth’s theorem for a number field
K
with a finite set of places
S
and demonstrate their equivalence. Next, we prove
Roth’s lemma
, which shows that the index of a polynomial vanishing at a distinguished point
\bar{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m})
defined over
K
is bounded. This point is constructed so that each coordinate
\beta_{i}
is close to
\alpha
. Roth’s lemma is proved by induction on the number of variables, beginning with the base case for polynomials in one variable and extending to higher dimensions by considering the decomposition of the polynomial into sums of products of lower-dimensional polynomials. On the other hand, we apply Siegel’s lemma to construct an auxiliary polynomial with controlled height in a large number of variables that vanishes to a high order at
\bar{\alpha}
. The construction requires that
\bar{\beta}
is sufficiently close to
\bar{\alpha}
, and thus one obtains a lower bound on the index at
\bar{\beta}
.
The contradiction is then achieved, completing the proof of Roth’s theorem.

Finally, Section
4
is devoted to proving Schlickewei’s refinement of Schmidt’s subspace theorem over a number field
K
. Denote by
K_{\mathbb{A}}
the ring of adeles over
K
. We begin by formulating a version of Minkowski’s second theorem adapted to adelic domains contained in
K_{\mathbb{A}}^{N}
for a suitable large number
N
. These adelic domains are associated to solutions of the subspace equation and are known as
approximation domains
. Assuming for contradiction that the subspace theorem is false, we construct an infinite family of solutions to the subspace inequality and associate to them approximation domains with adelic volumes that decrease asymptotically. The rank of an approximation domain is defined as the dimension
k
of the Euclidean space over
K
that embeds into it. The proof proceeds by induction on
k
, establishing that, outside of finitely many hyperplanes, the adelic approximation domains corresponding to solutions of the subspace inequality have dimension at most
k
. The induction step relies on the construction of an auxiliary polynomial with bounded height and prescribed vanishing properties, and a generalized form of Roth’s lemma is employed to obtain crucial nonvanishing results.

2.
Preliminary notions

We begin this section with a brief review of absolute values on a number field
K
.

Definition 2.1
.

An
absolute value
(or
norm
) on a field
K
is a real-valued function
||\cdot||:K\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following properties for all
x,y\in K
:

•

||x||\geq 0
, with equality if and only if
x=0
.

•

||xy||=||x||\cdot||y||
.

•

||x+y||\leq||x||+||y||
.

An absolute value is called
non-archimedean
if it satisfies the stronger inequality

||x+y||\leq\max\{||x||,||y||\}\quad\text{for all }x,y\in K.

Otherwise, it is called
archimedean
.
Every absolute value
||\cdot||
defines a metric on
K
via the distance function
d(x,y)=||x-y||
, thereby endowing
K
with a corresponding metric topology. Two absolute values
||\cdot||_{1}
and
||\cdot||_{2}
on
K
are said to be equivalent if they induce the same topology on
K
.

Let
\mathcal{O}_{K}
denote the ring of integers of
K
. The following classification theorem, due to Ostrowski, describes all inequivalent absolute values on
K
.

Theorem 2.2

(Ostrowski’s Theorem)
.

Any non-trivial absolute value on
K
is equivalent to either

•

a
\mathfrak{p}
-adic absolute value for a unique prime
\mathfrak{p}
in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, or

•

an absolute value arising from a real or complex embedding of
K
.

Proof.

See
[
16
, Theorem 3.3]
.
∎

For
K=\mathbb{Q}
, the non-trivial absolute values, up to equivalence, consist of the usual (archimedean) absolute value and the
p
-adic absolute values for distinct primes
p
.

We now define the normalized valuations of a number field
K
. Let
\mathcal{O}_{K}
denote the ring of integers of
K
, and for any prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
, let
N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\mathfrak{p})
denote the absolute norm of
\mathfrak{p}
. For
\alpha\in K
, the normalized absolute value at
\mathfrak{p}
is given by

|\alpha|_{\mathfrak{p}}=N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\mathfrak{p})^{-\mathrm{ord}_{\mathfrak{p}}(\alpha)},

where
\mathrm{ord}_{\mathfrak{p}}(\alpha)
denotes the order of
\alpha
at
\mathfrak{p}
. For an archimedean absolute value corresponding to an embedding
\sigma:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
, we define

|\alpha|_{\sigma}=|\sigma(\alpha)|,

where
|\cdot|
denotes the usual absolute value on
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
depending on whether
\sigma
is real or complex, respectively.

In this manner, we have selected a representative absolute value for each inequivalent class of non-trivial absolute values of
K
. An equivalence class of non-trivial absolute values is referred to as a
place

v
of
K
.
Let

|\alpha|_{v}=|\alpha|_{\mathfrak{p}}^{1/[K:\mathbb{Q}]}

when
v
corresponds to the equivalence class of a non-zero prime
\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
and

|\alpha|_{v}=|\alpha|_{\sigma}^{d_{v}/[K:\mathbb{Q}]}

where
d_{v}=2
when
v
is an equivalence class of a complex embedding
\sigma
of
K
and
d_{v}=1
otherwise.

Let
M_{K}
be the set of all such places on
K
. Then
\alpha\in M_{K}\backslash\{0\}
satisfies the product formula

(2.1)

\prod_{v\in M_{K}}|\alpha|_{v}=1.

We denote by
M_{K}^{\infty}
(resp.
M_{K}^{f}
) the set of archimedean (resp. non-archimedean) places
v\in M_{K}
.

2.1.
Heights

The
natural height

H
is defined on the set of non-zero rationals as

H(p/q)=\max\{|p|,|q|\}.

We say that a set
\mathcal{A}
has the
Northcott property
with respect to a height
H
if
for any real
X>0
, there exist only finitely many elements
a\in\mathcal{A}
with
H(a)\leq X
.
The set of rational numbers has the Northcott property with respect to the natural height.

The notion of heights can be extended to non-zero algebraic numbers as follows.

Definition 2.3

(Weil height)
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{\bar{Q}}\backslash\{0\}
. Further, let
K
be a number field containing
\alpha
. The Weil height of
\alpha
is defined as

H(\alpha)=\prod_{v\in M_{K}}\max\{1,|\alpha|_{v}\}.

Lemma 2.4
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{\bar{Q}}\backslash\{0\}
,

•

H(\alpha)
is well-defined.

•

H(\alpha^{-1})=H(\alpha)
.

•

If
\alpha
and
\beta
are conjugates, then
H(\alpha)=H(\beta)
.

•

For any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
,
H(\alpha^{m})=H(\alpha)^{|m|}
.

Proof.

The proof is an easy consequence of the definition of height, we refer to
[
25
, chapter 3 sec 3.2]
for further details.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

The set
\mathbb{\bar{Q}}
does not have Northcott property with respect to the Weil height. For example, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
H(2^{1/n})=2^{1/n}
and thus the set of all
\alpha\in\mathbb{\bar{Q}}
such that
H(\alpha)<2
is an infinite set.

Theorem 2.6

(Northcott)
.

There are finitely many
\alpha\in\mathbb{\bar{Q}}
with bounded height and bounded degree. In other words, for any
M>0
, the set
\{\alpha\in\mathbb{\bar{Q}}\mid[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]<M\}
has the Northcott property with respect to the Weil height.

Proof.

Let
X>0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{\bar{Q}}\setminus\{0\}
such that
H(\alpha)\leq X
. Let
f(x)=a_{n}x^{n}+\ldots+a_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
, and let
\alpha=\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
be its roots. Writing

f(x)=a_{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-\alpha_{i})

we see that

|a_{i}|=|a_{n}|\left|\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\dots<j_{i}\leq n}\alpha_{j_{1}}\dots\alpha_{j_{i}}\right|\leq|a_{n}|\binom{n}{i}\operatorname{max}\{|\alpha_{1}|,\dots,|\alpha_{n}|\}^{i}.

Noting that for each
i
,

|\alpha_{i}|\leq\prod_{v\in M_{K}}\operatorname{max}\{1,|\alpha_{i}|_{v}\}\leq H(\alpha_{i})=H(\alpha),

we deduce that

|a_{i}|\leq|a_{n}|\binom{n}{i}H(\alpha)^{i}\leq X2^{n}H(\alpha)^{n}\leq 2^{n}X^{n+1}.

Since there are finitely many polynomials of degree
n
with coefficients bounded by
2^{n}X^{n+1}
, there are finitely many such algebraic numbers.
∎

The Kronecker theorem provides a remarkable characterization of algebraic numbers with minimal possible height. Specifically, it asserts that the only algebraic numbers with Weil height exactly equal to
1
are the roots of unity.

Theorem 2.7

(Kronecker)
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{\bar{Q}}
,
H(\alpha)=1
if and only if
\alpha
is a root of unity.

Proof.

First, suppose
\alpha
is a root of unity. Then, there exists a positive integer
n
such that
\alpha^{n}=1
. Since the Weil height satisfies the multiplicative property
H(\alpha^{n})=H(\alpha)^{n}
, we have

H(1)=1=H(\alpha^{n})=H(\alpha)^{n}.

Taking the
n
-th root of both sides yields
H(\alpha)=1
.

Conversely, suppose
H(\alpha)=1
. Then
|\alpha|_{v}\leq 1
for every
v\in M_{K}
. Let
d
be the degree of
\alpha
and let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}
be an ordering on the Galois conjugates of
\alpha
over
\mathbb{Q}
and set
\boldsymbol{\alpha}:=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})
. For
1\leq i\leq d
, let

s_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}):=\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\dots<j_{i}\leq d}x_{j_{1}}x_{j_{2}}\dots x_{j_{i}}

and for every positive integer
m
, set

s_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{m}):=s_{i}(\alpha_{1}^{m},\dots,\alpha_{d}^{m}).

Since
|\alpha|_{v}\leq 1
for all non-archimedean
places
v
,
\alpha
is an algebraic integer. We deduce that
s_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{m})\in\mathbb{Z}
for every
m>0
. Since
|\alpha|_{v}\leq 1
, for all
i
,

\sum_{i=1}^{d}|s_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{m})|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\binom{d}{i}=2^{d}.

In other words, the vector
(s_{1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{m}),\dots,s_{d}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{m}))
lies in the box
[-2^{d},2^{d}]^{d}
. By the Pigeon-hole principle, there exists
m\neq n
such that

(s_{1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{m}),\dots,s_{d}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{m}))=(s_{1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n}),\dots,s_{d}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n})).

This implies that

s_{j}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{m})=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n})\ \text{for all }\ j=1,\dots,d.

Thus, there is a permutation
\pi\in\operatorname{Aut}\left(\{1,\dots,d\}\right)
such that

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{m}=\pi(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n}).

Iterating this relation
k
times, one finds that

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{m^{k}}=\pi^{k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n^{k}}).

Choose
k\geq 1
such that
\pi^{k}=\text{id}
, to deduce that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{m^{k}}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{n^{k}}
. In particular,

\alpha^{m^{k}}=\alpha^{n^{k}}
.
Rearranging this equation, we find that
\alpha^{m^{k}-n^{k}}=1
, hence
\alpha
is a root of unity. This completes the proof.
∎

To facilitate certain estimates and inequalities, it is often convenient to work with the logarithm of the Weil height.

Definition 2.8
.

The
logarithmic Weil height
of a non-zero algebraic number
\alpha
is defined as

h(\alpha)=\log H(\alpha).

The logarithmic Weil height satisfies several useful inequalities, which make it particularly handy in many applications.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in K\setminus\{0\}
, where
K
is a number field. Then,

•

h(\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\alpha_{i})
.

•

h(\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\alpha_{i})+\log n
.

Proof.

The first inequality follows from the multiplicative property of the Weil height, as
H(\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n})\leq H(\alpha_{1})\cdots H(\alpha_{n})
, which translates into an additive inequality when taking logarithms:

h(\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n})=\log H(\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n})\leq\log\left(H(\alpha_{1})\cdots H(\alpha_{n})\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log H(\alpha_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\alpha_{i}).

The second inequality follows by using triangle inequality for archimedean places, strong triangle inequality for non-archimedean places and product formula (
2.1
).
For further details, see
[
1
, Chapter 1]
or
[
25
, Section 3.2]
.

∎

The height of points in affine space plays an important role in Diophantine geometry, where it provides a quantitative way to measure the complexity of algebraic points. Let
\mathbb{A}_{K}^{n}
denote the affine
n
-space over
K
. For a point
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{A}_{K}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, its height is defined as

H(\mathbf{x}):=\prod_{v\in M_{K}}\max_{j}\{1,|x_{j}|_{v}\},

where the maximum is taken over the coordinates of
\mathbf{x}
, and
|x_{j}|_{v}
denotes the
v
-adic absolute value of the coordinate
x_{j}
at the place
v
. The logarithmic height, often more convenient due to its additive properties, is defined as follows:

h(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\max_{j}\log^{+}|x_{j}|_{v},

where
\log^{+}t=\max\{\log t,0\}
for
t>0
and
\log^{+}0=0
.

Let
f(x)=a_{n}x^{n}+\ldots+a_{0}
be a polynomial with coefficients in a number field
K
. The height of the polynomial
f
is a measure of the arithmetic complexity of its coefficients and is defined as

H(f):=\prod_{v\in M_{K}}\operatorname{max}\{1,|f|_{v}\}

and the logarithmic height of
f
is defined as

h(f):=\log H(f)=\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\log^{+}|f|_{v}.

The local absolute value of
f
at a place
v
is given by

|f|_{v}=\max\{|a_{0}|_{v},\ldots,|a_{n}|_{v}\}.

In other words, we view a polynomial as a tuple of its coefficients in a suitable affine space and its height coincides with the affine height of that point.

Gauss’s lemma asserts that for non-archimedean places, the height behaves multiplicatively under the product of two polynomials.

Lemma 2.10

(Gauss’ lemma)
.

For polynomials
f,g\in K[x]
and a place
v\in M_{K}\setminus M_{K}^{\infty}
(i.e., a non-archimedean place), we have

|fg|_{v}=|f|_{v}|g|_{v}.

Proof.

Let
f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}a_{i}x^{i}
and
g(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}x^{i}
, and denote their product by
fg(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{m+n}c_{k}x^{k}
where
c_{k}=\sum_{i+j=k}a_{i}b_{j}
.
Since
v
is non-archimedean, we know that the non-archimedean triangle inequality gives

|fg|_{v}\leq|f|_{v}|g|_{v}.

Without loss of generality, assume
|f|_{v}=1
and
|g|_{v}=1
. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
|fg|_{v}<1
. This would imply
|c_{j}|_{v}<1
for all
j
. Let
j_{0}
be the smallest index for which
|a_{j_{0}}|_{v}=1
. Since
|f|_{v}=1
, such a
j_{0}
must exist. Now, consider the terms in the product
fg
involving
a_{j_{0}}
. Since
|c_{j_{0}}|_{v}<1
and
|a_{k}|_{v}<1
for all
k<j_{0}
, hence
|b_{0}|_{v}<1
. If we repeat the process for
c_{j_{o}+l}
, we get
|b_{l}|_{v}<1
. Applying a similar argument recursively to the coefficients of
g
, we deduce that
|b_{j}|_{v}<1
for all
j
, contradicting the assumption that
|g|_{v}=1
. Hence, we conclude
|fg|_{v}=1
, proving the lemma.
∎

The notion of height also extends to multivariable polynomials. Let

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=\sum_{\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})}a_{\mathbf{i}}\,x_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots x_{m}^{i_{m}}

be a polynomial with coefficients in a number field
K
.
The
height
of
f
is defined as follows:

H(f):=\prod_{v\in M_{K}}\operatorname{max}\{1,|f|_{v}\},

and the
logarithmic height
of
f
is defined as

h(f):=\log H(f)=\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\log^{+}|f|_{v},

where

|f|_{v}:=\max_{\mathbf{i}}\{|a_{\mathbf{i}}|_{v}\}.

Lemma 2.11
.

For polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
with
\deg_{x_{j}}f_{i}\leq d_{j}
for all
1\leq i\leq r
and
1\leq j\leq m
, we have

h(f_{1}\ldots f_{r})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}h(f_{i})+(d_{1}+\ldots+d_{m})\log 2+m(r-1)\log 2.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{m}
be such that
\mu_{j}\leq d_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,m
. For
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
, we write
\mathbf{x}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}:=x_{1}^{\mu_{1}}\cdots x_{m}^{\mu_{m}}
and
f_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}a_{i,\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbf{x}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
. Setting

F(\mathbf{x}):=f_{1}(\mathbf{x})\cdots f_{r}(\mathbf{x}),

we have that

F(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}+\ldots+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\mu}}a_{1,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}}\cdots a_{r,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{r}}\right)\mathbf{x}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}.

For
v\in M_{K}
, we have that

|F|_{v}=\max_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\left|\sum_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}+\ldots+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\mu}}a_{1,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}}\cdots a_{r,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{r}}\right|_{v}.

Let
N_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
be the number of ways in which
\boldsymbol{\mu}
can be written as a sum
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}+\dots+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\mu}
, and set
N:=\operatorname{max}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}N_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
. We find that

N_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\binom{\mu_{j}+r-1}{\mu_{j}}

and therefore,

(2.2)

N\leq\max_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\binom{\mu_{j}+r-1}{\mu_{j}}\leq\max_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}2^{\mu_{j}+r-1}\leq 2^{d_{1}+\cdots+d_{m}+m(r-1)}.

Therefore, by triangle inequality,

\displaystyle|F|_{v}

\displaystyle\leq N\max_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\left(\max_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}+\ldots+\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\mu}}|a_{1,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}}\cdots a_{r,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{r}}|_{v}\right)

\displaystyle\leq N\prod_{i=1}^{r}\max_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}}\{1,|a_{i,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i}}|_{v}\}

\displaystyle\leq N\prod_{i=1}^{r}\max\{1,|f_{i}|_{v}\}.

Therefore, we deduce that

\displaystyle H(f_{1}\cdots f_{r})

\displaystyle=\prod_{v\in M_{K}}\max\{1,|f_{1}\cdots f_{r}|_{v}\}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{v\in M_{K}}\left\{N\prod_{i=1}^{r}\max\{1,|f_{i}|_{v}\}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq N\prod_{i=1}^{r}H(f_{i}).

Taking logarithms of both sides, one finds that:

h(f_{1}\ldots f_{r})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}h(f_{i})+\log N\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}h(f_{i})+(d_{1}+\ldots+d_{m})\log 2+m(r-1)\log 2,

thus proving the result.
∎

Remark 2.12
.

If
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
and
g(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
are polynomials in different set of variables, then

h(fg)=h(f)+h(g).

Proposition 2.13
.

For
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{m}\in\mathbb{A}_{K}^{n}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
, we have

h(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\ldots+\mathbf{x}_{m})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}h(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\log m.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}_{i}=(x_{i1},\ldots,x_{in})
for
1\leq i\leq m
. Then we have

h(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\ldots+\mathbf{x}_{m})=\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\max_{j}\log^{+}|x_{1j}+\ldots+x_{mj}|_{v}.

If
v
is non-archimedean, then

|x_{1j}+\ldots+x_{mj}|_{v}\leq\max_{i}|x_{ij}|_{v}.

If
v
is archimedean, then using triangle inequality, we have

|x_{1j}+\ldots+x_{mj}|_{v}\leq|m|_{v}\max_{i}|x_{ij}|_{v}.

Using the fact that

\prod_{v\in M_{K}^{\infty}}|m|_{v}=m,

we get

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{x}_{1}+\ldots+\mathbf{x}_{m})

\displaystyle\leq\log m+\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\max_{i,j}\log^{+}|x_{ij}|_{v}

\displaystyle\leq\log m+\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{j}\log^{+}|x_{ij}|_{v}

\displaystyle=\log m+\sum_{i=1}^{m}h(\mathbf{x}_{i})

which proves the result.
∎

Proposition 2.14
.

Given non-zero polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
, we have that

h(f_{1}+\cdots+f_{r})\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq r}h(f_{i})+\log r.

Proof.

Since the coefficients of
f_{i}
are integers, for any non-archimedean
v
, we have

\max\{1,|f_{1}+\ldots+f_{r}|_{v}\}=\max\{1,|f_{1}|_{v}\}=\cdots=\max\{1,|f_{r}|_{v}\}=1.

this implies that

\displaystyle H(f_{1}+\cdots+f_{r})

\displaystyle=\prod_{v\in M_{\mathbb{Q}}}\max\{1,|f_{1}+\ldots+f_{r}|_{v}\}

\displaystyle\leq r\prod_{v\in M_{\mathbb{Q}}}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\{1,|f_{i}|_{v}\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq r\max_{1\leq i\leq r}\{|f_{i}|_{\infty}\}

\displaystyle\leq r\max_{1\leq i\leq r}H(f_{i}).

Hence, we have the required result.
∎

Proposition 2.15

(Fundamental inequality)
.

Let
S
be a finite subset of
M_{K}
. For
\alpha\in K\backslash\{0\}
,

-h(\alpha)\leq\sum_{v\in S}\log|\alpha|_{v}\leq h(\alpha).

Proof.

The right inequality follows by definition while the left inequality additionally uses the fact that
h(\alpha^{-1})=h(\alpha)
.
∎

Let
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{K}
denote the
n

.
The height of a point

Remark 2.16
.

The definition of height of

2.2.
Mahler measure

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{\bar{Q}}\backslash\{0\}
and
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be its minimal polynomial. Let

f(x)=a_{n}x^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\ldots+a_{0}=a_{n}(x-\alpha_{1})\cdots(x-\alpha_{n})

where
\alpha_{i}
’s are the roots of
f
. Then the
Mahler measure
of
\alpha
is defined as

M(\alpha):=|a_{n}|\prod_{j=1}^{n}\max\{1,|\alpha_{j}|\}.

Proposition 2.17
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{\bar{Q}}\backslash\{0\}
and
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be its minimal polynomial of degree
n
. Then we have

H(\alpha)=M(\alpha)^{1/n}.

Proof.

Let
f(x)=a_{n}x^{n}+\cdots+a_{0}
be the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
over
\mathbb{Z}
. In other words,
f(x)
is a non-zero irreducible polynomial with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}
such that
f(\alpha)=0
, and moreover,
\operatorname{gcd}(a_{n},a_{n-1},\dots,a_{0})=1
. Without loss of generality, we choose a number field
K
such that
\alpha\in K
and
K
is a Galois extension over
\mathbb{Q}
with Galois group
G
.
We have

h(\alpha)=\log H(\alpha)=\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\log^{+}|\alpha|_{v}

where
\log^{+}x=\max\{0,\log x\}
for
x>0
and
\log^{+}0=0
. Factor
f(x)
into a product of linear polynomials

f(x)=a_{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-\alpha_{i}).

Given a nonarchimedian valuation
v\in M_{K}
, we have that
|f|_{v}=1
since the coefficients
a_{i}
of
f
are rational integers and
\operatorname{gcd}(\{a_{i}|\,i=1,\dots,n\})=1
. By Gauss’ lemma
2.10
,

1=|f|_{v}=|a_{n}|_{v}\prod_{i=1}^{n}|(x-\alpha_{i})|_{v}=|a_{n}|_{v}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{max}\{1,|\alpha_{i}|_{v}\}.

The conjugates of
\alpha
, say
\alpha_{j}

(j=1,\ldots,n)
appear exactly
[K:\mathbb{Q}]/n
many times in the list
(\sigma(\alpha))_{\sigma\in G}
. Therefore, we deduce that

(2.3)

\begin{split}|a_{n}|_{v}\prod_{\sigma\in G}\max(1,|\sigma(\alpha)|_{v})^{n/[K:\mathbb{Q}]}=1\end{split}

for any nonarchimedian valuation
v\in M_{K}
.
By the product formula, we find that

\displaystyle h(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in M_{K}^{\infty}}\log^{+}|\alpha|_{v}+\sum_{v\in M_{K}\backslash M_{K}^{\infty}}\log^{+}|\alpha|_{v}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\left(\frac{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log^{+}{|\alpha_{j}|}+\frac{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}{n}\log{|a_{n}|}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\left(|a_{n}|\prod_{j}\max\{1,|\alpha_{j}|\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log M(\alpha),

where we have invoked (
2.3
) in the second equality. This proves the result.
∎

2.3.
Siegel’s lemma

Consider the system of homogeneous linear equations

\displaystyle a_{11}x_{1}+a_{12}x_{2}+\ldots+a_{1n}x_{n}=0

(2.4)

\displaystyle a_{m1}x_{1}+a_{m2}x_{2}+\ldots+a_{mn}x_{n}=0

with
a_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}
, not all zero. If
m<n
, then the system of linear equations (
2.4
) always has a non-trivial solution in
\mathbb{Z}
.

Lemma 2.18

(Siegel’s lemma)
.

Consider a system of
M
linear equations in
N
variables (
2.4
) with
M<N
and coefficients
a_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}
, not all zero.
Then there is a non-trivial solution

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
of (
2.4
) in
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
with

|x_{i}|<(NA)^{M/(N-M)},

where
A=\max_{ij}|a_{ij}|
.

Proof.

For any
H>0
, the number of points
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
satisfying
0\leq x_{i}\leq H
is at most
(H+1)^{N}
. Let
T:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
be a linear map with

T(x_{1}\ldots,x_{N})=(a_{11}x_{1}+\ldots a_{1N}x_{N},\ldots,a_{M1}x_{1}+a_{M2}x_{2}+\ldots+a_{MN}x_{N}).

For any real
a
, we set
a^{+}=\max\{a,0\}
and
a^{-}=\max\{-a,0\}
. Then
a=a^{+}-a^{-}
and
|a|=a^{+}+a^{-}
.
We also set
L_{j}^{+}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{ij}^{+}
,
L_{j}^{-}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{ij}^{-}
and
L_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}|a_{ij}|
.
For any integer
H>0
, writing
T(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{M})
we see that
whenever
0\leq x_{i}\leq H
for all
i
,

-L_{j}^{-}H\leq y_{j}\leq L_{j}^{+}H

for all
j
.
The number of integers in the box
\prod_{j=1}^{M}[-L_{j}^{-}H,L_{j}^{+}H]
is at most

\prod_{j=1}^{M}(L_{j}^{+}H+L_{j}^{-}H+1)=\prod_{j=1}^{M}(L_{j}H+1).

If
(H+1)^{N}>\prod_{j=1}^{M}(L_{j}H+1)

then by pigeonhole principle, there exists
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
with
0\leq x_{i}^{\prime},x_{i}^{\prime\prime}\leq H
for all
i
such that
T(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=T(\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime})
.
Then
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x^{\prime}}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}\in\ker{T}
with
|x_{i}|=|x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i}^{\prime\prime}|\leq H
.
We choose
H
to be the natural number

H=\left[(NA)^{M/N-M}\right]

where
A=\max_{ij}|a_{ij}|
and
[t]
denotes the greatest integer part of
t
.
Then using the fact that
L_{j}\leq NA
for all
j
, we see that

(H+1)^{N}>(H+1)^{M}(NA)^{M}\geq(NAH+1)^{M}\geq\prod_{j=1}^{M}(L_{j}H+1).

Thus
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
is a non-trivial solution to the system of linear equations (
2.4
) satisfying

|x_{i}|<(NA)^{M/(N-M)}.

∎

Next we give a generalization of Siegel’s lemma for number fields in the form that will be used later.

Lemma 2.19

(Siegel’s lemma for number fields)
.

Let
K
be a number field of degree
d
over
\mathbb{Q}
and
M,N
be positive integers such that
N>dM
. Consider a homogeneous system of
M
linear equations in
N
variables (
2.4
) with coefficients
a_{ij}\in K
not all zero. Then, there exists a non-trivial solution

\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
of this system of linear equations such that

h(\mathbf{x})\leq\frac{dM}{N-dM}\left(h(B)+\log N+c(K)\right),

where
B=(\ldots,a_{ij},\ldots)
, the vector formed by all the coefficients and
c(K)
is a positive constant depending only on
K
.

Proof.

Let
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d}
be a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Then we can write

(2.5)

a_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{ij}^{(k)}\omega_{k},\phantom{mm}a_{ij}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Now for any
1\leq i\leq M
,

\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{ij}x_{j}=0\phantom{mm}{\rm~if~and~only~if~}\phantom{mm}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{ij}^{(k)}x_{j}\omega_{k}=0.

Since
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d}
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis, we conclude that for any
1\leq i\leq M
,

(2.6)

\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{ij}x_{j}=0\phantom{mm}{\rm~if~and~only~if~}\phantom{mm}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{ij}^{(k)}x_{j}=0,\phantom{mm}{\rm~for~all~}1\leq k\leq d.

Thus we have a system of
Md
linear equations in
N
variables with
N>Md
.
For each embedding
\sigma_{r}:K\to\mathbb{C}
,
1\leq r\leq d
, using (
2.5
) we have

\sigma_{r}(a_{ij})=\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{ij}^{(k)}\sigma_{r}(\omega_{k}).

Using the fact that
(\sigma_{r}(\omega_{k}))_{1\leq k,r\leq d}
is an invertible matrix, we obtain

(2.7)

\max_{k}|a_{ij}^{(k)}|\leq c_{1}\max_{r}|\sigma_{r}(a_{ij})|

for a positive constant
c_{1}
depending on
K
.
Let
A=\max_{r,i,j}|\sigma_{r}(a_{ij})|
.
Using Lemma
2.18

we get a non-trivial solution
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
to the system of linear equations
2.6
satisfying

|x_{i}|<(Nc_{1}A)^{dM/(N-dM)}.

Since
\log{A}\leq h(B)
, we get

h(\mathbf{x})\leq\frac{dM}{N-dM}(h(B)+\log N+c(K))

where
c(K):=\log c_{1}
only depends on
K
.
∎

2.4.
Index

Let
K
be a number field, and let
P\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
be a polynomial in
m
variables with coefficients in
K
. For a given variable
x_{h}
, the partial degree of
P
with respect to
x_{h}
is denoted by
\deg_{x_{h}}(P)
, which represents the highest exponent of
x_{h}
appearing in
P
. The notion of the index
\text{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(P)
provides a measure of the vanishing order of
P
at
\bar{\alpha}
relative to the weights
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
. We give the definition below.

Definition 2.20
.

The
index
of a polynomial
P\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
at the point
\bar{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})\in K^{m}
with respect to a given
m
-tuple of positive integers
\bar{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
is defined as follows:

\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(P)=\min_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})}\left\{\frac{i_{1}}{r_{1}}+\ldots+\frac{i_{m}}{r_{m}}:\partial_{I}P(\bar{\alpha})\neq 0\right\},

where
\partial_{I}
denotes the differential operator

\partial_{I}=\frac{1}{i_{1}!\cdots i_{m}!}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\right)^{i_{1}}\ldots\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{m}}\right)^{i_{m}}

corresponding to the multi-index
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{m}
.

Remark 2.21
.

With the above notation, the index
\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(P)
has the following properties:

•

\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(P)\geq 0
. This is immediate from the definition, as all terms
\frac{i_{h}}{r_{h}}
are non-negative.

•

\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(P)=0
if and only if
P(\bar{r})\neq 0
. Indeed, if
P(\bar{r})\neq 0
, then the partial derivative corresponding to the zero multi-index
I=(0,\ldots,0)
is nonzero, yielding an index of zero.

•

For any positive integer
t
,
\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},t\bar{r}}(P)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(P)/t
. This follows by definition.

•

If
\deg_{x_{h}}(P)\leq r_{h}
for all
1\leq h\leq m
, then
\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(P)\leq m
, with equality when

P(x)=\prod_{h=1}^{m}(x_{h}-\alpha_{h})^{r_{h}}.

In this case,
P
vanishes to order exactly
r_{h}
along each coordinate direction, leading to an index equal to the sum of the weights.

The next lemma gives a relation between height of a polynomial and its derivative.

Lemma 2.22
.

Let
P\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
be a polynomial with
\deg_{x_{h}}P=d_{h}
and
\partial_{I}
be a differential operator with multi-index
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})
. Then we have

h(\partial_{I}P)\leq(d_{1}+\ldots+d_{m})\log 2+h(P).

Proof.

The proof follows by observing that the coefficients of

\partial_{I}P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=\frac{1}{i_{1}!\cdots i_{m}!}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\right)^{i_{1}}\ldots\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{m}}\right)^{i_{m}}P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})

consist of products of
\binom{d_{1}}{i_{1}}\ldots\binom{d_{m}}{i_{m}}
and coefficients of
P
.

∎

We now establish several fundamental properties of the index under addition, multiplication, and differentiation of polynomials.

Lemma 2.23
.

Let
P,Q\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
, let
\bar{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
be an
m
-tuple of positive integers, and let
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{m}
be a multi-index. For any point
\bar{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})\in K^{m}
, the following properties hold:

(1)

Multiplicativity:

\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(PQ)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(P)+\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(Q).

(2)

Subadditivity under addition:

\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(P+Q)\geq\min\left(\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(P),\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(Q)\right).

(3)

Inequality under differentiation:

\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(\partial_{I}P)\geq\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(P)-\sum_{h=1}^{m}\frac{i_{h}}{r_{h}}.

Proof.

These properties are an easy consequence of the definition of the index and are left to the reader.
∎

2.5.
Generalized Wronskian

Let
K
be a field of characteristic zero, and let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
be a set of algebraically independent variables over
K
. Consider a family of polynomials
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
. To study the linear independence of these polynomials, we define a generalized version of the classical Wronskian determinant, which incorporates partial derivatives with respect to multiple variables.

Definition 2.24
.

A
generalized Wronskian determinant
associated with the polynomials
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}
is defined as

W_{\mathbf{\mu_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{\mu_{n}}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=\det(\partial_{\mathbf{\mu_{i}}}\varphi_{j})_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\leq n\\
j\leq n\end{subarray}}

where
\mathbf{\mu_{i}}=(\mu_{i1},\ldots,\mu_{im})
is a multi-index for each
1\leq i\leq n
, with

|\mathbf{\mu_{i}}|=\mu_{i1}+\ldots+\mu_{im}\leq i-1
.

We now state a criterion for linear independence based on the generalized Wronskian.

Lemma 2.25

(Wronskian criterion for linear independence)
.

Let
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
be polynomials. Then
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}
are linearly independent over
K
if and only if
W_{\mathbf{\mu_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{\mu_{n}}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
is not identically zero for some multi-indices
\mathbf{\mu_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{\mu_{n}}
with
|\mathbf{\mu_{i}}|\leq i-1
for all
i
.

Proof.

Suppose
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}
are linearly dependent over
K
. Then there exist constants
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\in K
, not all zero, such that

c_{1}\varphi_{1}+\ldots+c_{n}\varphi_{n}=0.

Applying the operator
\partial_{\mathbf{\mu}}
to this relation yields

c_{1}\partial_{\mathbf{\mu}}\varphi_{1}+\ldots+c_{n}\partial_{\mathbf{\mu}}\varphi_{n}=0,

for every choice of
\mathbf{\mu}
.
In particular, this holds for all operators
\partial_{\mathbf{\mu_{1}}},\ldots\partial_{\mathbf{\mu_{n}}}
used to construct the generalized Wronskian
W_{\mathbf{\mu_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{\mu_{n}}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
. Consequently, the columns of the Wronskian matrix are linearly dependent, implying that the determinant of the matrix is identically zero.

Conversely, suppose
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}
are linearly independent over
K
. Choose an integer
d>0
such that
\deg_{x_{h}}\varphi_{i}\leq d
, for all
1\leq i\leq n
and
1\leq h\leq m
. Consider the Kronecker substitution

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\mapsto(t,t^{d},\ldots,t^{d^{m-1}}),

where
t
is a new variable. This maps monomials in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
to powers of
t
and is injective for monomials with partial degrees less than
d
. Thus, the polynomials
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}
are linearly independent over
K
if and only if

\Phi_{j}(t)=\varphi_{j}(t,t^{d},\ldots,t^{d^{m-1}})

are linearly independent over
K
. By a well-known result of Wronski ( see
[
15
]
Lemma 6.4.3),
\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{n}
are linearly independent over
K
if and only if the Wronskian

(2.8)

W(t)=\det\left(\frac{1}{(i-1)!}\frac{d^{i-1}}{dt^{i-1}}\Phi_{j}\right)_{i,j=1,\ldots,n}

is not identically zero. Moreover, there exist universal polynomials
a_{\boldsymbol{\mu},i}(t;d,m)\in\mathbb{Q}[t]
such that

\frac{d^{i-1}}{dt^{i-1}}\Phi_{j}=\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\mu}|\leq i-1}a_{\boldsymbol{\mu},i}(t;d,m)\partial_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\varphi_{j}(t,\ldots,t^{d^{m-1}}).

Substituting this in (
2.8
) we see that
W(t)
is a linear combination of generalized Wronskians
W_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}}(t,t^{d},\ldots,t^{d^{m-1}})
with
|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}|\leq i-1
. Since
W(t)
is not identically zero, at least one of these generalized Wronskians must be non-zero.
∎

3.
Roth’s theorem

In this section, we present a proof of Roth’s theorem. By way of motivation, we give a quick proof of Liouville’s theorem first.

3.1.
Liouville’s theorem

Theorem 3.1

(Liouville’s Theorem)
.

Let
\alpha
be a real algebraic number of degree
n>1.
Then there exists a constant
c(\alpha)>0
such that for every
\dfrac{p}{q}\in\mathbb{Q}

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|>\frac{c(\alpha)}{q^{n}}.

Proof.

Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}
be the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
. Say
f(x)=a_{n}x^{n}+\ldots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
. Therefore, for any
p/q\in\mathbb{Q}
, we have

\left|f\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)\right|=\frac{|a_{n}p^{n}+a_{n-1}p^{n-1}q+\ldots+a_{0}q^{n}|}{q^{n}}\geq\frac{1}{q^{n}}.

Let
\alpha_{1}=\alpha
,
\alpha_{2}
,
\ldots
,
\alpha_{n}
be the distinct roots of
f(x)
. Then, we can write

f(x)=a_{n}(x-\alpha)\cdots(x-\alpha_{n}).

This implies

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|\geq\frac{1/q^{n}}{|a_{n}|\prod_{i=2}^{n}\left|\frac{p}{q}-\alpha_{i}\right|}.

Let
\displaystyle M=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\{|\alpha_{i}|\}
. If
|p/q|\geq 2M
, then by triangle inequality,

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|\geq M>\frac{M}{q^{n}}.

On the other hand if
|p/q|<2M
, then
|\alpha_{i}-p/q|<3M
for
2\leq i\leq n.
Therefore

\left|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\right|>\left(\frac{1}{|a_{n}|(3M)^{n-1}}\right)\frac{1}{q^{n}}.

Choosing
c(\alpha)=\min\left\{M,\dfrac{1}{|a_{n}|(3M)^{n-1}}\right\},
we get the required result.
∎

3.2.
Equivalent formulations of Roth’s theorem

We state two formulations of Lang’s generalization of Roth’s theorem over a number field
K
, and show that they are equivalent. Note that when
K=\mathbb{Q}
, one recovers the classical theorem of Roth.

Theorem 3.2

(Roth’s Theorem)
.

Let
K
be a number field and
S
be a finite set of places of
K
. Let
\alpha\in\bar{K}
and
\varepsilon>0
be fixed. Then there are finitely many
\beta\in K
such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\prod_{v\in S}\min(1,|\beta-\alpha|_{v})<\frac{1}{H(\beta)^{2+\varepsilon}}.

We will show that this formulation of Roth’s theorem is equivalent to the following statement, which we shall prove at the end of this section.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
K
be a number field and
S
be a finite set of places of
K
. Let
\alpha\in\bar{K}
and
\delta>0
be fixed. Define a map
\zeta:S\to[0,1]
such that
\sum_{v\in S}\zeta(v)=1
. Then there are finitely many
\beta\in K
such that

(3.2)

\displaystyle|\beta-\alpha|_{v}<\frac{1}{H(\beta)^{(2+\delta)\zeta({v})}}\phantom{mm}{\rm~for~all~}v\in S.

Proposition 3.4
.

Theorem
3.2
and Theorem
3.3
are equivalent.

Proof.

Assume that Theorem
3.2
is true. Let
\zeta:S\to[0,1]
be a map such that
\sum_{v\in S}\zeta(v)=1
. Assume
\beta
satisfies (
3.2
), then

\prod_{v\in S}|\beta-\alpha|_{v}<\frac{1}{H(\beta)^{2+\varepsilon}}.

Since

\prod_{v\in S}\min(1,|\beta-\alpha|_{v})\leq\prod_{v\in S}|\beta-\alpha|_{v}<\frac{1}{H(\beta)^{2+\varepsilon}}

by Theorem
3.2
there are finitely many such
\beta
.
Conversely, assume that Theorem
3.3
is true. Let
s
be the cardinality of the set
S
. Define
\zeta:S\to[0,1]
such that for some integer
a_{v}
with
0\leq a_{v}\leq s
,
\zeta(v)=a_{v}/s
and
\sum_{v\in S}\zeta({v})=1.
If
\mathcal{E}
denotes the set of all such maps
\zeta
, then cardinality of
\mathcal{E}
is finite. Next, let
\beta
be a solution to (
3.1
). Define
\lambda_{v}(\beta)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that

\min(1,|\beta-\alpha|_{v})=\frac{1}{H(\beta)^{(2+\varepsilon)\lambda_{v}(\beta)}}.

Therefore, we have

\prod_{v\in S}\min(1,|\beta-\alpha|_{v})=\frac{1}{H(\beta)^{(2+\varepsilon)\sum_{v\in S}\lambda_{v}(\beta)}}.

Comparing with (
3.1
) we see that
\sum_{v\in S}\lambda_{v}(\beta)\geq 1
. Therefore, we have

\sum_{v\in S}[2s\lambda_{v}(\beta)]\geq\sum_{v\in S}2s\lambda_{v}(\beta)-s\geq s.

This gives that for each
v\in S
, there exists
b_{v}(\beta)\in\mathbb{N}
such that
0\leq b_{v}(\beta)\leq 2s\lambda_{v}(\beta)
and
\sum_{v\in S}b_{v}(\beta)=s.
Then the function
\zeta:S\to[0,1]
defined as
\zeta({v})=b_{v}(\beta)/s
lies in
\mathcal{E}
. This yields that a
\beta
satisfying (
3.1
) also satisfies (
3.2
) for some
\zeta\in\mathcal{E}.
From Theorem
3.3
, for every
\zeta\in\mathcal{E}
, there exist only finitely many
\beta
satisfying (
3.2
) and there are finitely many
\zeta\in\mathcal{E}.
So, there are finitely many
\beta
satisfying (
3.1
). This completes the proof.
∎

3.3.
Auxiliary polynomial

Next we use Siegel’s lemma to construct an auxiliary polynomial with controlled height which vanishes to high order at the point
\bar{\alpha}:=(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)\in K^{m}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
r_{1},\dots,r_{m}
be positive integers and let
\mathcal{I}(m,\varepsilon)
denote the number of tuples
I=\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
such that
0\leq i_{h}\leq r_{h}
for all
1\leq h\leq m
satisfying:

\sum_{h=1}^{m}\frac{i_{h}}{r_{h}}\leq\frac{m}{2}(1-\varepsilon).

Then,

\#\mathcal{I}(m,\varepsilon)\leq(r_{1}+1)(r_{2}+1)\dots(r_{m}+1)\operatorname{exp}(-\varepsilon^{2}m/16).

Proof.

Note that
e^{t}\geq 1
for all
t\geq 0
. As a result, we find that

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{I}(m,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\right)\in\mathcal{I}(m,\varepsilon)}1

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\right)\in\mathcal{I}(m,\varepsilon)}\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\left(\frac{m}{2}(1-\varepsilon)-\frac{i_{1}}{r_{1}}-\cdots-\frac{i_{m}}{r_{m}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i_{1}=0}^{r_{1}}\cdots\sum_{i_{m}=0}^{r_{m}}\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\left(\frac{m}{2}(1-\varepsilon)-\frac{i_{1}}{r_{1}}-\cdots-\frac{i_{m}}{r_{m}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}m}{8}\right)\sum_{i_{1}=0}^{r_{1}}\cdots\sum_{i_{m}=0}^{r_{m}}\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\left(\frac{m}{2}-\frac{i_{1}}{r_{1}}-\cdots-\frac{i_{m}}{r_{m}}\right)\right)

(3.3)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}m}{8}\right)\prod_{h=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{r_{h}}\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{i}{r_{h}}\right)\right)\right).

Using the inequality
e^{t}\leq 1+t+t^{2}
, valid for all
|t|\leq 1
, we find that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{r}\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{i}{r}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{r}\left\{1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{i}{r}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{16}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{i}{r}\right)^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\left\{\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{64}\right)-\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{16}\right)\frac{i}{r}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{16}\frac{i^{2}}{r^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle=(r+1)\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{192}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{96r}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(r+1)\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{16}\right),\quad\text{ using }r\geq 1.

Plugging the above inequality in
3.3
and using
1+t\leq e^{t}
, we obtain the following estimates:

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{I}(m,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}m}{8}\right)\prod_{h=1}^{m}\left(\left(r_{h}+1\right)\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{16}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}m}{8}\right)\prod_{h=1}^{m}\left(\left(r_{h}+1\right)\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{16}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(r_{1}+1\right)\cdots\left(r_{m}+1\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}m}{16}\right),

which proves the result.
∎

Lemma 3.6

(Auxiliary polynomial)
.

Let
\alpha\in\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
be an algebraic integer with
d=[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let
m
be large enough so that

\exp(\varepsilon^{2}m/16)>2d

and
{\bar{r}}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
an
m
-tuple of positive integers. Then there exists a polynomial
P\in\mathbb{Z}\left[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\right]
with
\operatorname{deg}_{X_{h}}P\leq r_{h}
for
h=1,\dots,m
, such that the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(P)\geq\frac{m}{2}(1-\varepsilon)
,

(2)

h(P)\leq(r_{1}+r_{2}+\dots+r_{m})C_{\alpha}
, where
C_{\alpha}>0
is a constant depending only on
\alpha
.

Proof.

We write

P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{r_{1}}\cdots\sum_{j_{m}=0}^{r_{m}}c(j_{1},\ldots,j_{m})x_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots x_{m}^{j_{m}}

with
c(j_{1},\ldots,j_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}
. Note that the
height
H(P)
of
P
coincides with the maximum of the absolute values of the coefficients
|c(j_{1},\dots,j_{m})|
of
P
. Clearly, the number of coefficients is
N:=(r_{1}+1)\cdots(r_{m}+1)
. Writing
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})
and
\bar{\alpha}=(\alpha,\ldots,\alpha)
, the index condition requires that

(3.4)

\partial_{I}P(\bar{\alpha})=0\ \text{whenever}\ \sum_{h=1}^{m}\frac{i_{h}}{r_{h}}<\frac{m}{2}(1-\varepsilon).

By Lemma
3.5
, the number of such
m
-tuples
I
is at most

M:=(r_{1}+1)\cdots(r_{m}+1)\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}m/16).

It follows that

\frac{N}{dM}=\frac{(r_{1}+1)\cdots(r_{m}+1)}{d(r_{1}+1)\cdots(r_{m}+1)\cdot e^{-\varepsilon^{2}m/16}}>2

if
e^{\varepsilon^{2}m/16}>2d
. In particular, we find that

\frac{dM}{N-dM}=\frac{1}{\frac{N}{dM}-1}<1.

From the expression

\partial_{I}P=\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{r_{1}}\cdots\sum_{j_{m}=0}^{r_{m}}\binom{j_{1}}{i_{1}}\dots\binom{j_{m}}{i_{m}}c(j_{1},\ldots,j_{m})x_{1}^{j_{1}-i_{1}}\cdots x_{m}^{j_{m}-i_{m}},

one finds that

\partial_{I}P(\bar{\alpha})=\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{r_{1}}\cdots\sum_{j_{m}=0}^{r_{m}}\binom{j_{1}}{i_{1}}\cdots\binom{j_{m}}{i_{m}}\alpha^{\left(j_{1}+\cdots+j_{m}-i_{1}-\cdots-i_{m}\right)}c(j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}).

Let
B
be the vector formed by the coefficients of the equations, we find that

h(B)\leq\operatorname{max}_{J}\left\{h\left(\binom{j_{1}}{i_{1}}\cdots\binom{j_{m}}{i_{m}}\alpha^{\left(j_{1}+\cdots+j_{m}-i_{1}-\cdots-i_{m}\right)}\right)\right\}\leq(r_{1}+\dots+r_{h})(h(\alpha)+\log 2).

Applying Siegel’s lemma, there exists a solution for this system of linear equations, namely
\mathbf{x}=(c(J))_{J}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that

\begin{split}h(\mathbf{x})\leq&\frac{dM}{N-dM}\left(h(B)+\log N+c(K)\right)\\
<&(r_{1}+\dots+r_{m})(h(\alpha)+\log 2)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log(r_{i}+1)+c(K),\\
<&(r_{1}+\ldots+r_{m})(h(\alpha)+\log 2+1+c(K))\\
\end{split}

where
K:=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
and
c(K)
is a positive constant depending only on
K
. This in turn implies that

h(\mathbf{x})\leq(r_{1}+r_{2}+\dots+r_{m})C_{\alpha}

for some constant
C_{\alpha}>0
which depends only on
\alpha
.
∎

3.4.
A lower bound for the index

In this subsection, we prove lower bounds for the index of an auxiliary polynomial at certain points
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m})
which shall be prescribed later on in our proof.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{Z}[X_{1},\dots,X_{m}]
with
\deg_{X_{h}}(P)\leq r_{h}
, and let
\bar{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m})
be an
m
-tuple of algebraic numbers in a number field
K
. Then, for any nonnegative integer
m
-tuple
J=(j_{1},\dots,j_{m})
,

H(\partial_{J}P(\beta))\leq 4^{(r_{1}+\cdots+r_{m})}H(P)\prod_{h=1}^{m}H(\beta_{h})^{r_{h}}.

Proof.

Fixing
J
, we denote for ease of notation
T:=\partial_{J}P
.
We estimate
|T(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m})|_{v}
for
v\in M_{K}
. First, suppose that
v
is archimedean, then using triangle inequality we get

|T(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m})|_{v}\leq|(r_{1}+1)\cdots(r_{m}+1)|_{v}|T|_{v}\prod_{h=1}^{m}\max\{|\beta_{h}|_{v},1\}^{r_{h}}.

since the number of terms is at most
(r_{1}+1)\cdots(r_{m}+1)
.
On the other hand, if
v
is non-archimedean, by the strong triangle inequality, we have

|T(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m})|_{v}\leq\prod_{h=1}^{m}\max\{|\beta_{h}|_{v},1\}^{r_{h}}.

Multiplying over all
v\in M_{K}
, we obtain

H(T(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m}))\leq(r_{1}+1)\cdots(r_{m}+1)H(T)\prod_{h=1}^{m}H(\beta_{h})^{r_{h}}

Using Lemma
2.22
and the fact that
(r_{h}+1)\leq 2^{r_{h}}
for all
h
, we get

H(T(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m}))\leq 4^{r_{1}+\cdots+r_{m}}H(P)\prod_{h=1}^{m}H(\beta_{h})^{r_{h}}

and this completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.8

(Index Lemma)
.

Let
0<\delta<1
and
0<\varepsilon<\delta/22
. Choose
m
sufficiently large so that

\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}m}{16}\right)>2d.

Let
\bar{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
be an
m
-tuple of positive integers and
P\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}]
a polynomial, with
\deg_{x_{h}}(P)\leq r_{h}
for each
h
, satisfying the conditions of Lemma
3.6
. Given a finite set of places
S
, define a partition of unity
\zeta:S\to[0,1]
, and let
\bar{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m})\in K^{m}
be such that

|\beta_{h}-\alpha|_{v}\leq\frac{1}{H(\beta_{h})^{(2+\delta)\zeta(v)}}

for all
v\in S
and
1\leq h\leq m
. Suppose that
\bar{\beta}
satisfies the height bound

\max_{1\leq h\leq m}H(\beta_{h})^{r_{h}}\leq\left(\min_{1\leq h\leq m}H(\beta_{h})^{r_{h}}\right)^{1+\varepsilon}

and

H(\beta_{h})\geq(8e^{C_{\alpha}}H(\alpha))^{8/\alpha+4/11}
for all

, where
C_{\alpha}>0
is the constant depending only on
\alpha
from Lemma
3.6
.
Then, we have

\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)\geq\varepsilon m.

Proof.

For ease of notation, we set
\theta:=\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(P)
, let
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\theta_{0}\in(0,\theta)
. Set
D:=\min\left\{H\left(\beta_{h}\right)^{r_{h}}\mid 1\leq h\leq m\right\}
, and suppose that
J=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{m})
is a tuple such that
\sum_{h=1}^{m}\frac{j_{h}}{r_{h}}\leq\theta_{0}
. Then we claim that

(3.5)

\prod_{v\in S}|\partial_{J}P\left(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m}\right)|_{v}\leq(8H(\alpha))^{(r_{1}+\dots+r_{m})}H(P)D^{-(2+\delta)\left(\theta-\theta_{0}\right)}.

Before proving the claim, we show how the result follows from the above.

Given
J=\left(j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}\right)
such that
\sum_{h=1}^{m}j_{h}/r_{h}\leq\varepsilon m
, we show that
\partial_{J}P\left(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m}\right)=0
.
We set
\theta_{0}=\varepsilon m
and note that
\theta\geq\frac{m}{2}(1-\varepsilon)
by Lemma
3.6
. Since
\varepsilon<1/22
, one has that
\theta_{0}<\theta
.
We recall from Lemma
3.6
that

H(P)\leq e^{C_{\alpha}(r_{1}+\dots+r_{m})}
where
C_{\alpha}
depends on
\alpha
.
From (
3.5
),

(3.6)

\displaystyle\prod_{v\in S}|\partial_{J}P\left(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m}\right)|_{v}\leq\frac{(8H(\alpha))^{\left(r_{1}+\cdots+r_{m}\right)}H(P)}{D^{\left(\theta-\theta_{0}\right)(2+\delta)}}\leq\frac{B(\alpha)^{\left(r_{1}+\cdots+r_{m}\right)}}{D^{\left(\frac{m}{2}(1-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon m\right)(2+\delta)}}

where
B(\alpha):=8e^{C_{\alpha}}H(\alpha)
is a constant which only depends on
\alpha
.
On the other hand, Lemma
3.7
implies that

(3.7)

\displaystyle H\left(\partial_{J}P(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m})\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4^{\left(r_{1}+\cdots+r_{m}\right)}H(P)\prod_{h=1}^{m}H\left(\beta_{h}\right)^{r_{h}}

\displaystyle\leq B(\alpha)^{\left(r_{1}+\cdots+r_{m}\right)}D^{m(1+\varepsilon)}.

Using Lemma
2.15
we know that either the derivative
\partial_{J}P\left(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m}\right)
is zero, or else

\prod_{v\in S}|\partial_{J}P\left(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m}\right)|_{v}\geq H\left(\partial_{J}P\left(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m}\right)\right)^{-1}.

So it suffices to show that our hypotheses contradict the latter.
Assuming
\partial_{J}P\left(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m}\right)\neq 0
, taking the product of (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) and collecting terms, we obtain

D^{m((1+\delta/2)(1-3\varepsilon)-(1+\varepsilon))}\leq B(\alpha)^{2\left(r_{1}+\cdots+r_{m}\right)}.

Now, since we assumed
\delta<1
and
\varepsilon<\delta/22
, we get

(1+\delta/2)(1-3\varepsilon)-(1+\varepsilon)>\delta/2-11\varepsilon/2>\delta/4

and hence

\max_{1\leq j\leq m}\left\{H\left(\beta_{h}\right)^{r_{h}}\right\}\leq D^{1+\varepsilon}\leq B(\alpha)^{8\left(r_{1}+\cdots+r_{m}\right)(1+\varepsilon)/(m\delta)}.

Selecting

, we deduce that

Therefore, for
H\left(\beta_{j}\right)\leq(8e^{C_{\alpha}}H(\alpha))^{8/\delta+4/11}
, we obtain the desired contradiction, which proves the result.

Thus what remains is to prove the claim (
3.5
). For ease of notation, set
T:=\partial_{J}P
. For
v\in M_{K}
and
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})

(r_{1}+1)\cdots(r_{m}+1)

|T|_{v}\max\{|\alpha|_{v},1\}^{r_{1}+\dots+r_{m}},

we deduce that

(3.8)

|\partial_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}T(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)|_{v}\leq|(r_{1}+1)\cdots(r_{m}+1)|_{v}|T|_{v}(\max\{|\alpha|_{v},1\})^{r_{1}+\dots+r_{m}}.

On the other hand, since

\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}T=\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}\partial_{J}P\geq\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}P-\sum_{h=1}^{m}\frac{j_{h}}{r_{h}}\geq\theta-\theta_{0},

we find that the Taylor expansion of
T
at
\bar{\alpha}=(\alpha,\ldots,\alpha)
is

T(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})=\sum_{\frac{i_{1}}{r_{1}}+\dots+\frac{i_{m}}{r_{m}}\geq\theta-\theta_{0}}\partial_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}T(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)(X_{1}-\alpha)^{i_{1}}\cdots(X_{m}-\alpha)^{i_{m}}.

Substituting
X_{h}=\beta_{h}
and using the fact that there are at most
(r_{1}+1)\cdots(r_{m}+1)
terms, we obtain

\displaystyle|T(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m})|_{v}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\frac{i_{1}}{r_{1}}+\dots+\frac{i_{m}}{r_{m}}\geq\theta-\theta_{0}}|\partial_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}T(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)|_{v}|\beta_{1}-\alpha|_{v}^{i_{1}}\cdots|\beta_{m}-\alpha|_{v}^{i_{m}}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\frac{i_{1}}{r_{1}}+\dots+\frac{i_{m}}{r_{m}}\geq\theta-\theta_{0}}\left(|\partial_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}T(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)|_{v}|\beta_{1}-\alpha|_{v}^{i_{1}}\cdots|\beta_{m}-\alpha|_{v}^{i_{m}}\right)\sum_{\frac{i_{1}}{r_{1}}+\dots+\frac{i_{m}}{r_{m}}\geq\theta-\theta_{0}}1

\displaystyle\leq C_{v}\max_{\frac{i_{1}}{r_{1}}+\dots+\frac{i_{m}}{r_{m}}\geq\theta-\theta_{0}}\left(|\partial_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}T(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)|_{v}|\beta_{1}-\alpha|_{v}^{i_{1}}\cdots|\beta_{m}-\alpha|_{v}^{i_{m}}\right)

for all
v\in S
, where
C_{v}=\prod_{1\leq h\leq m}(r_{h}+1)
. By (
3.8
) we get

\displaystyle|T(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m})|_{v}

\displaystyle\leq C_{v}^{2}~|T|_{v}(\max\{|\alpha|_{v},1\})^{r_{1}+\dots+r_{m}}\max_{\frac{i_{1}}{r_{1}}+\dots+\frac{i_{m}}{r_{m}}\geq\theta-\theta_{0}}\left(\prod_{1\leq h\leq m}|\beta_{h}-\alpha|_{v}^{i_{h}}\right)

Using the fact that
\beta_{h}
is close to
\alpha
, we obtain

|T(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m})|_{v}\leq C_{v}^{2}~|T|_{v}(\max\{|\alpha|_{v},1\})^{r_{1}+\ldots+r_{m}}\max_{\frac{i_{1}}{r_{1}}+\dots+\frac{i_{m}}{r_{m}}\geq\theta-\theta_{0}}\left(\prod_{h=1}^{m}H(\beta_{h})^{-i_{h}(2+\delta)\zeta(v)}\right)

Using

H(\beta_{1})^{i_{1}}\cdots H(\beta_{m})^{i_{m}}=(H(\beta_{1})^{r_{1}})^{i_{1}/r_{1}}\cdots(H(\beta_{m})^{r_{m}})^{i_{m}/r_{m}}\geq D^{\theta-\theta_{0}},

we conclude

|T(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m})|_{v}\leq C_{v}^{2}~|T|_{v}(\max\{|\alpha|_{v},1\})^{r_{1}+\dots+r_{m}}D^{-(\theta-\theta_{0})(2+\delta)\zeta(v)}

Multiplying over
v\in S
, and using
\sum_{v\in S}\zeta(v)=1
, we obtain

\prod_{v\in S}|T(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m})|_{v}\leq\prod_{1\leq h\leq m}(r_{h}+1)^{2}~H(T)H(\alpha)^{(r_{1}+\ldots+r_{m})}D^{-(\theta-\theta_{0})(2+\delta)\zeta(v)}.

Now using Lemma
2.22
and the fact that
r_{h}+1\leq 2^{r_{h}}
for all

, we get

\prod_{v\in S}|T(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m})|_{v}\leq\frac{(8H(\alpha))^{(r_{1}+\dots+r_{m})}H(P)}{D^{(\theta-\theta_{0})(2+\delta)}}.

∎

3.5.
Roth’s lemma

Next we show that index of a polynomial
P
at a point
\bar{\beta}
with respect to certain
m
-tuples of positive integers cannot be very large.

Lemma 3.9

(Roth’s Lemma)
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
be a non-zero polynomial with
\deg_{x_{h}}(P)\leq r_{h}
, where
r_{h}>0
for all
h=1,\dots,m
. Let
{\bar{\beta}}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m})\in\mathbb{\bar{Q}}^{m}
and fix a real number
0<\eta\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Suppose that

•

the
m
-tuple
\bar{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
satisfies

\frac{r_{j+1}}{r_{j}}\leq\eta^{2^{m-1}};

•

the point
(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m})
satisfies

\eta^{2^{m-1}}\min_{1\leq h\leq m}(r_{h}h(\beta_{h}))\geq h(P)+2mr_{1}.

Then
\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)\leq 2m\eta
.

Proof.

We prove the result by using induction on
m
.
Let
\mathrm{ord}_{\bar{\beta}}(P)
denote the order of vanishing of
P
at
\bar{\beta}
.
For
m=1
, let

P(x)=(x-\beta_{1})^{l}Q(x)

for some polynomial
Q(x)
with
Q(\beta_{1})\neq 0
.
Then we have

l=\mathrm{ord}_{\beta_{1}}(P)=r_{1}\operatorname{Ind}_{\beta_{1},r_{1}}(P).

This implies

r_{1}\operatorname{Ind}_{\beta_{1},r_{1}}(P)h(\beta_{1})=l\log M(x-\beta_{1})\leq\log M(P)\leq h(P)+r_{1}.

Therefore

\operatorname{Ind}_{\beta_{1},r_{1}}(P)\leq\frac{h(P)+r_{1}}{r_{1}h(\beta_{1})}\leq\eta^{2}\leq 2\eta.

We assume that the result is true for polynomials in at most
m-1
variables. Let
P
be a polynomial in
m
variables so that
r_{h}\geq 1
for all
1\leq h\leq m
. Consider a decomposition of
P
of the form

P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\phi_{j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1})\psi_{j}(x_{m}),

where
\phi_{j}
’s and
\psi_{j}
’s are polynomials with coefficients in
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
. We pick a decomposition of minimal length, say
k\leq r_{m}+1
. Since in this decomposition
k
is minimal,
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k}
are linearly independent polynomials over
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
. Indeed if
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k}
are not linearly independent, then there exist
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\in\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
, not all zero, such that

c_{1}\phi_{1}+\ldots+c_{k}\phi_{k}=0.

Without loss of generality if
c_{k}\neq 0
, then

P=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\phi_{j}\left(\psi_{j}-\frac{c_{j}}{c_{k}}\psi_{k}\right).

This contradicts the minimality of
k
. In the same way one can show that,
\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{k}
are linearly independent over
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Applying the Wronskian criterion for linear independence (Theorem
2.25
), we see that the Wronskian

U_{1}(x_{m})\coloneq W(\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{k})=\det\left(\frac{1}{(i-1)!}\frac{\partial^{i-1}}{\partial x_{m}^{i-1}}\psi_{j}(x_{m})\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq k}

is not identically zero.
Similarly there exist differential operators

\partial_{\mu_{1}},\ldots,\partial_{\mu_{k}}
, with

|\mu_{i}|=\mu_{i1}+\ldots+\mu_{i(m-1)}\leq i-1\leq k-1\leq r_{m}

for all
1\leq i\leq k
, such that the Wronskian

U_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1})\coloneq W_{\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}}(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k})=\det(\partial_{\mu_{i}}\phi_{j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1}))_{1\leq i,j\leq k}

is not identically zero.

Multiplying the two determinants we see that

\displaystyle V(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\det\left(\frac{1}{(j-1)!}\frac{\partial^{j-1}}{\partial x_{m}^{j-1}}\partial_{\mu_{i}}P\right)_{{1\leq i,j\leq k}}

\displaystyle=\det\left(\left(\frac{1}{(j-1)!}\frac{\partial^{j-1}}{\partial x_{m}^{j-1}}\psi_{r}\right)_{1\leq j,r\leq k}(\partial_{\mu_{i}}\phi_{r})_{1\leq r,i\leq k}\right)

\displaystyle=U_{1}(x_{m})U_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1}).

Clearly,
\deg_{x_{m}}(U_{1})\leq kr_{m}
and
\deg_{x_{h}}(U_{2})\leq kr_{h}
for all
1\leq h\leq m-1
.
Moreover, each term of
V(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
in the first equation above is a product of
k
polynomials and the partial degree of each polynomial with respect to
x_{h}
is at most
r_{h}
. From Lemma
2.22
, we have

(3.9)

h\left(\frac{1}{(j-1)!}\frac{\partial^{j-1}}{\partial x_{m}^{j-1}}\partial_{\mu_{i}}P\right)\leq h(P)+(r_{1}+\ldots+r_{m})\log 2.

Since the determinant is a sum of
k!
terms, Proposition
2.14
gives us that:

(3.10)

h(V)\leq\log k!+\operatorname{max}_{\pi}\left\{h\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{(\pi(i)-1)!}\frac{\partial^{\pi(i)-1}}{\partial x_{m}^{\pi(i)-1}}\partial_{\mu_{i}}P\right)\right\},

where
\pi
ranges over all permutations of
\{1,\dots,k\}
. Lemma
2.11
then implies that

\begin{split}&h\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{(\pi(i)-1)!}\frac{\partial^{\pi(i)-1}}{\partial x_{m}^{\pi(i)-1}}\partial_{\mu_{i}}P\right)\\
\leq&\sum_{i=1}^{k}h\left(\frac{1}{(\pi(i)-1)!}\frac{\partial^{\pi(i)-1}}{\partial x_{m}^{\pi(i)-1}}\partial_{\mu_{i}}P\right)+(r_{1}+\dots+r_{m})\log 2+m(k-1)\log 2\\
\leq&kh(P)+k(r_{1}+\dots+r_{m})\log 2+(r_{1}+\dots+r_{m})\log 2+m(k-1)\log 2\\
\leq&kh(P)+2k(r_{1}+\dots+r_{m})\log 2,\end{split}

where the second inequality invokes (
3.9
). Substitute the above inequality into (
3.10
), we deduce that:

h(V)\leq\log(k!)+kh(P)+2k(r_{1}+\ldots+r_{m})\log 2.

Since
r_{j+1}\leq\eta^{2^{m-1}}r_{j}
with
\eta\leq 1/2
and
m\geq 2
we get

r_{1}+\ldots+r_{m}\leq(1+\eta^{2^{m-1}}+\ldots+\eta^{(m-1)2^{m-1}})r_{1}\leq\frac{4}{3}r_{1}.

This along with the bound

\log(k!)\leq k\log k\leq k(k-1)\leq kr_{m}\leq k\frac{r_{1}}{2}

implies that

h(V)\leq k\left(h(P)+\left(\frac{8}{3}\log 2+\frac{1}{2}\right)r_{1}\right)\leq k\left(h(P)+\frac{5}{2}r_{1}\right).

Our next aim is to show that the hypotheses of Roth’s lemma are satisfied for
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
.
First we show that
U_{1}
satisfies the hypotheses of Roth’s lemma.
We note that the first condition is trivially satisfied as
U_{1}
is a polynomial in one variable with
\deg_{x_{m}}(U_{1})\leq kr_{m}
.
By Remark
2.12
we have
h(V)=h(U_{1})+h(U_{2})
. Therefore

h(U_{1})+2kr_{m}\leq k\left(h(P)+\frac{5}{2}r_{1}\right)+k\frac{r_{1}}{2}\leq k(h(P)+3r_{1})\leq k(h(P)+2mr_{1}).

By the assumption on
P
, we have that

h(P)+2mr_{1}\leq\eta^{2^{m-1}}r_{m}h(\beta_{m}),

and thus

h(U_{1})+2kr_{m}\leq k(\eta^{2^{m-1}}r_{m}h(\beta_{m}))=\eta^{2^{m-1}}(kr_{m})h(\beta_{m}).

Applying Roth’s lemma for polynomials in one variable, we get

(3.11)

\operatorname{Ind}_{\beta_{m},r_{m}}(U_{1})=k\operatorname{Ind}_{\beta_{m},kr_{m}}(U_{1})\leq k\eta^{2^{m-1}}.

Next we shall show that
U_{2}
satisfies the hypotheses of Roth’s lemma.
It is a polynomial in
m-1
variables with
\deg_{x_{h}}(U_{2})\leq kr_{h}
for all
1\leq h\leq m-1
. Now

\frac{kr_{j+1}}{kr_{j}}=\frac{r_{j+1}}{r_{j}}\leq\eta^{2^{m-1}}=(\eta^{2})^{2^{m-2}}.

Now using
h(U_{2})\leq h(V)
and the assumption on
P
, we have

h(U_{2})+2(m-1)kr_{1}\leq k(h(P)+2mr_{1})\leq k\eta^{2^{m-1}}(r_{h}h(\beta_{h}))=(\eta^{2})^{2^{m-2}}(kr_{h})h(\beta_{h})

for all
1\leq h\leq m-1
.
Applying Roth’s lemma for polynomials in
m-1
variables we get

\operatorname{Ind}_{(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m-1}),(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m-1})}(U_{2})=k\operatorname{Ind}_{(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m-1}),(kr_{1},\ldots,kr_{m-1})}(U_{2})\leq 2k(m-1)\eta^{2}.

Now using Lemma
2.23
we get

(3.12)

\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(W)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(U_{1})+\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(U_{2})\leq k\eta^{2^{m-1}}+2k(m-1)\eta^{2}.

The next step would be to relate the index of
W
with the index of
P
to finally get a bound on index of
P
.
Using Lemma
2.23
, for each entry in the matrix corresponding to
W
we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}\left(\frac{1}{(j-1)!}\frac{\partial^{j-1}}{\partial x_{m}^{j-1}}\partial_{\mu_{h}}P\right)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)-\sum_{t=1}^{m-1}\frac{\mu_{ht}}{r_{t}}-\frac{j-1}{r_{m}}

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{Ind}(P)-\frac{\mu_{h1}+\ldots+\mu_{h(m-1)}}{r_{m-1}}-\frac{j-1}{r_{m}}

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{Ind}(P)-\frac{r_{m}}{r_{m-1}}-\frac{j-1}{r_{m}}.

Since the determinant is a sum of
k!
terms each of which is a product of
k
polynomials, using Lemma
2.23
we get,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(W)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\min_{\mu_{h1},\ldots,\mu_{h(m-1)}}\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}\left(\frac{1}{(j-1)!}\frac{\partial^{j-1}}{\partial x_{m}^{j-1}}\partial_{\mu_{h}}P\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\max\left\{\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)-\frac{r_{m}}{r_{m-1}}-\frac{j-1}{r_{m}},0\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\max\left\{\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)-\frac{j-1}{r_{m}},0\right\}-\frac{kr_{m}}{r_{m-1}}.

When
\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)>\frac{k-1}{r_{m}}
, we get

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\max\left\{\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)-\frac{j-1}{r_{m}},0\right\}=k\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)-\frac{(k-1)k}{2r_{m}}\geq\frac{k}{2}\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P).

On the other hand when

\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)\leq\frac{k-1}{r_{m}}
, we take

N=[r_{m}\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)]
. This implies

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\max\left\{\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)-\frac{j-1}{r_{m}},0\right\}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}\left(\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)-\frac{j-1}{r_{m}}\right)=(N+1)\left(\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)-\frac{N}{2r_{m}}\right).

Since
N\leq r_{m}\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)\leq N+1
, we get

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\max\left\{\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)-\frac{j-1}{r_{m}},0\right\}\geq\frac{r_{m}}{2}\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)^{2}\geq\max\left\{\frac{k-1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right\}\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)^{2}\geq\frac{k}{4}\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)^{2}

Therefore using the fact that
\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)\leq m
and
m\geq 2
, we get

(3.13)

\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(W)+\frac{kr_{m}}{r_{m-1}}\geq\min\left\{\frac{k}{2}\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P),\frac{k}{4}\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)^{2}\right\}\geq\frac{k}{2m}\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)^{2}.

Using (
3.12
), (
3.13
) and the fact that
r_{m}\leq\eta^{2}r_{m-1}
we obtain

\frac{k}{2m}\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)^{2}\leq k\eta^{2^{m-1}}+2k(m-1)\eta^{2}+\frac{kr_{m}}{r_{m-1}}\leq 2km\eta^{2}.

Hence

\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)\leq 2m\eta.

∎

3.6.
Proof of Roth’s Theorem

We now provide a proof of Theorem
3.3
, which is equivalent to Roth’s theorem. Suppose that there exists
\delta>0
such that

(3.14)

|\beta-\alpha|_{v}\leq\frac{1}{H(\beta)^{(2+\delta)\zeta(v)}}

has infinitely many solutions
\beta\in K
. Let
0<\delta<1
and choose
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{\delta}{22})
. Next we choose
m
large enough so that

\exp(\varepsilon^{2}m/16)>2d,

where
d=[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]
. Let
\eta=\varepsilon/4
.
Since (
3.14
) has infinitely many solutions in
K
and using the fact that
K
has only finitely many elements of bounded height we can find a solution
\beta_{1}
of (
3.14
) such that

h(\beta_{1})\geq\left({\frac{8}{\alpha}+\frac{4}{11}}\right)\left(\log 8+C_{\alpha}+h(\alpha)\right)\quad{\rm and}\quad h(\beta_{1})\geq\frac{(m\log C_{\alpha}+2)}{\eta^{2^{m-1}}}

where
C_{\alpha}
is the constant appearing in Lemma
3.6
. Note that
C_{\alpha}
depends only on the choice of
\alpha
.
Next, we successively choose solutions
\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{m}
satisfying

\eta^{2^{m-1}}h(\beta_{j+1})\geq 2h(\beta_{j})

for all
2\leq j\leq m-1
.
Since
\eta<1
, this implies that
h(\beta_{j})
is strictly increasing.
We choose a positive integer
r_{1}
such that

\eta^{2^{m-1}}r_{1}h(\beta_{1})\geq 2h(\beta_{m}).

Now we choose
r_{2},\ldots,r_{m}
such that

r_{j}=\left[\frac{r_{1}h(\beta_{1})}{h(\beta_{j})}\right]+1.

Since we chose
m
that satisfies
\exp(\varepsilon^{2}m/16)>2d
, by Lemma
3.6
there exists a polynomial
P\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
such that
\deg_{x_{h}}(P)\leq r_{h}
for
h=1,\dots,m
satisfying

\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{r}}(P)\geq\frac{m}{2}(1-\varepsilon)

and

h(P)\leq(r_{1}+\ldots+r_{m})\log C_{\alpha}

where

\bar{\alpha}=(\alpha,\ldots,\alpha)
,
\bar{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
.
The choice of
r_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
allows us to get

r_{1}h(\beta_{1})\leq r_{j}h(\beta_{j})\leq r_{1}h(\beta_{1})+h(\beta_{j}).

Using the fact that
h(\beta_{j})
is increasing and by choice of
r_{1}
, we obtain

(3.15)

r_{1}h(\beta_{1})\leq r_{1}h(\beta_{1})+h(\beta_{m})\leq(1+\varepsilon)r_{1}h(\beta_{1}).

Therefore the assumptions of Lemma
3.8
are satisfied and we get

(3.16)

\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)\geq\varepsilon m.

Again, by our choice of
r_{j}
and
\beta_{j}
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{r_{j+1}}{r_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{r_{1}h(\beta_{1})}{h(\beta_{j+1})}+1\right)\left(\frac{r_{1}h(\beta_{1})}{h(\beta_{j})}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{h(\beta_{j})}{h(\beta_{j+1})}+\frac{h(\beta_{j})}{r_{1}h(\beta_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta^{2^{m-1}}}{2}+\frac{\eta^{2^{m-1}}}{2}=\eta^{2^{m-1}}.

Therefore
r_{j+1}\leq\eta^{2^{m-1}}r_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq m-1
.
Now using the bounds on
h(P)
and
h(\beta_{1})
that we have above, we get

h(P)+2mr_{1}\leq(r_{1}+\ldots+r_{m})\log C_{\alpha}+2mr_{1}\leq mr_{1}(\log C_{\alpha}+2)\leq\eta^{2^{m-1}}r_{1}h(\beta).

Since

\min_{1\leq h\leq m}r_{j}h(\beta_{j})=r_{1}h(\beta_{1})

we see that the conditions of Lemma
3.9
are satisfied. Therefore we get

(3.17)

\operatorname{Ind}_{\bar{\beta},\bar{r}}(P)\leq 2m\eta=\frac{m\varepsilon}{2}.

This is a contradiction to (
3.16
) and hence we get the result.

4.
The subspace theorem

4.1.
Geometry of numbers and Minkowski’s second theorem

We begin by recalling several fundamental notions from the geometry of numbers, particularly in the context of Euclidean spaces over the ring of adeles of a number field
K
. These notions are essential for understanding subsequent results, including the application of Minkowski’s second theorem in the adelic setting.

Let
G
be a locally compact topological group equipped with a Haar measure
\mu_{G}
. A Haar measure is a Borel measure on
G
that is invariant under left translation. Specifically, for every
y\in G
and any continuous function
f:G\to\mathbb{R}
with compact support, the measure satisfies the invariance property:

\int_{G}f(yx)\,d\mu_{G}(x)=\int_{G}f(x)\,d\mu_{G}(x).

This invariance condition characterizes the Haar measure uniquely up to multiplication by a positive scalar. The existence and uniqueness of Haar measure on any locally compact group are classical results that play a central role in harmonic analysis and number theory.

Let
K
be a number field of degree
r=[K:\mathbb{Q}]
. Denote by
M_{K}
the set of places of
K
. For each place
v\in M_{K}
, the completion of
K
at
v
is denoted by
K_{v}
, and we let
|\cdot|_{v}
be a corresponding normalized absolute value. It is well known that
K_{v}
is locally compact for each
v
. Define

R_{v}:=\{\alpha\in K_{v}\mid|\alpha|_{v}\leq 1\}.

The set
R_{v}
serves as a local analogue of the ring of integers for non-archimedean places
v
. For any measurable subset
\Omega\subseteq K_{v}
and any
\alpha\in K_{v}
, the Haar measure
\mu_{v}
on
K_{v}
satisfies the scaling property:

\mu_{v}(\alpha\Omega)=|\alpha|_{v}\mu_{v}(\Omega).

This property highlights how
\mu_{v}
interacts with the multiplicative structure of
K_{v}
, making it a crucial tool for studying adelic measures.

The adele ring
K_{\mathbb{A}}
of
K
is defined as

K_{\mathbb{A}}:=\left\{x=(x_{v})_{v\in M_{K}}\in\prod_{v\in M_{K}}K_{v}\mid x_{v}\in R_{v}\text{ for all but finitely many }v\right\}.

The topology on
K_{\mathbb{A}}
is
not
the product topology, as the latter would not yield a locally compact space. Instead,
K_{\mathbb{A}}
is endowed with a topology that arises from the restricted product of the local fields
K_{v}
with respect to the subsets
R_{v}
at non-archimedean places.

Given any finite set
S\subset M_{K}
that contains all the archimedean places, define

H_{S}:=\prod_{v\in S}K_{v}\times\prod_{v\notin S}R_{v}\subset K_{\mathbb{A}}.

The set
H_{S}
is open in the restricted product topology, and each such
H_{S}
is locally compact. By specifying that
H_{S}
is open for every such
S
, we endow
K_{\mathbb{A}}
with the structure of a locally compact topological ring. This topological structure ensures that
K
embeds discretely into
K_{\mathbb{A}}
via the diagonal embedding

\iota:K\hookrightarrow K_{\mathbb{A}},\quad\iota(x):=(x)_{v\in M_{K}}.

With respect to this embedding, the quotient
K_{\mathbb{A}}/K
is compact, a fundamental result in adelic analysis that underpins much of the theory of heights and Diophantine approximation.

We now describe the normalization of the Haar measure
\mu_{v}
on each local field
K_{v}
:

•

If
v
is non-archimedean and lies above a rational prime
p
, the Haar measure
\mu_{v}
is normalized so that
\mu_{v}(R_{v})=|D_{K_{v}/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}|_{p}^{1/2}
, where
D_{K_{v}/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}
denotes the discriminant of
K_{v}
over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and
|\cdot|_{p}
denotes the
p
-adic norm.

•

If
v
is real, then
\mu_{v}
is taken to be the standard Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
.

•

If
v
is complex, then
\mu_{v}
is twice the standard Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{C}
.

This normalization ensures compatibility of the measures across different local fields, which is crucial for the product formula and for defining a well-behaved measure on the adele ring
K_{\mathbb{A}}
. Such a choice is motivated by the need to maintain consistency when comparing measures across archimedean and non-archimedean components, facilitating the study of heights, lattices, and fundamental domains in the adelic setting.
For a finite subset
S
of
M_{K}
containing the archimedean primes, the product measure

\mu_{S}:=\prod_{v\in S}\mu_{v}\times\prod_{v\notin S}\mu_{v|R_{v}}

is a Haar measure on the open topological subgroup
H_{S}
of
K_{\mathbb{A}}
. These measures fit together to give a Haar measure
\mu:=\varprojlim_{S}\mu_{S}
on
K_{\mathbb{A}}
.

Now fix a natural number
N
and denote by
E,E_{v},E_{\mathbb{A}}
the spaces
K^{N}
,
K_{v}^{N}
and
K_{\mathbb{A}}^{N}
respectively. We set
\beta
to be the Haar measure on
E_{\mathbb{A}}
which is the
N
-fold product of
\mu
.

Definition 4.1
.

For a finite place
v
, a
K_{v}
lattice in
E_{v}
is an open and compact
R_{v}
sub-module of
E_{v}
. A lattice in a finite dimensional real or complex vector space
V
is a discrete subgroup
\Lambda\subset V
such that
V/\Lambda
is compact.

There is a natural diagonal inclusion
\iota:E\hookrightarrow E_{\mathbb{A}}
. We let
\Lambda
be a lattice in
E
and for each place
v\in M_{K}
let
\Lambda_{v}
denote the
K_{v}
-lattice in
E_{v}
which is spanned by
E
. It is easy to see that for all but finitely many non-archimedean places
v
,
\Lambda_{v}=R_{v}^{N}
.

Before stating the adelic version of Minkowski’s second theorem, we introduce some further notation. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
x=(x_{v})\in E_{\mathbb{A}}
we let
\lambda x\in E
be given by

(\lambda x)_{v}:=\begin{cases}\lambda x_{v}&\text{ if }v\text{ is archimedean,}\\
x_{v}&\text{ if }v\text{ is non-archimedean.}\\
\end{cases}

For
v|\infty
, consider a non-empty, convex, symmetric, bounded subset
S_{v}
contained in
E_{v}
. By symmetric, we mean that
-s\in S_{v}
for all
s\in S_{v}
. Let
\Lambda
be an
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-lattice in
E
and for each prime
v
, denote by
\Lambda_{v}
the completion of
\Lambda
at
v
. In other words,
\Lambda_{v}
is the
R_{v}
-submodule of
E_{v}
which is spanned by
\Lambda
. Consider the subset

S:=\prod_{v|\infty}S_{v}\times\prod_{v\in M_{K}^{f}}\Lambda_{v}

of
E_{\mathbb{A}}
. For
n=1,\dots,N
the
n
-th successive minimum is defined as follows:

\lambda_{n}:=\operatorname{inf}\{t>0\mid tS\text{ contains }n\text{ linearly independent vectors of }\Lambda\text{ over }K\}.

Note that
\lambda S\cap E
is a discrete compact subset of
E_{\mathbb{A}}
and hence it is finite. We find that

0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{N}<\infty.

Theorem 4.2

(Minkowski’s second theorem)
.

With respect to the notation above,

(\lambda_{1}\dots\lambda_{N})^{r}\operatorname{vol}(S)\leq 2^{rN}

where the volume is computed with respect to the Haar measure on
E_{\mathbb{A}}
. Moreover suppose that for every complex place
v
, we have that
\alpha S_{v}=S_{v}
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\alpha|=1
. Let
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
denote the number of real and complex places of
K
respectively. Then we have that:

\frac{2^{rN}\pi^{r_{2}N}}{(N!)^{r_{1}}((2N)!)^{r_{2}}}|D_{K/\mathbb{Q}}|^{-N/2}\leq(\lambda_{1}\dots\lambda_{N})^{r}\operatorname{vol}(S),

where
D_{K/\mathbb{Q}}
is the discriminant of
K
over
\mathbb{Q}
.

4.2.
Reformulations of the subspace theorem

We begin by presenting different versions of the subspace theorem and establishing their equivalence. Given a point
\mathbf{x}:=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})\in K^{n+1}
, define

|\mathbf{x}|_{v}:=\max\{|x_{i}|_{v}\mid i=0,\dots,n\}.

Recall that the multiplicative height of the associated projective point is then given by

H(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{v\in M_{K}}|\mathbf{x}|_{v}.

Theorem 4.3

(Subspace Theorem – Projective Version)
.

With the above notation, let
S\subset M_{K}
be a finite set of places, and for each
v\in S
, suppose we are given linear forms
L_{v,0},\dots,L_{v,n}\in K_{v}[X_{0},\dots,X_{n}]
with coefficients in
K
. Assume that
L_{v,0},\dots,L_{v,n}
are
K_{v}
-linearly independent. Let
\varepsilon>0
be a fixed constant. Then, there exist finitely many proper linear subspaces
T_{1},\dots,T_{h}
in
\mathbb{P}_{K}^{n}
such that all solutions
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{P}^{n}(K)
of

(4.1)

\prod_{v\in S}\prod_{i=0}^{n}\frac{|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}}{|\mathbf{x}|_{v}}<H(\mathbf{x})^{-n-1-\varepsilon}

are contained in
T_{1}\cup\cdots\cup T_{h}
.

It is easy to see that if the above result holds for
S
and
S^{\prime}
is a subset of
S
, then it does also hold for
S^{\prime}
. Denote by
\mathcal{O}_{K,S}
the ring of
S
-integers of
K
. The set
S
can be enlarged so that it contains all archimedean places and
\mathcal{O}_{K,S}
is a principal ideal domain.

Theorem 4.4

(Subspace Theorem – Affine Version)
.

Let
S
be a finite subset of
M_{K}
containing the archimedean places and let
\varepsilon>0
be a fixed constant. Let
L_{v,0},\dots,L_{v,n}\in K_{v}[X_{0},\dots,X_{n}]
be linear forms with coefficients in
K
and assume that
L_{v,0},\dots,L_{v,n}
are
K_{v}
-linearly independent. Then there exist finitely many hyperplanes
T_{1},\dots,T_{h}
of
K^{n+1}
such that all solutions
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{O}_{K,S}^{n+1}\backslash\{0\}
of

(4.2)

\prod_{v\in S}\prod_{i=0}^{n}|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}<H(\mathbf{x})^{-\varepsilon}

are contained in
T_{1}\cup\dots\cup T_{h}
.

We show that the two formulations above are equivalent.

Proposition 4.5
.

The projective subspace theorem
4.3
is equivalent to the affine subspace theorem
4.4
.

Proof.

First, we assume the assertion of the projective subspace theorem
4.3
and derive the affine version
4.4
as a consequence. Let
\mathbf{x}
be an
S
-integral solution to the inequality

\prod_{v\in S}\prod_{i=0}^{n}|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}<H(\mathbf{x})^{-\varepsilon}.

Then we have

\prod_{v\in S}\prod_{i=0}^{n}\frac{|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}}{|\mathbf{x}|_{v}}<\frac{H(\mathbf{x})^{-\varepsilon}}{\left(\prod_{v\in S}|\mathbf{x}|_{v}\right)^{n+1}}.

Since
\mathbf{x}
is
S
-integral, it satisfies
|\mathbf{x}|_{v}\leq 1
for all
v\notin S
. Consequently, we find that

\prod_{v\in S}|\mathbf{x}|_{v}\geq\prod_{v\in M_{K}}|\mathbf{x}|_{v}=H(\mathbf{x}),

where
M_{K}
is the set of all places of
K
. Therefore,
\mathbf{x}
is a solution to the inequality

\prod_{v\in S}\prod_{i=0}^{n}\frac{|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}}{|\mathbf{x}|_{v}}<H(\mathbf{x})^{-n-1-\varepsilon}.

By the projective subspace theorem, there exist finitely many hyperplanes
T_{1},\dots,T_{h}
such that all solutions
\mathbf{x}
lie on
T_{1}\cup\dots\cup T_{h}
. This establishes the affine subspace theorem
4.4
.

Conversely, suppose the affine subspace theorem
4.4
holds. If the projective subspace theorem
4.3
holds for a set
S
, it also holds for any subset
S^{\prime}\subset S
. Thus, we may enlarge
S
to ensure the following conditions:

•

S
contains all archimedean places,

•

\mathcal{O}_{K,S}
is a principal ideal domain.

Let
\mathbf{x}\in K^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
satisfy the projective Diophantine inequality

(4.3)

\prod_{v\in S}\prod_{i=0}^{n}\frac{|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}}{|\mathbf{x}|_{v}}<H(\mathbf{x})^{-n-1-\varepsilon}.

Define the fractional ideal
\mathcal{I}=x_{0}\mathcal{O}_{K,S}+\dots+x_{n}\mathcal{O}_{K,S}
, and let
\delta\in K^{\times}
satisfy
\mathcal{I}=\delta\mathcal{O}_{K,S}
. For any
y\in\mathcal{O}_{K,S}
and
v\notin S
, we have
|x_{i}y|_{v}=|x_{i}|_{v}|y|_{v}\leq|x_{i}|_{v}
. Hence,

|\mathbf{x}|_{v}=\max_{i}|x_{i}|_{v}=\max_{z\in\mathcal{I}}|z|_{v}=|\delta|_{v}.

Define
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\delta^{-1}\mathbf{x}=(\delta^{-1}x_{0},\dots,\delta^{-1}x_{n})
. Then
|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|_{v}=|\delta|_{v}^{-1}|\mathbf{x}|_{v}=1
for
v\notin S
, so
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{K,S}^{n+1}
and
\prod_{v\in S}|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|_{v}=H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
. Since the projective height is invariant under scalar multiplication,
H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=H(\mathbf{x})
. From (
4.3
), we deduce that

\prod_{v\in S}\prod_{i=0}^{n}|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})|_{v}<H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})^{-n-1-\varepsilon}\cdot\left(\prod_{v\in S}|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|_{v}\right)^{n+1}=H(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})^{-\varepsilon}.

Thus,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
satisfies the affine subspace inequality and must lie on
T_{1}\cup\dots\cup T_{h}
, where
T_{1},\dots,T_{h}
are finitely many hyperplanes. Since
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
is a scalar multiple of
\mathbf{x}
, it follows that
\mathbf{x}\in T_{1}\cup\dots\cup T_{h}
. This establishes the projective subspace theorem
4.3
.
∎

4.3.
Approximation domains

We assume that the affine subspace theorem
4.4
is false and derive a contradiction. Assume without loss of generality that
S
is large enough so that
\mathcal{O}_{K,S}
is a principal ideal domain. A solution
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{O}_{K,S}^{n+1}\backslash\{0\}
to (
4.2
) is said to be
primitive
if
\prod_{v\in S}|\mathbf{x}|_{v}=H(\mathbf{x})
. The proof of Proposition
4.5
shows that every solution to the affine subspace equation (
4.2
) is a scalar multiple of a primitive one. Note that if
\mathbf{x}
is a primitive solution and
u
is an
S
-unit, then
u\mathbf{x}
is also a primitive solution. Two primitive solutions
\mathbf{x}_{1}
and
\mathbf{x}_{2}
are said to be equivalent if there exists
u\in\mathcal{O}_{K,S}^{\times}
such that
\mathbf{x}_{2}=u\mathbf{x}_{1}
. We assume therefore by way of contradiction that there are infinitely many equivalence classes of primitive solutions of (
4.2
) no
(n+1)
of which lie on the same hyperplane. Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{O}_{K,S}^{n+1}
be a primitive solution, the affine height of
\mathbf{x}
is defined as

h_{\operatorname{aff}}(\mathbf{x}):=h((1,x))=\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\operatorname{max}_{i}\log^{+}|x_{i}|_{v}.

On the other hand, the height of
\mathbf{x}
is
h(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\operatorname{max}_{i}\log|x_{i}|_{v}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

There exists an
S
-unit
u
such that

h_{\operatorname{aff}}(u\mathbf{x})\leq h(\mathbf{x})+C_{1},

where
C_{1}>0
is a constant depending on
K
and
S
.

Proof.

Suppose for example that
x_{0}\neq 0
. Note that
|x_{0}|_{v}\leq 1
for
v\notin S
since
x_{0}\in\mathcal{O}_{K,S}
. We have by the product formula

\sum_{v\in S}\log|x_{0}|_{v}=-\sum_{v\notin S}\log|x_{0}|_{v}\geq 0.

On the other hand, consider the embedding
\lambda:\mathcal{O}_{K,S}^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{|S|}
defined by
\lambda(u):=(\log|u|_{v})_{v}
. By Dirichlet’s unit theorem,
\Lambda:=\lambda(\mathcal{O}_{K,S}^{\times})
is a lattice of the space
\{(t_{v})_{v}\mid\sum_{v\in S}t_{v}=0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{|S|}
. Write
S=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{s}\}
where
s=|S|
and let
t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{s})\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
be defined as follows:

t_{i}=\begin{cases}\log|x_{0}|_{v_{i}}&\text{ for }i<s;\\
-\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}\log|x_{0}|_{v_{i}}&\text{ for }i=s,\\
\end{cases}

and note that
\sum_{i=1}^{s}t_{i}=0
. Clearly, there exists a lattice point in
\Lambda
close to
t
. This means that there exists an
S
-unit
u
such that for all
i
,

\log|u|_{v_{i}}+t_{i}\geq-C_{1}

for some positive constant
C_{1}>0
. In particular, this implies that for all
v\in S
,

\log|ux_{0}|_{v}=\log|u|_{v}+\log|x_{0}|_{v}\geq-C_{1}.

Note that for
v\in S
,

\log|u\mathbf{x}|_{v}=\operatorname{max}_{i}\log|ux_{i}|_{v}

is either
\geq 0
or
<0
. If this quantity is
\geq 0
then

\log|u\mathbf{x}|_{v}=\operatorname{max}_{i}\log^{+}|ux_{i}|_{v}.

On the other hand, if it is
<0
, then
\operatorname{max}_{i}\log^{+}|ux_{i}|_{v}=0
and we have that

\log|u\mathbf{x}|_{v}\geq\log|ux_{0}|_{v}\geq-C_{1}=-C_{1}+\operatorname{max}_{i}\log^{+}|ux_{i}|_{v}.

Thus, in both cases, we have that

\begin{split}h(\mathbf{x})=h(u\mathbf{x})=&\sum_{v\in S}\operatorname{max}_{i}\log^{+}|ux_{i}|_{v}\\
\geq&\sum_{v\in S}\left(-C_{1}+\operatorname{max}_{i}\log^{+}|ux_{i}|_{v}\right)\\
=&-|S|C_{1}+\sum_{v\in S}\operatorname{max}_{i}\log^{+}|ux_{i}|_{v}\\
=&-|S|C_{1}+\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\operatorname{max}_{i}\log^{+}|ux_{i}|_{v}\\
=&-C_{1}+h_{\operatorname{aff}}(u\mathbf{x}),\end{split}

where we relabel
|S|C_{1}
with
C_{1}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.7
.

Given
B\geq 0
, there are only finitely many equivalence classes of primitive solutions
\mathbf{x}
to the affine subspace inequality (
4.2
) for which
h(\mathbf{x})\leq B
.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
4.6
that there exists
u\in\mathcal{O}_{K,S}^{\times}
such that

h_{\operatorname{aff}}(u\mathbf{x})\leq C_{1}+B.

Note that

h_{\operatorname{aff}}(u\mathbf{x})=\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\operatorname{max}_{i}\log^{+}|ux_{i}|_{v}\geq\operatorname{max}_{i}\left(\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\log^{+}|ux_{i}|_{v}\right)=\operatorname{max}_{i}h(ux_{i}).

Thus each coordinate
ux_{i}
has height at most
(C_{1}+B)
and the conclusion follows from Northcott’s theorem.
∎

Before proceeding, let’s set up some further notation. Writing

L_{v}(\mathbf{x})=(L_{v,0}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,L_{v,n}(\mathbf{x}))=(L_{v,0}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n}),\dots,L_{v,n}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n}))

we have a linear isomorphism
L_{v}:K^{n+1}\xrightarrow{\sim}K^{n+1}
. We write
L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{i,j}^{(v)}x_{j}
, where
a_{i,j}^{(v)}
are in
K
. Thus we may identify
L_{v}=(a_{i,j}^{(v)})_{i,j}
. Setting
|L_{v}|_{v}:=\operatorname{max}_{i,j}|a_{i,j}^{(v)}|_{v}
, and
|L_{v,i}|_{v}:=\operatorname{max}_{j}|a_{i,j}^{(v)}|_{v}
, we find that

|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}=|\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{i,j}^{(v)}x_{j}|_{v}\leq\begin{cases}(n+1)|L_{v,i}|_{v}|\mathbf{x}|_{v}&\text{ if }v\text{ is archimedean};\\
|L_{v,i}|_{v}|\mathbf{x}|_{v}&\text{ if }v\text{ is non-archimedean};\end{cases}

and therefore we deduce that

|L_{v}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}\leq\begin{cases}(n+1)|L_{v}|_{v}|\mathbf{x}|_{v}&\text{ if }v\text{ is archimedean};\\
|L_{v}|_{v}|\mathbf{x}|_{v}&\text{ if }v\text{ is non-archimedean}.\end{cases}

In particular, we find that for non-archimedean
v
,

\log|L_{v}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}-\log|\mathbf{x}|_{v}\leq\log|L_{v}|_{v}

and

\log|L_{v}^{-1}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}-\log|\mathbf{x}|_{v}\leq\log|L_{v}^{-1}|_{v}.

In the second equation above, replace
\mathbf{x}
with
L_{v}(\mathbf{x})
to deduce that

\log|\mathbf{x}|_{v}-\log|L_{v}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}\leq\log|L_{v}^{-1}|_{v}.

Thus, we have proved the following result.

Lemma 4.8
.

For each prime
v\in M_{K}
, there is a constant
\gamma_{v}\geq 0
such that

|\log|L_{v}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}-\log|\mathbf{x}|_{v}|\leq\gamma_{v}

where
\gamma_{v}=0
for all but finitely many places
v\in M_{K}
.

In view of Proposition
4.7
we may assume without loss of generality that
h(\mathbf{x})>0
since there are only finitely many equivalence classes of primitive solutions for which
h(\mathbf{x})\leq 0
.

Lemma 4.9
.

With respect to the notation above, there is a positive constant
C_{2}>0
for which the following assertions hold:

(1)

every equivalence class of primitive solutions contains an element
\mathbf{x}
such that

h_{\operatorname{aff}}(L_{v}(\mathbf{x}))\leq h(\mathbf{x})+C_{2}.

(2)

If
L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})\neq 0
then
|\log|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}|\leq h(\mathbf{x})+C_{2}
.

Proof.

From Lemma
4.8
it follows that
h_{\operatorname{aff}}(L_{v}(\mathbf{x}))
and
h_{\operatorname{aff}}(\mathbf{x})
differ by a bounded quantity. Therefore, there is an element
\mathbf{x}
in the equivalence class for which

h_{\operatorname{aff}}(L_{v}(\mathbf{x}))\leq h_{\operatorname{aff}}(\mathbf{x})+C_{2}^{\prime}\leq h(\mathbf{x})+C_{2}^{\prime}+C_{1},

where in the second inequality, we have appealed to Lemma
4.6
. Setting
C_{2}:=C_{2}^{\prime}+C_{1}
, we get the statement of part (1).

By the fundamental inequality (Proposition
2.15
) applied to
S:=\{v\}
and
\alpha:=L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})
, we deduce that

|\log|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}|\leq\sum_{w\in M_{K}}\log^{+}|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})|_{w}\leq h_{\operatorname{aff}}(L_{v}(\mathbf{x}))

and by part (1), we have that
h_{\operatorname{aff}}(L_{v}(\mathbf{x}))\leq h(\mathbf{x})+C_{2}
. This proves part (2).
∎

Part (2) of the above lemma implies that every equivalence class of primitive solutions has a representative for which the point

P^{\mathbf{x}}:=\left(\frac{\log|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}}{h(\mathbf{x})}\right)_{v,i}

lies in
[-(1+C_{2}/h(\mathbf{x})),(1+C_{2}/h(\mathbf{x}))]^{|S|(n+1)}
. Hence by Proposition
4.7
, for all but finitely many equivalence classes of solutions
\mathbf{x}
, we have that
P^{\mathbf{x}}\in[-2,2]^{|S|(n+1)}
. We subdivide
[-2,2]^{|S|(n+1)}
into boxes of side length
1/N
for a large value of
N
. By the pigeon hole principal there are infinitely many equivalence classes of solutions which lie in one of these boxes. Let
(c_{v,i})_{v,i}
be the "north-east" corner point of this box, which is to say that the following inequalities are satisfied:

(4.4)

(c_{v,i}-1/N)\leq\frac{\log|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}}{h(\mathbf{x})}\leq c_{v,i}

for all
v,i
. On the other hand, the inequality (
4.2
) gives

\sum_{v\in S}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{\log|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}}{h(\mathbf{x})}<-\varepsilon.

This implies that

\sum_{v\in S}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(c_{v,i}-1/N)<-\varepsilon

i.e.,

\sum_{v\in S}\sum_{i=0}^{n}c_{v,i}\leq\frac{|S|(n+1)}{N}-\varepsilon.

Assume that
N\geq\frac{2|S|(n+1)}{\varepsilon}
, and we arrive at the following:

(4.5)

\sum_{v\in S}\sum_{i=0}^{n}c_{v,i}\leq-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Recall that
K_{\mathbb{A}}
is the ring of adeles of
K
. For
v\in S
and
Q\geq 1
, we define the
v
-adic approximation domain:

\Pi_{v}(Q):=\{\mathbf{y}=(y_{0},\dots,y_{n})\in K_{v}^{n+1}\mid|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{y})|_{v}\leq Q^{c_{v,i}}\text{ for }i=0,\dots,n\}.

Observe that
\Pi_{v}(Q)
is the inverse image

L_{v}^{-1}\left(\{\mathbf{y}=(y_{0},\dots,y_{n})\in K_{v}^{n+1}\mid|y_{i}|_{v}\leq Q^{c_{v,i}}\text{ for }i=0,\dots,n\}\right)

and thus we see that:

•

if
v
is archimedean then
\Pi_{v}(Q)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
or
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
is a compact, convex and symmetric subset.

•

For non-achimedian
v
,
\Pi_{v}(Q)
is a lattice in
K_{v}^{n+1}
.

It follows from (
4.4
) that

|L_{v,i}(\mathbf{x})|_{v}\leq H(\mathbf{x})^{c_{v,i}}

and this motivates the following definition.

Definition 4.10
.

The approximation domain is defined in
E_{\mathbb{A}}:=K_{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}
by

\Pi(Q):=\prod_{v\in S}\Pi_{v}(Q)\times\prod_{v\notin S}R_{v}.

We set
E_{K}:=K^{n+1}
and
\iota:E_{K}\rightarrow E_{\mathbb{A}}
be the embedding defined by
\iota(z):=(z)_{v\in M_{K}}
. We have that
\iota(\mathbf{x})\in\Pi(H(\mathbf{x}))
.

We now compute the volume of
\Pi(Q)
. Recall that
r=[K:\mathbb{Q}]
. For each prime
v\in M_{K}
let
\mu_{v}
be the Haar measure on
K_{v}
normalized as follows. If
v
is real (resp. complex) archimedean place
\mu_{v}
is the Lebesgue measure (resp. twice the Lebesgue measure). For a non-archimedean place
v
,
\mu_{v}
is normalized by setting
\mu_{v}(R_{v}):=|D_{K_{v}/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}|^{1/2}
. Take
\beta_{v}
to denote the measure on
K_{v}^{n+1}
defined by
\beta_{v}:=\mu_{v}^{n+1}
. Let
\beta
be the product measure
\prod_{v\in M_{K}}\beta_{v}
on
E_{\mathbb{A}}
. The volume of a region in
E_{\mathbb{A}}
(resp.
K_{v}^{n+1}
) is defined to be the measure with respect to
\beta
(resp.
\beta_{v}
). We set
\Delta_{v}
to denote the determinant of the linear transformation
L_{v}
.

Lemma 4.11
.

With respect to notation above, the following assertions hold:

(1)

For
v\notin S
, we have that
\operatorname{Vol}\left(\Pi_{v}(Q)\right)=|D_{K_{v}/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}|^{\frac{n+1}{2}}
.

(2)

For
v\in S
a non-archimedean prime,

\operatorname{Vol}\left(\Pi_{v}(Q)\right)=|D_{K_{v}/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}|^{\frac{n+1}{2}}|\Delta_{v}|_{v}^{-r}Q^{r\left(\sum_{i}c_{v,i}\right)}.

(3)

If
v
is real, then

\operatorname{Vol}\left(\Pi_{v}(Q)\right)=2^{n+1}|\Delta_{v}|_{v}^{-r}Q^{r\left(\sum_{i}c_{v,i}\right)}.

(4)

If
v
is complex, then

\operatorname{Vol}\left(\Pi_{v}(Q)\right)=(2\pi)^{n+1}|\Delta_{v}|_{v}^{-r}Q^{r\left(\sum_{i}c_{v,i}\right)}.

Proof.

For part (1), we note that

\operatorname{Vol}(\Pi_{v}(Q))=\mu_{v}(R_{v})^{n+1}=|D_{K_{v}/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}|^{\frac{n+1}{2}}.

For part (2), let

B_{v}:=\{\mathbf{y}=(y_{0},\dots,y_{n})\in K_{v}^{n+1}\mid|y_{i}|_{v}\leq Q^{c_{v,i}}\}

and note that

\operatorname{Vol}(B_{v})=Q^{r\left(\sum_{i}c_{v,i}\right)}\mu_{v}(R_{v})^{n+1}=Q^{r\left(\sum_{i}c_{v,i}\right)}|D_{K_{v}/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}|^{\frac{n+1}{2}}.

Note that
\Pi_{v}(Q)=L_{v}^{-1}(B_{v})
and hence

\operatorname{Vol}(\Pi_{v}(Q))=|\Delta_{v}|_{v}^{-r}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{v})=|\Delta_{v}|_{v}^{-r}Q^{r\left(\sum_{i}c_{v,i}\right)}|D_{K_{v}/\mathbb{Q}_{p}}|^{\frac{n+1}{2}}.

Parts (3) and (4) are similar to part (2) and their proofs are omitted.
∎

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
Q\geq 1
and
\Pi(Q)
be the approximation domain associated with
(c_{v,i})
. Then, the volume of
\Pi(Q)
satisfies the asymptotic

\operatorname{Vol}(\Pi(Q))\ll Q^{-r\varepsilon/2}.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
4.11

\operatorname{Vol}(\Pi(Q))\ll Q^{r\left(\sum_{v,i}c_{v,i}\right)}.

Recall that (
4.5
) asserts that
\sum_{v,i}c_{v,i}\leq-\varepsilon/2
and the result follows.
∎

Since we assume that for a choice of
\varepsilon>0
, the affine subspace equation (
4.2
) is false, there exists an infinite set of solutions
\mathcal{X}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
such that no
(n+1)
solutions lie on the same hyperplane in
K^{n+1}
.
Setting
Q_{i}=H(\mathbf{x}_{i})
we note that
\iota(\mathbf{x}_{i})\in\Pi(Q_{i})
. Let
V(Q)
be the
K
-vector space spanned by the vectors in
\iota^{-1}(\Pi(Q))
. The rank of
\Pi(Q)
is defined to be
\operatorname{rank}_{K}V(Q)
. Note that by construction
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in V(Q_{i})
, where
Q_{i}:=H(\mathbf{x}_{i})
.

Lemma 4.13
.

For all but finitely many
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
, we have that
\operatorname{rank}\Pi(Q_{i})\leq n
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
,
Q:=H(\mathbf{x})
and set
\Lambda:=\iota(\mathcal{O}_{K,S}^{n+1})
. Let
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n+1}
be the successive minima of
\Pi(Q)
and let
x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(n+1)}\in\mathcal{O}_{K,S}^{n+1}
be linearly independent points defining the successive minima. This is to say that
\lambda_{i}\Pi(Q)\cap\Lambda
is generated by
x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(i)}
. We find that
V(Q)
is spanned by
x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(j)}
where
j=\operatorname{max}\{i\mid\lambda_{i}\leq 1\}
. Since
\lambda_{n+1}\geq\lambda_{i}
, one deduces that

\lambda_{n+1}^{r(n+1)}\geq(\lambda_{1}\dots\lambda_{n+1})^{r}.

On the other hand, by Minkowski’s second theorem, we find that

\frac{2^{r(n+1)}\pi^{r_{2}(n+1)}}{((n+1)!)^{r_{1}}(2(n+1)!)^{r_{2}}}|D_{K/\mathbb{Q}}|^{-\frac{(n+1)}{2}}\leq(\lambda_{1}\dots\lambda_{n+1})^{r}\operatorname{Vol}(\Pi(Q)).

Proposition
4.12
asserts that

\operatorname{Vol}(\Pi(Q))\ll Q^{-r\varepsilon/2}.

Thus we find that

\lambda_{n+1}\gg Q^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2(n+1)}}
and therefore when
Q
is large,
\lambda_{n+1}>1
and thus,
\operatorname{dim}V(Q)\leq n
for large enough values of
Q
. It then follows from Proposition
4.7
that the set of
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
for which
\operatorname{rank}\Pi(Q_{i})=n+1
is finite.
∎

In view of Lemma
4.13
we assume that for all
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
, the vector space
V(Q_{i})
has
K
-dimension
n
and moreover, that
V(Q_{i})\neq V(Q_{j})
for
i\neq j
. Using generalizations of Roth’s machinery, we prove the following result.

Theorem 4.14
.

For all but finitely many
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
, we have that
\operatorname{rank}\Pi(Q_{i})\leq n-1
.

4.4.
Auxiliary polynomials

Let
m\geq 1
be an integer, and let
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{m}\in\mathcal{X}
. Assume that for each
i=1,\dots,n
, the vector space
V(Q_{i})
has dimension
\dim_{K}V(Q_{i})=n
. For
i=1,\dots,n
, let
E_{i}:=K^{n+1}
represent the ambient space in which
V(Q_{i})
is contained. We aim to construct an auxiliary polynomial on the Cartesian product
(K^{n+1})^{m}=E_{1}\times\cdots\times E_{m}
, ensuring it has controlled height and prescribed vanishing properties.

We introduce a multi-vector variable
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{m})
, where each
\mathbf{X}_{h}=(X_{h,0},\dots,X_{h,n})
represents the
h
-th coordinate vector in
K^{n+1}
. This notation allows us to describe points in the product space
(K^{n+1})^{m}
succinctly. Given any vector
\mathbf{u}=(u_{0},\dots,u_{n})
with non-negative components, define its norm by

|\mathbf{u}|:=\sum_{i=0}^{n}u_{i}.

Now, fix a tuple
d=(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})
, where each
d_{h}
is a non-negative integer representing the degree of the desired polynomial in the variables corresponding to the
h
-th factor of the product
(K^{n+1})^{m}
. Define
\mathcal{A}(d)
as the set of integer multi-indices
I=(\mathbf{i}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{i}_{m})
, where each

\mathbf{i}_{h}=(i_{h,0},\dots,i_{h,n})\quad\text{with}\quad i_{h,j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

is a vector of non-negative integers satisfying the degree condition

|\mathbf{i}_{h}|=d_{h}\quad\text{for all }h=1,\dots,m.

In other words,
\mathcal{A}(d)
consists of all tuples of multi-indices whose total degrees in each set of variables match the specified degrees in
d
.

For each multi-index
I\in\mathcal{A}(d)
, define the corresponding monomial

\mathbf{X}^{I}:=\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\cdots\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\mathbf{i}_{m}},

where the notation
\mathbf{X}_{h}^{\mathbf{i}_{h}}
represents the monomial

\mathbf{X}_{h}^{\mathbf{i}_{h}}:=X_{h,0}^{i_{h,0}}\cdots X_{h,n}^{i_{h,n}}.

Thus,
\mathbf{X}^{I}
denotes a product of monomials, one for each coordinate vector
\mathbf{X}_{h}
, with the degrees specified by the corresponding multi-index
\mathbf{i}_{h}
.

Finally, let
P(d)
denote the
K
-vector space of multi-homogeneous polynomials of degree
d
in the variables
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{m})
. A polynomial
P\in P(d)
is a
K
-linear combination of the monomials
\mathbf{X}^{I}
with multi-indices
I\in\mathcal{A}(d)
, meaning

P(\mathbf{X})=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{A}(d)}c_{I}\mathbf{X}^{I},\quad c_{I}\in K.

To facilitate manipulation of these polynomials, we define partial derivatives with respect to each multi-index
I\in\mathcal{A}(d)
. Specifically, set

\partial_{I}:=\frac{1}{\mathbf{i}_{1}!\cdots\mathbf{i}_{m}!}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{X}_{1}}\right)^{\mathbf{i}_{1}}\cdots\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{X}_{m}}\right)^{\mathbf{i}_{m}},

where
\mathbf{i}_{h}!:=i_{h,0}!\cdots i_{h,n}!
for each
h
. For a polynomial
f\in P(d)
, we observe the expansion

f(\mathbf{X})=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{A}(d)}(\partial_{I}f)(0)\mathbf{X}^{I},

expressing
f
in terms of its partial derivatives evaluated at
\mathbf{X}=0
.
We shall write

\begin{split}L_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{h}):=&\left(L_{v,0}(\mathbf{X}_{h}),\dots,L_{v,i}(\mathbf{X}_{h}),\dots,L_{v,n}(\mathbf{X}_{h})\right)\\
=&\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{0,j}^{(v)}X_{h,j},\dots,\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{i,j}^{(v)}X_{h,j},\dots,\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{n,j}^{(v)}X_{h,j}\right)\end{split}

for linearly independent linear forms
L_{v,i}
over
K
.
We write

\partial_{I}P(\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{h},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{m})=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{A}(d-I)}a(L_{v};J;I)L_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{1})^{\mathbf{j}_{1}}\dots L_{v}(\mathbf{X}_{m})^{\mathbf{j}_{m}}.

For
i=0,\dots,n
we write
J_{i}=(j_{1,i},\dots,j_{m,i})
. Given a vector
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})
set

\left(\frac{v}{d}\right):=\frac{|v_{1}|}{d_{1}}+\dots+\frac{|v_{m}|}{d_{m}}.

Here, the
v_{i}
could also consist of vectors.

Lemma 4.15
.

Given the notation above, let

0<\eta\leq\frac{2}{n+1}

and define
r:=[K:\mathbb{Q}]
. Assume that

m\geq\frac{4}{(n+1)(n+2)\eta^{2}}\log\big(2(n+1)r|S|\big).

Then, for all sufficiently large
d_{1},\dots,d_{m}
, there exists a nonzero polynomial
P\in P(d)
satisfying the following height bounds:

h(P)\leq C_{3}|d|\quad\text{and}\quad h\big(a\big(L_{v};J,I\big)\big)\leq C_{3}|d|,

where
C_{3}
is a positive constant that depends only on
K
and the linear forms
L_{v,i}
.

Furthermore,
a\big(L_{v};J;I\big)=0
for
v\in S
whenever
J
and
I
satisfy the conditions:

\left(\frac{J_{i}}{d}\right)\leq\frac{m}{n+1}-2m\eta\quad\text{or}\quad\left(\frac{J_{i}}{d}\right)\geq\frac{m}{n+1}+2mn\eta\quad\text{for some }i=0,\ldots,n,

and
\left(\frac{I}{d}\right)\leq m\eta
.

Proof.

The proof can be viewed as significant generalization to Lemma
3.6
. The dimension of the vector space
P(d)
is the cardinality of
\mathcal{A}(d)
, i.e., the number of
m
-tuples
J=\left(\mathbf{j}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{j}_{m}\right)
for which
|\mathbf{j}_{h}|=d_{h}
for
h=1,\dots,m
. Let
V_{0}^{\prime}
be the region defined by

V_{0}^{\prime}=\{(x_{h,i})\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)m}\mid 1\leq h\leq m,0\leq i\leq n,x_{h,i}\geq 0,\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_{h,i}=d_{h}\}.

Notice that
\mathcal{A}(d)
consists of the lattice points in the region
V_{0}^{\prime}
. As
d_{1},\dots,d_{m}\rightarrow\infty
, we find that
\#\mathcal{A}(d)\sim\operatorname{Vol}(V_{0}^{\prime})
. By change of variables,
\operatorname{Vol}(V_{0}^{\prime})=(d_{1}\dots d_{m})^{n}\operatorname{Vol}(V_{0})
where

V_{0}:=\{(x_{h,i})\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)m}\mid 1\leq h\leq m,0\leq i\leq n,x_{h,i}\geq 0,\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_{h,i}=1\}.

We write
V_{0}=\prod_{h=1}^{m}V_{0}^{(h)}
where

V_{0}^{(h)}=\{(x_{h,i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\mid x_{h,i}\geq 0\text{ and }\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_{h,i}=1\}.

An elementary exercise in multivariable calculus gives us that
\operatorname{Vol}(V_{0}^{(h)})=\frac{1}{n!}
. Putting these steps together, we find that

\operatorname{dim}P(d)\sim(d_{1}\dots d_{m})^{n}\left(\frac{1}{n!}\right)^{m}.

Let us now fix
v\in S
and compute the asymptotic upper bound for the number of linear conditions with coefficients in
K
for

\left(\frac{J_{i}}{d}\right)\leq\frac{m}{n+1}-m\eta

for some fixed
i
. We work out the computation for
i=0
and also set
I=0
. The number of such
m
-tuples is asymptotic to
(d_{1}\dots d_{m})^{n}\operatorname{Vol}(V)
where
V
is defined as follows:

V:=\left\{(x_{h,i})\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)m}\mid\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_{h,i}=1,\sum_{h=1}^{m}x_{h,0}\leq\left(\frac{m}{n+1}\right)-m\eta\right\}

\chi_{[a,b]}(x):=\begin{cases}1&\text{ of }x\in[a,b];\\
0&\text{ of }x\notin[a,b].\end{cases}

The volume of
V
is expressed as the following integral:

\operatorname{Vol}(V)=\int_{0}^{1}\cdots\int_{0}^{1}\chi_{\left[0,\frac{m}{n+1}-m\eta\right]}\left(\sum_{h=1}^{m}x_{h0}\right)\left\{\prod_{h=1}^{m}\chi_{\left[0,1-x_{h0}\right]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{hi}\right)\right\}\prod_{h=1}^{m}\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}dx_{hi}.

In the integral above, we think of
x_{h,i}
as corresponding to
j_{h,i}/d_{i}
. The variable
x_{h,n}
is given by
1-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}x_{h,i}
and is thus determined by
x_{h,i}
for
i\leq n-1
. Noting that

\int_{0}^{1}\dots\int_{0}^{1}\chi_{[0,1-y_{0}]}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}y_{i}\right)dy_{1}\dots dy_{n}=\frac{(1-y_{0})^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}.

Therefore, we have

\operatorname{Vol}(V)=\int_{0}^{1}\cdots\int_{0}^{1}\chi_{\left[0,\frac{m}{n+1}-m\eta\right]}\left(\sum_{h=1}^{m}x_{h0}\right)\prod_{h=1}^{m}\frac{\left(1-x_{h0}\right)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}dx_{10}\cdots dx_{m0}.

In order to obtain a good upper bound for
\operatorname{Vol}(V)
we shall make use of the fact that

\chi_{[a,b]}(x)\leq e^{\lambda\cdot(b-x)},

which is valid for
\lambda\geq 0
. This decouples the variables and we obtain:

\operatorname{Vol}(V)\leq\left(e^{\lambda/(n+1)-\lambda\eta}\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\lambda x}\frac{(1-x)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}dx\right)^{m}

for any
\lambda\geq 0
. Expand
e^{-\lambda x}
into a Maclaurin series at
x=1
to obtain:

\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\lambda x}\frac{(1-x)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}dx=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}}{(n+k)!}.

Now assume that
\lambda\in(0,n+4]
to arrive at the following inequalities:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}}{(n+k)!}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n!}\left\{1-\frac{\lambda}{n+1}+\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}\lambda^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{n!}\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{n+1}+\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}\lambda^{2}\right).

We choose
\lambda:=\frac{\eta(n+1)(n+2)}{2}
with
\eta\leq\frac{2}{(n+1)}
and get:

\operatorname{Vol}(V)<\frac{1}{(n!)^{m}}\operatorname{exp}\left(\frac{-(n+1)(n+2)\eta^{2}m}{4}\right).

The number of
(i,v)
is
|S|(n+1)
. Let
M
denote the number of equations and
N
be the number of variables. We shall insist on the inequality
\frac{rM}{N}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore we have that as
d_{1},\dots,d_{m}\rightarrow\infty
, the following asymptotic:

\frac{rM}{N}=\frac{r(d_{1}\dots d_{m})^{n}\operatorname{Vol}(V)(n+1)|S|}{(d_{1}\dots d_{m})^{n}\operatorname{Vol}(V_{0})}\leq\frac{1}{2},

which is to say that

\operatorname{exp}\left((n+1)(n+2)\frac{\eta^{2}m}{4}\right)\geq 2(n+1)|S|r.

Thus we require that

m\geq\frac{4}{(n+1)(n+2)\eta^{2}}\log\left(2(n+1)|S|r\right).

By an application of Siegel’s lemma, the auxiliary polynomial satisfies

h(P)\leq C_{3}|d|.

This part of the proof is similar to the construction in Roth’s theorem.
Note that the vanishing of
a(L_{v};J;I)
whenever
\left(\frac{J_{i}}{d}\right)\leq\frac{m}{(n+1)}-m\eta
for some
i=0,\dots,n
implies that
a(L_{v};J;I)=0
whenever
\left(\frac{J_{i}}{d}\right)\leq\frac{m}{(n+1)}-2m\eta
for some
i=0,\dots,n
and every
I
such that
\left(\frac{I}{d}\right)\leq m\eta
. For
\left(\frac{I}{d}\right)\leq m\eta
the vanishing of
a(L_{v};J;I)
for
\left(\frac{J_{i}}{d}\right)\geq\frac{m}{(n+1)}+2nm\eta
for all
i
is automatic. This is because
a(L_{v};J;I)\neq 0
only if
\left(\frac{J_{i}}{d}\right)>\frac{m}{(n+1)}-2m\eta
for every
i
. We find that in this case,

\begin{split}\left(\frac{J_{i}}{d}\right)=&\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left(\frac{J_{j}}{d}\right)-\sum_{j\neq i}\left(\frac{J_{j}}{d}\right)\\
<&\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left(\frac{J_{j}}{d}\right)-\left(\frac{m}{(n+1)}-2m\eta\right)n\\
\leq&m-\frac{mn}{(n+1)}+2mn\eta=\frac{m}{(n+1)}+2mn\eta\end{split}

for every
i
. This completes the proof of the result.
∎

4.5.
A generalized Roth lemma

Let
V
be vector space over
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
of dimension
(n+1)
and let
e_{0},\dots,e_{n}
be a prescribed basis for
V
. Given an integer
m
in the range
1\leq m\leq(n+1)
, the
m
-fold exterior product
\wedge^{m}V
is a vector space of
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
with basis vectors
e_{i_{1}}\wedge e_{i_{2}}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{i_{m}}
, where
0\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{m}\leq n
. Thus
\wedge^{m}V
is a vector space over
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
of dimension
\binom{n+1}{m}
. Let
W
be an
m
-dimensional
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
-subspace of
V
. Then,
\wedge^{m}W
is a
1
-dimensional subspace of
\wedge^{m}V
. Therefore we may view
W
as a point
P_{W}
in the projective space
\mathbb{P}(\wedge^{m}V)=\mathbb{P}^{\binom{n+1}{m}-1}
. We define the height of
W
as follows
h(W):=h(P_{W})
.

Definition 4.16
.

Let
m
and
n
be positive integers with
m\leq n
. Let
A
be an
n\times m
matrix with entries in
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then the height
h(A)
is defined to be the height of the column span of
A
. We also set
h(A^{t}):=h(A)
.

Let
A
be an
n\times m
matrix with entries in
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
and let
I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\}
be a subset of
\{0,\dots,n\}
with
i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{m}
. Let
e_{I}
denote the basis vector
e_{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{i_{m}}
and let
A_{I}
be the
m\times m
submatrix of
A
formed by the
i
-th rows for
i\in I
. Consider the line passing through the origin in
\wedge^{m}V
and the point

S_{A}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\}\\
0\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{m}\leq n\end{subarray}}\operatorname{det}(A_{I})e_{I}.

This defines a point in
\mathbb{P}(\wedge^{m}V)
corresponding to
A
.

Proposition 4.17
.

Given an
m
-dimensional subspace
W
of
V
set

W^{\perp}:=\{w\in V^{*}\mid w(v)=0\text{ for all }v\in W\}.

Then one has
h(W)=h(W^{\perp})
.

Proof.

Given
x\in\wedge^{m}V
, we have a linear map
y\mapsto x\wedge y
in
\operatorname{Hom}(\wedge^{n+1-m}V,\wedge^{n+1}V)\simeq\wedge^{n+1-m}V^{*}
. In fact, this gives an isomorphism

\wedge^{m}V\xrightarrow{\sim}\wedge^{n+1-m}V^{*}

and its inverse can be defined in a similar way. It is easy to see that the line
\wedge^{m}W
is mapped to the line
\wedge^{n+1-m}W^{\perp}
under this transformation, and it follows that
h(W)=h(W^{\perp})
.
∎

Corollary 4.18
.

Given an
m\times n
matrix
A
with coordinates in
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
and
m\leq n
, the height of the space of solutions to
Ax=0
equals
h(A)
.

Proof.

The rows of
A
form a basis of
W:=\{x:Ax=0\}^{\perp}
and thus by definition,
h(A)=h(W)
. On the other hand,
h(W)=h(W^{\perp})
by Proposition
4.17
. Thus we deduce that
h(A)=h(\{x:Ax=0\})
, which completes the proof.
∎

Let
M_{1},\dots,M_{m}\in\bar{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{0},\dots,x_{m}]
be non-zero linear forms and let
M=(M_{1},\dots,M_{m})
and
d=(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})
. We shall also recall that
\mathbf{X}_{h}
denotes the variable multivector
(X_{h,0},\dots,X_{h,n})
for
h=1,\dots,m
.

Definition 4.19
.

Denote by
\mathcal{I}(t;d;M)
the ideal in
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{m}]
generated by the monomials
M_{1}(\mathbf{X}_{1})^{j_{1}}\dots M_{m}(\mathbf{X}_{m})^{j_{m}}
with
\left(\frac{J}{d}\right)=\frac{j_{1}}{d_{1}}+\dots+\frac{j_{m}}{d_{m}}\geq t
. Then for a multihomogeneous polynomial
P
of degree
d
, set

\operatorname{Ind}(P;d;M):=\operatorname{sup}\{t\geq 0\mid P\in\mathcal{I}(t;d;M)\}.

Remark 4.20
.

We remark that when
n=1
,
M_{j}:=x_{1}-\alpha_{j}x_{0}
and
Q
is the dehomogenized polynomial defined by:

Q(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}):=P(1,x_{1},\dots,1,x_{m}),

one has that
\operatorname{Ind}(P;d;M)=\operatorname{Ind}(Q;d;\alpha)
. We are now in a position to state the generalized Roth Lemma.

Lemma 4.21
.

Let
P
be a multihomogeneous polynomial of degree
d
and
\sigma\in(0,1/2]
. Moreover suppose that:

(1)

d_{j+1}/d_{j}\leq\sigma
for
j=1,\dots,m-1
;

(2)

\operatorname{min}_{j}d_{j}h(M_{j})\geq n\sigma^{-1}(h(P)+4md_{1})
.

Then,
\operatorname{Ind}(P;d;M)\leq 2m\sigma^{\frac{1}{2^{m-1}}}
.

Proof.

The result is obtained from the classical version of Roth’s lemma upon specializing all variables except
(X_{h,0},X_{h,1})
to
0
. By Remark
4.20
, when
n=1
, the statement is equivalent to Roth’s lemma. Thus assume without loss of generality that
n\geq 2
. Let
b=(b_{0},\dots,b_{n})\in K^{n+1}\backslash\{0\}
and assume for simplicity that
b_{0}\neq 0
. Then we have that

\begin{split}h(b)=&\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\log\operatorname{max}_{i}\{|b_{i}|_{v}\}\\
\leq&\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\operatorname{max}\{|b_{0}|_{v},|b_{i}|_{v}\}\\
\leq&n\operatorname{max}_{i}h((b_{0},b_{i})).\end{split}

We relabel the variables and write

M_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{j})=b_{j,0}X_{j,0}+\dots+b_{j,n}X_{j,n}

with
b_{j,i}\in K
and
b_{j,0}\neq 0
for all
j
. Now specialize
X_{j,i}=0
for
i=2,\dots,n
to get
\widetilde{M}_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{j})=b_{j,0}X_{j,0}+b_{j,1}X_{j,1}
. One has that

\begin{split}h(\widetilde{M}_{j})=&\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\operatorname{max}\{\log|b_{j,0}|_{v},\log|b_{j,1}|_{v}\}\\
\geq&\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\operatorname{max}_{i}\{\log|b_{j,i}|_{v}\}\geq\frac{1}{n}h(M_{j}).\end{split}

This implies that
h(\widetilde{M}_{j})\geq\frac{1}{n}h(M_{j})
. Since
b_{j,0}\neq 0
for all
j
, we have that
M_{j},X_{j,1},X_{j,2},\dots,X_{j,n}
are
K
-linearly independent linear forms. This implies that

K[M_{1},\dots,M_{m},\{X_{j,i}\mid i\geq 1,j=1,\dots,m\}]=K[\{X_{j,i}\mid i\geq 0,j=1,\dots,m\}].

Thus
P
can be written uniquely as

P=\sum_{J}c(J)M_{1}(\mathbf{X}_{1})^{j_{1}}M_{2}(\mathbf{X}_{2})^{j_{2}}\dots M_{m}(\mathbf{X}_{m})^{j_{m}}q_{J}(\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{m}^{\prime})

where the coefficients
q_{J}
are polynomials in
\mathbf{X}_{j}^{\prime}=(X_{j,1},\dots,X_{j,n})
. Let us pick
i
and
j
with
j\geq 2
, remove the highest power of
X_{i,j}
dividing
P
and specialize
X_{i,j}=0
to obtain a new polynomial
P^{*}
. Since the coefficients of
P^{*}
forms a subset of
P
,

\begin{split}&h(P^{*})\leq h(P),\\
&\operatorname{Ind}(P^{*};M^{*};d)=\operatorname{Ind}(P;M;d),\end{split}

where
M^{*}:=M_{|X_{i,j}=0}
. In this way we can inductively specialize all variables
X_{i,j}=0
for
j\geq 2
and obtain a new polynomial
\widetilde{P}(\widetilde{X}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{X}_{m})
in the variables
\widetilde{X}_{j}=(X_{j,0},X_{j,1})
such that

\begin{split}&h(\widetilde{P})\leq h(P);\\
&\operatorname{Ind}(\widetilde{P};\widetilde{M};d)=\operatorname{Ind}(P;M;d);\\
&h(\widetilde{M}_{j})\geq h(M_{j})/n.\end{split}

Apply Roth’s lemma to
\widetilde{P}
and find that

d_{j}h(\widetilde{M}_{j})\geq n^{-1}d_{j}h(M_{j})\geq\sigma^{-1}(h(P)+4md_{1}).

This implies that

\operatorname{Ind}(P;M;d)=\operatorname{Ind}(\widetilde{P};\widetilde{M};d)\leq 2m\sigma^{1/2^{m-1}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.6.
A lower bound on the height of
V(Q)

Given
Q\geq 1
, we would like a better control on the height
of
V(Q)
.
Before we commence, we recall some basic notation about exterior algebras. Let
V
be a vector space over
K
with basis
e_{0},\dots,e_{n}
then
\wedge^{k}V
has basis
e_{i_{1}}\wedge e_{i_{2}}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{i_{k}}
where
0\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}\leq n
.
Define the inner product on
\wedge^{k}V
by:

(4.6)

(u_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge u_{k})\cdot(v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{k}):=\operatorname{det}\left((u_{i}\cdot v_{j})\right).

This gives the standard inner product with respect to the basis

\{e_{i_{1}}\wedge e_{i_{2}}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{i_{k}}\mid 0\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}\leq n\}.

We also define a
K
-linear
\ast
-operator on the exterior algebra
\wedge V:=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n+1}\wedge^{k}V
by setting

(e_{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{k})^{\ast}:=(-1)^{\operatorname{sign}(\pi)}e_{j_{0}}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{j_{n-k}}

where
\pi=(j_{0},\dots,j_{n-k},i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
is a permutation of
\{0,\dots,n\}
. It is easy to check that:

•

\ast
is an isometry,

•

one has the following expansion

(4.7)

x_{0}\cdot(x_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge x_{n})^{\ast}=\operatorname{det}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})

for vectors
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\in V
.

Lemma 4.22
.

Assume that
\log Q\geq C_{4}\varepsilon^{-1}
for a suitably large constant
C_{4}>0
and that
V(Q)
has dimension
n
. There exists a hyperplane
W\subset V
, independent of
\Pi(Q)
and
\varepsilon>0
such that either

•

V(Q)=W
, or,

•

\left((4|S|)^{-1}\varepsilon\log(Q)-C_{4}\right)\leq h(V(Q))
.

Proof.

Recall that
\iota:K^{n+1}\hookrightarrow K_{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1}
is the natural inclusion map. We let
y^{(1)},\dots,y^{(n)}\in K^{n+1}
be a basis of
V(Q)
such that
\iota(y^{(i)})\in\Pi(Q)
for all
i
. Observe that
V(Q)
is the kernel of the map

x\mapsto(y^{(1)}\wedge\dots\wedge y^{(n)})\wedge x.

Thus, it follows that the height of
V(Q)
is given by
h(y^{(1)}\wedge\dots\wedge y^{(n)})
.
Note that in particular,
y^{(i)}
has coordinates in
\mathcal{O}_{K,S}
. For ease of notation, we set
V:=K^{n+1}
and
V^{\vee}
shall denote its dual. We consider the linear forms

\widehat{L}_{v,k}:=\left(L_{v,0}\wedge\dots\wedge L_{v,k-1}\wedge L_{v,k+1}\wedge\dots\wedge L_{v,n}\right)^{\ast}\in V^{\vee}

and set

D_{v,k}:=\widehat{L}_{v,k}\left((y^{(1)}\wedge\dots\wedge y^{(n)})^{\ast}\right).

Since
\ast
is an isometry, we find that

\begin{split}D_{v,k}=&\left(L_{v,0}\wedge\dots\wedge L_{v,k-1}\wedge L_{v,k+1}\wedge\dots\wedge L_{v,n}\right)(y^{(1)}\wedge\dots\wedge y^{(n)})\\
=&\operatorname{det}\left(L_{v,i}(y^{(j)})\right)_{(i,j);i\neq k}.\end{split}

Note that since
\iota(y^{(j)})\in\Pi(Q)
, we have that
|L_{v,i}(y^{(j)})|_{v}\leq Q^{c_{v,i}}
. Expanding the determinant, we find that:

\begin{split}|D_{v,k}|_{v}\leq&\operatorname{max}\left(1,|n!|_{v}\right)\times\operatorname{max}_{\pi}\prod_{i\neq k}|L_{v,i}(y^{\pi(i)})|_{v}\\
\leq&\operatorname{max}\left(1,|n!|_{v}\right)Q^{-c_{v,k}+\sum_{i}c_{v,i}}\end{split}

where
\pi
ranges over all bijections
\pi:\{0,\dots,n\}\backslash\{k\}\rightarrow\{1,\dots,n\}
.

The argument involves two steps. First, we assume that there exists a tuple
\{i_{v}\}_{v\in S}
such that

\sum_{v\in S}c_{v,i_{v}}\geq-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}

and
D_{v,i_{v}}\neq 0
for all
v\in S
. Thus we have that

\prod_{v\in S}|D_{v,i_{v}}|_{v}\leq n!Q^{-\sum_{v}c_{v,i_{v}}+\sum_{v,i}c_{v,i}}\leq n!Q^{\varepsilon/4-\varepsilon/2}=n!Q^{-\varepsilon/4}.

We have that

\begin{split}\sum_{v\in S}\log|D_{v,i_{v}}|_{v}\geq&-\sum_{v\in S}h(D_{v,i_{v}})\\
=&-\sum_{v\in S}h\left(\widehat{L}_{v,k}\left((y^{(1)}\wedge\dots\wedge y^{(n)})^{\ast}\right)\right)\\
\geq&-|S|h(V(Q))-C_{4}^{\prime},\end{split}

for some constant
C_{4}^{\prime}>0
which arises from the coefficients of the forms
\widehat{L}_{v,k}
. Putting these inequalities together, one finds that

-|S|h(V(Q))-C_{4}^{\prime}\leq-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\log(Q)+\log(n!).

Setting
C_{4}^{\prime\prime}:=|S|^{-1}\left(C_{4}^{\prime}+\log(n!)\right)
, we find that
\left((4|S|)^{-1}\varepsilon\log(Q)-C_{4}^{\prime\prime}\right)\leq h(V(Q))
.

Next, we show that there is a hyperplane
W
such that
V(Q)=W
if there does not exist an
S
-tuple
\{i_{v}\}_{v\in S}
such that

(4.8)

\sum_{v\in S}c_{v,i_{v}}\geq-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}

and
D_{v,i_{v}}\neq 0
for all
v\in S
. Setting
\mathcal{I}_{v}:=\{i\in\{0,\dots,n\}\mid D_{v,i}\neq 0\}
the system of equations

\widehat{L}_{v,i}(z)=0\text{ for }i\in\{0,\dots,n\}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{v}

has a nontrivial solution
w=(y^{(1)}\wedge\dots\wedge y^{(n)})^{\ast}
. Setting

, we show that if

is sufficiently large then

is contained in

. Since
V(Q)
has dimension
n
and

is a hyperplane, it follows then that
V(Q)=W
. As a consequence of (
4.7
), we have the following algebraic identity:

x\cdot w=\operatorname{det}(L_{v})^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}L_{v,i}(x)\widehat{L}_{v,i}(w)

for any

and
w
. Note that
\widehat{L}_{v,i}(w)=0
for

and hence,

Now for each
v\in S
, choose

such that

Note that if

|L_{v,i}(x)|_{v}\leq Q^{c_{v,i}}\leq Q^{c_{v,j_{v}}}
. Thus we get the bound:

On the other hand, since we assume the non-existence of an

-tuple satisfying (

), we have that

\sum_{v\in S}c_{v,j_{v}}<-\varepsilon/4.

By the product formula,

\begin{split}1=&\prod_{v\in M_{K}}|x\cdot w|_{v}=\prod_{v\in S}|x\cdot w|_{v}\times\prod_{v\notin S}|x\cdot w|_{v},\\
\leq&\prod_{v\in S}|x\cdot w|_{v}\times\prod_{v\notin S}|w|_{v}\ll Q^{\sum_{v\in S}c_{v,j_{v}}}\ll Q^{-\varepsilon/4}.\end{split}

Therefore, we find that
\log Q\ll\varepsilon^{-1}
. We choose

large so that

and this gives a contradiction. Thus, we find that

∎

), we obtain

\frac{1}{mD}\sum_{v\in S}\log\left|L_{v}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{1})^{\mathbf{j}_{1}}\cdots L_{v}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{m})^{\mathbf{j}_{m}}\right|_{v}\leq-\frac{\epsilon}{n+1}+O(\eta).

Choosing
\eta
sufficiently small in terms of
\epsilon
, we get

(5.5)

\frac{1}{mD}\sum_{v\in S}\log\max_{a\left(L_{v};J\right)\neq 0}\left|L_{v}\left(\mathbb{x}_{1}^{\prime}\right)^{\mathbf{j}_{1}}\cdots L_{v}\left(\mathbb{x}_{m}^{\prime}\right)^{\mathbf{j}_{m}}\right|_{v}\leq-\frac{\varepsilon}{4(n+1)}.

5.1.2.
Lower bound of A

For a non-archimedean valuation
v
, we have

\sum_{v\in S}\log\left|T\left(x^{\prime}\right)\right|_{v}\leq\sum_{v\in S}\log\max_{J}\left|a\left(L_{v};J\right)\right|_{v}+\sum_{v\in S}\log\max_{a\left(L_{v};J\right)\neq 0}\left|L_{v}\left(\mathbb{x}_{1}^{\prime}\right)^{\mathbf{j}_{1}}\cdots L_{v}\left(\mathbb{x}_{m}^{\prime}\right)^{\mathbf{j}_{m}}\right|_{v}

Therefore, we write

\sum_{v\in S}\log\max_{a\left(L_{v};J\right)\neq 0}\left|L_{v}\left(\mathbb{x}_{1}^{\prime}\right)^{\mathbf{j}_{1}}\cdots L_{v}\left(\mathbb{x}_{m}^{\prime}\right)^{\mathbf{j}_{m}}\right|_{v}\geqslant\sum_{v\in S}\log\left|T\left(x^{\prime}\right)\right|_{v}-\sum_{v\in S}\log\max_{J}\left|a\left(L_{v};J\right)\right|_{v}.

Since
T\left(x^{\prime}\right)\neq 0
and

\prod_{v\in M_{K}}\left|T\left(x^{\prime}\right)\right|_{v}=1
, we have

\sum_{v\in S}\log\left|T\left(x^{\prime}\right)\right|_{v}=-\sum_{v\not\in S}\log\left|T\left(x^{\prime}\right)\right|_{v}.

Write

T(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})=\sum_{I}b_{I}X^{I},

where
I
ranges over the multi-indices occurring in
T
. We set

|T|_{v}:=\max_{I}|b_{I}|_{v}.

Since
T
has multidegree at most
d=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{m})
and nonarchimedian
v
, we have

|T(x^{\prime})|_{v}\leq|T|_{v}\,\max\{1,|x^{\prime}|_{v}\}^{|d|},

where

|d|:=d_{1}+\cdots+d_{m}.

In particular,

\log|T(x^{\prime})|_{v}\leq\log|T|_{v}+|d|\log^{+}|x^{\prime}|_{v}.

This yields

\displaystyle\sum_{v\notin S}\log|T(x^{\prime})|_{v}\leq\sum_{v\notin S}\log|T|_{v}+|d|\sum_{v\notin S}\log^{+}|x^{\prime}|_{v}

where

|x^{\prime}|_{v}=\max_{h}\{|x^{\prime}_{h}|_{v}\}=\max_{h}\left\{\left|\sum_{l}z_{hl}y_{h}^{(l)}\right|_{v}\right\}.

Note that
i(y_{h}^{(l)})\in\prod\left(Q_{h}\right)
. So, in particular

|y_{h}^{(l)}|_{v}\leqslant 1
for all
v\notin S.

This implies
\left|x^{\prime}\right|_{v}\leqslant 1
for all
v\notin S
and so
\sum_{v\notin S}\log|x^{\prime}|_{v}\leqslant 0.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{v\notin S}\log\left|T\left(x^{\prime}\right)\right|_{v}\leq\sum_{v\notin S}\log|T|_{v}.

This can be rewritten as

(5.6)

\sum_{v\in S}\log\left|T\left(x^{\prime}\right)\right|_{v}\geqslant-\sum_{v\notin S}\log|T|_{v}.

On the other hand, we have

(5.7)

\sum_{v\in S}\log\max_{J}\left|a\left(L_{v},J\right)\right|_{v}=\sum_{v\in S}\log|T|_{v}+O\left(d_{1}\right).

Putting (
5.6
) and (
5.7
) together, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{mD}\sum_{v\in S}\log\max_{a\left(L_{v};J\right)\neq 0}\left|L_{v}\left(\mathbb{x}_{1}^{\prime}\right)^{\mathbf{j}_{1}}\cdots L_{v}\left(\mathbb{x}_{m}^{\prime}\right)^{\mathbf{j}_{m}}\right|_{v}

\displaystyle\geqslant-\frac{1}{mD}\sum_{v\notin S}\log|T|_{v}-\frac{1}{mD}\sum_{v\in S}\log|T|_{v}+O\left(\frac{d_{1}}{mD}\right).

Choose
d_{1}=\left\lfloor\frac{D}{\log Q_{1}}\right\rfloor\sim\frac{D}{\log Q_{1}},

This implies

\frac{d_{1}}{mD}\sim\frac{1}{m\log Q_{1}}.

It is small when
Q_{1}\gg 1
.
Combining the two sums, we obtain

-\sum_{v\notin S}\log|T|_{v}-\sum_{v\in S}\log|T|_{v}=-\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\log|T|_{v}.

Since

\log|T|_{v}\leq\log^{+}|T|_{v}

for every
v\in M_{K}
, it follows that

-\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\log|T|_{v}\geq-\sum_{v\in M_{K}}\log^{+}|T|_{v}=-h(T).

Therefore

-\frac{1}{mD}\sum_{v\notin S}\log|T|_{v}-\frac{1}{mD}\sum_{v\in S}\log|T|_{v}\geq-\frac{h(T)}{mD}.

Recall that
h(T)\ll d_{1}
, so

-\frac{h(T)}{mD}=O\left(\frac{d_{1}}{mD}\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{m\log Q_{1}}\right).

Hence we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{mD}\sum_{v\in S}\log\max_{a\left(L_{v};J\right)\neq 0}\left|L_{v}\left(\mathbb{x}_{1}^{\prime}\right)^{\mathbf{j}_{1}}\cdots L_{v}\left(\mathbb{x}_{m}^{\prime}\right)^{\mathbf{j}_{m}}\right|_{v}\gg\frac{-1}{m\log Q_{1}}

which is arbitrarily close to
0
for large
Q_{1}
. Also the upper bound of this term is
\frac{-\varepsilon}{4(n+1)}
by (
5.5
). Hence we get a contradiction.

5.2.
Evertse’s lemma

A significant simplification of Schmidt’s original proof was later achieved by Evertse, who introduced an elegant lemma that circumvented the need for Schmidt’s reliance on Mahler’s results on successive minima of compound convex bodies and Davenport’s lemma on stretched parallelopipeds.

Lemma 5.2

(Evertse’s lemma)
.

Let
K
be a number field, and let
S
be a finite set of places of
K
that includes all the archimedean places. Suppose
\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(n+1)}
form a basis for a
K
-vector space
V
. For each
v\in S
, let
L_{v0},\ldots,L_{vn}
be linearly independent linear forms on
V
with coefficients in
K_{v}
. Further, let
\mu_{v,j}
be real numbers such that

0<\mu_{v,1}\leq\mu_{v,2}\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{v,n+1},\quad\text{and}\quad\left|L_{vk}\left(\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\right)\right|_{v}\leq\mu_{v,j}

for all
k
and
j
. Then, there exist vectors

\mathbf{v}^{(1)}=\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\quad\mathbf{v}^{(i)}=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\xi_{ij}\mathbf{x}^{(j)}+\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\quad\text{with}\quad\xi_{ij}\in\mathcal{O}_{K,S}\quad(1\leq j<i\leq n+1),

along with bijective maps
\pi_{v}:\{1,\ldots,n+1\}\to\{0,\ldots,n\}
, and a positive constant
C_{6}
such that for any
v\in S
and
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
, the following inequality holds:

\left|L_{v,\pi_{v}(i)}\left(\mathbf{v}^{(j)}\right)\right|_{v}\leq\begin{cases}C_{6}\min\left(\mu_{v,i},\mu_{v,j}\right)&\text{if }v\text{ is archimedean},\\
\min\left(\mu_{v,i},\mu_{v,j}\right)&\text{if }v\text{ is non-archimedean}.\end{cases}

The constant
C_{6}
depends only on
K
,
S
and the set of linear forms
L_{vi}
.

Proof.

The proof proceeds by induction on
n
. The base case
n=0
is straightforward, as the result holds trivially when there is only one vector. Now assume
n\geq 1
and suppose that the lemma holds for
n-1
; we aim to establish it for
n
.

Fix a place
v\in S
, and consider the
K
-vector subspace
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
spanned by the first
n
basis vectors
\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(n)}
.
The linear system

\sum_{k=0}^{n}L_{vk}\left(\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\right)\alpha_{vk}=0\quad\text{for }j=1,\ldots,n

has a non-trivial solution
(\alpha_{v0},\ldots,\alpha_{vn})\in K_{v}^{n+1}
. Let
\pi_{v}(n+1)
denote an index where the maximum norm is attained, i.e.,

|\alpha_{v,\pi_{v}(n+1)}|_{v}=\max_{i}|\alpha_{vi}|_{v}.

Since the solution is non-trivial, we have
\alpha_{v,\pi_{v}(n+1)}\neq 0
. Define the ratios

\beta_{vi}=-\frac{\alpha_{vi}}{\alpha_{v,\pi_{v}(n+1)}}\quad\text{for }i\neq\pi_{v}(n+1).

Substituting these ratios into the system yields

(5.8)

L_{v,\pi_{v}(n+1)}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)})=\sum_{k\neq\pi_{v}(n+1)}\beta_{vk}L_{vk}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)})\quad\text{with }|\beta_{vk}|_{v}\leq 1.

The restrictions to
V^{\prime}
of the linear forms
L_{vk}
,

k\neq\pi_{v}(n+1)
, are linearly independent over
K_{v}
. Applying the inductive hypothesis to these restricted forms, there exist bijections

\pi_{v}:\{1,\ldots,n\}\to\{0,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{\pi_{v}(n+1)\},

and vectors

\mathbf{v}^{(1)}=\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\quad\mathbf{v}^{(i)}=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\xi_{ij}\mathbf{x}^{(j)}+\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\quad\text{for }i=2,\ldots,n,

with coefficients
\xi_{ij}\in\mathcal{O}_{K,S}
, such that

|L_{v,\pi_{v}(i)}(\mathbf{v}^{(j)})|_{v}\leq\begin{cases}C_{6}^{\prime}\min(\mu_{v,i},\mu_{v,j})&\text{if }v\text{ is archimedean},\\
\min(\mu_{v,i},\mu_{v,j})&\text{if }v\text{ is non-archimedean}.\end{cases}

Now, to extend this construction to include
\mathbf{v}^{(n+1)}
, we need coefficients
\xi_{n+1,j}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{K,S}
such that

\mathbf{v}^{(n+1)}=\mathbf{x}^{(n+1)}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{n+1,j}^{\prime}\mathbf{v}^{(j)}

satisfies the desired bounds. Specifically, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
v\in S
, we require

|L_{v,\pi_{v}(i)}(\mathbf{v}^{(n+1)})|_{v}\leq\begin{cases}C_{6}\mu_{v,i}&\text{if }v\text{ is archimedean},\\
\mu_{v,i}&\text{if }v\text{ is non-archimedean}.\end{cases}

For
i=n+1
, we use (
5.8
) to get the result.
To achieve this, note that
L_{v,\pi_{v}(i)}
for
i\neq n+1
remains linearly independent on
V^{\prime}
, which is generated by
\mathbf{v}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{v}^{(n)}
. Thus, we can solve the linear system

L_{v,\pi_{v}(i)}(\mathbf{x}^{(n+1)})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\gamma_{vj}L_{v,\pi_{v}(i)}(\mathbf{v}^{(j)})\quad\text{for }i\neq n+1,

with
\gamma_{vj}\in K_{v}
. Since
\mathcal{O}_{K,S}
forms a discrete lattice in
\prod_{v\in S}K_{v}
with a bounded fundamental domain, there exists a vector
(\xi_{n+1,1}^{\prime},\ldots,\xi_{n+1,n}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{O}_{K,S}^{n}
such that

|\xi_{n+1,j}^{\prime}+\gamma_{vj}|_{v}\leq A_{v},

where
A_{v}
is bounded in terms of
K
and
S
. For non-archimedean places
v
, we may choose
A_{v}=1
using a scaling argument with a suitable nonzero element
m\in\mathcal{O}_{K,S}
. This ensures the desired bound on
|L_{v,\pi_{v}(i)}(\mathbf{v}^{(n+1)})|_{v}
, completing the proof.
∎

5.3.
Proof of the subspace theorem

Let
\Pi(Q)
denote an approximation domain associated with the forms
L_{v,i}
and the parameters
c_{v,i}
. Assume that the rank
R:=\operatorname{rank}(\Pi(Q))
satisfies
1\leq R\leq n
. The value of
R
is determined by the inequalities
\lambda_{R}\leq 1<\lambda_{R+1}
. Moreover, as noted earlier in the proof of Lemma
4.13
, an application of Minkowski’s second theorem gives us that

\lambda_{n+1}\gg Q^{\varepsilon/(2n+2)}.

We now introduce
k
as the smallest integer within the range
[R,n]
for which the ratio
\lambda_{k}/\lambda_{k+1}
is minimized. To estimate this minimum ratio, observe that the product of all successive ratios from
j=R
to
j=n
can be expressed as

\prod_{j=R}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{j+1}}=\frac{\lambda_{R}}{\lambda_{n+1}}.

Taking the geometric mean of these ratios yields

\left(\prod_{j=R}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{j+1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n+1-R}}=\left(\frac{\lambda_{R}}{\lambda_{n+1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n+1-R}}.

Since
k
is chosen such that
\lambda_{k}/\lambda_{k+1}
is minimal, it follows that

\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k+1}}\leq\left(\frac{\lambda_{R}}{\lambda_{n+1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n+1-R}}.

By applying the lower bound
\lambda_{n+1}\gg Q^{\varepsilon/(2n+2)}
, we deduce that

\frac{\lambda_{R}}{\lambda_{n+1}}\ll Q^{-\varepsilon/(2n+2)},

which implies

(5.9)

\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k+1}}\ll Q^{-\varepsilon/\{2(n+1)n\}}.

Setting

\varepsilon_{v}=\begin{cases}{\left[K_{v}:\mathbb{R}\right]/[K:\mathbb{Q}]}&\text{ if }v\text{ is archimedean }\\
0&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

we then define
\mu_{v,j}=\lambda_{j}^{\varepsilon_{v}}
for
j=1,\ldots,n+1
. Since
\sum_{v\mid\infty}\varepsilon_{v}=1
, it is clear that

\prod_{v\mid\infty}\mu_{v,j}=\lambda_{j}
and
\mu_{v,j}=1
if
v\nmid\infty,v\in S
. Let
\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\in O_{K,S}^{n+1}
for
j=1,\ldots,n+1
be linearly independent points, chosen so that their images under the map
\iota\left(\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\right)
correspond to the successive minima of the domain
\Pi(Q)
. Then, for each
v\in S
,
i=0,\ldots,n
, and
j=1,\ldots,n+1
, the following inequality holds:

\left|L_{v,i}\left(\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\right)\right|_{v}\leq\mu_{v,j}Q^{c_{v,i}},

.

Applying Evertse’s lemma (Lemma
5.2
) in this context, we deduce the existence of vectors
\mathbf{v}^{(i)}\in O_{K,S}^{n+1}
for
i=1,\ldots,n+1
, defined recursively by

\mathbf{v}^{(1)}=\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\quad\mathbf{v}^{(i)}=\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\xi_{ij}\mathbf{x}^{(j)}+\mathbf{x}^{(i)},

where
\xi_{ij}\in K
are coefficients. For each
v\in S
, there exists a bijection
\pi_{v}:\{1,\ldots,n+1\}\to\{0,\ldots,n\}
that establishes a correspondence between the indices of the vectors
\mathbf{v}^{(j)}
and the linear forms
L_{v,\pi_{v}(i)}
at the place
v
. This bijection enables us to express bounds for the values of these forms evaluated on the vectors
\mathbf{v}^{(j)}
as follows:

\left|L_{v,\pi_{v}(i)}\left(\mathbf{v}^{(j)}\right)\right|_{v}\leq\begin{cases}C_{6}\min\left(\mu_{v,i},\mu_{v,j}\right)Q^{c_{v,\pi_{v}(i)}}&\text{if }v\mid\infty,\\
Q^{c_{v,\pi_{v}(i)}}&\text{if }v\nmid\infty,\end{cases}

for all
i,j=1,\ldots,n+1
.

We introduce the following notation to handle exterior products of the vectors and forms:

•

\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n+1-k})
with indices ordered such that
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{n+1-k}
,

•

L_{v,\pi_{v}(\mathbf{i})}=L_{v,\pi_{v}(i_{1})}\wedge L_{v,\pi_{v}(i_{2})}\wedge\cdots\wedge L_{v,\pi_{v}(i_{n+1-k})}
,

•

\mathbf{v}^{(\mathbf{i})}=\mathbf{v}^{(i_{1})}\wedge\mathbf{v}^{(i_{2})}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathbf{v}^{(i_{n+1-k})}
,

•

c_{v,\pi_{v}(\mathbf{i})}=c_{v,\pi_{v}(i_{1})}+c_{v,\pi_{v}(i_{2})}+\cdots+c_{v,\pi_{v}(i_{n+1-k})}
,

•

\lambda_{\mathbf{i}}=\prod_{u=1}^{n+1-k}\lambda_{i_{u}},\quad\mu_{v,\mathbf{i}}=\prod_{u=1}^{n+1-k}\mu_{v,i_{u}}.

The set of linear forms
L_{v,\pi_{v}(\mathbf{i})}
is linearly independent, as are the points
\mathbf{v}^{(\mathbf{i})}
. Using (
4.6
), it follows directly that for any
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}
and any
v\in S
, we have

(5.10)

\left|L_{v,\pi_{v}(\mathbf{i})}\left(\mathbf{v}^{(\mathbf{j})}\right)\right|_{v}\leq\begin{cases}C_{7}\mu_{v,\mathbf{i}}Q^{c_{v,\pi_{v}(\mathbf{i})}}&\text{if }v\mid\infty\\
Q^{c_{v,\pi_{v}(\mathbf{i})}}&\text{if }v\nmid\infty,\end{cases}

for some constant
C_{7}>0
. Additionally, when
\mathbf{i}=(k+1,k+2,\ldots,n+1)
but
\mathbf{j}\neq(k+1,k+2,\ldots,n+1)
, applying Evertse’s lemma allows for a sharper bound:

(5.11)

\left|L_{v,\pi_{v}(\mathbf{i})}\left(\mathbf{v}^{(\mathbf{j})}\right)\right|_{v}\leq C_{7}\cdot\left(\frac{\mu_{v,k}}{\mu_{v,k+1}}\right)\cdot\mu_{v,\mathbf{i}}Q^{c_{v,\pi_{v}(\mathbf{i})}}\quad\text{if }v\mid\infty

since in this case, the index
j_{1}

j_{1}\leq k
and so

\mu_{v,\mathbf{j}}\leq\mu_{v,k}\mu_{v,k+2}\cdots\mu_{v,n+1}=\left(\frac{\mu_{v,k}}{\mu_{v,k+1}}\right)\mu_{v,\mathbf{i}}.

We now proceed to establish the following result:

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}(Q)
denote a symmetric convex domain in
\wedge^{n+1-k}(K_{\mathbb{A}}^{n+1})

\displaystyle\left|L_{v,\pi_{v}(\mathbf{i})}(\mathbf{X})\right|_{v}

\displaystyle\leq C_{8}\mu_{v,\mathbf{i}}Q^{c_{v,\pi_{v}(\mathbf{i})}}\quad

\displaystyle\text{if }v\notin S

for some constant
C_{8}>\operatorname{max}\{C_{6},C_{7}\}
.
Denote by
\lambda_{j}(\mathcal{S}(Q))

\ll\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k+1}}\left(\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{n+1}({\rm vol}(\Pi(Q)))^{1/r}\right)^{\binom{n}{k}}\ll\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k+1}}\ll Q^{-\varepsilon/(2n(n+1))}.

The result then follows by using the left inequality of (
5.12
).
∎

\mathbf{x}_{i}

is large enough, which we may assume without loss of generality. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in S}\sum_{\mathbf{i}}d_{v\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\mid\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\log(Q_{i})}\left(\log\frac{\mu_{v,k}}{\mu_{v,k+1}}+\sum_{\mathbf{i}}\log\left(C_{11}\mu_{v,\mathbf{i}}\right)\right)+\gamma\varepsilon_{v}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\log(Q_{i})}\left(\log\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k+1}}+\log\left(\prod_{\mathbf{i}}\lambda_{\mathbf{i}}\right)+O(1)\right)+\gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\log(Q_{i})}\left(\log\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k+1}}+\binom{n}{k}\log\left(\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{n+1}\right)+O(1)\right)+\gamma.

Therefore, using (
5.9
), we get

\sum_{v\in S}\sum_{\mathbf{i}}d_{v\mathbf{i}}\leq-\frac{\varepsilon}{2(n+1)n}-\binom{n}{k}\sum_{v,i}c_{v,i}+\gamma+O\left(\frac{1}{\log Q_{i}}\right)

Now we note that

\sum_{\mathbf{i}}\sum_{v\in S}\left(c_{v,\pi_{v}(\mathbf{i})}+d_{v\mathbf{i}}\right)=\sum_{v\in S}\sum_{\mathbf{i}}c_{v,\pi_{v}(\mathbf{i})}+\sum_{v\mathbf{i}}d_{v\mathbf{i}}=\binom{n}{k}\sum_{v,i}c_{v,\pi_{v}(i)}+\sum_{v\mathbf{i}}d_{v\mathbf{i}}=\binom{n}{k}\sum_{v,i}c_{v,i}+\sum_{v\mathbf{i}}d_{v\mathbf{i}}

Thus the above and our choice of
\gamma
gives

\sum_{\mathbf{i}}\sum_{v\in S}\left(c_{v,\pi_{v}(\mathbf{i})}+d_{v\mathbf{i}}\right)\leq-\frac{\varepsilon}{2(n+1)n}\left(1-\frac{1}{2\binom{n+1}{k}}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{\log Q_{i}}\right)

which is negative for
Q_{i}
sufficiently large.
Now we are able to apply Theorem
4.14
to this situation, concluding that the vector spaces
V\left(\Pi_{k}\left(Q_{i}\right)\right)
form a finite set, which in turn implies that the associated spaces
W_{k}\left(Q_{i}\right)
, defined as the
K
-vector space generated by the vectors corresponding to the first
k
successive minima of
\Pi_{k}\left(Q_{i}\right)
, also form a finite set. Since
k\geq\operatorname{rank}\left(\Pi\left(Q_{i}\right)\right)
, we conclude that
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is a linear combination of the vectors in
K^{n+1}
, denoted by
\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
, determining the first
k
successive minima. By construction, this holds also for
\mathbf{v}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{v}^{(k)}
and hence the points
\mathbf{x}_{i}
belong to finitely many proper subspaces of
K^{n+1}
. This completes the proof of the affine subspace theorem
4.4
.
