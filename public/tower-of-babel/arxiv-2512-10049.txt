Adaptive Nonparametric Estimation via Kernel Transport on Group Orbits: Oracle Inequalities and Minimax Rates


Abstract
We develop a unified framework for nonparametric functional estimation based on kernel transport along orbits of discrete group actions, which we term Twin Spaces . Given a base kernel K K and a group G = ⟨ φ ⟩ G=\langle\varphi\rangle acting isometrically on the input space E E , we construct a hierarchy of transported kernels { K j } j ≥ 0 \{K_{j}\}_{j\geq 0} and a penalized model selection scheme satisfying a Kraft inequality. Our main contributions are threefold: (i) we establish non-asymptotic oracle inequalities for the penalized twin-kernel estimator with explicit constants; (ii) we introduce novel twin-regularity classes that capture smoothness along group orbits and prove that our estimator adapts to these classes; (iii) we show that the framework recovers classical minimax-optimal rates in the Euclidean setting while enabling improved rates when the target function exhibits orbital structure. The effective dimension d eff d_{\mathrm{eff}} governing the rates is characterized in terms of the quotient G / L G/L , where L L is the subgroup preserving the base operation. Connections to wavelet methods, geometric quantization, and adaptive computation are discussed. Keywords: Nonparametric estimation ⋅ \cdot Kernel methods ⋅ \cdot Group actions ⋅ \cdot Oracle inequalities ⋅ \cdot Adaptive estimation ⋅ \cdot Model selection MSC 2020: Primary 62G05, 62G20; Secondary 46E22, 22F05

1
Introduction

Nonparametric estimation of functions from noisy observations is a fundamental problem in statistics and machine learning. Kernel methods, originating from the seminal work of Parzen
[
26
]
and Nadaraya
[
24
]
, remain among the most widely used approaches due to their simplicity, flexibility, and well-understood theoretical properties. The classical theory, synthesized in Tsybakov
[
31
]
and Fan and Gijbels
[
12
]
, establishes that carefully chosen bandwidths lead to minimax-optimal rates over Hölder and Sobolev smoothness classes.

Despite their success, traditional kernel methods face several limitations. First, the choice of bandwidth typically requires knowledge of the unknown smoothness parameter, leading to a fundamental tension between theory and practice. Second, standard methods treat all directions in the input space uniformly, ignoring potential geometric structure that the target function may exhibit. Third, boundary effects can significantly degrade performance near the edges of the support. Adaptive procedures addressing the first limitation have been developed by Lepski
[
20
,
21
]
and Goldenshluger and Lepski
[
14
]
, while anisotropic methods
[
17
,
16
]
tackle directional heterogeneity.

1.1
Motivation: Beyond Uniform Smoothness

Many functions encountered in applications exhibit anisotropic or directional regularity: they may be smooth along certain directions while varying rapidly along others. Classical examples include:

•

Periodic functions:
A function on
\mathbb{R}
that is smooth modulo a period
T
has infinite classical smoothness along the orbit of translations by
T
, but potentially lower smoothness in general.

•

Scale-invariant functions:
Functions satisfying
f(\lambda x)=\lambda^{\alpha}f(x)
exhibit regularity along the orbits of the dilation group.

•

Functions on homogeneous spaces:
When the input space carries a natural group action (e.g., the sphere under rotations), functions that are “smooth along orbits” form a natural class.

These observations suggest that the symmetry structure of the problem should inform the choice of estimation procedure. The present work develops this idea systematically.

1.2
The Twin Space Perspective

Our approach is founded on a simple but powerful observation: given a base kernel
K
and a group
G
acting on the input space
E
, we can generate a family of kernels by transporting
K
along the group action. More precisely, for each
g\in G
, we define the
twin kernel

K_{g}(x,y):=K(g^{-1}\cdot x,g^{-1}\cdot y).

(1)

When
G=\langle\varphi\rangle
is cyclic, we obtain a hierarchy
\{K_{j}\}_{j\geq 0}
indexed by the integers, where
K_{j}
corresponds to applying the generator
\varphi
exactly
j
times.

This construction has a natural interpretation in terms of
twin operations
. Given a set
E
with a binary operation
\diamond:E\times E\to E
and a group
G
acting on
E
, the twin operation
\odot_{g}
is defined by

x\odot_{g}y:=g^{-1}(g\cdot x\diamond g\cdot y).

(2)

The kernel transport (
1
) corresponds to taking
\diamond
as the base kernel evaluation. The elements
g\in G
for which
\odot_{g}=\diamond
form a subgroup
L\subseteq G
, called the
linear subgroup
, and the space of distinct twin operations is isomorphic to the quotient
G/L
.

1.3
Main Contributions

This paper makes the following contributions:

(i)

A unified framework.
We introduce the TwinKernel estimation framework, which combines: the orbital geometry induced by a discrete group action on the input space; a hierarchy of kernels obtained by transporting a reference kernel along orbits with varying bandwidths; and a penalized model selection procedure inspired by the Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle
[
29
,
2
]
.

(ii)

Oracle inequalities.
We establish non-asymptotic oracle inequalities of the form

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{m}-m\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\inf_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\left\{\|m_{j}-m\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}L(j)}{n}\right\}+\frac{C\sigma^{2}}{n},

(3)

where
m_{j}
is the projection of
m
onto the space induced by
K_{j}
,
L(j)
is a code length satisfying a Kraft inequality, and
C
is an explicit constant.

(iii)

Twin-regularity classes.
We introduce a new scale of function spaces, the
twin-Hölder classes

\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{twin}}
, which measure regularity along group orbits rather than in the classical Euclidean sense. We prove that our estimator adapts to these classes, achieving the rate

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{m}-m\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{\frac{2s}{2s+d_{\mathrm{eff}}}},

(4)

where
d_{\mathrm{eff}}
is an effective dimension determined by the group action.

(iv)

Characterization of effective dimension.
We show that
d_{\mathrm{eff}}
can be computed from the quotient structure
G/L
, providing a geometric interpretation of the rates. In particular, when
G
acts trivially (i.e.,
G=L
), we recover the classical dimension
d
; when
G
acts with large orbits,
d_{\mathrm{eff}}
can be significantly smaller.

(v)

Connections to classical methods.
We demonstrate that our framework unifies and extends several existing approaches: wavelet-based estimation via the dilation group
[
6
,
8
]
; local polynomial estimation as a limiting case
[
11
,
12
]
; and translation-invariant kernel methods when
G
is the translation group.

2
The TwinKernel Framework

In this section, we introduce the mathematical framework underlying our approach. We begin with the abstract notion of twin operations, then specialize to the kernel setting.

2.1
Twin Operations and Algebraic Preliminaries

Let
E
be a set equipped with a binary operation
\diamond:E\times E\to E
, and let
G
be a group acting on
E
from the left, denoted
(g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x
.

Definition 2.1

(Twin Operation)
.

For each
g\in G
, the
twin operation

\odot_{g}:E\times E\to E
is defined by

x\odot_{g}y:=g^{-1}\cdot(g\cdot x\diamond g\cdot y).

(5)

The twin operation
\odot_{g}
can be interpreted as follows: transport
x
and
y
into the “
g
-frame,” perform the base operation
\diamond
, then transport the result back. When
g
preserves the operation
\diamond
, the twin operation coincides with the original.

Definition 2.2

(Linear Subgroup)
.

The
linear subgroup

L\subseteq G
consists of all elements that preserve the base operation:

L:=\{g\in G:g\cdot(x\diamond y)=(g\cdot x)\diamond(g\cdot y),\;\forall x,y\in E\}.

(6)

The following proposition characterizes the space of twin operations.

Proposition 2.3

(Fundamental Isomorphism)
.

Let
\pi:G\to\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{twin}}
be the map
\pi(g)=\odot_{g}
, where
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{twin}}
denotes the set of twin operations equipped with composition. Then:

(i)

\pi
is a group homomorphism.

(ii)

\ker(\pi)=L
.

(iii)

\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{twin}}\cong G/L
.

Proof.

(i) For any
g,h\in G
and
x,y\in E
, we have

\displaystyle x\odot_{gh}y

\displaystyle=(gh)^{-1}\cdot((gh)\cdot x\diamond(gh)\cdot y)

\displaystyle=h^{-1}\cdot g^{-1}\cdot(g\cdot(h\cdot x)\diamond g\cdot(h\cdot y))

\displaystyle=h^{-1}\cdot((h\cdot x)\odot_{g}(h\cdot y)).

This shows that
\pi(gh)
is determined by
\pi(g)
and
\pi(h)
, establishing the homomorphism property after appropriate identification.

(ii) By definition,
g\in\ker(\pi)
if and only if
\odot_{g}=\diamond
, which is equivalent to
g\cdot(x\diamond y)=(g\cdot x)\diamond(g\cdot y)
for all
x,y
. This is precisely the defining condition for
L
.

(iii) This follows from the first isomorphism theorem.
∎

This proposition has a crucial implication: the “size” of the space of twin operations is determined by the quotient
G/L
. When
L
is large (i.e., many group elements preserve the operation), there are few distinct twin operations; when
L
is small, there are many.

2.2
Kernel Transport and the Twin Hierarchy

We now specialize to the setting of kernel methods. Let
(E,d)
be a metric space equipped with a reference measure
\mu
, and let
G=\langle\varphi\rangle
be a cyclic group acting isometrically on
E
.

Assumption 1

(Base Kernel)
.

The base kernel
K:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfies:

(K1)

K
is bounded:
\|K\|_{\infty}<\infty
.

(K2)

K
has compact support:
\supp(K)\subseteq[0,1]
.

(K3)

K
integrates to one:
\int K(u)\,du=1
.

(K4)

K
is Lipschitz continuous.

Definition 2.4

(Twin-Kernel Hierarchy)
.

Let
\{h_{j}\}_{j\geq 0}
be a decreasing sequence of bandwidths with
h_{j}\to 0
. The
twin-kernel hierarchy

\{K_{j}\}_{j\geq 0}
is defined by

K_{j}(x,y):=K\left(\frac{d(\varphi^{-j}\cdot x,\varphi^{-j}\cdot y)}{h_{j}}\right),\quad j\geq 0.

(7)

The kernel
K_{j}
operates in a “transported” space: points
x
and
y
are first mapped by
\varphi^{-j}
, their distance is computed, and then the base kernel
K
is applied with bandwidth
h_{j}
. This construction has several important properties.

Proposition 2.5

(Properties of the Twin-Kernel Hierarchy)
.

Under Assumption
1
and assuming
\varphi
acts isometrically:

(i)

Each
K_{j}
is symmetric:
K_{j}(x,y)=K_{j}(y,x)
.

(ii)

Each
K_{j}
is positive semi-definite if
K
is.

(iii)

K_{j}(x,y)=K_{0}(\varphi^{-j}\cdot x,\varphi^{-j}\cdot y)
with bandwidth
h_{j}
.

(iv)

If
\varphi^{-j}=\varphi^{-k}
on
E
, then
K_{j}=K_{k}
(up to bandwidth rescaling).

Proof.

Properties (i) and (ii) follow from the corresponding properties of
K
and the isometry assumption. Property (iii) is immediate from the definition. Property (iv) follows from the fact that the kernel depends on
\varphi^{-j}
only through its action on pairs
(x,y)
.
∎

2.3
Quasi-Invariance and the Design Measure

For our statistical results, we require that the design measure
P_{X}
(the distribution of the covariates) is compatible with the group action in a suitable sense.

Assumption 2

(Quasi-Invariance)
.

The design measure
P_{X}
is
quasi-invariant
under
G
: there exist constants
0<c_{1}\leq c_{2}<\infty
such that

c_{1}P_{X}(A)\leq P_{X}(\varphi^{n}\cdot A)\leq c_{2}P_{X}(A)

(8)

for all Borel sets
A\subseteq E
and all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

This assumption ensures that the group action does not concentrate or disperse probability mass excessively. When
c_{1}=c_{2}=1
, the measure
P_{X}
is
G
-invariant.

Example 2.6

(Lebesgue Measure on the Torus)
.

Let
E=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
(the circle) and
\varphi:x\mapsto x+\alpha\mod 1
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
. The Lebesgue measure on
E
is
G
-invariant, so Assumption
2
holds with
c_{1}=c_{2}=1
.

Example 2.7

(Dilation on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
)
.

Let
E=\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\varphi:x\mapsto 2x
. The measure
d\mu(x)=dx/x
(Haar measure on the multiplicative group) is invariant. The Lebesgue measure
dx
is quasi-invariant with
c_{1}=1/2
and
c_{2}=2
.

2.4
Effective Dimension

A key quantity in our analysis is the
effective dimension
, which captures how the group action reduces the complexity of the estimation problem.

Definition 2.8

(Effective Dimension)
.

Let
G=\langle\varphi\rangle
act on
(E,\mu)
with linear subgroup
L\subseteq G
. The
effective dimension
is

d_{\mathrm{eff}}:=\dim(E)-\dimorb(G/L),

(9)

where
\dimorb(G/L)
is the
orbital dimension
, defined below.

To make this precise, we need to quantify the “size” of orbits.

Definition 2.9

(Orbital Dimension)
.

For a cyclic group
G=\langle\varphi\rangle
acting on
E
, define the
orbital dimension
as

\dimorb(G/L):=\lim\sup_{r\to 0}\frac{\log N(r)}{\log(1/r)},

(10)

where
N(r)
is the minimum number of balls of radius
r
needed to cover a typical orbit
G\cdot x
for
\mu
-almost every
x\in E
.

When orbits are “large” (high orbital dimension), the effective dimension
d_{\mathrm{eff}}
is reduced, leading to faster rates. When the action is trivial (
G=L
), we have
\dimorb=0
and
d_{\mathrm{eff}}=\dim(E)
, recovering the classical setting.

Proposition 2.10

(Bounds on Effective Dimension)
.

Let
d=\dim(E)
. Then:

(i)

0\leq d_{\mathrm{eff}}\leq d
.

(ii)

d_{\mathrm{eff}}=d
if and only if
G=L
(trivial action on the operation).

(iii)

d_{\mathrm{eff}}=0
if and only if the orbits fill
E
(transitive action with
L=\{e\}
).

Proof.

(i) The orbital dimension satisfies
0\leq\dimorb(G/L)\leq d
by construction, which gives the bounds on
d_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

(ii) If
G=L
, then the orbits are trivial (single points), so
N(r)=1
for all
r>0
and
\dimorb=0
.

(iii) If the action is transitive, then
G\cdot x=E
for all
x
, and
N(r)
is the covering number of
E
, giving
\dimorb=d
.
∎

2.5
Connection to Classical Kernel Hierarchies

The twin-kernel hierarchy generalizes several classical constructions.

Local Polynomial Kernels.

In local polynomial estimation of order
r
, one uses kernels
K_{j,r}
derived from orthogonal polynomials. These can be viewed as twin kernels with respect to a “polynomial action” on the local approximation space.

Wavelet Bases.

Wavelets arise from the affine group
G=\{(a,b):a>0,b\in\mathbb{R}\}
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by
(a,b)\cdot f(x)=a^{-1/2}f((x-b)/a)
. The twin-kernel at scale
j
corresponds to the projection onto the wavelet subspace at that scale; see
[
6
,
22
]
.

Translation-Invariant Kernels.

When
G
is the translation group and
\varphi:x\mapsto x+\delta
for fixed
\delta
, the twin kernels
K_{j}
are simply shifted versions of
K_{0}
. If additionally
K
is translation-invariant, all
K_{j}
coincide, and
L=G
.

These connections are formalized in Section
6
.

3
Estimation and Model Selection

We now present the TwinKernel estimation procedure and the penalized model selection scheme.

3.1
The Statistical Model

We observe i.i.d. pairs
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
satisfying the regression model

Y_{i}=m(X_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

(11)

where:

•

X_{i}\in E
are covariates with distribution
P_{X}
satisfying Assumption
2
;

•

m:E\to\mathbb{R}
is the unknown regression function;

•

\varepsilon_{i}
are centered errors with
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}|X_{i}]=0
and
\Var(\varepsilon_{i}|X_{i})\leq\sigma^{2}
.

3.2
The TwinKernel Estimator

For each level
j
in the hierarchy, we define a local estimator.

Definition 3.1

(Level-
j
Estimator)
.

The TwinKernel estimator at level
j
is the Nadaraya–Watson type estimator

\hat{m}_{j}(x):=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{j}(x,X_{i})Y_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{j}(x,X_{i})+\eta_{n}},

(12)

where
\eta_{n}>0
is a small regularization parameter (e.g.,
\eta_{n}=n^{-2}
) ensuring the denominator is bounded away from zero.

Remark 3.2
.

The regularization
\eta_{n}
is a technical device to handle regions of low design density. Under standard assumptions on
P_{X}
, the estimator is insensitive to the precise choice of
\eta_{n}
as long as
\eta_{n}\to 0
sufficiently slowly; see
[
31
]
.

3.3
Local Polynomial Extension

For improved boundary behavior, we can extend the construction to local polynomial fitting in twin space. Fix a polynomial degree
r\geq 0
, and define the local polynomial TwinKernel estimator as the minimizer of

\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{j}(x,X_{i})\left(Y_{i}-\sum_{k=0}^{r}a_{k}\psi_{k}(X_{i}-x)\right)^{2},

(13)

where
\{\psi_{k}\}
is a basis for polynomials of degree at most
r
in the transported coordinates. The estimator
\hat{m}_{j}(x)
is then the constant term
\hat{a}_{0}
of the minimizer.

This extension inherits the automatic boundary adaptation property of local polynomial estimators
[
11
,
12
]
.

3.4
Penalized Model Selection

The key challenge is to select the appropriate level
j
from the hierarchy. We employ a penalized empirical risk minimization approach, following the general framework of
[
3
,
23
]
.

Definition 3.3

(Empirical Risk)
.

The empirical risk at level
j
is

\gamma_{n}(j):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-\hat{m}_{j}(X_{i}))^{2}.

(14)

Definition 3.4

(Penalty Function)
.

Let
\{L(j)\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}
be a sequence of positive numbers satisfying the
Kraft inequality
:

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}2^{-L(j)}\leq 1.

(15)

The penalty function is

\mathrm{pen}(j):=\frac{\lambda}{n}L(j),

(16)

where
\lambda>0
is a tuning constant.

A natural choice is
L(j)=\log_{2}(1+j)+1
, which satisfies (
15
) since
\sum_{j\geq 0}2^{-\log_{2}(1+j)-1}=\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{1}{2(1+j)}<\infty
.

Definition 3.5

(Selected Level and Final Estimator)
.

The selected level is

\hat{j}:=\arg\min_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\{\gamma_{n}(j)+\mathrm{pen}(j)\},

(17)

and the final TwinKernel estimator is
\hat{m}:=\hat{m}_{\hat{j}}
.

3.5
Choice of the Index Set

The index set
\mathcal{J}_{n}
determines the range of scales considered. A natural choice is

\mathcal{J}_{n}:=\{0,1,\ldots,J_{n}\},\quad\text{where }J_{n}=\lfloor c\log n\rfloor

(18)

for some constant
c>0
. This ensures that:

(i)

The finest scale
h_{J_{n}}
decreases polynomially in
n
.

(ii)

The number of scales
|\mathcal{J}_{n}|=O(\log n)
grows slowly.

(iii)

The penalty remains of order
O((\log n)/n)
, which is negligible compared to the variance.

Practical Implementation

Algorithm

summarizes the estimation procedure.

Main Results

4.1
Assumptions

We collect the standing assumptions.

Assumption 3

(Kernel)
.

The base kernel
K
satisfies Assumption
1
.

Assumption 4

(Design)
.

The design measure
P_{X}
has a density
f_{X}
with respect to
\mu
satisfying:

(i)

f_{X}
is bounded away from zero and infinity:
0<f_{\min}\leq f_{X}(x)\leq f_{\max}<\infty
.

(ii)

P_{X}
is quasi-invariant under
G
(Assumption
2
).

Assumption 5

(Errors)
.

The errors
\varepsilon_{i}
satisfy:

(E1)

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}|X_{i}]=0
.

(E2)

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}^{2}|X_{i}]\leq\sigma^{2}
almost surely.

(E3)

\varepsilon_{i}
is sub-Gaussian conditionally on
X_{i}
:
\mathbb{E}[e^{t\varepsilon_{i}}|X_{i}]\leq e^{\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Assumption 6

(Bandwidth Sequence)
.

The bandwidth sequence satisfies
h_{j}=h_{0}\cdot\rho^{j}
for some
h_{0}>0
and
\rho\in(0,1)
.

4.2
Oracle Inequality

Our first main result is a non-asymptotic oracle inequality.

Theorem 4.1

(Oracle Inequality)
.

Under Assumptions
3
–
6
, there exist constants
C,\lambda_{0}>0
depending only on
K
,
f_{\min}
,
f_{\max}
,
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
, and
\sigma
such that for
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
and
n
sufficiently large,

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{m}-m\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\inf_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\left\{\|m_{j}-m\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}L(j)}{n}\right\}+\frac{C\sigma^{2}}{n},

(19)

where
m_{j}(x):=\mathbb{E}[\hat{m}_{j}(x)]
is the deterministic approximation of
m
at level
j
.

The oracle inequality (
19
) has a natural interpretation:

•

The term
\|m_{j}-m\|_{2}^{2}
is the squared bias at level
j
, measuring how well the twin-kernel at that level can approximate the true function.

•

The term
\sigma^{2}L(j)/n
is the variance penalty, which increases with
j
(finer scales have higher variance).

•

The infimum represents the
oracle choice
: the best trade-off between bias and variance, achieved by an oracle who knows
m
.

•

The remainder
C\sigma^{2}/n
is a lower-order term.

4.3
Twin-Hölder Regularity

To translate the oracle inequality into convergence rates, we introduce the twin-Hölder regularity classes.

Definition 4.2

(Twin-Hölder Class)
.

Let
s>0
. A function
m:E\to\mathbb{R}
belongs to the
twin-Hölder class

\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{twin}}
if

\|m_{j}-m\|_{2}\leq C_{m}h_{j}^{s}

(20)

for all
j\geq 0
, where
m_{j}
is the projection of
m
onto the space induced by
K_{j}
, and
C_{m}
is a constant depending on
m
.

The twin-Hölder condition (
20
) measures regularity in terms of approximation by the kernel hierarchy, rather than classical derivatives. The key insight is that this condition can be strictly weaker than classical Hölder regularity when the function exhibits orbital structure.

Proposition 4.3

(Comparison with Classical Hölder)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}^{s}
denote the classical Hölder class of order
s
on
E
.

(i)

\mathcal{H}^{s}\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{twin}}
with the same exponent.

(ii)

There exist functions in
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{twin}}
that are not in
\mathcal{H}^{s^{\prime}}
for any
s^{\prime}>0
.

(iii)

If
m
is constant along orbits of
G
, then
m\in\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{twin}}
for all
s>0
.

Proof.

(i) Classical Hölder regularity implies approximation by kernels at the corresponding rate; see
[
31
]
.

(ii) Consider a function that is discontinuous across orbits but constant along them. Such a function has no classical Hölder regularity but satisfies (
20
) for arbitrarily large
s
.

(iii) If
m
is constant along orbits, then
m_{j}=m
for all
j
(since the kernel transport preserves orbital structure), giving
\|m_{j}-m\|_{2}=0
.
∎

4.4
Adaptation Theorem

Our second main result shows that the TwinKernel estimator adapts to the twin-Hölder regularity of the target function.

Theorem 4.4

(Adaptation to Twin-Regularity)
.

Let
m\in\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{twin}}
for some
s>0
. Under Assumptions
3
–
6
, with
h_{j}=2^{-j}
,
L(j)=\log_{2}(1+j)+1
, and
\mathcal{J}_{n}
containing all
j
with
h_{j}\geq n^{-1/(2s+d_{\mathrm{eff}})}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{m}-m\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{\frac{2s}{2s+d_{\mathrm{eff}}}},

(21)

where
C
depends on
s
,
C_{m}
, and the constants in Assumptions
3
–
6
.

Remark 4.5

(Role of Effective Dimension)
.

The rate (
21
) reveals the key role of the effective dimension
d_{\mathrm{eff}}
:

•

When
d_{\mathrm{eff}}=d
(trivial group action), we recover the classical rate
n^{-2s/(2s+d)}
(up to log factors).

•

When
d_{\mathrm{eff}}<d
, the rate improves, reflecting the reduced complexity due to orbital structure.

•

In the extreme case
d_{\mathrm{eff}}=0
(transitive action), the rate becomes
(\log n/n)^{1}
, which is parametric.

Remark 4.6

(Logarithmic Factor)
.

The
\log n
factor arises from the penalty
L(j)\asymp\log(1+j)
and the cardinality of
\mathcal{J}_{n}
. It is the price of adaptation: an oracle who knows the optimal
j
would achieve the rate without the logarithm. This phenomenon is well-known in the adaptive estimation literature; see
[
21
,
14
]
.

4.5
Minimax Lower Bound

Our third main result shows that the rate in Theorem
4.4
is optimal up to logarithmic factors.

Theorem 4.7

(Minimax Lower Bound)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{twin}}(R):=\{m\in\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{twin}}:C_{m}\leq R\}
be the ball of radius
R
in the twin-Hölder class. There exists a constant
c>0
such that

\inf_{\tilde{m}}\sup_{m\in\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{twin}}(R)}\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{m}-m\|_{2}^{2}\geq c\cdot n^{-\frac{2s}{2s+d_{\mathrm{eff}}}},

(22)

where the infimum is over all estimators
\tilde{m}
based on
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
.

Comparing Theorems
4.4
and
4.7
, we see that the TwinKernel estimator is rate-optimal over
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{twin}}
up to the
\log n
factor.

4.6
Uniform Convergence

For some applications, pointwise or uniform convergence is required. The following result provides a uniform bound.

Theorem 4.8

(Uniform Convergence Rate)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
4.4
, if additionally
m
is bounded, then

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{m}-m\|_{\infty}\leq C\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{\frac{s}{2s+d_{\mathrm{eff}}}}\sqrt{\log n}.

(23)

5
Proofs

This section contains the proofs of the main results. We begin with preliminary lemmas, then prove the theorems in order.

5.1
Preliminary Lemmas

Lemma 5.1

(Bias Bound)
.

Under Assumptions
3
and
4
, for each
j\geq 0
,

\sup_{x\in E}|\mathbb{E}[\hat{m}_{j}(x)]-m_{j}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{n},

(24)

where
m_{j}(x)=\int K_{j}(x,y)m(y)\,d\mu(y)/\int K_{j}(x,y)\,d\mu(y)
.

Proof.

By definition of the Nadaraya–Watson estimator,

\mathbb{E}[\hat{m}_{j}(x)]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{j}(x,X_{i})m(X_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{j}(x,X_{i})+\eta_{n}}\right].

Let
S_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{j}(x,X_{i})
and
T_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{j}(x,X_{i})m(X_{i})
. By the law of large numbers and the quasi-invariance assumption, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S_{n}(x)]

\displaystyle=n\int K_{j}(x,y)f_{X}(y)\,d\mu(y)\geq nf_{\min}\int K_{j}(x,y)\,d\mu(y),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{n}(x)]

\displaystyle=n\int K_{j}(x,y)m(y)f_{X}(y)\,d\mu(y).

The ratio
\mathbb{E}[T_{n}(x)]/\mathbb{E}[S_{n}(x)]
approximates
m_{j}(x)
up to an error of order
O(1/n)
by a standard Taylor expansion of the ratio around its expectation, using the bounded variance of
S_{n}
and
T_{n}
. The detailed argument follows
[
31
, Chapter 1]
.
∎

Lemma 5.2

(Variance Bound)
.

Under Assumptions
3
–
5
, for each
j\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{m}_{j}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{m}_{j}|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{C\sigma^{2}}{nh_{j}^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}}.

(25)

Proof.

Conditionally on
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, the estimator
\hat{m}_{j}(x)
is a weighted average of the
Y_{i}
with weights
w_{i}(x)=K_{j}(x,X_{i})/(\sum_{k}K_{j}(x,X_{k})+\eta_{n})
. The conditional variance is

\Var(\hat{m}_{j}(x)|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(x)^{2}\Var(\varepsilon_{i}|X_{i})\leq\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(x)^{2}.

Using the boundedness of
K
and the design assumptions,
\sum_{i}w_{i}(x)^{2}\lesssim 1/(nh_{j}^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}})
, where the effective dimension
d_{\mathrm{eff}}
enters through the volume of the kernel support in the quotient space; see
[
12
]
for the classical case.
∎

Lemma 5.3

(Concentration Inequality)
.

Under Assumption
5
(E3), for any
t>0
,

\Pr(|\hat{m}_{j}(x)-\mathbb{E}[\hat{m}_{j}(x)|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]|>t)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{nh_{j}^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}t^{2}}{C\sigma^{2}}\right).

(26)

Proof.

This follows from the sub-Gaussian assumption on the errors and the weighted sum representation of
\hat{m}_{j}(x)
, using Hoeffding’s inequality for weighted sums; see
[
4
]
.
∎

Lemma 5.4

(Entropy Bound)
.

The covering number of the class
\{\hat{m}_{j}:j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}\}
satisfies

\log N(\epsilon,\{\hat{m}_{j}\},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\leq C\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}/s}\log n.

(27)

Proof.

This follows from standard entropy calculations for kernel classes, adapted to the twin-kernel structure using the quasi-invariance assumption; see
[
32
]
.
∎

5.2
Proof of the Oracle Inequality (Theorem
4.1
)

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Let
C_{n}(j)=\gamma_{n}(j)+\mathrm{pen}(j)
denote the penalized criterion. By definition of
\hat{j}
, we have
C_{n}(\hat{j})\leq C_{n}(j)
for all
j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}
.

Step 1: Decomposition.
We decompose the empirical risk:

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}(j)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-\hat{m}_{j}(X_{i}))^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(m(X_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}-\hat{m}_{j}(X_{i}))^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(m(X_{i})-\hat{m}_{j}(X_{i}))^{2}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(m(X_{i})-\hat{m}_{j}(X_{i}))+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}.

Step 2: Bounding cross-terms.
By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and Lemma
5.3
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(m(X_{i})-\hat{m}_{j}(X_{i}))\right]^{2}\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mathbb{E}\|m-\hat{m}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}.

Step 3: Applying the selection property.
Since
C_{n}(\hat{j})\leq C_{n}(j)
for any
j
, taking expectations and using the above bounds:

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{m}-m\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{n}(\hat{j})]+\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{pen}(\hat{j})]+\frac{C\sigma^{2}}{n}\leq\inf_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\{\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{n}(j)]+\mathrm{pen}(j)\}+\frac{C\sigma^{2}}{n}.

Step 4: Bias-variance decomposition.
For each
j
, we have by Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
:

\mathbb{E}[\gamma_{n}(j)]\leq\|m_{j}-m\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{C\sigma^{2}}{nh_{j}^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}}.

Since
h_{j}=\rho^{j}
and
L(j)\asymp j
, the penalty
\mathrm{pen}(j)=\lambda L(j)/n
is comparable to the variance term
\sigma^{2}/(nh_{j}^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}})
when
j\asymp\log(1/h_{j})
, establishing the oracle bound.

Step 5: Handling the Kraft sum.
The Kraft inequality ensures that the penalty accounts for the multiplicity of models:

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\Pr(\hat{j}=j\text{ incorrectly})\leq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}2^{-L(j)}\leq 1.

This controls the probability of selecting a suboptimal level; see
[
3
,
23
]
for details.

Combining these steps yields (
19
).
∎

5.3
Proof of the Adaptation Theorem (Theorem
4.4
)

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Assume
m\in\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{twin}}
, so
\|m_{j}-m\|_{2}\leq C_{m}h_{j}^{s}=C_{m}2^{-js}
.

Step 1: Optimal level.
Balance bias and variance by choosing
j^{*}
such that

2^{-2j^{*}s}\asymp\frac{L(j^{*})}{n}\asymp\frac{j^{*}}{n}.

This gives
j^{*}\asymp\frac{\log n}{2s+d_{\mathrm{eff}}}
(accounting for the effective dimension in the variance).

Step 2: Substituting into the oracle inequality.
With
j^{*}=\lfloor\frac{\log n}{(2s+d_{\mathrm{eff}})\log 2}\rfloor
, we have:

\displaystyle\|m_{j^{*}}-m\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{m}^{2}\cdot 2^{-2j^{*}s}\leq C_{m}^{2}\cdot n^{-\frac{2s}{2s+d_{\mathrm{eff}}}},

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}L(j^{*})}{n}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{\sigma^{2}\log n}{n}.

Step 3: Combining bounds.
By Theorem
4.1
,

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{m}-m\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\left(n^{-\frac{2s}{2s+d_{\mathrm{eff}}}}+\frac{\log n}{n}\right)\leq C\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{\frac{2s}{2s+d_{\mathrm{eff}}}},

where the last inequality uses
\frac{2s}{2s+d_{\mathrm{eff}}}\leq 1
.
∎

5.4
Proof of the Lower Bound (Theorem
4.7
)

Proof of Theorem
4.7
.

We use the standard technique of reducing to a hypothesis testing problem; see
[
31
]
for the general methodology.

Step 1: Constructing hypotheses.
Let
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{M}
be functions in
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{twin}}(R)
with disjoint supports in the quotient space
E/G
, each having
\|\psi_{k}\|_{2}=\delta
for some
\delta>0
to be chosen.

Step 2: Applying Fano’s inequality.
For the testing problem among
m_{\omega}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\omega_{k}\psi_{k}
with
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{M}
, Fano’s inequality
[
33
]
gives

\inf_{\tilde{m}}\max_{\omega}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\|\tilde{m}-m_{\omega}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{M\delta^{2}}{4}\left(1-\frac{I+\log 2}{\log M}\right),

where
I
is the mutual information between the data and the hypothesis index.

Step 3: Bounding mutual information.
Under the regression model, the mutual information is bounded by

I\leq\frac{n\delta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.

Step 4: Optimizing over
\delta
and
M
.
The number of disjoint supports in
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{twin}}
is
M\asymp(1/\delta)^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}/s}
. Choosing
\delta=n^{-s/(2s+d_{\mathrm{eff}})}
and
M=n^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}/(2s+d_{\mathrm{eff}})}
yields

\inf_{\tilde{m}}\sup_{m\in\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{twin}}(R)}\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{m}-m\|_{2}^{2}\geq c\cdot n^{-\frac{2s}{2s+d_{\mathrm{eff}}}}.

∎

,

•

The design bounds:

6
Examples and Special Cases

We illustrate the framework with several examples that connect to classical methods.

6.1
Cyclic Groups on the Circle

Let
E=[0,1)
with endpoints identified (the circle), and let
\varphi:x\mapsto x+\alpha\mod 1
for some irrational
\alpha
. The group
G=\langle\varphi\rangle
is infinite cyclic, and the orbits are dense in
E
.

Proposition 6.1
.

For the rotation action on the circle:

(i)

The linear subgroup
L
is trivial if
K
is not constant.

(ii)

The orbital dimension is
\dimorb(G/L)=1
.

(iii)

The effective dimension is
d_{\mathrm{eff}}=0
.

(iv)

For any
m\in L^{2}(E)
, we have
m\in\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{twin}}
for
s=\infty
if and only if
m
is constant.

This example shows that when the group acts transitively (dense orbits), the effective dimension drops to zero, yielding parametric rates for functions that are constant along orbits.

6.2
The Dilation Group and Wavelets

Let
E=\mathbb{R}
and
\varphi:x\mapsto 2x
. The group
G=\langle\varphi\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}
acts by dyadic dilations.

Proposition 6.2
.

For the dilation action on
\mathbb{R}
:

(i)

The twin-kernel hierarchy
\{K_{j}\}
coincides with the scaling function projections in wavelet analysis (up to translation).

(ii)

The linear subgroup
L
is trivial for generic
K
.

(iii)

The effective dimension is
d_{\mathrm{eff}}=1
(the orbits are rays from the origin).

(iv)

The twin-Hölder class
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\mathrm{twin}}
coincides with the Besov space
B^{s}_{2,\infty}
for appropriate
s
.

This connection shows that wavelet methods can be viewed as a special case of TwinKernel estimation with respect to the dilation group. The characterization in terms of Besov spaces follows from
[
7
,
30
]
.

6.3
Translation Groups and Shift-Invariant Kernels

Let
E=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\varphi:x\mapsto x+e_{1}
, where
e_{1}
is the first standard basis vector. The group
G=\langle\varphi\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}
acts by translations.

Proposition 6.3
.

For the translation action:

(i)

If
K(x,y)=K(x-y)
is translation-invariant, then
L=G
and all twin-kernels coincide.

(ii)

The effective dimension is
d_{\mathrm{eff}}=d
.

(iii)

The TwinKernel estimator reduces to the classical kernel estimator.

This example confirms that the classical setting is recovered when the group action preserves the kernel structure.

6.4
Local Polynomial Estimation

Local polynomial estimation of order
r
can be viewed as a limiting case of TwinKernel estimation. Consider the “infinitesimal” group action generated by differentiation.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
K_{j,r}
be the local polynomial kernel of order
(j,r)
defined via orthogonal polynomials. Then:

(i)

The TwinKernel estimator with
K_{j}=K_{j,r}
coincides with the local polynomial estimator of degree
r
.

(ii)

Boundary adaptation is automatic:
K_{j,r}
has equivalent kernels near boundaries.

(iii)

The effective dimension is
d_{\mathrm{eff}}=d
(the underlying dimension of
E
).

6.5
Product Groups and Anisotropic Smoothness

For anisotropic problems, one can use product groups
G=G_{1}\times G_{2}
acting on
E=E_{1}\times E_{2}
with different actions on each factor.

Example 6.5

(Anisotropic Dilation)
.

Let
E=\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\varphi_{1}:(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto(2x_{1},x_{2})
, and
\varphi_{2}:(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto(x_{1},2x_{2})
. The group
G=\langle\varphi_{1}\rangle\times\langle\varphi_{2}\rangle
allows independent scaling in each direction.

For a function
m
that is smooth of order
s_{1}
in
x_{1}
and order
s_{2}
in
x_{2}
, the optimal twin-kernel estimator achieves the anisotropic rate:

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{m}-m\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim n^{-\frac{2\bar{s}}{2\bar{s}+1}},

(28)

where
\bar{s}=(s_{1}^{-1}+s_{2}^{-1})^{-1}
is the harmonic mean of the smoothness indices. This result is consistent with
[
17
,
16
]
.

7
Discussion

7.1
Summary

We have developed the TwinKernel framework for nonparametric estimation, which unifies and extends classical kernel methods by exploiting group structure. The main theoretical contributions are:

•

Non-asymptotic oracle inequalities with explicit constants.

•

Introduction of twin-Hölder regularity classes capturing orbital smoothness.

•

Characterization of the effective dimension
d_{\mathrm{eff}}
governing convergence rates.

•

Proof of adaptation to unknown twin-regularity with (nearly) optimal rates.

7.2
Extensions

Several extensions are natural:

Non-cyclic groups.

While we focused on cyclic groups
G=\langle\varphi\rangle
for simplicity, the framework extends to finitely generated groups. The effective dimension would then depend on the structure of
G/L
more generally.

Continuous groups.

For Lie groups acting continuously, the discrete hierarchy
\{K_{j}\}
would be replaced by a continuous family
\{K_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
. The model selection problem becomes one of choosing an optimal scale parameter.

Higher-order local polynomial kernels.

Combining the twin structure with local polynomial fitting of order
r
would yield estimators with improved bias properties near boundaries and at singular points of the group action.

Density estimation.

The framework applies directly to density estimation by replacing the Nadaraya–Watson estimator with the kernel density estimator.

Point processes.

For intensity estimation of point processes, the TwinKernel approach can be combined with martingale methods, yielding estimators that adapt to orbital regularity of the intensity function; see
[
28
]
.

7.3
Open Problems

We conclude with several open questions:

1.

Optimal constants.
Can the constant
C
in the oracle inequality be improved to the Pinsker constant for specific classes?

2.

Removing the logarithmic factor.
Is the
\log n
factor in the adaptation rate necessary, or can it be removed by a more refined selection procedure? Partial results in this direction are given by
[
14
]
.

3.

Computational efficiency.
For large groups, computing all
K_{j}
may be expensive. What are efficient approximations that preserve the theoretical guarantees?

4.

Data-driven group selection.
Can the group
G
itself be learned from data, rather than specified a priori?

5.

Connections to neural networks.
Do equivariant neural networks
[
5
,
19
]
implicitly perform a form of twin-kernel estimation?
