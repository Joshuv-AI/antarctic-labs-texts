Assessment of coastal exposure of climate-driven oceanic hazards along the East Coast of India using geospatial techniques
Authors: Gaurav Khairnar, R S Mahendra, P C Mohanty, Sudheer Joseph, T M Balakrishnan Nair
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoastal regions face increasing threats from a range of natural and anthropogenic hazards, such as sea level rise, storm surges, coastal erosion and flooding. These hazards are intensified by climate change and rapid coastal development, placing both human populations and ecosystems at significant risk. Vulnerability to coastal hazards is influenced by geographic, socio-economic and infrastructural factors, with densely inhabited areas in low elevation being particularly susceptible. In this regard, the coastal zones are exposed to composite ocean hazards, including extreme water levels, sea level rise and shoreline change rate are used to generate the coastal multi-hazard zone (CMZ). Further, the Exposure Index (EI) at the village and town levels along the east coast was calculated to fill the gap in localised risk assessment. The study highlights the coastal villages/towns exposed to different degrees along the entire east coastline of India, spanning from Tamil Nadu to West Bengal. The most vulnerable districts from each state are Nagapattinam in Tamil Nadu, Krishna in Andhra Pradesh, Jagatsinghpur in Odisha and the North 24 Parganas in West Bengal have recorded more villages/towns under very high EI that are facing frequent inundation and need immediate attention. The top five districts in terms of percentage of population exposed are Karikal, North 24 Paraganas, Nagapattinam, Jagatsingpur and Thanjavur. The study provides different scenarios by utilizing the EI at inhabited villages/towns, through mere exposure. Though most of the deltaic and riverine environments have recorded large areas under exposure, the objective classification of villages/towns in high exposure zones adopted in this study differs in results. Hence, the EI derived in this work helps policymakers to draw site-specific interventions and disaster managers to focus on preparedness and adaptive measures to mitigate risks associated with coastal hazards in order to build resilient coastal communities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52919-4.Keywords: Climate change, Extreme sea level, Coastal multi-hazard, Exposure index, Risk, VulnerabilitySubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Geography, Geography, Natural hazards

Introduction

Coastal areas are dynamic yet highly vulnerable environments, facing increasing risks from climate change-driven hazards such as tidal surges, long-term sea level changes and coastal erosion1. Exposure to these hazards, both environmentally as well as socioeconomically, makes coastal regions more susceptible to disasters. The term ‘exposure’ describes the concentration of population, infrastructures, and ecosystems within the areas that are prone to coastal hazards2. It determines the extent to which a region is at risk, based on its location and physical characteristics3. Higher exposure increases the likelihood of damage and disruption during disasters, making it a critical factor in vulnerability assessments. Hence, assessing coastal exposure on a systematic scientific basis is essential for effective disaster management and risk reduction. Coastal hazards can be classified into two categories as short-term and long-term. Short-term coastal hazards can range from minutes to several days, such as cyclones, tsunamis and storm surges. Over extended timeframes, coastal areas face long-term hazards like erosion, rising sea level and gradual inundation, which develop through gradual processes4,37. The cumulative effects of these hazards result in substantial vulnerability for coastal communities and their supporting systems. Rising sea levels will result in both temporary and permanent inundation5,6. Beyond the permanent flooding of low-lying areas and increased inundation, climate change is projected to cause increased erosion, damage to coastal ecosystems (especially wetlands and deltas) and saltwater intrusion into freshwater sources (Change, M.C.,20101,7;. Low elevation coastal zones have a high risk of inundation during storm surges and cyclonic disturbances8; Sindhu et al.9,). Additionally, climate change is driving a trend toward more powerful and recurrent cyclonic storms in the Indian Ocean, leading to intensified coastal inundation and erosion. Determining which nearshore areas are most critically exposed to sea level rise and climate-related impacts is critical for these nearshore communities1. Human occupation of coastal zones increases the vulnerability of the littoral system, as both population presence and socioeconomic activities contribute to higher exposure to coastal hazards2,10–12. Trends in socio-economic development, including increased urbanisation and population shifts, are contributing to higher vulnerability to future climatic hazards13,14. The occurrence of a disaster is not only determined by the magnitude of a physical event. Contextual factors, including geographic, physical and social conditions of coastal zones, play a critical role (Richard15 in determining the degree of vulnerability. While extreme events are often involved, non-extreme events can also trigger disasters when these contextual vulnerabilities are prominent. Empirical data from disaster databases confirms that many recorded disasters arise from physical events that are not extreme, still they cause significant social and economic impacts on local communities and governments16.

Several researchers have estimated the coastal exposure based on the geospatial and statistical approaches on different parts of the globe2,5,10,17. Similar studies have been reported in India, comprising the various aspects of the coastal exposure were estimated based on the different approaches18–21,41.

Previous studies have largely limited in delineating coastal physical vulnerability along selected coastal stretches. In contrast, the present study adopts a more comprehensive and granular approach by examining the impact of the Coastal Management Zone (CMZ) at the level of individual villages and towns across the entire east coast of India. By developing an exposure index for these settlements, the study provides a spatially explicit assessment of vulnerability that extends beyond localized case studies and offers a broader regional perspective. Additionally, the study quantifies and tabulates the population residing within the identified exposed villages and towns. This integration of demographic data with spatial exposure analysis enhances the practical relevance of the research. Hence, the current study aims to assess seamless coastal exposure from ocean-induced multi-hazard events along the east coast of India using the geospatial tools. The exposure index derived in this study helps to understand the spatial variations in risk across the different coastal areas. This provides vital input to disaster management, coastal planning and policymakers and helps in improving decisions. It provides valuable insights for prioritising mitigation measures, such as infrastructure development, early warning systems and adaptive strategies to reduce the impact of coastal hazards on vulnerable communities13. The analysis of the exposure index provides an understanding of the vulnerability of the coast by identifying areas prone to coastal hazards17. However, it does not quantify the potential impact or consequences of disasters10 on the exposed vulnerable elements predominantly the communities. Hence, in order to fill this gap, the exposure index has been calculated in this study at the village and towns, which are the lowest administrative level in India as per the Panchayat Raj. Additionally, the population and the portion of the village’s area falling under exposure-prone zones have been assessed for each village and town, offering comprehensive insights into the spatial distribution of communities at risk.

Study area

India’s coastlines are highly vulnerable to tropical cyclones and tsunamis22, with storm-surge-induced flooding being the primary cause of their destructive power and danger to lives (Sindhu et al.9,). India’s eastern coast is most affected by tropical cyclones that develop in both the Southeast Arabian Sea and the Northern Bay of Bengal, with an average of 1–2 cyclones impacting the coast annually20. The Bay of Bengal experiences significantly more tropical disturbances than the Arabian Sea in the northern Indian Ocean20,23. India holds the sixth position in Asia and the fifth among all tropical countries globally in terms of the coastal length14. The study area of this work is the East Coast of India, which comprises the coastal zones of the states of Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Puducherry, Odisha and West Bengal (Fig. 1). The geographic constraints of the study area are 77.0–89.1 E longitudes and 8.0–22.8 N latitudes. The coastal zones of these states are highly vulnerable to natural hazards due to their geographical location along the Bay of Bengal19.

Study area.

India’s eastern coastline is traversed by several significant river systems, notably the Ganga, Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, and Kaveri, which significantly influence coastal dynamics and hazards. These rivers drain vast catchment areas and discharge large volumes of freshwater and sediment into the Bay of Bengal, shaping the coastal landscape. During extreme weather events, such as cyclones and heavy monsoonal rains, these river systems contribute to widespread flooding in low-lying coastal areas. Additionally, the deltas formed by these rivers, particularly the Ganga-Brahmaputra and Mahanadi deltas, are highly prone to sea level rise, storm surges and erosion due to their flat topography and high population density. The east coast of India encompasses not only major river basins but also critical ecological zones, including mangrove forests of Sundarbans (West Bengal), Bhitarkanika (Odisha), Godavari and Krishna Delta Mangroves (Andhra Pradesh), and Pichavaram and Muthupet (Tamil Nadu), which protect the coast from hazards along with mudflats, sand-dunes, coral reefs, sea grass, etc. (Nayak24.). Chilika Lake in Odisha, the largest brackish water lagoon in Asia, holds immense ecological, economic and hydrological importance, which supports one of India’s largest inland fisheries25. A mosaic of all these vulnerable and important habitats comprises megacities (Chennai, Kolkata) and other major towns (Visakhapatnam, Puducherry, Cuddalore, Nagapattinam, Kakinada, Machilipatnam, Puri and Paradip etc.) that are situated on the east coast of India. These densely populated urban centers form an important economic hub associated with critical infrastructures such as ports, harbours, industries, etc.

The ALTM and Cato-DTM obtained from the National Remote Sensing Centre (NRSC) had the MSL datum and Copernicus has the WGS-84 datum, which was converted to MSL using EGM2008. The highest accurate data is ALTM with its vertical accuracy of 30 cm as per the data suppliers. The ALTM and Carto-DTM data were resampled to 30 m spatial resolution to make spatial normalisation using a ‘nearest neighbour’ resampling kernel. These spatially normalized ALTM and Carto-DTM, merged with Copernicus data into a single grid (seamless DEM of the study area), with ALTM assigned as the primary (top-priority) elevation layer. The resampled ALTM data were compared with the Carto-DTM and Copernicus DEM at randomly selected locations. The analysis revealed relative vertical accuracies of 1.43 m and 1.3 m, and relative horizontal accuracies of 5 m and 6 m for Carto-DTM and Copernicus DEM, respectively.

Sea level data from the Global Sea Level Observing System (GLOSS) and sea level rise assessment from NOAA available at URL:https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/sltrends/ are being used as a source of Sea Level Rise (SLR). Whereas long-term hourly tide gauge data obtained from the Survey of India (SOI) is used to derive the extreme water levels. In addition, the extreme water levels about historical events were also collected from published literature based on availability. Landsat Multi-Spectral Scanner (MSS), Thematic Mapper (TM) and Enhanced Thematic Mapper (ETM) imagery were used to estimate shoreline change rate. These multi-temporal datasets provided consistent, long-term observations for assessing coastal dynamics.

Village Boundary shapefile data have been downloaded from livingatlas-dcdev.opendata.arcgis.com. Census of India (2011) population data were used for towns and villages.

Methodology

The current study includes the generation of Coastal Multi-Hazard Zones (CMZ), classification of the CMZ based on the coastal topography using the high-resolution DEMs and estimation of the village-level exposure index.

Generation of coastal multi-hazard zones (CMZ)

CMZ were generated using parameters: High-Resolution topography, extreme water level and their return periods, sea level change rate and Shoreline change rate. The holistic method used to generate CMZ is similar to Mahendra et al.4,11,28, carried out for Andhra Pradesh and Cuddalore coasts.

Calculation of total extreme water level

The annual maximum water levels are derived from hourly tide gauge data pertaining to each station listed in Table 2. These maximum water levels represent all extreme waves recorded at the tide gauge (storm, tsunami, high waves, swells, etc.). These water levels are applied with the probability of non-exceedance and the extreme water levels in a 100-year return period are calculated using the extreme value distribution function. The return periods of the extreme water levels were calculated to understand the probability of the flooding level in 100 years. Additionally, relative sea level rise has been calculated using the gloss data for these tide gauge stations. The expected sea level rise after 100 years has been calculated based on the rate of relative sea level rise for each of these tide gauge station. The total water level depicting the flooding at each tide gauge station has been derived by summing the extreme water level and sea level rise after 100 years.

Calculation of coastal erosion

Besides, the shoreline change rate for the study area was obtained from the INCOIS project. That was derived using ortho-rectified Landsat MSS and TM images pertaining to the period 1972, 1990 and 2000. Though this data pertains to 1972–2000, the 29-year long-term data is feasible to understand the shoreline change trend. The satellite datasets of different periods are selected pertaining to the same season and similar tidal conditions in order to avoid the uncertainties. Further, data of different resolution were spatially normalized by resampling TM/ETM data on 56 m spatial resolution to match with MSS. The shoreline was digitised using an on-screen digitisation technique using the Infra-Red (IR) band (suitable for land-water boundary delineation). These shorelines are predominantly sea-land boundaries separating dry-wet beaches in the intertidal zone. The shoreline change rate has been calculated using the statistical tool Digital Shoreline Analysis System (DSAS). The shoreline position after 100 years has been derived for the erosion sites based on the rate of shoreline change calculated for 29 years.

Estimation of CMZ

The extent of the coastal multi-hazard zone (CMZ) was estimated based on the total water level [(extreme water level + sea level rise in 100 years) rounded off to the next whole number] by the bathtub method, using the high-resolution DEMs. The areas eroding in the next 100 years also merged with CMZs. The areas eroding in the next 100 years are predominantly falling within the extents of the total water levels. The CMZ is a low-elevation coastal zone exposed to flooding originating from the ocean due to rising sea level and coastal erosion, with a 100-year recurrence interval. The inundation levels considered in the current study, based on extreme water levels and sea level rise projections for 100 years at different tide gauge locations, are presented in Table 2.

The validation of the CMZ was carried out based on the earlier cyclone events, Phailin (2013) and Hudhud (2014) cyclones4. The surge heights were validated with a similar scenario of CMZ extents. It was observed that the extent of inundations was in good agreement along the open coasts. However, the results were not matched well in the vicinity of the river/creek mouths.

Classification of the CMZ

Further, this CMZ is categorised into four levels based on the coastal elevation using DEM generated from the datasets mentioned in Table 1. Level-1 is the area below 1 m, Level-2 is the area between 1 and 2 m, Level-3 is the area between 2 and 3 m and Level-4 is the area above 3 m. The purpose behind this classification is to categorize coastal zones into different degrees of exposure based on elevation. For example, the area below 1 m (Level-1) is highly exposed and experiences intense conditions during high-amplitude events and frequent inundations even from low-amplitude coastal flooding events. These four levels of the exposure categories were intersected with all the towns and villages of the east coast of India falling under the exposure zone i.e., CMZ. Calculation of village-level exposure index

The smallest administrative boundaries in India are villages and towns, which are local self-governments. Hence, exposure assessment at these levels is appropriate in order to manage and mitigate coastal hazards by local governing bodies. In this regard, the village boundaries obtained from the open sources available from livingatlas-dcdev.opendata.arcgis.com were downloaded. The corresponding village’s population (Census of India, 2011) available with the village boundaries are utilized to estimate the exposed population. The villages and towns of the exposure zone were separated, and geospatial overlay analysis was performed on the exposure categories to estimate the percentage of the villages’ geographic area falling under different exposure categories. Exposure Index (EI) at each village/town is derived using Eq. (1) given below41. The rationale for assigning differential weightage for calculating EI is to appropriately reflect variations in inundation risk. Very low-lying areas, which are more susceptible to higher inundation depths and frequencies, have been assigned a higher weightage of 4. In contrast, relatively elevated areas that experience lower inundation levels and less frequent flooding have been assigned a lower weightage of 1. Though both these scenarios experience the inundation but cannot treated equally, as level and frequency of inundation differs.

Where ‘a’ is the percentage of exposed areas below 1 m elevation (Level-1) in a village/town, ‘b’, ‘c’ and ‘d’ are the percentage of exposed areas within 1–2 m (Level-2), 2–3 m (Level-3) and greater than 3 m elevation (Level-4), respectively.

The resultant EI values of all the villages/towns are divided into 4 classes: ‘Very High’; ‘High’; ‘Moderate’ and ‘Low’ using natural breaks. The natural breaks approach is data driven which optimize class intervals to capture the spatial patterns to make better thematic map representation. It also suits for the skewed datasets and commonly used in vulnerability and risk mapping. The legend in all maps clarifying that the Exposure Index (EI) is dimensionless is a key component of the exposure categories. This classification will highlight villages susceptible to inundation from oceanic hazards and those at risk due to varying degrees of exposure. The entire boundary is treated as a single unit while calculating the exposure that includes the habituated and open areas within these boundaries.

Results

The study has brought out a comprehensive assessment of the area along the coastal zones exposed to the oceanogenic hazards. The findings of the current study have facilitated the identification of coastal villages in the Indian mainland that fall under the CMZ, which is a low-lying coastal zone exposed to oceanic multi-hazards 1 in 100-year recurrence. The total exposed population of the East Coast is 38.35 million as per the 2011 census data. Out of the population along the east coast, 59.3% population associated in villages and 40.7% in towns. It is recorded that a total of 11,414 villages and 279 towns are under CMZ along the east coast of India from Tamil Nadu to West Bengal (Table 3). Within coastal exposure zones, a significant portion of the population is concentrated in rural communities.

Discussions

The Exposure Index (EI) along the coastal zones estimated in this study provides a quantitative measure of the degree to which coastal areas, populations, infrastructure, and ecosystems are situated in the path of potential coastal hazards such as storm surges, erosion, sea-level rise, and tsunamis. Exposure reflects what is at stake, independent of its fragility or resilience. Vulnerability is the degree to which elements (humans, livestock, infrastructure, etc.) situated in the exposure zone are susceptible to harm.

The exposure assessment of the east coast of India reveals varying degrees of exposure index across states, with certain districts facing significantly higher risks. In Tamil Nadu, Nagapattinam, Thiruvarur and Thanjavur show maximum exposure, with a considerable number of villages falling under ‘very high’ and ‘high’ EI. Andhra Pradesh exhibits widespread exposure, particularly in Krishna, SPSR Nellore and East Godavari, where a large number of settlements are categorized as highly vulnerable and similar results were reported by4,37. Odisha has some of the most exposed districts, including Jagatsinghpur, Bhadrak, Kendrapara and Puri, with a significant proportion of villages under ‘very high’ EI due to low-lying coastal topography. In addition, these areas are highly vulnerable to inundation, which experiences severe loss due to frequent cyclones. Whereas, both 24 Parganas districts stand out with the highest population under exposure, while Medinipur East also shows extensive vulnerability32. The outcomes of this study are strongly supported by earlier coastal vulnerability assessments conducted across the east coast of India. Pramanik et al.20, coastal vulnerability index for the Krishna–Godavari delta highlighted the high susceptibility of deltaic districts to sea-level rise, a pattern that closely parallels the elevated exposure observed in the present analysis. A similar agreement is found with the findings of Bharadwaz et al.29, who identified Kakinada Urban, and surrounding mandals as severely affected by climate-related hazards that likewise emerge as high-exposure clusters in this study. For Odisha, the results correspond strongly with Mani Murali et al.33, who identified Kendrapara and Jagatsinghpur coasts as highly vulnerable, and with the work of Das and Das34, who reported extreme susceptibility in Puri, Jagatsinghpur, Baleswar, Bhadrak, and Kendrapara districts that also show dense concentrations of high and very-high exposure villages in the present assessment. Along the Tamil Nadu and Puducherry coasts, the spatial patterns mirror those reported by Arun and Kunte (2012), who identified Ennore and Chennai as high-CVI zones, and by Arokiyadoss et al.35, whose findings similarly emphasized vulnerability in Cuddalore, Nagapattinam, Mayiladuthurai, Chennai, and Puducherry. The assessment by Paul and Das36 reported the Sundarbans Islands and East Midnapur as very highly vulnerable agrees with the current study, which also reveals extensive clusters of villages falling under the high and very-high exposure categories in these regions. Collectively, the strong correspondence between previous research and the present exposure-index outcomes reinforces the robustness, validity, and regional relevance of this assessment across the entire east coast of India.

The following policy interventions, among others, may be considered as key adaptation measures for reducing coastal vulnerability. Strengthening national initiatives such as Integrated Coastal Zone Management Plans (ICZMP) and National Coastal Missions (NCM) is essential to ensure coordinated, science-based coastal governance. Structural (hard) protection measures including sea walls, geo-tubes, and offshore breakwaters may be constructed in open, low-lying coastal stretches where risk exposure is high and socio-economic assets are significant. In parallel, soft or nature-based solutions should be promoted wherever feasible. These include the development of coastal green belts, mangrove restoration, coral reef rehabilitation, and beach nourishment programs, which enhance natural buffering capacity while supporting ecosystem services. Hydrological interventions such as the installation of flood gates at river mouths and the construction or regulation of upstream dams may help reduce downstream flooding and storm surge impacts, provided that basin-scale environmental impacts are carefully assessed. Strict enforcement of Coastal Zone Regulations is necessary to restrict new development in highly exposed areas. This should be complemented by mandatory compliance with resilient building codes and the implementation of targeted protective infrastructure designed to withstand projected coastal hazards. Finally, strengthening early warning systems and ensuring effective last-mile dissemination are critical for minimizing disaster losses. Continuous monitoring of coastal zones through scientific assessments, hazard mapping, and shoreline change analysis is equally important to support adaptive management and long-term risk reduction.

Limitations of the study

The current study provides a holistic estimation of the CMZ extent using a bathtub approach applied to high-resolution DEMs. The composite inundation levels are derived solely from extreme water levels and relative sea-level rise based on tide-gauge data at each location. However, this approach neither accounts for compound flooding processes (that occur in marine–fluvial transition zones, such as river mouths/deltas) nor natural defences. The sea level rise has been calculated using the GLOSS data available up to 2014 and the subsidence and upliftment factors are not considered in the current study.

Conclusions

The study highlights that a substantial extent of the eastern coastline is exposed to oceanic hazards along the East Coast, especially in the vicinity of the deltas and creeks such as the Sundarbans, Mahanadi, the Krishna-Godavari and Point Calimere. However, areas with higher concentrations of inhabited villages and towns falling under elevated exposure levels were systematically identified and quantified for each coastal district. The top two vulnerable districts in each state are Nagapattinam and Thiruvarur in Tamil Nadu, Krishna, SPSR Nellore in Andhra Pradesh, Jagatsinghpur and Bhadrak in Odisha and the North and South 24 Parganas in West Bengal. These results will facilitate the state-level administration in prioritizing targeted management and mitigation interventions.

The current study has demonstrated a systematic assessment of the impact of the extreme ocean waves that inundate coastal zones and the implications of climate change at the village/town level based on available data. In this regard, an accurate high-resolution Digital Elevation Model derived from Airborne LiDAR terrain mapping (ALTM) has been used in the study. The use of ALTM-derived elevation data significantly improved the precision of coastal exposure delineation across multiple hazard levels. These exposure levels were further objectively translated into individual villages using a weightage approach for each exposure level to generate a meaningful exposure index. Furthermore, the study recorded a district-wise profile on the percentage of coastal communities exposed to different EIs. This study has effectively estimated the spatial variation of low-lying areas and their risks from coastal flooding, erosion and sea level rise at the village level, which emphasize the importance of developing targeted disaster risk management strategies, particularly in districts with greater vulnerability as well as with high population densities and large areas classified under different EI to enhance resilience against coastal hazards. In this direction, outcomes of the current study provide vital baseline information on the total number of villages/towns recorded in the different EI and cascading implications on communities living in these regions. The EI derived in this work helps policy-makers to draw site-specific interventions and disaster managers to focus on preparedness and adaptive measures to mitigate risks associated with coastal hazards in order to build resilient coastal communities.

This study uses available Census 2011 data, which may limit in reflecting current demographic conditions. But the study focuses on the calculation of the exposure at village levels, the core spatial exposure outcomes remain largely unaffected. The overall relative demographic exposure presented herein remains indicative, even though the population in towns varies due to migration. Overall, the methodological framework is scalable, transferable, and cross-sectoral. With the incorporation of finer-resolution socio-economic and infrastructure datasets, it has strong potential to evolve into an integrated coastal risk assessment tool capable of informing long-term climate adaptation and resilience planning at regional and national scales37–39.
