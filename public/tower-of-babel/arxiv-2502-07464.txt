Effective description of Taylor dispersion in strongly corrugated channels
Arthur Alexandre, Thomas Guérin, David S. Dean

Abstract
We consider Taylor dispersion in periodic but highly corrugated channels. Exact analytical expressions for the long-time diffusion constant and drift along the channel are derived to next-to-leading order in the limit of small channel period. Using these results, we show how an effective model for Taylor dispersion in porous media with tortuous pores can be framed in terms of dispersion in a uniform channel with absorption/desorption at its surface, an effective slip length for the flow at the surface and an effective, universal, diffusion constant on the surface. This work thus extends the concept of an effective slip-length for hydrodynamics flows to Taylor dispersion by those flows. The analytical results are confirmed by numerical calculations, and present a robust method to understand and upscale the transport properties of flows in pore like geometries .

A similar, but much less studied, question arises as to whether one can define effective boundary conditions for the advection-diffusion of particles moving near a surface presenting protrusions as shown in Figs.
1

Clearly, when trapped in a protrusion tracer dispersion parallel to the channel is reduced. It is natural to ask if this reduction can be captured using a model of a uniform channel with a sticky surface, that is to say a surface having absorption/desorption of particles (corresponding to entering/leaving a protrusion), as well as an effective surface diffusion constant (modeling the fact that there is still some dispersion along the channel due to the finite width of the protrusions).

However, for channels with a generic shape, for which protrusions are not necessarily thin, the mapping onto a flat sticky channel is not obvious since particles in the entrance of the protrusion zones are clearly not completely immobile nor completely mobile.

Here, we show how, in the limit of small period, an effective model can be derived which takes into account this effect, and we derive the precise parameters of the effective model depend on the geometry of the protrusions. This means that, in a way similar to the description of the effective electrical properties of complex circuits in terms of simpler ones, the transport in this problem can be described by a cylindrical (or planar) geometry [Fig.

As well as its underlying physical interest, the theory here clearly has potential to be used for upscaling methods for transport in tortuous porous media,
where the effect of dead ends (in which the flow cannot penetrate) could be taken into account via effective (and potentially spatially varying) adsorption and desorption coefficients as well as effective surface diffusivity.

II
Physical problem

Consider a point-like tracer particle diffusing with microscopic isotropic diffusivity
D
and advected by incompressible flow
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle v=\frac{\overline{[x(t)-x(0)]}}{t},\ D_{e}\underset{t\to\infty}{\simeq}\frac{\overline{[x(t)-x(0)-vt]^{2}}}{2t},

(1)

where

denotes the ensemble average over stochastic trajectories. The average local drift is

\Omega
the volume of one period of the channel. To calculate the effective diffusivity, many approaches have been proposed, such as the homogenization approach
[
42

30

43
,
28
,
29
]
, macrotransport theory
[

4

[

46

\displaystyle D_{e}=D-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}t\ \overline{[V_{x}(\mathbf{r}(t))-\overline{V_{x}}][V_{x}^{*}(\mathbf{r}(0))-\overline{V_{x}^{*}}]}.

(2)

Here,
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{u}+D\,\mathbf{n}\,\delta_{s}(\mathbf{r})
is the local drift, where

(see Appendix
A

defined such that
\int_{\Omega}\mbox{d}\mathbf{r}\,\delta_{s}(\mathbf{r})g(\mathbf{r})=\int_{\partial\Omega}\mbox{d}S\,g(\mathbf{r})

is the drift
after time reversal

and are submitted to the same obstacles. The correlation function in
Eq.

2
can then be calculated by defining an auxiliary field

as

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{r})=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}t\int_{\Omega}\mbox{d}\mathbf{r}_{0}\ P(\mathbf{r},t|\mathbf{r}_{0})[V_{x}^{*}(\mathbf{r}_{0})-\overline{V_{x}^{*}}],

(3)

where
P(\mathbf{r},t|\mathbf{r}_{0})
is the probability density of observing
\mathbf{r}
(modulo the period) at
t
, starting from
\mathbf{r}_{0}
. This probability density
satisfies the Fokker-Planck equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P=-\nabla\cdot[\mathbf{u}\,P-D\nabla P],

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}\cdot[\mathbf{u}\,P-D\nabla P]_{\mathbf{r}\in\partial\Omega}=0

(4)

and reaches a steady state which is uniform due to the incompressibility of the flow,
P(\mathbf{r},t\to\infty)=1/\Omega
. With the definition
(
3
) of

2

\displaystyle D_{e}

\displaystyle=D+\frac{1}{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\mbox{d}\mathbf{r}\ (u_{x}f-D\partial_{x}f).

(5)

Furthermore, one can show that the auxiliary field
f(\mathbf{r})
satisfies a partial differential equation. Clearly, using the Fokker-Planck equation (
4

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot

\displaystyle[\mathbf{u}\,f(\mathbf{r})]+D\,\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{r})=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}t\int_{\Omega}\mbox{d}\mathbf{r}_{0}\,\partial_{t}P(\mathbf{r},t|\mathbf{r}_{0})\,[V_{x}^{*}(\mathbf{r}_{0})-\overline{V_{x}^{*}}].

(6)

The integration over
t
can be performed, leading to

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot

\displaystyle(\mathbf{u}f)+D\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\mbox{d}\mathbf{r}_{0}\left[\frac{1}{\Omega}-\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0})\right][V_{x}^{*}(\mathbf{r}_{0})-\overline{V_{x}^{*}}]

\displaystyle=-u_{x}(\mathbf{r})+\overline{u_{x}}+D\,\mathbf{n}\,\cdot\mathbf{e}_{x}\,\delta_{s}(\mathbf{r}).

(7)

If
\mathbf{r}
is in the bulk, the delta-surface term vanishes and one obtains

\displaystyle-\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla f+D\nabla^{2}f=v-u_{x}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{r}\in\Omega).

(8)

To find the boundary condition for
f
, we argue that
P(\mathbf{r},t)
, and therefore
f
vanishes for all
\mathbf{r}
inside the obstacles. Therefore, if one integrates

7
over a small volume that includes a portion of the surface, taking into account the fact that
\mathbf{u}
vanishes at the boundary (the no-slip boundary condition),
one finds directly

\displaystyle D\ \mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla f=D\ \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{x}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{r}\in\partial\Omega),

(9)

where

In addition, it is clear from

\int_{\Omega}\mbox{d}\mathbf{r}f(\mathbf{r})=0

The flow field is assumed to be a steady Stokes flow

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0,

\displaystyle\eta\ \nabla^{2}\mathbf{u}-\nabla\Pi=0,

(10)

where
\Pi
the pressure field and

the fluid viscosity. At the boundaries, the flow vanishes. Since the channel is periodic, the flow is also periodic, and so is the pressure gradient. As a consequence, we can define a parameter
(\nabla\Pi)_{\infty}
, which sets the magnitude of the flow, so that
\Pi(\mathbf{r}+L\mathbf{e}_{x})=\Pi(\mathbf{r})+(\nabla\Pi)_{\infty}L
.
While the case of weakly varying channels has been studied analytically at length

III
Analytical solution for the strong corrugation limit

For fast varying channel
L/a\ll 1
, where
a=\text{min}[h(x)]
, our method of solution relies on identifying the asymptotic behavior of
\mathbf{u}
and
f
in three different regions, and using the matched asymptotic expansion method. First, we define the central region (“
c
”) as the region where
|y|<a
, and the peripheral regions (“
p
”) as those with
a<|y|<h(x)
, corresponding to the locations in the lateral protrusions. Here
y
is the distance to the central axis. We also define an
inner
region that connects these peripheral and the central regions at the length scale
L
, which is small in the limit considered here. For the flow, we expect that in the central region it tends to a finite Poiseuille flow for vanishing
L
, while it vanishes in the lateral pores (as will be justified later), and it may be weak (of order
L/a
) in the inner region, so that its structure reads

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\underset{L\to 0}{\simeq}\begin{cases}\mathbf{u}_{0}(X,y)+L\mathbf{u}_{1}(X,y)+...&[y<a],\\
L\ \mathbf{u}^{*}(X,Y)+...&[Y=\mathcal{O}(1)],\\
0&[y>a],\end{cases}

(11)

where we have defined the rescaled coordinates

\displaystyle X=x/L,

\displaystyle Y=(y-a)/L.

(12)

For the function
f
, we assume the following series expansion in each region

\displaystyle f=\begin{cases}\sum_{n\geq 0}L^{n}f_{n}^{c}(X,y)&[y<a],\\
f_{0}^{*}(X,Y)+Lf_{1}^{*}(X,Y)+...&[Y=\mathcal{O}(1)],\\
\sum_{n\geq 0}L^{n}f_{n}^{p}(X,y)&[y>a].\end{cases}

(13)

The solutions in each region are found by inserting the above
ansätze
into the equations for
f
and
\mathbf{u}
, solving the resulting equations, and requiring that the solutions can be matched. For example, we require that
f_{0}^{*}(X,Y\to\infty)=f_{0}^{p}(X,a)
,
f_{0}^{*}(X,Y\to-\infty)=f_{0}^{c}(X,a)
,
\partial_{Y}f_{1}^{*}(X,Y\to+\infty)=\partial_{y}f_{0}^{p}(X,a)
, etc
[
48
]
.

We now describe the solution in
d=2
dimensions, all results will be generalized to
d=3
in the SM
[
47
]
.
For the flow, at leading order in the central region, it is readily identified to be the Poiseuille flow induced by the pressure gradient with effective vanishing velocities at
y=a
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{0}=U\left(1-\frac{y^{2}}{a^{2}}\right)\mathbf{e}_{x},

\displaystyle U=-\frac{a^{2}(\nabla\Pi)_{\infty}}{2\eta}.

(14)

Then, in the inner region, the flow satisfies

\displaystyle\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{u}^{*}=0,

\displaystyle\eta\tilde{\nabla}^{2}\mathbf{u}^{*}-\tilde{\nabla}\Pi^{*}=0,

(15)

where
\tilde{\nabla}=\mathbf{e}_{x}\partial_{X}+\mathbf{e}_{y}\partial_{Y}
and
\Pi=L\Pi^{*}
in the inner region. Furthermore,
\mathbf{u}^{*}(X,Y)
vanishes for
Y>0
and
X=-1/2+n
, with
n
integers, and far from the inner region one must recover the flow in the central region, so that
\mathbf{u}^{*}(X,Y\to-\infty)\simeq-\frac{2U}{a}Y\mathbf{e}_{x}
. The problem for
\mathbf{u}^{*}
is therefore formally equivalent to that of a two dimensional flow next to a periodic array of parallel semi-infinite lines, with a constant shear far from the plates. This problem has been solved independently in papers by Luchini
[
49
]
and Jeong
[
33
]
in which the explicit form of
\mathbf{u}^{*}
can be found. Note that, for
Y\to\infty
, the flow takes the form of a series of eddies whose magnitude decreases exponentially with
Y
, which means that we can safely consider that the flow vanishes at leading order in the lateral pores, as anticipated in
Eq.

11
. These eddies can actually be observed on Fig.
1
(b).
For
Y\to-\infty
, the flow becomes

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{*}(X,Y\to-\infty)=\frac{2U}{a}\left(-Y+\frac{\beta}{2}\right)\mathbf{e}_{x},

(16)

with
\beta\simeq 0.1772
, which means that there is an effective slip velocity
u_{s}=UL\beta/a
at the walls; this phenomenon is well known as the reduction in drag by surface roughness, and the flow at first order in the central region is
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{0}+u_{s}\mathbf{e}_{x}
. This enables one to define an effective slip length

\displaystyle b=\frac{u_{s}}{|\partial_{y}u_{x}|_{y=a}}=\frac{\beta L}{2},

(17)

which is an intrinsic property of the local surface geometry since it neither depends on the flow nor on the width of the channel. We now apply the same approach to find parameters describing effective boundary conditions for the transport.

Next, the equations for
f_{i}
in the central and the peripheral regions are recurrence equations, here written for
n=0,1,2,3
:

\displaystyle D(\partial_{X}^{2}f_{n}^{w}+\partial_{y}^{2}f_{n-2}^{w})

\displaystyle-\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}u_{x,m}\partial_{X}f_{n-1-m}^{w}=v_{n-2}-u_{x,n-2},

(18)

\displaystyle[(\partial_{X}h)(\partial_{X}f_{n}^{p}-\delta_{n,1})-\partial_{y}f_{n-2}^{p}]_{y=h(X)}=0

(19)

(all terms with negative indices are by convention zero), with
w\in\{c,p\}
and
v=\sum_{n\geq 0}v_{n}L^{n}
. In the inner region,
f^{*}
satisfies the Laplace problem

\displaystyle(\partial_{X}^{2}+\partial_{Y}^{2})f_{n}^{*}=0,

\displaystyle(\partial_{X}f_{n}^{*})_{X=\pm 1/2;Y>0}=\delta_{n,1}.

(20)

In Appendix
B
, we find the solution of these equations, for successive values of
n
, via complex analysis for
Eq.

20
(generalizing the analysis of
[
50
]
, without flow, to our situation where the matching conditions are modified by the flow). The diffusivity is finally given by

\displaystyle D_{e}

\displaystyle=\frac{Da+DL(\ln 2)/\pi}{a+\delta}

\displaystyle+\frac{4U^{2}a^{2}}{9D(a+\delta)^{3}}\Bigg\{\frac{17a\delta^{2}}{35}+\frac{6a^{2}\delta}{35}+\frac{2a^{3}}{105}+\tau D\delta\Bigg\}

\displaystyle+\frac{4a^{2}Uu_{s}}{45D(a+\delta)^{3}}\Bigg\{6a\delta^{2}+a^{2}\delta+15D\tau\delta\Bigg\},

(21)

with

\delta=\langle h-a\rangle,\,\tau=\int_{a}^{h_{m}}\frac{dy}{\delta DW(y)}\bigg[\int_{y}^{h_{m}}dy^{\prime}W(y^{\prime})\bigg]^{2},

(22)

where
\langle g(X)\rangle=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}dXg(X)
is the uniform average over the period and
W(y)
is the width of the lateral pore at distance
y>a
from the channel center (divided by
L
).

IV
Correspondence with uniform channels

IV.1
Uniform channel with sticky boundaries

In order to obtain a physical interpretation of the parameters
\tau
and
\delta
, we

now consider a model in which the particle diffuses between two
flat
boundaries which are sticky, with attachment and detachment rates
k_{a}
and
k_{d}
respectively. In the bulk of the channel, particles diffuse with diffusion coefficient
D
in a velocity field
u_{e}(y)\mathbf{e}_{x}
, while on the surface they diffuse with surface diffusion coefficient
D_{s}
. In this model, the bulk and surface probability density functions
p_{b}
and
p_{s}
obey

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{b}=-u_{e}(y)\partial_{x}p_{b}+D(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})p_{b}

\displaystyle(|y|<a),

(23)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{s}=D_{s}\partial_{x}^{2}p_{s}-k_{d}p_{s}+k_{a}p_{b},

\displaystyle(|y|=a)

(24)

and the boundary condition
D\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla p_{b}=k_{a}p_{b}-k_{d}p_{s}
at
y=\pm a
ensures that the total probability is conserved. Dispersion in such kind of models has been widely studied
[
51
,
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
]
.
Using the formulas of Ref.
[
51
]
, we show in Appendix
C
that
D_{e}
for this model with flat sticky walls is exactly
Eq.

21
, as soon as we identify the following parameters

\displaystyle u_{e}=u_{x}^{(0)}(y)+u_{s},\,

\displaystyle D_{s}=\frac{DL\ln 2}{\pi\delta},

(25)

\displaystyle\delta=\frac{k_{a}}{k_{d}},\,

\displaystyle\tau=k_{d}^{-1}.

(26)

The length
\delta
can be identified as the adsorption length for the effective model by the following simple argument.
In the model with flat sticky walls, the fraction of time that a particle spends in the bound state is clearly
\mu=\delta/(a+\delta)
, while in the full problem the fraction of time spent in the lateral regions is
\mu=\langle h-a\rangle/\langle h\rangle
, because the steady state probability density function is uniform due to the flow incompressibility. Comparing these two expressions for
\mu
leads to
\delta=\langle h-a\rangle
.
Note that this length
\delta
is the area of the pores divided by
L
, so that it does not depend on the dimension
a
of the open part of the channel.
Next, the expression of
\tau
can be rationalized by noting that
\tau
is the average first escape time out of a lateral pore, starting with equilibrium initial condition in the pore. Indeed, when it is in a lateral pore, for small
L
, the motion of the particle is effectively a one-dimensional diffusion in an effective entropic potential
\varphi(y)=-k_{\text{B}}T\ln W(y)
, with
k_{\text{B}}
the Boltzmann’s constant and
T

the temperature. Then the mean first escape time
\tau
to escape at
y=a
starting from an equilibrium distribution in the lateral pore is known and is exactly
Eq.

22
, see Eq. (2.20) in Ref.
[
56
]
. Finally, the effective surface diffusivity
D_{s}
in
Eq.

26
takes into account the fact that particles in the vicinity of the line
y=a
are not completely immobile.

IV.2
Uniform channel with anisotropic diffusion

Alternatively, all terms of the effective diffusivity (
22
) can also be obtained by considering a particle diffusing in a uniform channel with anisotropic diffusivity. More specifically, we can approximate the motion in the longitudinal direction
x(t)
in the real channel as satisfying the stochastic differential equation

\mbox{d}x_{t}=\sqrt{2D_{\parallel}(y_{t})}\,\mbox{d}B_{\parallel,t}+u_{e}(y_{t})\theta(a-|y|)\,\mbox{d}t,

(27)

where
y_{t}
is the distance to the central axis and
\langle\mbox{d}B_{\parallel,t}^{2}\rangle=\mbox{d}t
. Here, we have

D_{\parallel}(y)=D\theta(a+\ell-|y|),

(28)

where
\theta
is the Heaviside step function. This means that the particle only diffuses when it is in the channel part
|y|<a+\ell
and we ignore its dispersion in the interior of the lobe or corrugated part, which is a valid approximation for the form of strong corrugation used here. The length
\ell
is a
diffusive incursion length
representing the distance from the protrusion entrance under which the particle, while it is still inside the protrusions, can nevertheless be considered as undergoing free diffusion along the channel. Similarly, the drift vanishes when
|y|>a
. The process in the lateral direction
y_{t}
obeys

\displaystyle\mbox{d}y_{t}=\sqrt{2D}\,\mbox{d}B_{\perp,t}+\frac{D}{k_{\text{B}}T}\varphi^{\prime}(y_{t})\,\mbox{d}t,

(29)

\displaystyle\varphi(y)=\begin{cases}-k_{\text{B}}T\ln[(y/a)^{d-2}W(y)]&(|y|>a),\\
-k_{\text{B}}T\ln[(y/a)^{d-2}]&(|y|<a),\end{cases}

(30)

where
\langle\mbox{d}B_{\perp,t}^{2}\rangle=\mbox{d}t
,
W(y)L
is the longitudinal distance between the pore’s wall at distance
y
from the center (divided by
L
). The above equation is valid for
d=2
or
d=3
and takes into account the fact that the number of configurations at fixed
y
is
\omega(y)=y^{d-2}W(y)
(up to a multiplicative factor), giving rise to an entropic potential
\varphi(y)=-k_{\text{B}}T\ln\omega=-k_{\text{B}}T\ln y^{d-2}W(y)
which couples a Fick-Jacobs approximation for the motion in the lateral direction, and the fact that
y
is the radial part of a Brownian motion. The above equation is derived for
|y|>a
and is trivially extended for
|y|<a
where the part due to
W(y)
is irrelevant and diffusion is free.

This approximate problem is therefore just an effective Taylor dispersion problem in a channel of height
h_{m}=\max(h)
with spatially dependent anisotropic diffusion tensor. The effective diffusivity for this problem is given in Ref.
[
57
]
as

\displaystyle D_{e}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}yD_{\parallel}(y)p_{e}(y)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y\frac{\left\{\int_{y}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y_{1}p_{e}(y_{1})[u_{e}(y_{1})-v]\right\}^{2}}{D_{\perp}(y)p_{e}(y)},

(31)

where
p_{e}(y)
is the stationary distribution of
|y|
,

\displaystyle p_{e}(y)=\frac{e^{-\varphi(y)/k_{\text{B}}T}}{\int_{0}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y_{1}e^{-\varphi(y_{1})/k_{\text{B}}T}},

(32)

and
v=\int dyp_{e}(y)u_{e}(y)
the average longitudinal velocity. Note that here we consider the line
y=0
as reflecting for the particle, this does not change the value of the final diffusivity. Applying these formulas for
u_{e}=[U(1-y^{2}/a^{2})+u_{s}]\theta(a-|y|)
and
d=2
, we show in Appendix
D
that the diffusivity (
31

21
) at order

VI
Generalization to 3D axisymmetric channels

Our approach can be generalized to the case of three-dimensional axisymetric channels, calculation details can be found in Appendix
E
. We find that, in the limit of strongly corrugated channels, the flow in the central region it is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=U(1-y^{2}/a^{2}+\beta L/a)\ {\mathbf{e}}_{x}+\mathcal{O}(L^{2}),

(34)

where
U=-a^{2}(\nabla\Pi)_{\infty}/(2\eta)
. The diffusivity in the strong corrugation limit reads

\displaystyle D_{e}=

\displaystyle\frac{Da}{a+2\delta}+\frac{DL2\ln 2}{\pi\,(a+2\delta)}

\displaystyle+\frac{U^{2}a^{2}\left(a^{3}+12a^{2}\delta+44a\delta^{2}+96D\tau\delta\right)}{192D\,(a+2\delta)^{3}}

\displaystyle+\beta LaU^{2}\frac{a^{2}\delta+8a\delta^{2}+24D\delta\tau}{12D\,(a+2\delta)^{3}},

(35)

where

\displaystyle\langle h^{2}\rangle=a^{2}+2a\delta,

(36)

\displaystyle\tau=\frac{1}{D\delta a}\int_{a}^{h_{m}}dy\frac{\left(\int_{y}^{h_{m}}dy^{\prime}W(y^{\prime})y^{\prime}\right)^{2}}{W(y)y},

(37)

Equation (
35
) corresponds to the result obtained for flat sticky walls in Ref.
[
51
]
(when one neglects the slip velocity), when one identifies
\delta=k_{a}/k_{d}
;
\tau=1/k_{d}
, and the effective surface diffusivity
D_{s}=\frac{DL\ln 2}{\pi\delta}
. Note that
\tau
is again interpreted as the escape time out of a lateral pore.
Alternatively, one can also compare with the model of uniform channels of Section
IV.2
, this leads to the diffusive incursion length
\ell=L\ln 2/\pi
, exactly as in the 2D case, just as the effective slip length
b=\beta L/2
for the flow is the same for 2D and 3D channels.

VII
Conclusion

We have studied the dispersion of particles in a channel in the strongly corrugated limit (small period limit) in the presence of a pressure driven flow. In this limit, all standard approximations (Fick-Jacobs, lubrication) fail, and we have found an explicit formula for effective transport coefficients which is exact at next-to-leading order in
L
. At this order, we find that transport here is equivalent to transport in a perfeclty flat but partially sticky tube. The effective attachement and detachment parameters are explicitly determined in terms of the geometry of the lateral regions, and of the exit time out of the lateral pores. Furthermore, the analysis at next-to-leading order in
L
enables us to see that (i) the effective flow is non-zero in the vicinity of the entrance to the lateral pore, and (ii) that when in a lateral branch the dispersion of particles along the longitudinal direction is not completely suppressed due to the finite size of the pore entrance. The first point (i) leads to the emergence of an effective slip length, while the second (ii) leads to an effective surface diffusivity that is determined by a universal numerical constant, or equivalently to the definition of the length at which the particles at the entrance of protrusions can nevertheless be considered as mobile.

Appendix A
Identification of the stochastic equation with surface terms

Here, we briefly explain how to show that the effective drift, taking into account the influence of boundaries, is

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{u}+D\,\mathbf{n}\,\delta_{s}(\mathbf{r}),

(38)

meaning that the stochastic differential equation (SDE) for the position
\mathbf{r}_{t}
of the particle at time
t
can be written as

\displaystyle\mbox{d}\mathbf{r}_{t}=\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{r}_{t})\,\mbox{d}t+\sqrt{2D}\,\mbox{d}B_{t},

\displaystyle\overline{\mbox{d}B_{t}}=0,\ \overline{\mbox{d}B_{t}^{2}}=\mbox{d}t.

(39)

Let us identify the generator
G
, defined as the operator such that the evolution of any test function
\phi(\mathbf{r})
reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\overline{\phi}=\overline{G\phi},

(40)

and it is related to the SDE by

\displaystyle G=\mathbf{V}\cdot\nabla+D\nabla^{2}.

(41)

Using the Fokker-Planck equation (
4
), we find

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\overline{\phi}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\int_{\Omega}\mbox{d}\mathbf{r}\,P(\mathbf{r},t)\phi(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mbox{d}\mathbf{r}\,\phi(\mathbf{r})\left\{-\nabla\cdot[\mathbf{u}\,P-D\nabla P]\right\}.

(42)

Using integration by parts (divergence theorem) and the boundary conditions for
P
, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\overline{\phi}=\int_{\Omega}\mbox{d}\mathbf{r}\left\{P(\mathbf{r},t)(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla+D\nabla^{2})\phi(\mathbf{r})\right\}

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Omega}\mbox{d}S(\mathbf{r})\mathbf{n}\cdot DP(\mathbf{r},t)\nabla\phi.

(43)

We can see the boundary term as a bulk term, so that we can identify the generator
G
as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\overline{\phi}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mbox{d}\mathbf{r}P(\mathbf{r},t)\left\{[\mathbf{u}+D\,\mathbf{n}\,\delta_{s}(\mathbf{r})]\cdot\nabla+D\nabla^{2}\right\}\phi(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\overline{G\phi},

(44)

so that
G
is given by
Eq.

41
, with an effective drift field
\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{r})
given by
Eq.

38
.

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{L}}\mbox{d}Y\partial_{X}[f^{*}(X,Y)-\partial_{X}f_{1}^{c}(X,a+YL)]

\displaystyle+\int_{a+\varepsilon}^{h(X)}\mbox{d}y\partial_{X}f_{2}^{p}\bigg\},

(100)

where we have used

in the boundary layer. Now, using
\partial_{X}f_{2}^{c}=\partial_{X}f_{1}^{c}=0
,
in the limit
L\to 0
, with
\varepsilon/L\gg 1
and
\varepsilon\ll a
, we obtain

\displaystyle J-J_{0}\simeq

\displaystyle L\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X\Bigg\{\int_{-\infty}^{0}\mbox{d}Y\partial_{X}f^{*}(X,Y)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}Y\partial_{X}[f^{*}(X,Y)-\partial_{X}f_{1}^{p}(X,a)]

\displaystyle+\int_{a}^{h(X)}\mbox{d}y\partial_{X}f_{2}^{p}\Bigg\}.

(101)

Noting that
\partial_{X}f_{1}^{p}(X,a)=1
, using the above expression to evaluate the integrals involving
\partial_{X}f
in
Eq.

91
, we obtain the terms
D_{B}
and
D_{C}
defined in
Eqs.

95
and
96
.

Using
Eqs.

76
,
78
and
86
, the integrals in the leading order term can be calculated, leading to

\displaystyle D_{e}^{(0)}=\frac{Da}{\langle h\rangle}+\frac{4U^{2}a^{2}}{9D\langle h\rangle^{3}}\Bigg\{\frac{17a\langle\eta\rangle^{2}}{35}+\frac{6a^{2}\langle\eta\rangle}{35}+\frac{2a^{3}}{105}

\displaystyle+\int_{a}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y\frac{\left(\int_{y}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y^{\prime}W(y^{\prime})\right)^{2}}{W(y)}\Bigg\},

(102)

where we have defined
\eta=h-a
.

Let us now calculate the terms for the diffusivity at next-to-leading order. First, using
Eq.

86
, we obtain

\displaystyle D_{A}=

\displaystyle-\frac{v_{0}\langle Xh(X)\rangle}{\langle h\rangle}+\frac{4aU^{2}\beta}{45D\langle h\rangle^{3}}\Bigg[6a\langle\eta\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle+a^{2}\langle\eta\rangle+15\int_{a}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y\frac{\left(\int_{y}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y^{\prime}W(y^{\prime})\right)^{2}}{W(y)}\Bigg].

(103)

Next, using
Eq.

55

\displaystyle D_{B}=\frac{(-1)}{\langle h\rangle}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X\int_{a}^{h(X)}\mbox{d}y\,\Big\{

\displaystyle[v_{0}-D(f_{0}^{p})^{\prime\prime}(y)]X

\displaystyle+\partial_{y}G_{2}^{p}(y)\Big\}.

(104)

Performing the integral over
y
, and using the property
\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}XX=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle D_{B}=-\frac{1}{\langle h\rangle}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X\Big\{

\displaystyle[v_{0}h(X)-D(f_{0}^{p})^{\prime}(h(X))]X

\displaystyle+G_{2}^{p}(h(X))-G_{2}^{p}(a)\Big\}.

(105)

Using
Eq.

61
, we can simplify this expression:

\displaystyle D_{B}=-\frac{1}{\langle h\rangle}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X

\displaystyle\Bigg[v_{0}h(X)X-G_{2}^{p}(a)

\displaystyle-v_{0}\int_{-1/2}^{X}\mbox{d}w\,w\,h^{\prime}(w)\Bigg].

(106)

Writing

61

X=-1/2
and
X=1/2
leads to

\displaystyle G_{2}^{p}(a)=-D(f_{0}^{p})^{\prime}(a)/2=-\frac{v_{0}\langle Xh^{\prime}(X)\rangle}{2}.

(107)

Using integrations by parts, we also note that

\displaystyle\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X

\displaystyle\int_{-1/2}^{X}\mbox{d}w\,w\,h^{\prime}(w)

\displaystyle=-\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}XX^{2}h^{\prime}(X)+\frac{\langle Xh^{\prime}(X)\rangle}{2}

\displaystyle=2\langle X(h(X)-a)\rangle+\frac{\langle Xh^{\prime}(X)\rangle}{2}.

(108)

As a consequence, we obtain for
D_{B}
(noting that, trivially,
\langle X\rangle=0

\displaystyle D_{B}=\frac{v_{0}}{\langle h\rangle}\langle h(X)X\rangle.

(109)

Last, the term
D_{C}
can be calculated by performing the integration over
X
in
Eq.

96

\displaystyle D_{C}=\frac{(-D)}{\langle h\rangle}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mbox{d}Y\left[f^{*}\left(\frac{1}{2},Y\right)-f^{*}\left(\frac{-1}{2},Y\right)-\theta(Y)\right].

(110)

Using the explicit value of
f^{*}
given by
Eq.

84
, we obtain

\displaystyle D_{C}

\displaystyle=-\frac{D}{\langle h\rangle}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mbox{d}Y\left[\frac{2}{\pi}\text{Arctan}\left(\sqrt{e^{2\pi Y}-1}\right)-1\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{D\ln 2}{\pi\,\langle h\rangle}.

(111)

Interestingly this is the same result as that computed without flow in Ref.
[
50

, although the function

itself is modified by the flow.

Collecting these results, we find our final expression for the effective diffusivity

\displaystyle D_{e}=

\displaystyle\frac{Da}{\langle h\rangle}+\frac{DL\ln 2}{\pi\,\langle h\rangle}+\frac{4U^{2}a^{2}}{9D\langle h\rangle^{3}}\Bigg\{\frac{17a\langle\eta\rangle^{2}}{35}+\frac{6a^{2}\langle\eta\rangle}{35}

\displaystyle+\frac{2a^{3}}{105}+\int_{a}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y\frac{\left(\int_{y}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y^{\prime}W(y^{\prime})\right)^{2}}{W(y)}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle+\frac{4aLU^{2}\beta}{45D\langle h\rangle^{3}}\Bigg\{6a\langle\eta\rangle^{2}+a^{2}\langle\eta\rangle

\displaystyle+15\int_{a}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y\frac{\left(\int_{y}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y_{1}W(y_{1})\right)^{2}}{W(y)}\Bigg\}.

(112)

Appendix C
Effective diffusivity for uniform 2D channels with sticky boundaries

Here we explain how the effective diffusivity for a flat channel with sticky walls can be obtained by using the results of Ref.
[
51
]
, where in 2D it was obtained that

\displaystyle D_{e}

\displaystyle=D_{b}P_{B}+D_{s}P_{S}

\displaystyle+\int_{-a}^{a}d{y}_{1}\int_{-a}^{a}d{y}_{2}u_{x}({y}_{1})u_{x}({y}_{2})B({y}_{1}|{y}_{2})p_{b}({y}_{2}),

(113)

with
P_{B}
the stationary probability to be observed in the bulk,
P_{S}
the stationary probability to be observed on the surfaces, and
B
defined by

\displaystyle B({y}_{1}|{y}_{2})=\frac{1}{2D_{b}(a+\delta)^{2}}\Bigg[\frac{a^{3}}{3}+a^{2}\delta+a\delta^{2}

\displaystyle-(a+\delta)^{2}|y_{1}-y_{2}|+(a+\delta)\frac{(y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2})}{2}\Bigg],

(114)

with
\delta=k_{a}/k_{d}
. Moreover, if
p_{b}(y,t)
is the marginal probability to observe the particle in the bulk at position
y
in the steady state, and
p_{s}^{+}
and
p_{s}^{-}
the probability to observe the particle attached to the upper (and lower) wall, respectively, one has

\displaystyle p_{b}k_{a}=p_{s}^{+}k_{d}=p_{s}^{-}k_{d},\ p_{s}^{+}=p_{s}^{-}=\frac{P_{S}}{2},\ P_{B}=2ap_{b},

(115)

from which it is easy to show that

\displaystyle p_{b}=\frac{1}{2(a+\delta)},

\displaystyle p_{s}^{+}=p_{s}^{-}=\frac{\delta}{2(a+\delta)},

(116)

\displaystyle P_{B}=\frac{a}{a+\delta},

\displaystyle P_{S}=\frac{\delta}{a+\delta}.

(117)

Applying
Eq.

113
with
u_{e}(y)=u_{x}^{(0)}(y)+Lu_{x}^{(1)}(y)
leads to Eq. (
21
) for the effective diffusivity in a planar channel.

Appendix D
Diffusivity for translationally invariant channel with anisotropic, spatially varying diffusivity

For
d=2
, the equilibrium probability (
32
) for the lateral variable

reads

\displaystyle p_{e}(y)=\frac{1}{\langle h\rangle}\times\begin{cases}W(y)&(|y|>a),\\
1&(|y|<a).\end{cases}

(118)

Noting that
u_{e}
vanishes for

reads

\displaystyle D_{e}=\frac{(a+\ell)D}{\langle h\rangle}+\frac{1}{D\langle h\rangle}\int_{a}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y\frac{\left\{v\int_{y}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y_{1}W(y_{1})\right\}^{2}}{W(y)}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{a}\frac{\mbox{d}y}{D\langle h\rangle}\left\{\int_{y}^{a}\mbox{d}y_{1}[u_{e}(y_{1})-v]-\int_{a}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y_{1}W(y_{1})v\right\}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(a+\ell)D}{\langle h\rangle}+\frac{1}{D\langle h\rangle}\int_{a}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y\frac{\left\{v\int_{y}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y_{1}W(y_{1})\right\}^{2}}{W(y)}

Using the form

.

Appendix E
Calculation details in 3D for axisymmetric channels

Here we explain how to generalize the calculation of the diffusivity to the case of a three-dimensional axisymmetric channel. The calculations here are essentially the same as those of Section
Appendix

B
but are presented in detail for the sake of clarity. Here we keep the notation
x
to represent the coordinate along the channel axis,
x=X/L
,
y>0
denotes the distance to the central axis, and
h(X)
is the local channel radius. Let us first consider the pressure-induced flow. As in 2D, the structure of the flow is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\underset{L\to 0}{\simeq}\begin{cases}\mathbf{u}_{0}(X,y)+L\mathbf{u}_{1}(X,y)+...&[y<a],\\
L\,\mathbf{u}^{*}(X,Y)+...&[y-a=\mathcal{O}(L)],\\
0&[y>a],\end{cases}

(120)

At leading order, the flow
\mathbf{u}_{0}
is a pressure induced flow in a uniform channel of radius
a
, since it depends only on
y
the Stokes equations take the form

\displaystyle\eta\left(\frac{1}{y}\partial_{y}(y\partial_{y}u_{x})+\partial_{x}^{2}u_{x}\right)-\partial_{x}\Pi=0.

(121)

This equation, with the boundary condition
\mathbf{u}_{0}(y=a)=0
is readily solved:

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{0}=U(1-y^{2}/a^{2})\,{\mathbf{e}}_{x},

\displaystyle U=-\frac{a^{2}(\nabla\Pi)_{\infty}}{2\eta}.

(122)

Next, near the entrance of the lateral regions, the field
\mathbf{u}^{*}(X,Y)
satisfies exactly the same equations as in the two-dimensional case, so that the effective slip velocity is still given by
u_{s}=UL\beta/a
. This leads to a flow at next-to-leading order which is uniform

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{1}=(U\beta/a)\,{\mathbf{e}}_{x}.

(123)

As a consequence the average flow in the
x
direction reads

\displaystyle v=\frac{\int_{0}^{a}\mbox{d}yy[U(1-y^{2}/a^{2})+UL\beta/a]}{\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X\int_{0}^{h(X)}\mbox{d}yy}=v_{0}+Lv_{1}+\mathcal{O}(L^{2}),

(124)

with

\displaystyle v_{0}=\frac{a^{2}U}{2\langle h^{2}\rangle},

\displaystyle v_{1}=\frac{Ua\beta}{\langle h^{2}\rangle}.

(125)

Next, we focus on the calculation of
f
, which satisfies the equations

\displaystyle-\frac{u_{x}}{L}\partial_{X}f+D\left(\frac{1}{L^{2}}\partial_{X}^{2}f+\frac{1}{y}\partial_{y}(y\partial_{y}f)\right)=v-u_{x},

(126)

in both peripheral and central regions. In the peripheral region, the boundary condition is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}(\partial_{X}h)\left(\frac{\partial_{X}f^{p}}{L}-1\right)-\partial_{y}f^{p}=0

\displaystyle[y=h(X)],

(127)

while for the central region one has the condition that
f
is periodic of period
1
and the condition
\partial_{y}f^{c}=0
at
y=0
.

Inserting the series expansion in powers of
L
,
f^{w}=\sum_{n\geq 0}L^{n}f_{n}^{w}(X,y)
(
w\in\left\{c,p\right\}
) in both central and peripheral regions into
Eqs.

126
and
127
, we find that
Eqs.

47
,
48
,
49
and
50
found for the two-dimensional case are unchanged, so that the general form of solutions for
f
is unchanged at order
1
and
L
:

\displaystyle f_{0}^{c}(X,y)=f_{0}^{c}(y),

\displaystyle f_{0}^{p}(X,y)=f_{0}^{p}(y),

(128)

\displaystyle f_{1}^{c}(X,y)=f_{1}^{c}(y),

\displaystyle f_{1}^{p}(X,y)=X+b_{1}^{p}(y),

(129)

where
f_{0}^{c},f_{0}^{p},f_{1}^{c},b_{1}^{p}
are functions of
y
only.

To determine these functions, we need to consider the next orders in the power expansion of
Eqs.

126
and
127
. First, for the peripheral region (where
u_{x}=0
), we obtain

\displaystyle D\left[\partial_{X}^{2}f_{2}^{p}+\frac{1}{y}\partial_{y}(y\partial_{y}f_{0}^{p})\right]=v_{0},

(130)

\displaystyle D\left[\partial_{X}^{2}f_{3}^{p}+\frac{1}{y}\partial_{y}(y\partial_{y}f_{1}^{p})\right]=v_{1},

(131)

Integrating over the variable
X
, we obtain

\displaystyle D\partial_{X}f_{2}^{p}=\left[v_{0}-D\frac{1}{y}\partial_{y}(y\partial_{y}f_{0}^{p}(y))\right]X+A_{2}^{p}(y),

(132)

\displaystyle D\partial_{X}f_{3}^{p}=\left[v_{1}-D\frac{1}{y}\partial_{y}(y\partial_{y}b_{1}^{p}(y))\right]X+A_{3}^{p}(y),

(133)

where
A_{2}^{p}
and
A_{3}^{p}
are functions of
y
only. The boundary conditions are

\displaystyle(h^{\prime}(X)\partial_{X}f_{2}^{p}-\partial_{y}f_{0}^{p})_{y=h(X)}=0,

(134)

\displaystyle(h^{\prime}(X)\partial_{X}f_{3}^{p}-\partial_{y}f_{1}^{p})_{y=h(X)}=0,

(135)

so that, using
Eqs.

132
and
133
, we obtain

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(X)\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\left[v_{0}-D(f_{0}^{p})^{\prime\prime}(h(X))-D\frac{(f_{0}^{p})^{\prime}(h(X))}{h(X)}\right]X

\displaystyle+A_{2}^{p}(h(X))\Bigg\}=D(f_{0}^{p})^{\prime}(h(X)),

(136)

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(X)\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\left[v_{1}-D(b_{1}^{p})^{\prime\prime}(h(X))-D\frac{(b_{1}^{p})^{\prime}(h(X))}{h(X)})\right]X

\displaystyle+A_{3}^{p}(h(X))\Bigg\}=D(b_{1}^{p})^{\prime}(h(X)).

(137)

Multiplying these equations by
h(X)
and integrating once leads to

\displaystyle DXh(X)(f_{0}^{p})^{\prime}(h(X))=v_{0}\int_{-1/2}^{X}\mbox{d}w\,h^{\prime}(w)h(w)w

\displaystyle+G_{2}^{p}(h(X)),

(138)

\displaystyle DXh(X)(b_{1}^{p})^{\prime}(h(X))=v_{1}\int_{-1/2}^{X}\mbox{d}w\,h^{\prime}(w)h(w)w

\displaystyle+G_{3}^{p}(h(X)),

(139)

where
G_{2}^{p}
and
G_{3}^{p}
are primitive functions of
yA_{2}^{p}
and
yA_{3}^{p}
, respectively:
\partial_{y}[G_{i}^{p}(y)]=yA_{i}^{p}(y)
(
i\in\left\{2,3\right\}
).

Now, for a given value of
y
, we can find two values of
X
, say
X_{1}(y)
and
X_{2}(y)
so that
y=h(X_{1}(y))=h(X_{2}(y))
, with
X_{1}(y)<X_{2}(y)
.
If we write the above equations for
X_{1}(y)
and
X_{2}(y)
and take the difference between the two, we obtain

\displaystyle D[X_{2}(y)-X_{1}(y)]\,y\,(f_{0}^{p})^{\prime}(y)=v_{0}\int_{X_{1}(y)}^{X_{2}(y)}\mbox{d}w\,w\,h(w)h^{\prime}(w),

(140)

\displaystyle D[X_{2}(y)-X_{1}(y)]\,y\,(b_{1}^{p})^{\prime}(y)=v_{1}\int_{X_{1}(y)}^{X_{2}(y)}\mbox{d}w\,w\,h(w)h^{\prime}(w).

(141)

Using the change of variable
y_{1}=h(w)
in the above integrals (after having separated the integration interval between the intervals
[X_{1}(y);X_{1}(h_{m})]
and
[X_{2}(h_{m});X_{2}(y)]
, we remark that

\displaystyle\int_{X_{1}(y)}^{X_{2}(y)}\mbox{d}w\,w\,h(w)h^{\prime}(w)=-\int_{y}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y_{1}y_{1}W(y_{1}),

(142)

where
W(y)=X_{2}(y)-X_{1}(y)
as before. We thus obtain

\displaystyle D(f_{0}^{p})^{\prime}(y)=-\frac{v_{0}}{y\,W(y)}\int_{y}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y_{1}y_{1}W(y_{1}),

(143)

\displaystyle D(b_{1}^{p})^{\prime}(y)=-\frac{v_{1}}{y\,W(y)}\int_{y}^{h_{m}}\mbox{d}y_{1}y_{1}W(y_{1}).

(144)

For the central region, we have

\displaystyle D\left[\partial_{X}^{2}f_{2}^{c}+\frac{1}{y}\partial_{y}(y\partial_{y}f_{0}^{c}(y))\right]=v_{0}-u_{x}^{(0)}(y),

(145)

\displaystyle D\left[\partial_{X}^{2}f_{3}^{c}+\frac{1}{y}\partial_{y}(y\partial_{y}f_{1}^{c}(y))\right]=v_{1}-u_{x}^{(1)}(y).

(146)

The fact that
f_{2}^{c}
and
f_{3}^{c}
are periodic functions of
X
then imposes

\displaystyle D\frac{1}{y}\partial_{y}(y\partial_{y}f_{0}^{c}(y))=v_{0}-u_{x}^{(0)}(y)=v_{0}-U(1-y^{2}/a^{2}),

(147)

\displaystyle D\frac{1}{y}\partial_{y}(y\partial_{y}f_{1}^{c}(y))=v_{1}-u_{x}^{(1)}(y)=v_{1}-U\beta/a.

(148)

Therefore, noting that
\partial_{y}f=0
at
y=0
(by symmetry, at all orders), we have

\displaystyle D\partial_{y}f_{0}^{c}(y)=v_{0}\frac{y}{2}-U\left(\frac{y}{2}-\frac{y^{3}}{4a^{2}}\right),

(149)

\displaystyle D\partial_{y}f_{1}^{c}(y)=\left(v_{1}-\frac{U\beta}{a}\right)\frac{y}{2}.

(150)

We recall that
v_{0}
and
v_{1}
are given by
Eq.

125
and that the volume of the lateral region
V_{L}
can be written as

\displaystyle V_{L}=\pi L(\langle h^{2}\rangle-a^{2})=\frac{2\pi L}{W(a)}\int_{a}^{h_{m}}dy_{1}W(y_{1})y_{1}

\displaystyle=2\pi L\int_{a}^{h_{m}}dy_{1}W(y_{1})y_{1},

(151)

with
W(a)=1
. Using these values, we see by comparing
Eqs.

149
and
143
that
\partial_{y}[f_{0}^{c}-f_{0}^{p}]_{y=a}=0
, so there is no discontinuity of derivative of
f_{0}
at
y=a
at this order. The same property holds for
f_{1}
. We can thus impose that
f_{0}
is regular (continuous; with continuous derivative) at
y=a
, leading to

\displaystyle Df_{0}(y)=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(v_{0}-U)(y^{2}-a^{2})/4+U\frac{(y^{4}-a^{4})}{16a^{2}}+C_{0},&(y<a),\\
-v_{0}\int_{a}^{y}\frac{dy^{\prime}}{W(y^{\prime})y^{\prime}}\int_{y^{\prime}}^{h_{m}}dy^{\prime\prime}W(y^{\prime\prime})y^{\prime\prime}+C_{0},&(y>a),\end{cases}

(152)

where the integration constant
C_{0}
is fixed by the normalization condition
\int_{\Omega}\mbox{d}\mathbf{r}f=0
, which gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}dy\,yf_{0}^{c}(y)+\int_{a}^{h_{m}}dy\,yW(y)f_{0}^{p}(y)=0.

(153)

Using the previous expressions for
f_{0}^{c},f_{0}^{p}
, the above equation leads to

\displaystyle C_{0}=

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{\langle h^{2}\rangle}\Bigg\{U\frac{a^{4}}{24}-\frac{a^{4}v_{0}}{16}

\displaystyle-v_{0}\int_{a}^{h_{m}}dy\frac{\left[\int_{y}^{h_{m}}dy^{\prime\prime}y^{\prime\prime}W(y^{\prime\prime})\right]^{2}}{W(y)y}\Bigg\},

(154)

where we have used the fact that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}dy\,y

\displaystyle C_{0}+\int_{a}^{h_{m}}dy\,yW(y)C_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X\int_{0}^{h(X)}dy\,yC_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X\,\frac{h^{2}(X)}{2}C_{0}=\frac{C_{0}\langle h^{2}(X)\rangle}{2}.

(155)

At the boundary layer
y\simeq a
we can perform the exact same analysis as in the 2D case, we find that
f^{*}
has exactly the same form as in 2D, with the consequence that solutions can be matched only if

\displaystyle b_{1}^{p}(a)=f_{1}^{c}(a).

(156)

Applying this matching condition, we obtain

\displaystyle Df_{1}^{c}(y)=\left(v_{1}-\frac{U\beta}{a}\right)\frac{y^{2}-a^{2}}{4}+C_{1},

(157)

\displaystyle Db_{1}^{p}(y)=-v_{1}\int_{a}^{y}\frac{dy^{\prime}}{W(y^{\prime})y^{\prime}}\int_{y^{\prime}}^{h_{m}}dy^{\prime\prime}W(y^{\prime\prime})y^{\prime\prime}+C_{1},

(158)

and the constant
C_{1}
is found by requiring that
\int_{V}f\mbox{d}\mathbf{r}=0
at order
L
, so that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}dy

\displaystyle\,yDf_{1}^{c}(y)

\displaystyle+\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X\int_{a}^{h(X)}dy\,y[Db_{1}^{p}(y)+DX]=0.

(159)

This leads to

\displaystyle C_{1}=-\frac{2}{\langle h^{2}\rangle}\Bigg\{-\left(v_{1}-\frac{U\beta}{a}\right)\frac{a^{4}}{16}+\frac{D\langle Xh^{2}(X)\rangle}{2}

\displaystyle-v_{1}\int_{a}^{h_{m}}\frac{dy^{\prime}}{W(y^{\prime})y^{\prime}}\left[\int_{y^{\prime}}^{h_{m}}dy^{\prime\prime}W(y^{\prime\prime})y^{\prime\prime}\right]^{2}\Bigg\}.

(160)

At this stage, we have fully determined
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
in all the regions of the channel. To compute the diffusivity at next-to-leading order in
L
, we also need to compute
\partial_{X}f_{2}
at the channel boundary, this can be obtained from
Eq.

132
(up to a constant) as

\displaystyle D\partial_{X}f_{2}^{p}=\left[v_{0}-D\frac{1}{y}\partial_{y}(y\partial_{y}f_{0}^{p}(y))\right]X+\frac{1}{y}\partial_{y}G_{2}^{p}(y).

(161)

In 3D, the effective diffusivity
D_{e}
is estimated as follows:

\displaystyle D_{e}=

\displaystyle D+\frac{1}{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\mbox{d}\mathbf{r}\,[u_{x}f-D\partial_{x}f]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D+\frac{2}{\langle h^{2}\rangle}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X\int_{0}^{h(X)}dy\,y\left[u_{x}f-\frac{D}{L}\partial_{X}f\right].

(162)

As in 2D, we write the general expansion

\displaystyle D_{e}=D_{e}^{(0)}+L[D_{A}+D_{B}+D_{C}]+\mathcal{O}(L^{2}),

(163)

where the leading-order term is given by

\displaystyle D_{e}^{(0)}=D+\frac{2}{\langle h^{2}\rangle}

\displaystyle\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X\Bigg[\int_{0}^{a}dy\,yu_{x}^{(0)}f_{0}^{c}

\displaystyle-D\int_{a}^{h(X)}dy\,y\partial_{X}f_{1}^{p}\Bigg],

(164)

where the above expression takes into account the fact that
u_{x}
vanishes in the peripheral region, while
\partial_{X}f_{0}^{p}=\partial_{X}f_{0}^{c}=0
, and
\partial_{X}f_{1}^{c}
vanishes in the central region. The next-to-leading order components of the effective diffusivity read:

\displaystyle D_{A}=\frac{2}{\langle h^{2}\rangle}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X\int_{0}^{a}dy\,y[u_{x}^{(0)}f_{1}^{c}+u_{x}^{(1)}f_{0}^{c}],

(165)

\displaystyle D_{B}=-\frac{2D}{\langle h^{2}\rangle}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X\int_{a}^{h(X)}dy\,y\partial_{X}f_{2}^{p},

(166)

\displaystyle D_{C}=-\frac{2Da}{\langle h^{2}\rangle}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dY[\partial_{X}f^{*}(X,Y)-\theta(Y)].

(167)

Using
Eqs.

152
,
154
and
157
, the integrals in the leading order term can be calculated, and we find that

\displaystyle D_{e}^{(0)}=\frac{Da}{a+2\delta}+\frac{U^{2}a}{192D(a+2\delta)^{3}}\Bigg\{a^{4}+12a^{3}\delta

\displaystyle+44a^{2}\delta^{2}+96\int_{a}^{h_{m}}dy\frac{\left(\int_{y}^{h_{m}}dy^{\prime}W(y^{\prime})y^{\prime}\right)^{2}}{W(y)y}\Bigg\},

(168)

where we have defined
\delta
via the formula

\displaystyle\langle h^{2}\rangle=a^{2}+2a\delta.

(169)

Next, the term
D_{A}
, defined in
Eq.

165
, can be calculated by using the previously found expressions for
f_{n}
, leading to

\displaystyle D_{A}=\beta U^{2}

\displaystyle\frac{a^{3}\delta+8a^{2}\delta^{2}+24\int_{a}^{h_{m}}dy\frac{\left(\int_{y}^{h_{m}}dy^{\prime}W(y^{\prime})y^{\prime}\right)^{2}}{W(y)y})}{12D(a+2\delta)^{3}}

\displaystyle-\frac{v_{0}}{\langle h^{2}\rangle}\langle Xh^{2}(X)\rangle.

(170)

Now, we evaluate the term
D_{B}
, which reads

\displaystyle D_{B}=

\displaystyle-\frac{2D}{\langle h^{2}\rangle}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X\int_{a}^{h(X)}dy\,y\partial_{X}f_{2}^{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{\langle h^{2}\rangle}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}dX\int_{a}^{h(X)}dy\,y

\displaystyle\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\left[v_{0}-D\frac{1}{y}\partial_{y}(y\partial_{y}f_{0}^{p}(y))\right]X+\frac{1}{y}\partial_{y}G_{2}^{p}(y)\Bigg\},

(171)

where we have used
Eq.

161
. Integrating over
y
and noting that
\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}dX\,X=0
we obtain

\displaystyle D_{B}=

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{\langle h^{2}\rangle}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}dX\Bigg\{G_{2}^{p}(h(X))-G_{2}^{p}(a)+

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(\frac{v_{0}}{2}h^{2}(X)-Dh(X)(f_{0}^{p})^{\prime}(h(X))\right)X\Bigg\}.

(172)

We recall
Eq.

138
,

\displaystyle DXh(X)(f_{0}^{p})^{\prime}(h(X))=

\displaystyle v_{0}\int_{-1/2}^{X}\mbox{d}w\,h^{\prime}(w)h(w)w+G_{2}^{p}(h(X)).

(173)

Using this equation, we obtain

\displaystyle D_{B}=

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{\langle h^{2}\rangle}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X\Bigg\{\frac{v_{0}}{2}h^{2}(X)X

\displaystyle-G_{2}^{p}(a)-v_{0}\int_{-1/2}^{X}\mbox{d}w\,h^{\prime}(w)h(w)w\Bigg\}.

(174)

Writing

173

X=-1/2

\displaystyle G_{2}^{p}(a)=-\frac{v_{0}}{2}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}w\,h^{\prime}(w)h(w)w.

Next, using integrations by parts, we have

\displaystyle\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X\int_{-1/2}^{X}\mbox{d}w\,h^{\prime}(w)h(w)w

\displaystyle=-\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}XX^{2}h^{\prime}(X)h(X)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}w\,h^{\prime}(w)h(w)w

\displaystyle=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}X(2X)\frac{h^{2}(X)-a^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mbox{d}w\,h^{\prime}(w)h(w)w.

(176)

With these arguments we finally obtain

\displaystyle D_{B}=

\displaystyle\frac{v_{0}}{\langle h^{2}\rangle}\langle Xh^{2}(X)\rangle.

(177)

Last, the term
D_{C}
is almost the same as in the 2D case, and is given by

\displaystyle D_{C}=\frac{Da\,2\ln 2}{\pi\,\langle h^{2}\rangle}.

(178)

We define

\displaystyle\tau=

\displaystyle k_{d}^{-1}=\frac{2}{D\langle h^{2}-a^{2}\rangle}\int_{a}^{h_{m}}dy\frac{\left(\int_{y}^{h_{m}}dy^{\prime}W(y^{\prime})y^{\prime}\right)^{2}}{W(y)y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D\delta a}\int_{a}^{h_{m}}dy\frac{\left(\int_{y}^{h_{m}}dy^{\prime}W(y^{\prime})y^{\prime}\right)^{2}}{W(y)y}.

Using this value and the obtained expressions for

\displaystyle D_{e}=

\displaystyle\frac{Da}{a+2\delta}+\frac{DL2\ln 2}{\pi\,(a+2\delta)}

This agrees with the results of Ref.

when one identifies
\delta=k_{a}/k_{d}
