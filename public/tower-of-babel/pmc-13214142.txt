Targeting the astrocyte-microglia EFEMP1-GALNT10 axis: a spatially programmable therapeutic strategy for hippocampal vulnerability in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Junting Liu, Jing Liu, Yujian Fan, Congcong Liu, Jingrong Cao, Haixia Ma, Yuli Hou, Shuo Gao, Peichang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe hippocampus is selectively vulnerable to Alzheimer’s disease (AD), but the spatially resolved, cell-type-specific drivers of this susceptibility remain unknown, hindering the development of targeted therapies.MethodsWe employed an integrated causal-spatial approach, combining single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) from 53 post-mortem AD brains, multi-omics triangulation (Mendelian randomization and Bayesian colocalization), and cross-species spatial mapping in human cohorts and 5xFAD mice to identify drivers of hippocampal vulnerability.ResultsWe identified a novel astrocyte-microglia co-pathology axis centered on EFEMP1 and GALNT10. Causal inference prioritized this axis, supported by an EFEMP1 plasma protein quantitative trait locus (pQTL) with an exceptional effect size (OR = 7.96) and dual epigenetic-transcriptional regulation of microglial GALNT10. The axis demonstrated hippocampus-specific co-expression in humans (R = 0.81, p < 0.001), pathological amplification in 5xFAD mice (tM1 = 0.77 ± 0.04 vs. WT 0.69 ± 0.04, p < 0.001), and focal enrichment near Aβ plaques (EFEMP1: r = -0.79; GALNT10: r = -0.67, p < 0.001). Mechanistically, it forms a core interactome with EGFR and TIMP3, coupling extracellular matrix (ECM) dysregulation with neuroinflammation.ConclusionsOur study defines the EFEMP1-GALNT10 axis as a spatially coordinated driver of hippocampal vulnerability in AD. Introduction

AD pathology progresses through the brain in a stereotypical, spatially ordered pattern, with the hippocampus exhibiting early and selective vulnerability. This spatial heterogeneity is a fundamental clinical feature but remains a major therapeutic challenge, as global interventions often fail to rescue vulnerable circuits [1, 2]. A critical barrier is the inability to map statistically associated genetic risk onto the specific cells and microenvironments where they drive dysfunction.

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified numerous risk loci, yet translating these statistical associations into causal, spatially resolved, and cell-type-specific driver pathways within vulnerable tissue remains a formidable gap [3–5]. While single-cell transcriptomics reveals cellular diversity, it primarily provides correlative snapshots, limiting causal inference [6–8]. Bridging this divide, such as connecting genetic causality to spatial pathology, is essential for developing targeted, effective therapies.

Recent multi-omics efforts have begun to systematically prioritize potential targets. For instance, large-scale proteome-wide Mendelian randomization has recently nominated novel protein targets for AD [9–11], yet their spatial expression and cell-type-specific roles are often uncharacterized. Concurrently, advancements in spatial transcriptomic atlases have mapped gene expression gradients across AD brain regions [12–14], but often without integrating causal genetic evidence to distinguish drivers from passengers. Together, these studies highlight the complementary strengths and limitations of causal inference versus spatial mapping. A unified framework that integrates these approaches is needed to pinpoint not just where and in what cells dysregulation occurs, but which dysregulated nodes are causal and spatially coordinated in driving regional vulnerability.

To address this, we developed an integrated causal-spatial paradigm. This framework bridges snRNA-seq of AD brains, multi-omics triangulation via Mendelian randomization and colocalization to establish causality, and cross-species spatial mapping for validation. Applying this pipeline, we identified astrocyte-dominated ECM dysmetabolism as a principal driver of hippocampal vulnerability. Causal inference prioritized a novel, hippocampus-confined EFEMP1-GALNT10 co-pathology axis, which we mechanistically linked to neuroinflammation and spatially validated around Aβ plaques.

The translation of such spatially resolved targets into effective therapies, however, faces the formidable challenge of the blood-brain barrier. Nanoparticle-based targeted delivery strategies have emerged as a promising approach to overcome this hurdle, yet achieving cell-type-specificity within the brain remains a significant obstacle [15–17]. Building on this mechanistic foundation, we propose a receptor-guided targeting framework that leverages glia-enriched receptors (e.g., transferrin receptor [TfR], receptor for advanced glycation end products [RAGE], low-density lipoprotein receptor [LDLR]) to achieve cell-type-specific disruption of pathogenic cascades while preserving neuroprotective functions. By integrating causal genetics with high-resolution spatial mapping, this work provides a scalable framework for moving from genetic association to anatomically informed, spatially programmable therapeutic strategy.

Data sources

This study integrated multi-omics data from canonical publicly available resources. Selection prioritized datasets with large sample sizes, peer-reviewed provenance, and diversity of molecular modalities to enable a comprehensive investigation.

snRNA-seq data were obtained from three independent AD cohorts in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database: GSE188545 (6 AD cases and 6 controls), GSE157827 (12 AD cases and 9 controls), and GSE22207 (10 AD cases and 10 controls). Quality control (QC) was performed based on standard metrics. Following QC, cell types were annotated based on canonical marker expression. We performed cell-type-specific differential expression analysis for major brain cell types.

Quantitative trait loci (QTL) data were collated from: methylation QTLs (mQTLs) from McRae et al. [18] ; blood eQTLs from the eQTLGen Consortium [19]; protein QTLs (pQTLs) from Wingo et al. [20] (brain), Yang et al. [21] (CSF; providing isoform-resolved proteomics data), and Ferkingstad et al. [22] (plasma); and expression QTLs (eQTLs) from GTEx V8 [23] (hippocampus) and Qi et al. [24] (whole-brain). Complete metadata are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

GWAS data of AD were integrated from four independent cohorts: FinnGen R11 [25], IEU OpenGWAS ieu-b-5067 [26], LeeLab phenocode-290.11 [27], and GWAS Catalog GCST90435837 [28]. Detailed cohort characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Independent validation

To assess the generalizability of our core findings, we performed the following independent validation: (i) The EFEMP1 pQTL-AD association was compared with a large-scale independent plasma proteome study [11]. (ii) GALNT10 genetic signals were validated by performing SMR analysis using independent brain eQTL and mQTL summary statistics from the SMR database [29, 30] and our AD GWAS data. (iii) Region-specific co-expression of EFEMP1 and GALNT10 was tested in two independent bulk RNA-seq datasets of AD brains (GSE173955, hippocampus; GSE159699, temporal cortex) from GEO.

Differential expression analysis

Cell type-specific differential gene expression analysis was performed separately for neurons, microglia, and astrocytes across all three snRNA-seq datasets. Functional annotation of these cell type-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed through Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway and Gene Ontology (GO) [31] enrichment analyses, using a false-discovery rate (FDR) cutoff of 0.05.

Mendelian randomization and colocalization

Summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) [30] was performed using SMR v1.3.1 with a ± 1000 kb cis-window around target genes. To ensure causal directionality, Steiger filtering was applied to all significant associations.

QTL selection and heterogeneity assessment

Initially, significant cis-QTLs were selected at p < 5.0 × 10⁻⁸, excluding SNPs with allele frequency differences > 0.2 [32]. Next, heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) testing was performed with a primary threshold of p > 0.01 (conventional threshold: p > 0.05) [30]. For the APOE locus, which showed marginal heterogeneity (HEIDI p = 0.02–0.03) under standard thresholds, we retained the association based on its well-established AD causality [33–37], as exclusion would contradict biological knowledge. All other significant loci exceeded the conventional HEIDI threshold (e.g., GALNT10: chr5, rs889025, HEIDI p = 0.1; EFEMP1: chr2, rs1346790, HEIDI p = 0.42), confirming analysis robustness.

FDR control and threshold justification

We applied FDR correction via the Benjamini-Hochberg method [38]. To empirically validate our FDR threshold selection, we performed a negative control analysis using 100 matched loci from AD-unassociated genomic regions (GWAS p > 0.05, Supplementary Table S5). The primary significance threshold was set at FDR < 0.10 based on the following considerations: (1) EFEMP1 (plasma-pQTL OR = 7.96, 95% CI [2.17–29.21]) exhibited borderline significance (FDR = 0.062) under conventional FDR < 0.05 [39]; (2) Its extreme effect size (OR > 5.0) satisfied our tiered confidence criteria for moderate signals [40, 41]; (3) Field standards support FDR < 0.1 for novel biomarker discovery in proteomics [42]; (4) Empirical validation showed 0% false positive rate at FDR < 0.1 in the negative control analysis.

Bayesian colocalization with sensitivity analysis

Bayesian colocalization was performed using the coloc R package [43] (v.5.1.1) with the coloc.abf method within ± 1000 kb of target genes. Default priors (P(H₄) = 0.02) were applied [43]. To test robustness, we repeated analyses with alternative priors (p12 = 10⁻4 and p12 = 10⁻5) and window sizes (± 500 kb and ± 2000 kb) (Supplementary Table S6).

To ensure rigorous interpretation, we implemented a tiered confidence framework where high-confidence signals required posterior probability for hypothesis 4 (PPH4) ≥ 0.8, while moderate-confidence signals (0.7 ≤ PPH4 < 0.8) required supporting evidence such as established pathological roles or extreme QTL effects.

Therapeutic target prioritization and validation framework

Integrated target screening and therapeutic expansion

Eight candidate genes (EFEMP1, APOE, GALNT10, CRIM1, HES4, ITGAV, STXBP6, PLOD2) were prioritized through unified multi-omics filtering with progressively relaxed inclusion criteria: initial screening required concurrent significance across three core metrics (SMR FDR < 0.1, colocalization PPH4 ≥ 0.7, HEIDI test p > 0.01), followed by strategic expansion to include targets meeting one or two criteria. Candidate gene screening metrics visualized in Fig. 3. Therapeutic target identification employed a bidirectional strategy: (1) Drug-centric screening: Integrated 274 AD-annotated human targets from ChEMBL (v32) and Drug Bank (v5.0), identifying protein-protein interactions (PPI) with candidate genes via STRING (confidence score > 0.7). Hub targets were prioritized by: maximal colocalization confidence (highest PPH4), network centrality (degree connectivity ≥ 15), pathological relevance, and therapeutic tractability. (2) Network-based expansion: Constructed a high-confidence interactome (STRING v11.5; score > 0.9) centered on candidate genes to identify direct binding partners, followed by pharmacological database cross-referencing.

Target-optimized co-expression network analysis

We performed a target-restricted weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) [44, 45] using bulk RNA-seq data (GSE110226) to construct AD-specific co-expression networks centered on three core genetic anchors: APOE, EFEMP1, and GALNT10. The input gene set included these anchors along with complementary elements: ChEMBL/DrugBank targets filtered by direct protein-protein interaction (confidence score > 0.7 in STRING); pathway genes related to ECM, lipid metabolism, and neuroinflammation (KEGG:04512, 04979, 04620, 04621, 04064, 04610, 04060, 04630); and high-confidence interactors (confidence > 0.9) of the three anchors from STRING. This curated set of 919 genes (Supplementary Table S3) was analyzed using the R WGCNA package under strict parameters: soft-thresholding power β = 12 (scale-free topology fit R² = 0.853), minimum module size of 30 genes, and dendrogram cut height of 0.2. Hub genes were defined by absolute module eigengene connectivity (|KME| ≥ 0.8).

Spatial validation and concordance assessment

Spatial pathological specialization was resolved through GEPIA2 [46] validation with GTEx v8 brain transcriptomes, querying region-specific expression correlations for frontal cortex (BA9), hippocampus, and cortical tissues via native analysis module, revealing statistically significant hippocampal-specific GALNT10-EFEMP1 co-expression (R = 0.81, p<0.001) under thresholds |R| > 0.3 and p<0.05, with observed concordance to WGCNA global co-expression patterns.

Immunofluorescence and western blot validation

Immunofluorescence (IF) validation was performed on paraffin-embedded brain sections from 2- and 8-month-old 5xFAD transgenic mice and age-matched WT C57BL/6J controls (n = 3 per group). Animal studies were conducted under Xuanwu Hospital of Capital Medical University approval. Sections underwent multiplex fluorescence staining using the Treble-Fluorescence IHC kit (Immunoway, #RS0068; pH 9.0) following antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and blocking with 5% BSA in PBS. Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4 °C at the following dilutions: anti-GALNT10 (K112076P, Solarbio, 1:200), anti-EFEMP1 (YT1708, Immunoway, 1:200), anti-6E10 (80314, BioLegend, 1:4000), anti-NeuN (F0103, Selleck, 1:400), anti-Iba1 (YM8165, Immunoway, 1:400), and anti-GFAP (YM8172, Immunoway, 1:400).

Whole-slide imaging was performed using the 3DHISTECH P250 FLASH slide scanner at 20× magnification, with z-stacks captured at 5-µm intervals and standardized exposure times applied across DAPI, FITC, TxRED, and Cy5 channels to maintain signal consistency. To eliminate analyst bias, a blinding procedure was implemented: all acquired whole-slide images from 5xFAD mice were randomly re-coded by a researcher not involved in the subsequent quantitative analysis. The analyst performing the image quantification was therefore completely blinded to sample identity (e.g., animal ID, genotype batch) during all measurements.

Image processing and analysis were conducted in ImageJ (v1.54p), incorporating rolling-ball background subtraction using a 50-pixel radius and threshold-based masking where signals exceeding three times the background intensity were defined as positive. Region of interest (ROI) definition followed pre-registered criteria. For spatial gradient analysis, the peri-plaque ROI was predefined as a 50 μm wide concentric band extending outward from the border of each dense-core Aβ plaque (identified by 6E10 signal). A control ROI was defined for each analyzed plaque as a region of plaque-free tissue located at least 100 μm from any plaque edge. Co-localization analysis utilized the Coloc2 plugin within the same software environment, calculating Manders, overlap coefficients (tM1 and tM2) through Costes automatic thresholding to identify positive signals as pixels surpassing background intensity by three standard deviations. Hippocampal regions of interest were centered on Aβ plaques detected via 6E10 immunoreactivity, with statistical significance thresholds set at tM1 > 0.5 based on comparisons to no-primary-antibody controls. Pixel-wise correlations between EFEMP1 and GALNT10 were computed using the Coloc2 plugin, while proximity analysis categorized plaque-adjacent signals as those within 20 μm of plaque edges to ensure precise spatial mapping.

Western Blot analysis was performed to quantify protein levels in hippocampal tissue from 8-month-old male 5xFAD mice and age-matched WT controls (n = 4 per group). Proteins were extracted with RIPA buffer containing protease/phosphatase inhibitors, and concentrations were determined by BCA assay. Equal protein amounts (30 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-EGFR (1:4000, Immunoway, YM8344), anti-TIMP3 (1:1000, Immunoway, YM9422), anti-EFEMP1 (1:1000, Immunoway, YT1708), anti-GALNT10 (1:5000, Solarbio, K112076P), and anti-β-Actin (1:2000, Proteintech, 20536-1-AP). After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, bands were visualized by ECL, imaged, and quantified using ImageJ (normalized to β-Actin). Statistical significance was determined by unpaired t-test.

Statistical analysis

A dedicated description of all statistical thresholds, multiple-testing corrections, and reporting standards is provided here. Detailed parameters for specific analyses (e.g., SMR, colocalization) are documented in their respective subsections.

Significance Thresholds: Unless otherwise specified, a two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Specific adjusted thresholds for different analyses are as follows: (1) For differential expression and pathway enrichment, an FDR < 0.05 was applied. (2) SMR, a cis-QTL p < 5.0 × 10⁻⁸ was used for instrument selection, and an FDR < 0.10 was applied for significance testing of SMR associations, with justification provided in the corresponding subsection. (3) For Bayesian colocalization, a PPH4 ≥ 0.70 was used to define a shared causal variant.

Multiple-Testing Correction: The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was used to control the FDR across multiple hypothesis tests, as applied in differential expression, pathway enrichment, and SMR analyses. The specific FDR thresholds for each analysis are stated above.

Effect Size and Confidence Intervals: All reported effect size estimates (including odds ratios (OR) from SMR, correlation coefficients, and regression coefficients) are presented alongside their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) in the main text, figures, and supplementary tables.

Sensitivity and Validation Analyses: Key findings were subjected to rigorous sensitivity analyses. These included the use of HEIDI testing (p > 0.01) to assess pleiotropy in SMR, varying priors and window sizes in colocalization analysis, and negative control analyses to validate FDR thresholds, as detailed in the respective method subsections.

Results

Integrated single-cell data reveals astrocyte-driven ECM dysregulation as a key pathological feature in AD

To systematically delineate cell-type-specific pathological alterations in the AD brain, we integrated and re-analyzed three published post-mortem human brain single-nucleus snRNA-seq datasets, encompassing a total of 53 samples (Supplementary Fig. 1). Intersection analysis across these independent cohorts identified robust, consensus DEGs for each major brain cell type. We found that astrocytes harbored the greatest number of consensuses DEGs (208), substantially exceeding the numbers observed in neurons (155) and microglia (87) (Fig. 2A-C). To elucidate the biological processes associated with these astrocytic DEGs, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses. The astrocytic DEG signature was significantly enriched for terms related to ECM organization, including the cellular component “collagen-containing extracellular matrix,” the molecular function “glycosaminoglycan binding,” and the biological process “regulation of neuron protective development” (Fig. 2D, E). In contrast, DEGs from microglia and neurons were predominantly associated with inflammatory response and synaptic signaling pathways, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2A-D). Together, these convergent findings nominate astrocyte-driven ECM dysmetabolism as a predominant and central pathological driver in the AD hippocampus.

Multi-omics causal inference identifies EFEMP1 and GALNT10 as core pathogenic factors in glial cells

Given the prominence of astrocytic pathology, we next sought to identify which specific dysregulated genes act as causal drivers of AD. To this end, we deployed an integrated multi-omics causal inference pipeline. We instrumented differences in gene expression using various quantitative trait loci (including eQTLs, mQTLs, plasma pQTLs and CSF pQTLs) and integrated these with large-scale AD GWAS data through SMR and Bayesian colocalization analyses. This framework was designed to distinguish causal drivers from reactive changes. The resulting causal evidence for cell-type-specific factors is summarized in a dual-ring plot, which visualizes the SMR-derived FDR (inner ring) and Bayesian colocalization PPH4 (outer ring) (Fig. 3A). This analysis identified astrocyte-derived EFEMP1, evidenced by a plasma pQTL (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. 2F), and microglial GALNT10, supported by both expression and methylation QTLs (Fig. 3E, F, Supplementary Fig. 2E, G), as high-confidence causal drivers within their respective cell types. Notably, the established high-risk AD gene APOE also showed significant causal evidence in astrocytes, involving both brain mQTL and CSF protein (APOE ε2 isoform) levels (Fig. 3C, D, Supplementary Fig. 2E, F). This result not only validates the robustness of our analytical pipeline but also suggests that EFEMP1 may reside within a critical AD pathological pathway alongside APOE.

EFEMP1 and GALNT10 form a hippocampus-specific co-pathological axis amplified by Aβ pathology

We next hypothesized that these two causal drivers, originating from distinct glial cell types, may functionally interact. To test this, we performed WGCNA on bulk RNA-seq data from AD patient brains. This revealed a disease severity-associated module (blue module: β = 0.7, p = 0.008) wherein APOE, EFEMP1, and GALNT10 were co-embedded, forming a tightly interconnected regulatory network (Fig. 4A, B). To assess the regional specificity of this interaction, we analyzed transcriptomic data from normal human brain subregions in the GTEx v8 dataset. Strikingly, EFEMP1 and GALNT10 mRNA levels showed a strong, hippocampus-specific positive correlation (R = 0.81; Fig. 4C), which was significantly weaker in cortical areas like the prefrontal cortex. This computational evidence strongly suggests that EFEMP1 and GALNT10 form a spatially constrained interaction axis, potentially underlying the hippocampus’s selective vulnerability in AD.

The robustness and regional specificity of this axis were further supported by orthogonal evidence: replication of the EFEMP1 pQTL association in an independent cohort [11], genetic confirmation of GALNT10 regulation via brain QTLs, and precise recapitulation of the hippocampus-specific co-expression pattern in two independent transcriptomic datasets (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B).

To validate these predictions, we performed immunofluorescence in 5xFAD and WT mouse hippocampi. EFEMP1 and GALNT10 protein co-localization was significantly enhanced in 5xFAD mice (Fig. 4D, E), confirming the specific activation of this axis under AD pathology. Spatial gradient analysis revealed that both proteins were markedly enriched proximal to Aβ plaques. A linear mixed-effects model, accounting for nesting within animals and tissue sections, confirmed a highly significant negative association between protein intensity and distance from the plaque core for both EFEMP1 (β = -0.53, p < 0.0001) and GALNT10 (β = -0.51, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4F-H). This spatial gradient was visually apparent in per-plaque trajectory plots (spaghetti plots, Supplementary Fig. 3C-H) and its statistical robustness was further confirmed by a random-shift permutation test, which yielded an empirical p-value < 0.00005 (Supplementary Fig. 3I, J). To exclude confounding by glial accumulation, we measured local astrocyte (GFAP+) and microglia (Iba1+) area fractions. After controlling for these, the negative association between protein intensity and plaque distance remained highly significant for both EFEMP1 and GALNT10. Complementing this, digital deconvolution of bulk hippocampal RNA-seq data showed that the positive associations between AD pathology and EFEMP1/GALNT10 gene expression persisted after controlling for estimated astrocyte and microglia proportions (Supplementary Table S7).

These results demonstrate that the EFEMP1-GALNT10 axis forms an Aβ-plaque-centered pathological microenvironment through the specific upregulation of both proteins within their resident glial cells, likely contributing to local hippocampal damage.

The EFEMP1 – GALNT10 axis forms a distinct glial co-pathology module coupled to ECM dysregulation in AD

To functionally contextualize the prioritized causal drivers EFEMP1 and GALNT10, we first sought to define their expression characteristics within the spectrum of glial reactivity. Re-analysis of snRNA-seq data from AD patient brains revealed that neither EFEMP1 (astrocytes) nor GALNT10 (microglia) exhibited significant difference in canonical activation sub-states (e.g., A1/A2 astrocytes or disease-associated microglia; Supplementary Fig. 4A-D), suggesting they may mark a distinct glial response.

We next hypothesized that these molecules might function through a coordinated interaction network. A PPI network built around our high-priority candidates revealed an astrocyte-centered hub involving APOE, EGFR, and EFEMP1 (Fig. 5). We then performed interaction analysis between these candidates and annotated AD-related drug targets from the ChEMBL and DrugBank databases. This PPI analysis revealed an astrocyte-centered hub involving APOE, EGFR, and EFEMP1, where EGFR appears to integrate signals from APOE (STRING confidence score = 0.61), EFEMP1 (0.88), and the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL1B (0.87) (Fig. 6A). To further explore direct interactors, we built a high-confidence interaction subnetwork (STRING score > 0.9) seeded with EFEMP1, APOE, and GALNT10. Notably, a high-confidence subnetwork showed that the ECM regulator TIMP3 is a high-confidence direct interactor of EFEMP1 (score = 0.958), while GALNT10 connected to glycosylation-related molecules like GCNT1 (Fig. 6B).

Statistical interrogation in the GSE48350 cohort further revealed that the strong association between EFEMP1 and GALNT10 expression. This association was not statistically mediated by EGFR or APOE (Supplementary Fig. 4E, F), supporting a model of direct or parallel co-regulation within a shared module. To test if this physical interaction network reflects functional co-regulation, we analyzed co-expression relationships. Strikingly, in the disease-free human hippocampus (GTEx data), EFEMP1, GALNT10, and TIMP3 already exhibited significant co-expression (Supplementary Fig. 4G, H), forming a constitutive network, while showing no correlation with the pro-degradative factor MMP3. This inherent co-regulatory architecture was pathologically amplified in AD, as evidenced by significant positive correlations between EFEMP1 and TIMP3 and between GALNT10 and TIMP3 in independent AD patient cohorts (GSE48350, GSE173955; Supplementary Fig. 4I, J). Critically, we experimentally validated the in-situ relevance and concerted dysregulation of this network. In the hippocampus of 5xFAD mice, co-immunofluorescence demonstrated significant spatial co-localization of EFEMP1 with both EGFR and TIMP3 in plaque-adjacent regions (Fig. 6C-H). Western blot analysis further revealed a coordinated upregulation of EFEMP1, GALNT10, EGFR, and TIMP3 proteins compared to WT controls (Fig. 6I, J), confirming their parallel alteration at the protein level.

Together, multiple layers of evidence converge to indicate that the EFEMP1-GALNT10 axis forms a distinct glial co-pathology module. This conclusion is supported by findings that span the definition of its unique molecular identities, the mapping of its physical interactions, the demonstration of its functional co-regulation, and experimental validation of its spatial co-localization and protein co-dysregulation. This module is intrinsically linked to ECM homeostasis via its interaction with and co-regulation of TIMP3. Our findings suggest that AD pathology does not create a new pathway de novo, but rather hijacks and amplifies this pre-existing, hippocampus-inherent network, ultimately driving ECM dysregulation.

A receptor-guided targeting strategy for spatial uncoupling of the co-pathology axis

Given the compelling mechanistic evidence but the absence of existing agents targeting EFEMP1 or GALNT10, we performed a comprehensive draggability assessment via the Open Targets Platform. This analysis established a clear modality-specific dichotomy: EFEMP1 was deemed suitable for antibody-based blockade due to its extracellular domain exposure, while GALNT10 presented strong potential for small-molecule inhibition or targeted protein degradation (Supplementary Table S4). To achieve precise targeting, we designed a receptor-mediated spatial delivery framework that leverages astrocyte-preferential transcytosis receptors (such as TfR, RAGE and LDLR). Preclinical studies indicate that engaging these receptors can enhance brain exposure, with TfR, RAGE, and LDLR targeting achieving approximate fold-increases of 5–10 [47–54], 1.5-3 [55–58], and 2–4 [59–63], respectively, and can confer regional enrichment such as in the hippocampus (Table 1), enabling the targeted delivery of a core therapeutic module.

The central component of this therapeutic module is an anti-EFEMP1 antibody, designed to directly and selectively disrupt the astrocyte-centric EFEMP1-EGFR-APOE axis at its origin. Complementary agents include GALNT10 modulators and the TIMP3-EFEMP1 interaction modulator Pimagedine (DB05383) to simultaneously target the regulatory node, and IL-1R antagonists [64–66] to disrupt the upstream microglial IL1B-EGFR cascade. Importantly, targeting EFEMP1, rather than EGFR directly, presents a mechanistically safer strategy by exploiting the spatial enrichment of EFEMP1 in disease-associated astrocytes (Supplementary Fig. 5A-D), thereby aiming to inhibit the pathological feedback loop while sparing physiological EGFR signaling essential for neuronal function. In the near term, before anti-EFEMP1 biologics are available, repurposed EGFR inhibitors (e.g., Erlotinib [67]) delivered via this spatial framework could serve as an alternative, proxy strategy, with the receptor-guided delivery itself mitigating systemic exposure and associated liabilities.

Simple, first-order pharmacokinetic estimates based on these delivery efficiencies and standard drug properties suggest that achieving pharmacologically relevant concentrations of such agents (e.g., an anti-EFEMP1 antibody or a GALNT10 inhibitor) for target engagement within hippocampal peri-plaque microdomains is feasible. We further propose plasma EFEMP1 and CSF APOE (ε2) as mechanistically grounded biomarkers, a choice based on their unique dual advantages: high causal priority derived from our upstream multi-omics analyses, and direct accessibility in peripheral biofluids. This integrated strategy provides a blueprint for the therapeutic disruption of this co-pathology axis (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Our integrated causal-spatial paradigm establishes astrocyte-dominated ECM dysmetabolism as the nodal driver of regional vulnerability in AD. This approach identifies the EFEMP1-GALNT10 axis as a conserved mechanistic hub. The axis is structurally mapped through molecular networks and spatially validated by regional co-expression patterns (Figs. 4 and 6). This axis exemplifies disease-rewired signaling. On one hand, EFEMP1-TIMP3 binding stabilizes pathological ECM deposits by suppressing MMP9 [68]. On the other, EGFR is hijacked in Aβ-enriched niches, transforming its physiological tumor-suppressive role [69, 70] into a profibrotic activator in neurodegeneration [71].

Consequently, EFEMP1-driven EGFR hyperactivation recruits microglial IL-1B sources [72, 73], fueling inflammatory feedforward loops that synergize with GALNT10’s O-GalNAc glycosylation. This cascade orchestrates tripartite pathology: neuroinflammatory escalation via glycan-dependent inflammasome/tau-receptor tuning [74, 75]; mitochondrial-cytoskeletal collapse through reactive oxygen species (ROS)/mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) dysregulation [76, 77] and Aβ-exploited O-glycan remodeling perpetuating neuroinflammation [78].

Spatial analyses reveal that AD microenvironments co-opt this machinery, with EFEMP1-GALNT10 interaction enhanced in peri-plaque microdomain as demonstrated by distance-dependent intensity correlations converting homeostatic crosstalk into self-perpetuating cascades. The evolutionary conservation of this axis is demonstrated by robust human hippocampal co-expression (R = 0.81, p < 0.001; Fig. 4C) and significantly increased murine co-localization strength (5xFAD tM1 = 0.77 ± 0.04 vs. WT 0.69 ± 0.04; Fig. 4D, E), underscoring its fundamental role in AD progression and providing a spatial blueprint for therapeutic targeting.

The EGFR-EFEMP1-GALNT10 hub represents a key example of signaling reprogramming in AD. Its spatial enrichment within Aβ microdomains directly inspired the development of our receptor-guided therapeutic strategy (Fig. 7). Unlike monotherapies targeting single hallmarks (Aβ or tau), our co-pathology-targeting approach aims to dismantle the self-perpetuating ECM-inflammation feedback loop, a core driver of regional vulnerability. This paradigm leverages endogenous transcytosis pathways for cell-type-specific biodistribution, with components such as nanoparticles or receptor targets (e.g., TfR, RAGE, LDLR) as modular tools rather than fixed requirements. For instance, TfR -bispecific antibodies enable astrocyte-preferential targeting and > 2-fold blood-brain barrier penetration targeting, and enable hippocampal enrichment in AD models (Table 1). The receptor-guided delivery framework provides the necessary precision to surgically intervene within the hippocampal niche, potentially mitigating the systemic or broad CNS side-effects associated with global anti-inflammatory or ECM-modulatory agents. The core innovation lies in the framework’s adaptability: it accommodates any strategy that exploits glia-enriched receptors for precision targeting of co-pathology hotspots, including emerging nanocarriers or alternative receptor-ligand pairs. Thus, we provide a translational bridge that connects genetically-derived target lists [79] to anatomically-informed delivery solutions, offering a scalable strategy to therapeutically dismantle pathogenic hubs in the diseased brain.

The translational viability of targeting this axis is supported by three pillars: First, receptor-guided delivery platforms (TfR, RAGE, LDLR) enhance brain exposure by 1.5- to 10-fold in AD models, providing a validated toolkit for hippocampal targeting (Table 1). Second, preliminary pharmacokinetic calculations indicate that achieving effective target coverage is feasible. Third, the safety profile is strengthened by a multi-layered precision design: (i) CNS-restricted delivery minimizes peripheral risks; (ii) Selective inhibition of the pathological GALNT10 isoform aims to spare the physiological functions of other GALNT family members [80–82]; (iii) Prioritizing the inhibition of its upstream ligand EFEMP1, rather than EGFR itself, constitutes a mechanistically safer strategy. This approach leverages the spatial enrichment of EFEMP1 in reactive astrocytes to selectively disrupt the pathological APOE-EGFR-EFEMP1 loop, while aiming to preserve physiological EGFR signaling in neurons. This integrated framework presents a de-risked translational pathway.

The convergence of EFEMP1 and APOE (ε2) biomarkers on EGFR-mediated dysmetabolism provides a translational scaffold for real-time monitoring. Their core value lies in their mechanistic specificity for the identified astrocyte-microglia co-pathology axis. Plasma EFEMP1 (pQTL OR = 7.96) reflects structural decline downstream of neuroinflammatory-ECM synergy [11]. Similarly, APOE (ε2) tracks compartmentalized ECM metabolic burden. Thus, they are positioned not as generic diagnostic tools, but as unique pharmacodynamic and stratification biomarkers for therapies aimed at this pathway. Therefore, they are positioned not as generic diagnostic tools, but as unique pharmacodynamic and stratification biomarkers for therapies aimed at this pathway. Their combined use may enable Aβ-independent staging, particularly in prodromal cohorts. Future studies should leverage iPSC-based tri-culture systems to dissect EGFR context-dependency, validate these biomarkers in larger prospective cohorts against established markers, and design biomarker-guided trials to optimize receptor selection across proteinopathies with shared glial ECM dysregulation [83–85]. Furthermore, the therapeutic delivery platform itself can be advanced by integrating emerging green nanomaterials (e.g., plant-derived or biocompatible polymer-based nanoparticles) for receptor targeting. Such carriers could enhance the biocompatibility, sustainability, and manufacturability [86–88] of the final therapeutic product, aligning with next-generation biotherapeutic design. Beyond Alzheimer’s disease, this framework offers a generalizable precision medicine toolkit for targeting spatially organized co-pathology in neurodegenerative disorders.

Conclusions

In summary, our study uncovers the EFEMP1–GALNT10 co-pathology axis as a spatially conserved, causal hub driving hippocampal vulnerability in AD through ECM-inflammation coupling. By integrating multiscale evidence from causal genetics to cross-species spatial validation, we not only delineate a novel pathogenic circuit but also establish a receptor-guided targeting framework. This work provides a foundational blueprint for a new class of spatially programmable therapeutic strategy, moving beyond singular target inhibition toward the precise uncoupling of co-pathology networks in the diseased brain.
