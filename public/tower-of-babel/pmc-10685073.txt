Effects of glycine 64 substitutions in RNA-dependent RNA polymerase on ribavirin sensitivity and pathogenicity of coxsackievirus A6
Authors: Rui Wang, Qiang Sun, Jinbo Xiao, Congcong Wang, Xiaoliang Li, Jichen Li, Yang Song, Huanhuan Lu, Ying Liu, Shuangli Zhu, Zhijun Liu, Yong Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The 64th amino acid of the naturally prevalent 3D polymerase of CVA6 is a glycine amino group (G), but the mutation to serine (S) and threonine (T) will reduce the viral pathogenicity and is an attenuated site.•The mutation of glycine amino group (G) to serine (S) and threonine (T) at position 64 of 3D polymerase of CVA6 decreased ribavirin susceptibility.•The 64th glycine amino group (G) of the 3D polymerase of CVA6 was mutated to serine (S) and threonine (T) to improve the replication fidelity of the strain.Keywords: Coxsackievirus A6, 3D polymerase, Glycine 64, Replication fidelity, Attenuated virulence, Ribavirin insensitivity

Introduction

Enteroviruses (EVs) are the most common members of the Picornaviridae family. Human EVs comprise seven species, EV-A–D and rhinoviruses A–C (Lefkowitz et al., 2018; Simmonds et al., 2020), which are the main causative agents of pediatric infectious diseases (Chen et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2017). EV-A species cause hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD); among them, coxsackievirus A6 (CVA6) has emerged as the predominant pathogen responsible for HFMD outbreaks in recent years: first in 2008 in Finland and then in several other countries (Song et al., 2017). Although CVA6 infection is usually mild and self-limiting, some of the reported severe cases indicate that CVA6 may also lead to serious complications in the central nervous system, such as aseptic meningitis, brainstem encephalitis, and acute flaccid paralysis (Blomqvist et al., 2010; Logotheti et al., 2009; Rao et al., 2012; Richter et al., 2006). Therefore, the public health problems associated with CVA6 should not be underestimated.

Currently, there is no vaccine for CVA6. Live attenuated vaccines represent the oldest and most effective immunization method, and its low cost contributes to mass vaccination (Vignuzzi et al., 2008). However, there is some concern about the safety of live attenuated vaccines because the attenuated virus can potentially revert to the pathogenic phenotype, triggering disease in vaccinated individuals or their contacts (Burns et al., 2014; Minor, 2015; Vignuzzi et al., 2008). In addition, there are reports that selective pressure imposed by the environment enhances virus fitness, often leading to the reverse of the vaccine strain to the wild-type pathogenic strain, which could results in rare but serious diseases such as vaccine-associated paralytic poliomyelitis (Agol, 2006; Platt et al., 2014). It has been suggested that the development of a high-fidelity vaccine should help solve these problems (Cochi and Pallansch, 2021; Vignuzzi et al., 2008).

CVA6 is a single positive stranded RNA virus. Replication relies on RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), which lacks proofreading function. Therefore, these viruses are prone to mutations, which are thought to contribute to viral genome diversity and adaptability and the preservation of RNA viruses in pandemics (Korboukh et al., 2014; Sadeghipour et al., 2013). In EVs, RdRp is represented by nonstructural protein 3D called 3D polymerase (Lee et al., 2020). It has been reported that mutations of residue 64 in 3D polymerase due to ribavirin pressure result in the emergence of drug-resistant poliovirus strains with high fidelity of replication and a low replication rate; later, these findings have been confirmed in enterovirus A71 (EV-A71) (Pfeiffer and Kirkegaard, 2003, 2005; Sadeghipour et al., 2013). In addition, the 2C region of enterovirus has an ATPase/Helicase that can uncoil the double-stranded structure of the RNA viral genome and form a stable RNA replication complex, which has essential functions for viral replication and is an attractive antiviral target (El Kazzi et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2020). However, there is no information regarding the functional role of such mutations in CVA6.

In this study, we aimed to explore the relationship between CVA6 biological characteristics and amino acid substitutions at position 64 in 3D polymerase using reverse genetics. Our results should provide a foundation for the development of a live attenuated CVA6 vaccine.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

Pregnant ICR mice were purchased from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing). All animal experiments were approved by the Second Ethical Review Committee of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, Institute of Viral Disease Prevention and Control (Approval No. 20221012098).

Virus strains, cells, and culture conditions

The CVA6 stain (54203/HeB/2012) used in this study was originally isolated from feces of patients with HFMD in Hebei in 2012. This strain was selected because of its strong virulence, obvious symptoms in infected animals, easy culture, and sequence availability (GenBank access number: MK106189). The virus was expanded in human rhabdomyosarcoma (RD) cells (WHO) cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) in an incubator at 37 °C with CO2 concentration of 5 %.

Cloning

Viral RNA was extracted using RNeasy kit (QIAGEN). Two fragments of strain 54203/HeB/2012 were inserted into the pcDNA3.1(+) plasmid through homologous recombination. First, the F1 fragment (forward primer 5′−3′: AGGTCTATATAAGCAGAGCTGCCACCTTAAAACAGCCTGTGGGTTGTACCC, reverse primer 5′−3′: AGATCCACTCGAGTCCCTTAGCAGCGCTGGC) was amplified by RT-PCR using the SuperScript™ III one-step RT-PCR system and Platinum™Taq High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Invitrogen, USA), and the F2 fragment (forward primer 5′−3′: TAAGGGACTCGAGTGGATCTCTAATAAGATC, reverse primer 5′−3′: CTGGCAACTAGAAGGCACAGGCTATTCTGGTT), use a KOD - FX - Neo (TOYOBO, Japan) kit for plasmid pcDNA3.1 (+) of the PCR amplification (forward primer 5′−3′: CTGTGCCTTCTAGTTGCCAGCCATCTG, reverse primer 5′−3′: AGCTCTGCTTATATAGACCTCCCACCGTAC). Each fragment was recovered using TIANgel Maxi Purification Kit (TIANGEN, China) and then reconstituted using ClonExpress® Ultra One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, China). CVA6-pcDNA3.1 (+) was obtained. The construct was used as a template to mutate site 64 in 3D polymerase, which was targeted by site-directed mutagenesis performed with a special kit (Vazyme, China), yielding CVA6-G64S-pcDNA3.1 and CVA6-G64T-pcDNA3.1. The mutations were confirmed by whole plasmid sequencing.

In vitro transcription and cell transfection

The forward primer CVA6-S containing the T7 promoter (5′−3:TAATACGACTCACTATAGGGTTAAAACAGCCTGTGGGTTGTA) and the reverse primer CVA6-A with a polyA site (5′−3′:TTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTGCTATTCTGGTTATAACAAATTTA) were designed (SnapGene6.02) and used for PCR amplification (KOD FX Neo kit, TOYOBO, Japan) high fidelity enzyme was used, and the system was prepared according to the instructions, and the reaction was performed according to the following steps: pre-denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min, denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing and extension at 68 °C for 4 min for a total of 35 cycles and complete extension at 68 °C for 7 min for the last step. followed by gel purification. After in vitro transcription, DNA was removed from RNA by treatment with RNase-free DNase RQ1 at 37 °C for 15 min. RNA was purified using RNA purification kit (QIAGEN), its concentration measured according to absorbance at 260 nm, and the correct size of the transcribed RNA was verified by electrophoresis.

For cell transfection, Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) was divided into two types, Lipofectamine 3000 and P3000. 3 μL of Lipofectamine 3000 was added to 50 μl of Opti-MEM and labeled as tube A, followed by 2 μg of in vitro transcript viral RNA and 4 μl of P3000 to a new 50 μl of Opti-MEM and labeled as tube B. Tubes A and B tubes were left at room temperature (20 °C) for 5 min, then mixed and left at room temperature (20 °C) for 15 min before being added to separate wells of 12-well plates containing 5 × 105/well cells, which were then placed into a 5 % CO2 incubator at 37 °C. After 4 h, the medium was replaced with MEM containing 2 % FBS, and the plates were incubated for 48 h until appearance of the cytopathic effect. After multiple passages, the viruses were sequenced to verify the stability of the mutation site. The rescued viruses were designated as CVA6, CVA6-G64S, CVA6-G64T.

Immunofluorescence

RD cells were seeded on LAB-Tek II chamber coverslips (5 × 104 cells/well), infected with CVA6, CVA6-G64S, and CVA6-G64T at the multiplicity of infection (MOI) 0.01, incubated at 37 °C, 5 % CO2 for 2 h, washed three times with PBS, and grown in 200 μl of 2 % FBS-containing MEM for 24 h. Then, the supernatant was removed, and cell monolayers were washed with PBS three times and fixed with methanol: acetone (1:1) at −20 °C for 20 min. After the fixative was discarded, cells were dried on air, blocked with 10 % FBS in PBS for 45 min at 37 °C, washed five times with PBS, incubated with the primary anti-dsRNA antibody (Polyclonal mouse Anti-dsRNA J2, ABclonal, USA) (dilution 1:50) at 4 ℃ overnight, washed five times with PBS, and incubated with a secondary antibody (Bioss) at 37 °C for 1 h. Finally, cell monolayers were stained with one drop DAPI (solarbio) for 5 min and observed under a fluorescence microscope (leica, Germany).

Growth kinetics

RD cells were seeded on 12-well plates (5 × 105/mL per well), infected with CVA6, CVA6-G64S, and CVA6-G64T at MOI = 0.1, incubated at 37 ℃ and 5 % CO2 for 2 h, washed with PBS three times, and maintained in 2 % FBS-containing MEM for 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h. Cells and supernatants were collected, subjected to three cycles of freezing-thawing at −80 °C, and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatants were analyzed for the median tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) and RNA content by qRT-PCR. The experiments were performed in three replicates. Viral titers were finally calculated by the Kärber formula, RNA copies were calculated from standards in our laboratory.

Infection of suckling mouse pups with CVA6, CVA6-G64S, and CVA6-G64T strains

Our preliminary experiments showed that the infection dose of 107TCID50 virus could better simulate the pathogenic process of the virus in ICR suckling mice. When ICR suckling mice were infected with 106TCID50 virus, clinical symptoms were delayed until the 5th day, and some suckling mice remained asymptomatic. So we chose 107TCID50 as the challenge dose. Ten one-day-old pups from four pregnant ICR mice were intramuscularly infected with 107 TCID50 of CVA6, CVA6-G64S, and CVA6-G64T in 50 μL MEM, the control group was injected with an equal volume of MEM and their weights, clinical scores, and survival were recorded on a daily basis. Clinical scoring was performed as follows: 0, no disease; 1, folded fur; 2, weight loss; 3, single limb paralysis; 4, double hind limb paralysis; 5, near death or death (VanBlargan et al., 2018). At days 1, 3, and 4 post infection, mouse tissues were extracted, ground, and centrifuged at 5000 rpm to measure virus load. The experiment was repeated three times.

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry analyses

When pups infected with the most virulent strain showed severe symptoms, heart, brain, lung, and hind limb muscle tissues were taken from each group of pups, fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, dewaxed in xylene, and stained with HE. For immunohistochemistry, the dewaxed tissues were dehydrated, microwaved for 10 min for antigen retrieval, and incubated with a rabbit polyclonal antibody against VP2 of CVA6 (Gentex, USA) diluted 1:200 in 3 % bovine serum albumin. After incubation with horseradish peroxidase-labeled secondary antibody (1:1000 dilution) at room temperature for 50 min, sections were rinsing with 3,39-diaminobenzidine and counterstained with hematoxylin for 3 min. Images were taken with an AxioCam MRc5 camera (Carl Zeiss, Berlin, Germany) at magnification of 200× and 400×.

Cytotoxicity test

Ribavirin powder (Sigma, USA) was dissolved in MEM at a concentration of 100 mM and serially diluted 2-fold. Guanidine hydrochloride (8 M) was serially diluted 10-fold. After spreading 3 × 104 RD/well in 96-well plates and incubating overnight at 37 °C and 5 % CO2, 100 µL of each gradient dilution was added sequentially to the 96-well plates in one vertical row, and 4 replicate wells were performed for each gradient (MEM was used as blank control). After incubation at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 for 24 h, 10 μl of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (Beyotime) solution was added to each well for 1 h at 37 °C, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Ribavirin sensitivity assays

RD cells were infected with CVA6, CVA6-G64S, or CVA6-G64T at MOI = 0.1 and incubated for 2 h at 5 % CO2 and 37 °C; after washing three times with PBS, they were divided into experimental and viral control groups, with 0.8 mM ribavirin added to the experimental group, and the viral control group was the medium without these two reagents but with MEM containing 2 % FBS. Complete samples were collected after 24, respectively, and TCID50 was measured.

Plaque purification

10 cm cell culture dish was seeded with RD cells at a density of 5 × 106 and were grown overnight at 37 ℃ and 5 % CO2. 107-fold dilutions of virus (CVA6, CVA6-G64S, CVA6-G64T) were inoculated onto RD cell monolayers at a volume of 8 mL each cell culture dish and incubated for 2 h at 5 % CO2 and 37 ℃.The inoculum was removed and the cell were overlaid with 15 mL maintenance medium containing 0.8 % agarose was added to each cell culture dish. Incubated upside down for 72 h at 5 % CO2 and 37 ℃. Isolated plaques were selected using a wide muzzle tip and placed into 500 uL of maintenance medium. After three freeze-thaw cycles in a −80 ℃ refrigerator, the plaques were inoculated into RD cells for virus expansion. This protocol was repeated seven to select plaque-purified virus population.

Guanidine hydrochloride sensitivity test

Seven plaques (CVA6, CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T) were selected for guanidine hydrochloride resistance test. Each well of a 12-well plate was passed through 5 × 105 RD cells and incubated overnight at 37 ℃ and 5 % CO2. Each virus (CVA6, CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T) was used to infect RD cells in each well at a MOI = 0.1. Each virus was replicated in triplicate and incubated for 2 h, the virus solution was discarded, washed three times with PBS, and replaced with maintenance solution with or without 0.8 mM guanidine hydrochloride. Samples were collected 48 h after drug treatment and TCID50 was measured.

Ribavirin mutagenesis and nucleotide sequence analysis

Viral RNA was extracted from the CVA6, CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T, after 1 or 15 passages in RD cell in the presence of the 0.8 mM ribavirin, using viral RNA minikit (Qiagen). Primers with amplicon adaptor sequences in region 2C and 3D were designed and RT-PCR was performed using SuperScript Ⅲ One-Step RT-PCR kit (Invitrogen). The products were sent to the company for next-generation sequencing (5000reads, sequencing was supported by Beijing Qingke Biotechnology Co., LTD.). We quantified Intra-host viral diversity using " Intra-host single-nucleotide variants (iSNVs)". This value is above a threshold for a specific minor allele frequency (>2% –5 %) (Park et al., 2015). The results were then analyzed for bioinformatics data. Low-quality bases (q < 20) and adaptors of raw reads were trimmed using the Fastp program to obtain clean reads (Chen et al., 2018; Langmead and Salzberg, 2012). These were mapped to the reference genome using Bowtie2. Finally, we call for SNVs and indels from next-generation sequencing data.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.4.0 (GraphPad Software). All results were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Tissue viral titers in different mice, drug (ribavirin) susceptibility results and guanidine hydrochloride replication fidelity results were analyzed by a two-tailed Student's t tests with a P values of 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Cloning and replication kinetics of wild-type and mutant CVA6 strains

A virulent CVA6 strain (54203/HeB/2012), which could produce symptoms in ICR mice and for which sequence is available, was used in this study. The whole virus genome was cloned in two segments in the pcDNA3.1(+) plasmid, and glycine at position 64 (G64), which is known to play an important role in the active site of 3D polymerase in poliovirus and EV-A71 (Pfeiffer and Kirkegaard, 2003; Sadeghipour et al., 2013), was substituted with arginine (G64R), serine (G64S), or threonine (G64T) (Fig. 1A). The resultant constructs were used to obtain live clonogenic wild-type and CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T mutant viruses; the mutations were confirmed by sequencing after six consecutive passages in RD cells (Fig. 1B), indicates stable inheritance. The three "rescued" viruses were passaged six times in RD cells, and the genome sequences of the first and sixth generations were detected by Sanger sequencing. In addition, in CVA6, CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T, there was a mutation at nucleotide position 4425 from A to G, and the corresponding amino acid was mutated from methionine M (ATG) to valine V (GTG), This mutation is located at the amino acid position 108 of the virus 2C protein. However, no mutations were detected at the amino acid 64th site (CVA6, CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T). Therefore, we believe that the 4425th nucleotide mutation is an adaptive mutation in RD cells and will not affect subsequent work, because the amino acids encoded by the three viruses are always only different at 64th sites.

The ability of the rescued viruses to replicate in RD cells was confirmed by the immunofluorescence assay after 24 h in culture (Fig. 1C). Comparison of the viral replication rate based on growth kinetics revealed that there was almost no difference in the replication ability of the CVA6, CVA6-G64S, and CVA6-G64T strains in RD cells (Fig. 1D and E).

Survival, body weight, clinical scores, and clinical signs of ICR mice infected with wild-type and mutant strains

The effects of the G64S and G64T mutations on virus pathogenicity were investigated in the ICR mouse model, which can mimic pathological processes in humans. The results revealed that the suckling pups in the CVA6 group did not increase weight and had paralysis of both hind limbs; they started to die at day 3 and all were dead on day 4 post infection (Fig. 2). In contrast, pups in the CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T groups showed constant weight gain, and only some started to present signs of unilateral limb paralysis on day 4, demonstrating a significantly delayed onset of symptoms; furthermore, their survival rate was much higher than that of CVA6-infected pups (Fig. 2). These findings suggested that mutations G64S and G64T in 3D polymerase of CVA6 reduced virus pathogenicity.

Tissue titers of CVA6, CVA6-G64S, and CVA6-G64T strains in ICR mice

As CVA6-infected pups died on day 4 post infection, we compared viral titers in tissues (brain, lung, heart, and hind limb muscle) of each group on days 1, 3, and 4. The results showed that in all analyzed tissues at 3 and 4 days post infection, the CVA6 group had significantly higher virus titers than the CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T groups (P < 0.01 to P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3). Overall, the lowest viral titers were observed in the heart (Fig. 3C) and the highest – in the hind limb (Fig. 3D).

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry analyses of mouse tissues after infection with CVA6, CVA6-G64S, and CVA6-G64T strains

Brain, lung, heart, and hind limb muscle tissues were isolated from randomly selected pups at day 3 post infection and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE). In comparison with the brain of the control mice, that of the CVA6 infected mice showed swelling, lysis, and disappearance of many neural cells (Fig. 4A1 and A2), whereas those of the CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T infected mice showed almost no lesions (Fig. 4A3 and A4). Similarly, the lungs of CVA6 infected mice showed severe pathological changes: tissue congestion and obstruction together with breaks and fusion of alveolar septa into several larger cystic cavities (Fig. 4B1 and B2), whereas only minimal bruising and no tissue damage were observed in the lungs of CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T infected mice (Fig. 4B3 and B4). However, no significant changes were detected in the heart of infected compared to uninfected mice (Fig. 4C1–C4). Analysis of hind limb muscles indicated that those of the CVA6 group were almost completely broken and no normal muscle fibers could be detected (Fig. 4D1 and D2), whereas those of the CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T groups showed some muscle fiber breaks and a large number of inflammatory cell aggregates, but the overall tissue texture was clear, and the injuries were significantly weaker than those in the CVA6 group (Fig. 4D3 and D4).

Decreased ribavirin sensitivity and increased guanidine hydrochloride sensitivity of CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T

In poliovirus and EV-A71, mutations at residue 64 of 3D polymerase confer ribavirin resistance and replication fidelity (Pfeiffer and Kirkegaard, 2003; Sadeghipour et al., 2013), Virus-infected RD cells were treated with ribavirin at the concentration of 0.8 mM, which was not cytotoxic (cell survival > 95 %; Fig. 6A) and analyzed for virus titers. The results indicated that ribavirin decreased the titer of CVA6 by 50-fold (P < 0.001) and those of CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T by 10-fold (P < 0.01) compared to untreated controls, indicating that G64S and G64T mutations in 3D polymerase reduced CVA6 susceptibility to ribavirin (Fig. 6B and C).

(B) Ribavirin sensitivity of CVA6, CVA6-G64S, and CVA6-G64T. RD cells were infected with the viruses at MOI = 0.1 for 2 h, treated with ribavirin (0.8 mM), and analyzed for TCID50. Similarly, in order to verify the sensitivity of mutant strains to guanidine hydrochloride (Sadeghipour et al., 2013), we first used a plaque assay to purify the 'rescued' virus, and then treated it for 48 h in the presence or absence of 0.8 mM guanidine hydrochloride, finally, each mutant strain was selected from 7 plaques and the viral titer of each plaque was tested. The results showed that 0.8 mM guanidine hydrochloride had no cytotoxicity (cell survival rate > 95 %; Fig. 7A). In the presence of guanidine hydrochloride, the plaque-purified CVA6 grew faster (approximately 17–100 times) than the plaque-purified CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T populations (Fig. 7 –F). Therefore, the results showed that the mutation of 64th amino acid in 3D polymerase of CVA6 increases the sensitivity of the strain to guanidine hydrochloride, indicating that the growth ability of the mutant strain in the presence of guanidine hydrochloride decreased.

Nucleotide and amino acid mutations in the 2C and 3D coding regions of CVA6, 3D-G64S, and 3D-G64T after passage in the presence of ribavirin

Bioinformatics analysis of the nucleotides in the 2C and 3D regions of the virus showed that mutations occurred after 15 consecutive passages of CVA6, CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T strains in the presence of ribavirin. Eight nucleotide sites with a mutation frequency greater than 5 % were detected in the 2C region of CVA6 strain. According to the corresponding nucleotide positions in the whole viral genome, they were 4138th (C-T, alanine A-valine V, 2C-12th), 4191th (G-A, aspartic D-asparagine N, 2C-30th), 4191th (G-T, aspartic D-tyrosine Y, 2C-30th), 4355th (G-A, Alanine A-alanine A, 2C-84th), 4425th (G-A, tyrosine V-methionine M, 2C-108th), 4826th (G-A, arginine R-arginine R, 2C-241th), 5022th (G-A, valine V-isoleucine I, 2C-307th), 5050th (A-C, asparagine N-threonine T, 2C-316th). However, in the 2C region of CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64-T, only one nucleotide site with a mutation frequency greater than 5 % was detected, that is, the 5050th nucleotide, which mutated from A to C, corresponding to an amino acid mutation from asparagine N to threonine T.

In the 3D region of the CVA6 genome, a total of six nucleotide mutations with a mutation frequency greater than 5 % were found. Discussion

In recent years, CVA6 has emerged as a predominant HFMD pathogen in mainland China, which in severe cases can cause neurological diseases such as meningitis, thus becoming a matter of public health concern (Song et al., 2020, 2017). However, the pathogenic mechanisms induced by CVA6 are still poorly understood, and specific vaccines as well as effective drugs have not yet been developed.

RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (3D polymerase), which is critical for the replication of enteroviruses, lacks fidelity, thus allowing RNA viruses replicate fast and produce diverse progeny for better fitness in viral pandemics (Campagnola et al., 2011; Contreras et al., 2002; Mattenberger et al., 2021). Mutations of residue G64 in 3D polymerase have been reported to confer drug resistance and high-fidelity replication to CVA6 relatives poliovirus and EV-A71 (Pfeiffer and Kirkegaard, 2003; Sadeghipour et al., 2013), but their effects on CVA6 are unknown. Analysis of 16 CVA6 strains isolated in mainland China between 2011 and 2018 (two strains selected for comparison each year) indicates that G64 in 3D polymerase of CVA6 is not mutated in the natural epidemic state (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1).

Ribavirin, a nucleotide analogue which prevents viral replication by inhibiting hypoxanthine monophosphate nucleoside dehydrogenase and interfering with guanine biosynthesis, is effective against a variety of RNA and DNA viruses and is currently used as a broad-spectrum antiviral drug in clinical practice (Keppeke et al., 2019; Molinos-Quintana et al., 2013; Vignuzzi et al., 2005). However, ribavirin does not inhibit CVA6, and vaccination remains an important means of preventing CVA6 infection. Live attenuated vaccines are a common and effective method of preventing viral infections (Vignuzzi et al., 2008), but their safety has been a concern because some attenuated viruses can revert to a pathogenic phenotype. Therefore, rational design of highly attenuated, genetically stable vaccines is essential (Macadam et al., 2006; Yakovenko et al., 2009), and viruses with high-fidelity replication and attenuated virulence, such as a poliovirus variant with the G64S substitution, can serve as a basis for the development of attenuated vaccines (Meng and Kwang, 2014; Pfeiffer and Kirkegaard, 2003, 2005; Sadeghipour and McMinn, 2013).

In this study, we directly mutated the G64 site of 3D polymerase in CVA6 using reverse genetics and successfully rescued stable CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T mutants, the three "rescued" viruses were passaged six times in RD cells, and the genome sequences of the first and sixth generations were detected by Sanger sequencing. The results showed that the 64th amino acid mutation of 3D polymerase could be stably inherited. In addition, in CVA6, CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T, there was a mutation at nucleotide position 4425 from A to G, and the corresponding amino acid was mutated from methionine M (ATG) to valine V (GTG). We then conducted a high replication fidelity experiment to verify the strain, after 15 consecutive passages in the presence of ribavirin, nucleotide mutations in the 2C and 3D regions were detected by next-generation sequencing. Interestingly, it was found that the G mutation at nucleotide 4425 of CVA6 strain changed back from valine V (GTG) to methionine M (ATG), with a mutation rate of 88.5 %. However, no mutations were detected at the amino acid 64th site (CVA6, CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T). This phenomenon is worth thinking about. We believe that the mutation of the "rescued" strain during the passage of RD cells is an adaptive mutation, and the antiviral effect of ribavirin is mainly a "mutagenesis" effect, inducing the "lethal" mutation of RNA strains. Therefore, the 4425th nucleotide mutation of CVA6 may be related to this effect, because, the 4425th nucleotide mutation was not found in CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T, because their high fidelity is not easy to be induced by ribavirin. One-day-old ICR mice appeared a useful in vivo model for virulence assessment, as the rescued CVA6 caused signs such as mental depression and hind limb paralysis after intramuscular injection (Fig. 2D), whereas histopathological analysis revealed virus produced lesions in lung, muscle, and brain tissue. Our results revealed that ICR suckling mice infected with CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T mutants had reduced mortality, delayed onset of clinical signs, and significantly milder pathological manifestation in brain, lung, and hind limb muscles than the CVA6 infected group.

The results of ribavirin susceptibility test showed that the mutation of the 64th amino acid in the 3D region of CVA6 to serine S or threonine T, reducing the sensitivity to ribavirin. The guanidine hydrochloride experiment showed that after the 64th amino acid mutation in the 3D region, the CVA6 strain was more sensitive to the guanidine hydrochloride mutagen, further indicating that the growth ability of the mutant strain decreased in the presence of guanidine hydrochloride mutagens. Ribavirin induced mutations of CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T were much lower than that in CVA6, indicating that mutations at amino acid position 64 of 3D polymerase also improved the replication fidelity of CVA6. When plotting growth kinetics, although there was no statistical difference in the replication rates of CVA6, CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T in cells, it cannot be denied that from the growth kinetics, we can observe that the replication rate of CVA6 is slightly higher than that of CVA6-G64S and CVA6-G64T. Therefore, we believe that replication rate may have an impact on mutation frequency, but it is not the key factor. The results of this study showed that, without statistically significant differences in replication rates, the mutation at aa-64 did improve the replication fidelity of ribavirin.

The mutation of 64th amino acid in 3D polymerase can indeed serve as a starting point for the development of a safe live attenuated CVA6 vaccine, but introduction mutations at this site alone may not be sufficient. If combined with other attenuated sites or RdRp mutants with associated recombination defects, it may be more conducive to the research of safe live attenuated vaccines.

In summary, we found that the 3D polymerase G64S and G64T mutations attenuate the virulence and improve the replication fidelity of CVA6, providing further insights into the molecular basis of CVA6 pathogenesis and providing theoretical support for the development of live attenuated CVA6 vaccines with high safety. Although the 64-amino acid mutation of 3D polymerase can improve the replication fidelity of CVA6 and can be used as a starting point for the development of safe vaccines, it is not enough to study the mutation site alone. It may be a new idea to combine with other safety characteristics (such as recombination defective RdRp mutants) or other attenuation mutations.
