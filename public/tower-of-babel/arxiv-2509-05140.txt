Swarming and Flocking Unified Through Behavioral Imperfections
Joao Lizárraga, Marcus de Aguiar

Abstract
Natural flocks (aligned) and swarms (non-aligned) both exhibit features of criticality, challenging their treatment as two ends of the same phase transition. We present a model for the cohesion of active individuals, who spontaneously self-assemble into a near-critical state, whether aligned or not. In our framework, these individuals move in open space and possess differing self-propelling velocities. The emerging velocity fluctuations, shown to be correlated and scale-invariant, are triggered by inherent noise rooted in individual imperfections. Using the finite-size scaling theory, we show that our system exhibits criticality features observed in groups of midges and starlings.



Swarming and flocking are ubiquitous. Although commonly associated with insects
Yates et al. 2009
;
Sayin et al. 2025
and birds
Ballerini et al. 2008
;
Bialek et al. 2012
, similar behaviors are also exhibited by fish
Zada et al. 2024
;
Puy et al. 2024
and mammals
Gómez-Nava et al. 2022
;
Yan et al. 2024
, among others. In these systems, groups form mediated by the self-organization of individuals who seem to follow similar interaction rules. One of these, the alignment of orientations, paved the way for the theoretical study of collective phenomena, as it prompted the formulation of the seminal Vicsek model
Vicsek et al. 1995
.

In Vicsek’s framework, individuals with identical velocities are set to move in a periodic space; coherence is promoted by mirroring neighboring orientations. Then, depending on the noise level and individual density, a (pseudo) second order transition arises between aligned and non-aligned states
Chaté et al. 2008
;
Baglietto and Albano 2008
. Its success in reproducing these behaviors led to its subsequent adoption as an almost universal tool for addressing related problems
Peruani et al. 2008
;
Toner and Tu 1998
;
Farrell et al. 2012
;
Miguel et al. 2018
;
Grégoire and Chaté 2004
;
Barberis and Peruani 2016
;
Múgica et al. 2022
;
Cavagna et al. 2023a
;
Caprini and Löwen 2023
;
Shi et al. 2025
. However, as near-criticality is identified following the traditional theory of phase transitions, it raises the question of whether the critical state is truly flocking (aligned) or swarming (non-aligned).

Previous work in swarms of midges suggested that the Vicsek model is better suited for weakly polarized systems
Attanasi et al. 2014a
. Yet, since criticality is also observed in highly polarized ones
Cavagna et al. 2010
, it leads us to infer that their underlying nature may be inherently different. This hypothesis is particularly striking, as both types of systems seem to be governed by similar core behavioral rules. Moreover, since the Vicsek model exhibits a standard phase transition, it has also led to assuming that a control parameter, such as density
Attanasi et al. 2014a
or noise
Cavagna et al. 2010
, must be fine-tuned by natural systems to reach a near-critical state.

The problem we address in this Letter is framed by the hypotheses described above. Since our interest lies in systems studied thus far within the conventional theory of criticality, we examine our system’s properties using the finite-size scaling (FSS) theory.

Although our model is as simple as Vicsek’s, the approach we present rests on a different mechanism. Instead of focusing on velocity alignment, we prioritize spatial cohesion. We believe this represents a more realistic scenario, as in many natural systems individuals gather to search for food
Schrandt and Powers 2015
, to look for mating partners
Attanasi et al. 2014b
, or to face predators
Cavagna and Giardina 2014
. Once grouped, they behave as a whole. We assume, moreover, that individuals do not follow the system’s interaction rules perfectly. These imprecisions give rise to our model’s most important feature: fluctuations are considered to be a result of internal rather than external factors. Therefore, their emergence can be interpreted as the population’s engagement in restoring the system’s equilibrium, rather than as a sign of its malfunction.

In our system of
N
particles, the dynamics of the
i
-th individual are fundamentally governed by

\dot{\vec{r}}_{i}=\vec{f}_{i}(t)-\tilde{\alpha}\nabla_{\vec{r}_{i}}V_{i}(\vec{r}),

(1)

where
\vec{r}_{i}
denotes its
n
-dimensional position, and
V_{i}(\vec{r})
, scaled by a coupling strength
\tilde{\alpha}
, characterizes how the individual’s velocity is influenced by its own state and that of its neighbors; the argument
\vec{r}=(\vec{r}_{1},\vec{r}_{2},\dots,\vec{r}_{N})
indicates that
V_{i}
depends on the coordinates of all individuals. The individual’s self-propulsion (i.e., its velocity when isolated) is outlined by the function
\vec{f}_{i}(t)
.

Individual dynamics are coupled through the potential

V_{i}(\vec{r})=\frac{r_{i}^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\vec{r}_{i}\cdot\vec{r}_{j},

with
r_{i}=|\vec{r}_{i}|
. Depending on
\tilde{\alpha}
, then, this parabolic well drives
\vec{r}_{i}
either to instability or to a specific time-dependent state; and, when plugged into Eq. (
1
), it leads to

\dot{\vec{r}}_{i}=\vec{f}_{i}(t)+\tilde{\alpha}\left(\langle\vec{r}\rangle-\vec{r}_{i}\right),

(2)

which unveils the system’s core behavior. Here,
\langle\vec{r}\rangle
is the population’s center of mass (mean position), and under any condition, the equality
\langle\dot{\vec{r}}\rangle=\langle\vec{f}(t)\rangle
holds.

Rewriting Eq. (
2
) in terms of the distance between the focal individual and the center of mass,
\vec{u}_{i}=\vec{r}_{i}-\langle\vec{r}\rangle
, reveals a key insight: choosing
\tilde{\alpha}>0
will always drive the system towards a stable state;
\vec{r}_{i}
will be
pulled
by
\langle\vec{r}\rangle
. Despite this
attractive
effect, however, the only scenario in which
\vec{r}_{i}=\langle\vec{r}\rangle
can occur is yielded by
\vec{f}_{i}(t)=\vec{0}
, representing the collapse of all individuals into a static point. In contrast, when
\vec{f_{i}}(t)\neq\vec{0}
, the distance towards the center of mass,
\vec{u}_{i}
, converges to
\left[\vec{f}_{i}(t)-\langle\vec{f}(t)\rangle\right]/\tilde{\alpha}
. That is, cohesion is stable and individuals display an
apparent repulsion
from the center of mass. Once the system reaches this state, individual velocities are
identical
to that of the center of mass:
\dot{\vec{r}}_{i}=\langle\dot{\vec{r}}\rangle
, and so are their magnitudes (i.e., for
\langle\vec{f}(t)\rangle\neq\vec{0}
, velocities align). For the sake of simplicity, hereafter we consider constant preferred headings (self-propelling velocities),
\vec{f}_{i}(t)=\vec{\omega}_{i}
, which do not affect the system’s ability to undergo the phenomena of interest. See Supplemental Material for spiral motion.

Although so far not explicitly described as part of the model, behavioral rules are continuously at play. Each individual, driven by a preferred heading, seeks to follow its own trajectory, yet, this is disrupted by the
attraction
exerted from the population’s center of mass. Steady cohesion manifests, in general, for a positive
constant
coupling strength, as it guarantees that a
balance
between behavioral rules will be achieved effectively. During the transient, this dichotomy leads to the emergence of non-zero
\dot{\vec{u}}_{i}
, which then fade gradually as the population approaches the equilibrium. Once settled, shifting the population’s reference frame to that of its center of mass reveals that velocity fluctuations have vanished, as per the result of alignment shown earlier (
\dot{\vec{r}}_{i}=\langle\dot{\vec{r}}\rangle
).

In nature, nevertheless, achieving a
perfect balance
between behavioral rules is not the norm. Individual errors in obeying these, even while nearly aligned, give rise to non-zero
\dot{\vec{u}}_{i}

Cavagna et al. 2010
, also referred to as
inherent noise

Yates et al. 2009
. In our system, we emulate these errors by considering noise related directly to the individuals’ responses. For this, we redefine the coupling strength in Eq. (
2
) as
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha+\varphi(t)
, where
\alpha
is a constant and
\varphi(t)
is a random variable with
\langle\varphi\rangle=0
. Notably, independent of the statistics underlying
\varphi(t)
, as long as
\tilde{\alpha}>0
individuals will be
pulled
by the center of mass, as glimpsed earlier. This condition holds even if
\tilde{\alpha}
becomes temporarily negative.

For the results presented subsequently, we consider individuals moving in three dimensions while affected by
\varphi(t)\in[-D_{\varphi},D_{\varphi}]
. Their preferred headings have a reference norm
|\langle\vec{\omega}_{i}\rangle|=\upsilon
, yet components of
\vec{\omega}_{i}
are uniformly distributed between
\vec{\omega}_{\min}
and
\vec{\omega}_{\max}
(see Supplemental Material for details). Consistent with the convergence relationship,
\vec{u}_{i}=(\vec{\omega}_{i}-\langle\vec{\omega}\rangle)/\tilde{\alpha}
, the system’s linear size relates to the coupling strength as
L\sim\tilde{\alpha}^{-1}
[Fig.
1
(a)]. Moreover, as observed in the inset, since the average nearest-neighbor
distance scales with the density as
\ell\sim\rho^{-1/3}
, the relationship
L\sim N^{1/3}\ell
also holds. That is, the system’s linear size is affected by the number of individuals but also by the coupling strength. Using these relations, and given that the distribution governing
\varphi(t)
is symmetric, we can formulate the system’s defining feature of our system as

\alpha\ell\sim N^{-1/3}.

(3)

Ultimately, this structural restriction is what ensures its near-critical behavior, as we show below.

The polarization of the system is measured through the well-known order parameter
v_{a}=N^{-1}\left|\sum_{i}\left(\dot{\vec{r}}_{i}/\dot{r}_{i}\right)\right|

Vicsek et al. 1995
;
Cavagna et al. 2010
, and, as expected, the increase of
D_{\varphi}
results in the decrease of
v_{a}
[Fig.
1
(b)]. Despite the apparent independence of
v_{a}
on parameters other than
D_{\varphi}
, increasing
\alpha
induces a bend in the curve. However, since polarization before that region is already low, its relevance is disregarded. The inset, showing a snapshot of the collective state of the system, highlights the role of velocity fluctuations: as individuals divert, they are
pulled back
. Conversely, if individuals get too close to the center of mass, they are
pushed away
. This behavior depicts the system’s attempt at restoring its equilibrium.

From Fig.
1
, in accordance with models studied within the standard theory of phase transitions, the noise amplitude,
D_{\varphi}
, appears to be a control parameter. This hypothesis is supported by the noise-driven transition of the system between aligned and non-aligned states; suggesting, moreover, the existence of a critical state along the way. To address this, we study the scaling behavior of the system as it provides insights on its possible states.

We start by defining the correlation function

C(\epsilon)=\frac{\sum_{ij}\vec{\sigma}_{i}\cdot\vec{\sigma}_{j}\delta(\epsilon-r_{ij})}{\sum_{ij}\delta(\epsilon-r_{ij})},

(4)

where
r_{ij}=|\vec{r}_{i}-\vec{r}_{j}|
, and
\vec{\sigma}_{i}=\dot{\vec{u}}_{i}/\sqrt{\langle|\dot{\vec{u}}|^{2}\rangle}
. Fig.
2
(a) shows that, since the system is not in the thermodynamic limit, fluctuations are positively correlated up to a threshold, thus characterizing the zero-crossing correlation length:
C(\epsilon=\xi_{0})=0
(see Supplemental Material for a comparison with the second moment definition).

Given the system’s finite size, the correlation length is truncated such that, if the system is in the near-critical region, the scaling relationship
\xi_{0}\sim L
will hold. Therefore, if the role of
D_{\varphi}
were that of controlling the system’s state, its effect would be evidenced by the shape of
\xi_{0}(L)
, as it would flatten when the system is far from criticality. Nevertheless, as shown in Fig.
2
(b), the linear relationship between
\xi_{0}
and
L
is maintained for different noise levels. This strongly suggests that
D_{\varphi}
does not drive the system through any
traditional
phase transition (even though it affects the system’s polarization, as shown in Fig.
1
). Moreover, what is more remarkable is that correlations are scale invariant. To support this, in Fig.
2
(c) we present the collapse of correlation functions for different linear sizes of the system. Rescaling the index distance to
\hat{\epsilon}=\epsilon/\xi_{0}
ensures that all curves share the same zero-crossing position:
C(\hat{\epsilon}=1)=0
(i.e., the system’s linear size is bypassed). The fit of one curve onto one another thus implies that, at any finite scale, the system shares the same correlation features.

Similar to the insights revealed by analyzing the behavior of the correlation lengths, the near-critical state of a system can be characterized by exploring its susceptibility. Since our system is finite, we measure the susceptibility by using the proxy
Attanasi et al. 2014a

\chi=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{ij}\vec{\sigma}_{i}\cdot\vec{\sigma}_{j}H(\xi_{0}-r_{ij}),

where
H(.)
is the Heaviside function. Then, in the region of near-criticality, the effects associated to the population’s size are expected to follow the relation

\chi\sim N^{\gamma/3\nu},

(5)

which, as shown in Fig.
3
(a), holds for our system. Then, from Figs.
2
and
3
, we conclude that our system remains in a near-critical state, even for different degrees of polarization and population sizes.

To address the feature of self-tuned near-criticality, observed in natural swarms
Attanasi et al. 2014a
, we first need to establish a control parameter. In our system, distances between individuals (
\ell
) are observed to adjust actively during the formation and maintenance of the cohesive group. Then, as also hypothesized in natural swarms
Attanasi et al. 2014b
, the control parameter is chosen to be proportional to it; we define

\tau=\alpha\ell\upsilon^{-1}.

(6)

The importance of nondimensionalizing
\ell
is highlighted in Fig.
3
, where the curves corresponding to different events, caused by different coupling strengths, collapse.

In the finite-size domain, the near-criticality of a system is characterized by its susceptibility peaking at different size-dependent pseudocritical points. Thus, if the system is in this regime, the relation

(\tau-\tau_{c})\sim N^{-1/3\nu}

(7)

must hold. The critical point (
\tau_{c}
) can be estimated by extrapolating towards the thermodynamic limit. The data obtained in our system shows that, for any combination of parameters which contemplate the initial equilibrium condition (
\tilde{\alpha}>0
), the FSS relation introduced in Eq. (
7
) is met [Fig.
3
(b)]. To complement this finding, in Fig.
3
(c) we show that our system also fulfills the relation

\chi\sim(\tau-\tau_{c})^{-\gamma}.

(8)

These results thus suggest that the control parameter of our system [Eq. (
6
)], associated with the distances between neighbors, is self-tuned so that the system is maintained in the region of near-criticality.

.

Following the hyperscaling relation
\gamma=\nu(2-\eta)
, the anomalous dimension is estimated to be
\eta\approx-1
. To test the validity of this value, we independently estimate
\eta
by using the derivative of the correlation function [Eq. (
4
)], evaluated for the rescaled index distance as
Cavagna et al. 2010

C^{\prime}(\hat{\epsilon}=1)=-1/\xi_{0}^{1+\eta}.

The flat trend followed by
|C^{\prime}|
with respect to
\xi_{0}
, shown in Fig.
3
’s inset, reveals that the anomalous dimension is, indeed,
\eta\approx-1
.

The constant relative correlation (
\xi_{0}/L
) might be explained by the emergence of the so-called Goldstone modes, as these are expected to show up around the polarization direction when the rotational symmetry is broken
Amit and Martin-Mayor 2005
. However, consistent with the behavior observed in natural collectives
Cavagna et al. 2010
;
Attanasi et al. 2014a
, correlations in our system are also observed between speed fluctuations,
s_{i}=(\dot{r}_{i}-\langle\dot{r}\rangle)/\sqrt{\langle\dot{r}^{2}\rangle-\langle\dot{r}\rangle^{2}}
(i.e., perpendicular to the Goldstone modes). The definition of
C_{s}(\epsilon)
is similar to Eq. (
4
), except that the fluctuation variables are swapped (
\dot{\vec{u}}_{i}\rightarrow s_{i}
), and the inner product becomes a scalar product (see Supplemental Material). The speed correlation lengths,
\xi_{s}
, are calculated using the zero crossing of
C_{s}(\epsilon)
. Then, by rescaling the index distances as
\hat{\epsilon}_{s}=\epsilon/\xi_{s}
, we obtain the collapsed speed correlation functions shown in Fig.
4
(a). Consistent with the velocity correlations, speed correlations are also scale-free [Fig.
4
(b)], and, the anomalous dimension is
\eta_{s}\approx-1
[Fig.
4
(c)].

The anomalous dimension calculated for our system suggests that correlations decay extremely slowly across space. This value, however, aligns closely with the one estimated for real murmurations
Cavagna et al. 2010
. Although this result is striking, it is not a mere coincidence; rather, it reinforces the validity of our model describing the criticality of the referred type of systems. Since individuals own preferred headings, the fluctuation effects are different from those in equilibrium systems. If a single individual slows down suddenly, it forces the ones in its surroundings to slow down as well. This propagates, and the neighbors of those individuals eventually decelerate. This positive feedback promotes the emergence of long-range correlations, characterized by
\eta<0
(SM9 evidences this phenomenon). It is worth mentioning that, in swarms of midges, although different than ours, the estimated anomalous dimension is also negative (
\eta\approx-0.57
)
Attanasi et al. 2014a
.

The critical exponents obtained using the FSS relations [Eqs. (
5
), (
7
), and (
8
)] differ significantly from those found for natural systems
Cavagna et al. 2010
;
Attanasi et al. 2014a
and classical universality classes
Amit and Martin-Mayor 2005
. This contrast might suggest that while our core mechanism captures the essence of self-tuned criticality, additional factors like hydrodynamic interactions or anisotropic perception, present in real systems, could modify the universality class.

The characteristic length we use in Eq. (
6
),
\upsilon\alpha^{-1}
, can be interpreted as the length scale over which an individual
forgets
its deviation from the group. Although this ensures consistency with the structural restriction defined in Eq. (
3
), it differs from its counterpart in natural swarms: the interaction ranges
Attanasi et al. 2014a
. However, despite its mean-field nature, by means of a percolation argument
Attanasi et al. 2014b
we can show that
effective
local interactions emerge in our system. This thus allows us to interpret our model in the context of swarms with metric interactions (see the demonstration in Supplemental Material). Note that the explicit restriction on interacting neighbors, whether in a metric or topological sense, may carry a different meaning than in alignment-based models. This raises the issue of whether real individuals interact solely influenced by the perception of neighboring orientations, or whether they prefer moving towards a local center of mass. In our model, for instance, spatial anisotropy and local domains can be generated by redefining preferred headings rather than explicitly imposing local interactions. Specific related effects will be explored in a future paper.

We have demonstrated that flocking and swarming can coexist in a system governed by the same behavioral rules. In contrast to the traditional paradigm outlined by the Vicsek model, our framework allows both aligned and non-aligned states to exhibit features of criticality; achieved by focusing on individuals’ spatial cohesion and
inherent noise
. The latter, rooted in behavioral errors, promotes a positive feedback loop between velocities and local densities, crucial for the emergence of long-range correlations. The system’s convergence towards criticality is consistent with the self-tuning exhibited by natural swarms. This behavior, which stems from a structural property of the model, is reflected in the dynamic modulation of nearest-neighbor distances.

Appendix S1
Numerics

All computations were performed over
5\times 10^{4}
time-steps (with
\Delta t=0.01
), starting with individuals uniformly distributed within a cube of side length
10
(see SM4 for a case where the initial side length is
5
). In the case of constant preferred headings, these were chosen relative to a reference norm,
\upsilon=|\langle\vec{\omega}\rangle|
. That is, the components of
\vec{\omega}_{i}
were drawn from uniform distributions within the ranges defined by
\vec{\omega}_{\min}=[\omega_{x}^{\min},\omega_{y}^{\min},\omega_{z}^{\min}]
and
\vec{\omega}_{\max}=[\omega_{x}^{\max},\omega_{y}^{\max},\omega_{z}^{\max}]

\omega_{i,x}\in[0,13]
,

\omega_{i,z}\in[0,5]

\upsilon\approx 7
. Furthermore, using this definition, the system’s long-term linear size can be estimated by assuming that components of

10^{4}
time-steps of each simulation. Moreover, when calculating the correlation functions, the Dirac delta function,

Appendix S2
Effective locality

The characteristic length used in the main text is
\lambda_{\alpha}=\upsilon\alpha^{-1}
. In natural swarms, however, its definition relies on individual interaction ranges (
\lambda
), leading to the control parameter
\tau^{(\lambda)}=\ell/\lambda

Attanasi et al. 2014a
. Given the mean-field nature of our system, it seems impossible to define any
\lambda
under the same interpretation. However, we can estimate an
effective
range of interaction by following a percolation argument used in the study of midges
Attanasi et al. 2014b
.

On this basis, clusters are formed by individuals that meet the condition
\ell<\lambda
. The percolation threshold,
\lambda_{p}
, is defined as the range where the largest established cluster—labeled
giant
and composed of
n

\lambda
for which
n/N=0.6
[inset of Fig.
S1
(a)]. For our system, as shown in Fig.
S1
(a), the percolation thresholds estimated for different events relate linearly to the respective nearest-neighbor distances, as
\lambda_{p}\approx 1.66\ell
. It is worth noting that the proportionality constant is very close to the one estimated for swarms of midges
Attanasi et al. 2014b
.

Appendix S4
Macroscopic properties

All the relations used in the main text are standard in the study of near-critical active systems
Cavagna et al. 2010
;
Baglietto and Albano 2008
;
Attanasi et al. 2014a
;
Amit and Martin-Mayor 2005
. In this section, we briefly review their derivation. Following the scaling hypothesis, the ansatz for finite-size systems allows for the definition of the correlation length as

\xi(L)=Lf\left(\frac{L}{\xi}\right),

(S3)

which leads to the relation
\xi\sim L
, fitted to the data in Figs. 2(b) and 4(b) of the main text. The size dependence of the correlation length can also be written as

\xi(L)=(\tau-\tau_{c})^{-\nu}g\left(\frac{L}{\xi}\right),

which together with Eq. (
S3
) and the relation
L\sim N^{1/3}
, reveals that
(\tau-\tau_{c})\sim N^{-1/3\nu}
[Eq. (7) of the main text].

In the thermodynamic limit,
\xi
is expected to diverge if the state is critical; however, when the system is finite, the maximum correlation is restricted by
L
. Moreover, since
\sum_{i}\dot{\vec{u}}_{i}=0
, the correlation function is divided into regions with positive and negative amplitudes. For the results in the main text,
\xi
is chosen as a proxy for where the correlations become irrelevant; that is, when their amplitudes turn negative. This choice relies on our intention to contrast our results with those of natural systems
Cavagna et al. 2010
;
Attanasi et al. 2014a
, where a similar methodology was employed. The validity of
\xi_{0}
is complemented by Fig.
S6
, where the correlation length is calculated using its second moment definition

\xi_{2}^{2}=\frac{\int\epsilon^{2}C(\epsilon)d^{3}\epsilon}{\int\epsilon C(\epsilon)d^{3}\epsilon}.

Then, despite the different definitions,
\xi_{0}
and
\xi_{2}
are shown to relate linearly [Fig.
S6
(b)], and therefore, the scale invariance holds [Fig.
S6
(b)].

Figure S6:
Second moment correlation lengths against (a) the zero-crossing definition and (b) the linear size of the system. The events in both panels correspond to
\alpha\in[0.8,5.2].

In the finite-size domain, the correlation function can be written as

C(\epsilon;L)=\epsilon^{-(1+\eta)}g\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\xi};\frac{L}{\xi}\right),

which, by taking advantage of the system’s scaling properties, takes the form

C(\epsilon)=\xi^{-(1+\eta)}\hat{g}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\xi}\right).

Then, its derivative at the zero-crossing point,
|C^{\prime}(\hat{\epsilon}=1)|\sim\xi^{-(1+\eta)}
, allows us to calculate the system’s anomalous dimension by fitting values of
C^{\prime}(\hat{\epsilon}=1)
for different system sizes [Figs. 3(c) and 4(c) in the main text]. If the evaluation, instead, centers on the speed fluctuations, the only difference is given by the definition of the correlation function:

C_{s}(\epsilon)=\frac{\sum_{ij}s_{i}s_{j}\delta(\epsilon-r_{ij})}{\sum_{ij}\delta(\epsilon-r_{ij})},

as the subsequent procedure to calculate the anomalous dimension is the same.

Using the scaling hypothesis, we can define the susceptibility as

\chi(L)=L^{\gamma/\nu}f\left(\frac{L}{\xi}\right),

(S4)

which leads to
\chi\sim N^{\gamma/3\nu}
, as defined in Eq. (5) of the main text. By redefining the scaling function of Eq. (
S4
), the size dependence also leads to
\chi\sim(\tau-\tau_{c})^{-\gamma}
, which is defined in Eq. (8) of the main text.

The susceptibility proxy defined in the main text is a special result of the fluctuation-dissipation relation

\chi\sim\int C(r)d^{3}r,

evaluated up to
\xi
, with the constant of proportionality chosen to be the density
\rho
. This form, defined to deal with natural swarms, has been shown to be consistent with the definition of the standard susceptibility
Attanasi et al. 2014a

\hat{\chi}=\frac{N}{\sigma^{2}}\mathrm{Var}[v_{a}].

In Fig.
S7
, we show the equivalence between these definitions by using data obtained from our system. It is worth noting that the definition of the finite-size proxy provides additional robustness against noise-based deviations. This, in turn, allows susceptibility data from different events to be collapsed. Using the standard definition, different noise levels may cause variations in susceptibility measurements, as depicted by the differences between Figs.
S7
(a) and
S7
(b). Despite these, however, another signature of the system’s criticality is evidenced: the relation
\chi\sim\hat{\chi}
is maintained.

Appendix S5
The characteristic length

Even though the coupling strength determines the rate at which the system converges to equilibrium, understanding its physical significance can be intricate. The model’s dimensional consistency suggests that
\alpha
has units of
1/\text{time}
. We thus consider it a time
rescaler
, and interpret the system’s characteristic length as the distance traveled by an individual during a unit of rescaled time. A similar definition is found in Active Brownian Particle models
Marchetti et al. 2016
, where the characteristic length quantifies an individual’s
directional memory
. However, in natural swarms like those of mosquitoes, the characteristic length is assumed to depend explicitly on interaction ranges
Attanasi et al. 2014b
;
Attanasi et al. 2014a
. That is, it may have different meanings depending on the system’s nature.

Since our goal is that of describing collective states exhibited by natural systems, we seek a connection to their physical significance. We have already shown that, using the same argument applied to mosquitoes, the characteristic length of our system (
\lambda_{\alpha}
) can be associated to the individual interaction ranges. However, we can propose something bolder based on the system’s nature. Let us assume that the development of an individual’s body is governed by an allometric relation of the form
\lambda_{\beta}=k\alpha^{-1}
. Here,
\alpha
represents the frequency at which individuals communicate,
k
is the signal’s speed, and
\lambda_{\beta}
is the size of the receptor evolved to receive a specific signal wavelength. Thus, the characteristic length of the system could be defined by
\lambda_{\beta}
, which also satisfies the structural property of our model. This type of relation has already been studied in insects
Diakova et al. 2022
;
Cocroft and De Luca 2006
;
Virant-Doberlet et al. 2023
;
Yack 2016
. In midges, indeed, the range of interactions has been suggested to be proportional to individual body lengths
Attanasi et al. 2014b
, which essentially implies an allometric relation.

The way our hypothesis is presented adds relevance to the argument of natural individuals evolving to behave in a near-critical state. However, rather than doing so to experience optimal levels of noise, as suggested for starlings
Cavagna et al. 2010
and midges
Attanasi et al. 2014a
, their bodies (or parts of them) may have evolved to guarantee optimal communication with their neighbors.

Appendix S6
Additive noise

Our main interest has been in the effects of intrinsic individual errors that we assumed to emerge from internal sources. Given that additive noise is better suited for representing external factors, we did not place emphasis on it. There is, however, a point to remark on that does not require much analysis. Notice that if a random variable is considered, such that the system is governed by

\dot{\vec{r}}_{i}=\vec{f}_{i}(t)-\alpha\nabla_{\vec{r}_{i}}V(\vec{r})+\vec{\Omega}_{i}(t),

with a constant
\alpha
, then it follows an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. Consequently, the behavior of the system would be determined largely by the statistical properties of
\vec{\Omega}_{i}(t)
, masking the effects associated with the interaction rules (
inherent noise
). This does not mean, however, that additive noise cannot be considered in our system, but simply that it compromises its robustness. In fact, as the intensity of
\vec{\Omega}_{i}(t)
increases, the system’s polarization decreases; yet, in contrast to what occurs with multiplicative noise, correlations vanish.
